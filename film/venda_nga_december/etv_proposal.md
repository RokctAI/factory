## 1. LOGLINE

A man from Thohoyandou tattooed his dream on his chest before the world knew his name. This is the story of what it took to make that dream permanent — and what it cost to keep it there.

---

## 2. THE HIDDEN GEM — THOHOYANDOU AND VENRAP

South Africa knows Limpopo for the Kruger National Park. It knows it for the baobab trees and the Beit Bridge border. What it does not know — what it has never been shown — is that deep in the Vhembe District, in the streets and yards of Thohoyandou, a music culture grew without asking anyone's permission.

VenRap. Venda hip hop. A sound that carries the Tshivenda language, the Limpopo landscape, the spiritual depth of a people, and the hunger of young men who grew up in the northernmost corner of the country and decided the world needed to hear them.

Most South Africans have never heard of VenRap. Most South Africans could not find Thohoyandou on a map.

One man changed that. He carried Thohoyandou on his chest — literally — and took it to stages, studios and collaborations that the north had never seen before.

This documentary is the story of that man, that place, and that journey. It is a celebration of a hidden gem that has been producing extraordinary art in extraordinary circumstances, unseen and unsung, for decades.

It is also the most honest music documentary ever made about an artist from Limpopo — because it is not made by an outsider looking in. It is made by someone who was there at the beginning. Who designed the first album cover. Who watched from the same streets before anyone believed.

---

## 3. THE STORY

There is a tattoo on his chest. Three words: **VENDA NGA DECEMBER.**

He got it before the record deal. Before the national stages. Before the collaborations with artists the whole country knew. He got it when he was a young man from Thohoyandou with dreads, a deep faith, and a sound nobody had a name for yet.

The tattoo was a declaration. A vow. A flag planted in his own skin before the world decided whether he was worth anything.

This documentary never leaves that tattoo. Every conversation, every testimony, every moment of the film radiates outward from those three words and returns to them.

This is not a career documentary. It does not follow a timeline. It does not trace a discography or catalogue awards or interview industry executives about market positioning. It does not care about any of that.

It cares about one question:

**What does a man from Thohoyandou have to carry, sacrifice, and survive to make the world hear him — and what does he hold onto when everything else is negotiable?**

The answer lives in his chest.

---

## 4. THE SUBJECT — MIZO PHYLL

Mizo Phyll is the King of VenRap. He says so himself when he introduces himself in promotional videos. He says it in Tshivenda — *Ndi dzi king* — and he is not wrong.

He came from Thohoyandou when VenRap was not yet a genre anyone recognised. He carried the Rastafari faith — the dreads, the doctrine, the vows, the community of believers who saw in him a committed and serious member of their faith. He made hip hop that sounded like nowhere else in South Africa because it came from nowhere else in South Africa.

Then the music industry found him. And the industry had conditions.

The dreads came off.

What that meant — to him, to his faith community, to his wife, to the men who grew up alongside him in the same yards — is the emotional heart of this documentary.

He is also a man who worked with the late Ricky Rick. Who brought Venda to national stages. Who hosted shows that Thohoyandou still talks about. Who collaborated with artists from across the country while never removing the tattoo that said exactly where he was from.

He has agreed in principle to participate in this documentary. His name, his face, his chest — fully on camera. He has nothing to hide. He is the dream he tattooed on himself.

## 5. THE CHARACTERS

### Mizo Phyll — The Spine
*Full identity. Full face. The tattoo visible throughout.*

He is filmed first — before any other character — in one dedicated shoot day. He lays everything down. The childhood in Thohoyandou. The faith and what it meant. The dreads and what they cost to grow and what it cost to cut them. The early career when nobody believed. The moment the industry came. The collaboration with Ricky Rick and what that chapter meant and what was lost when it closed. The wife. The fans. The tattoo — when he got it, why, and what it means to still have it now.

He is the film's anchor. Its spine. Its senior voice.

Everything every other character says in this documentary is a response to his story — even though they will never hear it before they sit in that chair.

His estimated screen time: 10–12 minutes across 23 minutes.
His full recorded interview: 60–90 minutes.

---

### The Wife — The Most Intimate Voice
*Full identity. Full face. The most honest testimony in the film.*

She is the only person in this documentary who does not see the King of VenRap when she looks at him. She sees the man. The one who came home after the shows. The one whose dreads came off without her being the first to know. The one whose chest she has slept beside for years — the tattoo always there, always permanent, while everything else changed.

She is asked nothing about their private life. She is asked everything about what it feels like to watch someone become a symbol while you remain the person who knows them before breakfast.

Her testimony is the film's most intimate thread. Every other witness in the dark room sees an artist. She sees a husband. That difference is everything.

Questions she will be asked include:
- What did you think when you first heard his music?
- When did you understand that other women felt about him the way they did?
- What does the tattoo mean to you — you see it every day?
- Was there a moment you didn't recognise the person the world was talking about?
- What does Venda nga December mean in your house?

---

### Tman Gavin and Nicodemic — The Witnesses Who Stayed
*Negotiated identity. These are men who were in the same rooms, on the same streets, in the same early VenRap world — and the industry did not pick them. They are now driving taxis.*

They are not in this documentary to generate sympathy for themselves. They are here because they saw him before he was anything. They doubted him. They watched him prove them wrong. And now they drive taxis while he is the King of VenRap.

Their testimony is not about bitterness — it is about bearing witness. About what it means to be wrong about someone from your own streets. About what the same Thohoyandou produces differently in different people depending on what they were willing to carry and what they were willing to sacrifice.

Their most important contribution is this: they will tell the truth about what the early years actually looked like. Not the romantic version. The real version — the yards, the dismissal, the people who walked away from performances, the times nobody came.

And they will tell it with the particular honesty of men who have nothing left to protect.

---

### A Member of His Rastafari Community — The Faith Witness
*Identity negotiated with subject.*

Someone who knew him inside the Rastafari faith before the music career consumed everything. Someone who understood what the dreads represented — not as fashion, not as identity politics, but as genuine spiritual commitment.

When the dreads came off — this person felt it. Not necessarily as betrayal. Perhaps as grief. Perhaps as understanding. Perhaps as something more complicated than either.

They sit in the dark room and speak about what faith looks like when it meets ambition. About whether the music was always in tension with the doctrine. About what the Rastafari community in Thohoyandou makes of their most famous member now.

Their testimony gives the film its spiritual dimension — the part of his story that the industry chapter cannot contain.

---

### The Ricky Rick Chapter — Handled With Respect
The late Ricky Rick is not a character in this documentary. He is a presence. A chapter. A loss.

Mizo Phyll speaks about him from the dark room. A photograph appears — not cutting away, but floating into the frame behind him as he speaks, the way a memory surfaces. The collaboration, what it meant to be seen by someone at that level, what the KZN connection did for a young man from Thohoyandou, and what it felt like when that chapter closed the way it did.

This film honours that relationship with the restraint it deserves. One mention. One photograph. One moment of weight. Then it moves forward — the way he had to.

## 6. THE VISUAL APPROACH — THIS IS NOT A NORMAL DOCUMENTARY

Most South African music documentaries look the same. Archive footage. Township streets. Concert clips. Talking heads in familiar locations. A presenter walking the audience through a career.

This documentary does none of that.

### The Dark Room — One World, One Light

Every interview in this film takes place in a single controlled environment — a rented room in Thohoyandou dressed and lit as a cinematic dark studio. No natural light. No location establishing shots. No presenter walking anywhere.

One chair. One light. Four characters. One film.

This is a deliberate aesthetic decision that does several things simultaneously:

**It creates visual unity.** Every character exists in the same cinematic world regardless of their background, their outcome, their relationship to fame. The taxi driver and the king sit in the same chair under the same light. The room does not judge them. The audience does.

**It creates intimacy.** People speak differently in a dark controlled room. The outside world disappears. What remains is just the person, the light, and the truth they are willing to tell.

**It evokes the sacred.** The dark room with a single light source is instinctively read by audiences as a space of confession, revelation, and importance. The audience leans in without being asked to.

**It removes distraction.** There is nothing to look at except the person speaking and what appears beside or behind them. Every frame demands attention.

### The Light Knows Who the King Is

Mizo Phyll is lit fuller than anyone else in this film. More of his face. More of his chest. The tattoo visible when he leans forward. He owns the light because the film is his.

Every other character is lit slightly differently — one degree darker, one degree more shadow. The audience feels instinctively who the spine of this film is without being told.

### Images That Accompany, Never Interrupt

When a character references a photograph, a music video, an archive image or a moment from the past — that image does not interrupt the interview. There is no cut away. The speaker never disappears from the frame.

Instead the image appears alongside or partially behind the speaker — at the edge of the frame, or softly behind them at reduced opacity, like a memory surfacing in the room. The human being remains the dominant image at all times.

This is not a technical trick. It is a statement about how this film values its subjects. Their faces and their voices are always primary. Evidence supports them. It does not replace them.

### The Chest — The Film's Recurring Image

The tattoo on his chest is the film's visual heartbeat. It appears:

- As the opening image before any title or name
- As the transition device going into and coming out of each ad break — three words on skin, held in silence
- As a detail shot during his interview when the conversation touches the tattoo directly
- As the final image of the film

The audience sees those three words more than they see anything else in this documentary. By the end of 23 minutes they have memorised them. They understand them. They feel them.

**VENDA NGA DECEMBER** is not a tattoo by the time the film ends. It is a truth.

### Dramatic Reconstruction

For moments described by characters that cannot be filmed — the early years in Thohoyandou when nobody believed, the performances nobody came to, the first time someone laughed at the idea of a Rasta making hip hop — brief dramatic reconstructions are employed.

These sequences are:
- Shot in a warmer, slightly overexposed colour treatment that immediately signals reconstruction to the audience
- Silent — the character's own voice narrates over the image
- Brief — never more than 45 seconds per reconstruction
- Filmed in Thohoyandou using local performers

They are not used to dramatise anything that can be told in words. They are used only when an image will say what language cannot.

### The Final Performance

The documentary does not end with an interview. It ends with a performance.

After all testimony is complete — after the wife, the taxi drivers, the faith witness have all spoken — Mizo Phyll stands up in the dark room. The big coat goes on. The cameras that have watched him speak now watch him perform.

He raps. One or two verses. Alone in the room. No audience. No stage. No production. Just the dark room that held everyone's truth, and the King of VenRap doing what he was always going to do regardless of what anyone thought.

When he finishes — silence.

Cut to the chest. The tattoo. Three words.

**VENDA NGA DECEMBER.**

Fade to black.

## 7. THE MULTI-CAMERA SETUP

All interviews are captured using a multi-camera locked-off system. Multiple cameras are positioned and secured before each interview begins. This approach:

- Captures multiple angles simultaneously — wide, medium, close detail on hands and face — all from the same take
- Provides full editorial flexibility without interrupting the interview with camera movement
- Allows the director to function as the interviewer — fully present in the conversation, not managing equipment
- Is operated by a minimal crew of two people in the room — director/interviewer and sound recordist

This is not a limitation of budget. It is a deliberate production philosophy: the conversation is sacred. The cameras serve it. They do not direct it.

## 8. NARRATIVE ARC — 23 MINUTES

### Opening — Minutes 0 to 2
Black screen. A chest. The tattoo. **VENDA NGA DECEMBER.** No name. No title. No context.

The title sequence appears over the chest — one line at a time:

*AGAINST ALL ODDS*
**VENDA NGA DECEMBER**
*The King of VenRap*

Hold. Then cut to black. Then — his face. The dark room. The film begins.

---

### Act One: Thohoyandou Made Me — Minutes 2 to 7
Mizo Phyll speaks about where he came from. Not as nostalgia — as foundation. What Thohoyandou is. What VenRap is and where it grew from. What it meant to be a young man from the north with a sound that had no genre name yet.

The taxi drivers — separately, in the same chair — speak about the same streets. The same yards. The same early years. Their memory confirms and complicates his. The early dismissal. The laughing. The dramatic reconstruction of a young man performing to people who are not listening.

By the end of Act One the audience understands one thing: **this man had no reason to succeed. Every reason not to.**

---

### Act Two: The Faith and The Dreads — Minutes 7 to 13
The documentary's most spiritually complex chapter.

Mizo Phyll speaks about the Rastafari faith — what it meant to him, what the dreads represented, what kind of commitment he made and to whom. This is told with respect and specificity. Not explained for an audience that doesn't know. Shared by a man who lived it.

Then — the record company. The condition. The dreads that came off.

The faith witness sits in the same chair. They speak about what that meant from inside the faith community. Not necessarily as betrayal — but as a moment that required understanding, negotiation, or grief.

The wife speaks about it too. From the closest possible distance. What it looked like from home when something your husband vowed to keep was removed for reasons that had nothing to do with home.

The central question of this act is never asked directly. It hangs in the air between every answer:

*What are you willing to give up — and what are you not?*

The tattoo has never moved. The dreads are gone. The audience holds that.

---

### Act Three: The National Stage — Minutes 13 to 18
The chapter where Thohoyandou went with him everywhere he went.

Mizo Phyll speaks about the national stages. The collaborations. Working with the late Ricky Rick — a moment handled with weight and brevity and complete respect. What it meant to be seen at that level. What it cost. What it gave.

The taxi drivers respond — from their own chair, their own light — to the same journey from a different outcome. Not bitterness. Bearing witness. The particular honesty of men who watched someone from their streets go where they did not go.

The photographs and archive images float into the frame behind him as he speaks. They do not interrupt. They accompany. The audience sees the journey without leaving the room.

---

### Act Four: Venda Nga December — Minutes 18 to 23
The tattoo chapter. The permanent thing.

When did he get it. Why. What he knew about himself when he made it permanent in his skin before the world agreed.

The wife speaks about the tattoo from the place only she can speak from — the person who lives with it every day, who knows what those three words cost and what they refused to let go of.

Mizo Phyll's final words in the interview chair. What Thohoyandou means to him now. What VenRap is becoming. What he would say to the young man in the yard who nobody was listening to yet.

Then — he stands. The coat goes on. He performs.

Then — the chest. The tattoo. Silence. Black.

**VENDA NGA DECEMBER.**

## 13. PRESENTER / COMMENTATOR

This documentary does not use a presenter or narrator. The film is entirely built from the voices of its principal subjects.

This is a deliberate creative decision. A presenter's voice would impose a singular interpretation on a story whose power comes from allowing multiple truths to coexist in the same space. The dark room is the presenter. The tattoo is the narrator. The audience is the judge.

---

## 14. WHY THIS IS THE HIDDEN GEM

South Africa has never seen a documentary about VenRap. It has never been shown Thohoyandou as a music capital. It does not know that a man from the streets of Vhembe carried his hometown tattooed on his chest all the way to national stages and never took it off.

This is the hidden gem. Not just the artist — the entire culture he came from and represents. A music tradition that grew without permission, without industry support, without anyone in Johannesburg paying attention — and produced a King.

**VENDA NGA DECEMBER** is the story of that culture told through the one person who made it visible to the country. It celebrates Thohoyandou and Limpopo not with scenic shots and tourist framing but with the most powerful celebration available — **the honest story of what it took to come from there and make the world hear you.**

Every South African who has ever come from somewhere the country overlooked will recognise this film. Every person who has ever been told their hometown produces nothing worth hearing will feel this film. Every person who has ever had to negotiate between where they came from and where they are going will understand this film.

**That is a national audience. And this is their hidden gem.**
