### subtopic_1 — Satellites and Remote Sensing
**Assistant:** How does a satellite get its pictures down to Earth if it never lands?

**Tutor:** By radio. A satellite's sensors record the image as numbers, one value for each pixel in each band, and the satellite's computer stores and transmits those numbers to receiving stations on the ground using radio waves, much as a phone sends a photo over a mobile network, only from much further away and at much higher speeds. Large dish antennas track the satellite as it passes overhead and catch the signal. A polar-orbiting satellite is visible to any one ground station for only about ten to fifteen minutes on each pass, so it either sends its data during that window or stores it until it passes over another station. That is why there are receiving stations spread around the world, including SANSA's station at Hartebeesthoek, west of Pretoria, whose position in the Southern Hemisphere makes it valuable to international space agencies as well as to South Africa. Geostationary satellites are easier: because they stay in one place in the sky, a fixed dish pointing at them can receive their images continuously. Once the numbers reach the ground, computers correct them for the curvature of the Earth, the angle of the satellite and the effects of the atmosphere, fit them to a map grid so that each pixel has a latitude and longitude, and combine the bands into true-colour or false-colour pictures. The picture that appears on a weather report or a map app is therefore the end of a long chain of measurement, transmission and processing. Nothing is photographed on film, and nothing has to be dropped from space, as some early spy satellites once did with film canisters.

### subtopic_2 — Two Kinds of Orbit
**Assistant:** Why do geostationary satellites have to be above the equator?

**Tutor:** Because only an orbit centred on the equator can match the Earth's rotation exactly and stay fixed over one place. Every orbit is a circle or ellipse around the Earth's centre, so its path always cuts the Earth into two halves. An orbit tilted towards the poles must swing north of the equator for half of each orbit and south of it for the other half. Even if it took exactly twenty-four hours, a satellite in such an orbit would drift north and south in the sky over the course of each day, tracing a figure of eight as seen from the ground, and a fixed dish would lose it. Only an orbit lying in the plane of the equator keeps the satellite always over the same latitude, zero degrees. Then the height is fixed by physics. The higher a satellite flies, the slower it travels and the longer each orbit takes. Low satellites at a few hundred kilometres circle the Earth in about an hour and a half; the moon, nearly four hundred thousand kilometres away, takes about a month. At one particular height, about thirty-five thousand eight hundred kilometres, an orbit takes exactly one rotation of the Earth. Put a satellite there, above the equator, moving eastwards with the Earth's spin, and it appears to hang still. That is why the weather satellites watching Africa sit over the Gulf of Guinea, and why a satellite television dish in South Africa points permanently towards the northern sky, where the satellites are, slightly above the horizon. The one weakness of this arrangement is that the far polar regions are seen at a very low angle or not at all, which polar orbiters fill in.

### subtopic_3 — Pixels, Resolution and Colour
**Assistant:** If finer resolution shows more, why not use the sharpest images for everything?

**Tutor:** Because sharper images bring costs that make them the wrong choice for many questions. The first cost is data. Halve the size of the pixels and you need four times as many to cover the same area, because the number grows in both directions. An image with ten-metre pixels has nine times as many pixels as a thirty-metre image of the same area. Covering the whole of South Africa, about one point two million square kilometres, at under one metre would produce an enormous amount of data to store, send, process and pay for. The second cost is coverage. Very high-resolution satellites usually image narrow strips, often only ten or twenty kilometres wide, so covering a province can take weeks or months of passes, and clouds can spoil many of them. A thirty-metre Landsat image covers about one hundred and eighty-five kilometres by one hundred and eighty-five in a single scene. The third cost is money: most of the sharpest images are sold commercially, while Landsat and Sentinel images are free. So the choice follows the question. To map the extent of maize fields across the Free State, or to measure how much natural vegetation has been lost across a province over thirty years, ten- or thirty-metre images are ideal: they cover the area, they go back decades, and they cost nothing. To count the shacks in an informal settlement or check damage to individual houses after a flood, sub-metre images are needed. To follow a cold front, a weather satellite with kilometre-sized pixels is perfect. Matching the resolution to the purpose is the same skill as choosing the scale of a map.

### subtopic_4 — Uses of Satellite Images and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Can satellites see through clouds to the ground?

**Tutor:** Ordinary satellite cameras cannot, but some satellites can, and the difference is a useful lesson in how sensors work. Most Earth observation satellites, including Landsat and Sentinel-2, record reflected sunlight, visible and near-infrared. Clouds reflect that light strongly, so a cloudy day produces an image of white cloud tops and nothing of the ground beneath. This is a real problem in regions with frequent cloud, such as the tropics or the coast of KwaZulu-Natal in summer, and it is one reason why images must be chosen carefully by date. Thermal sensors measure heat, and clouds block that too, which is why weather satellites can measure the temperature of cloud tops: the coldest, highest cloud tops show the strongest thunderstorms. But there is a third kind of sensor that does see through cloud: radar. A radar satellite sends out its own pulses of microwave energy and records the echoes that bounce back. Microwaves pass through cloud, rain and smoke, and because the satellite supplies its own energy, it works at night as well as by day. The European Sentinel-1 satellites carry radar and are widely used to map floods, because floods usually happen under cloudy skies, and water shows up clearly on radar as very dark, smooth areas. Radar images look strange, in shades of grey with a grainy texture, and they need practice to interpret, but they are invaluable for disaster response. So the honest answer is: it depends on the sensor. Choosing the right sensor for the conditions is part of using satellite images well.
