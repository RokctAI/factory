# Part 1 — Expert

This session moves from how satellite images are made to how they are read. The anchor is a single true-colour image of South Africa on a winter's day: dark ocean on three sides, a long band of white cloud curving towards the Cape, a brown and red interior, green strips along the eastern escarpment and coast, and the grey sprawl of Gauteng. The governing idea is that an image carries no symbols or labels, so every feature must be identified from visual clues: tone and colour, texture, shape, size, pattern, shadow and location. Applied systematically, those clues reveal water, vegetation, land use and weather. Image interpretation is examinable this term, and it is extended in Term 3 to aerial photographs and in Grade 10 to 12 to orthophoto maps.

## Subtopic: The Interpreter's Clues

Experienced image interpreters use a short list of clues, and they use them in combination, because no single clue is usually enough.

Tone and colour describe how light or dark, and what colour, a feature appears. Deep clear water is very dark; fresh snow and thick cloud are brilliant white; bare sand is pale; forests are dark green in true colour.

Texture describes how smooth or rough a surface looks: the smooth even tone of a calm dam or a ploughed field compared with the rough, mottled look of a natural forest or a dense settlement.

Shape describes outlines. Human features usually have straight lines, right angles and regular curves: rectangular fields, circular irrigated plots, straight roads, the neat outline of an airport runway. Natural features usually have irregular shapes: winding rivers, ragged coastlines, branching valleys.

Size must be judged against the scale of the image. A dark patch a few pixels across might be a farm dam; one fifty kilometres long is a large reservoir or a lake.

Pattern describes how features are arranged: the regular grid of a planned suburb, the branching pattern of a river system, the patchwork of farms, the long lines of sand dunes.

Shadow, on high-resolution images, shows height: tall buildings, mountain ridges and cliffs cast shadows away from the sun, which in the Southern Hemisphere is usually in the northern sky.

Location and association describe what is found together: a pale scar beside a city is likely to be a mine or a quarry if roads and railways lead to it; a dark line winding through a valley lined with green is a river with trees along its banks.

Before applying the clues, an interpreter checks four things: the direction of north, usually at the top; the scale or resolution; the date; and whether the image is true colour or false colour. Then the work begins with the largest and most obvious features, the sea, the coastline, the major mountains, and moves to smaller ones.

The questions for this section are with you now: the clues used to interpret images, the four checks before starting, and the difference between natural and human shapes.

## Subtopic: Water and Vegetation

Water is usually the easiest feature to find. In true-colour images, deep clear water is very dark blue or almost black, because water absorbs most of the light that falls on it. Shallow water near a coast or over a sandbank looks lighter, often turquoise. Water carrying mud looks brown or khaki; after heavy rain, rivers such as the Orange and the Thukela carry plumes of brown sediment out into the sea, clearly visible on images. In false-colour images, where near-infrared is shown as red, water appears very dark, because water absorbs near-infrared almost completely, so dams and rivers stand out sharply from the red vegetation around them.

Dams have a recognisable shape: a smooth dark body of water with a straight edge where the dam wall holds it back, and branching arms reaching up the valleys that were flooded. The Gariep Dam on the Orange River, South Africa's largest reservoir by volume, and the Vaal Dam are easy to find on images of the interior. Salt pans and dry riverbeds are pale white or grey, a sign of water that has evaporated.

Vegetation shows in green in true colour and in red in false colour, and its tone and texture help to identify its type. Dense natural forest has a dark, rough, mottled texture. Commercial timber plantations of pine and eucalyptus, common in Mpumalanga and KwaZulu-Natal, are equally dark but appear as neat blocks with straight edges, often crossed by firebreaks. Grassland, such as the Highveld in summer, is lighter green with a smooth texture, turning brown in the dry winter. The semi-desert Karoo and Kalahari appear in shades of brown, red and grey with only faint green.

Farmland makes geometric patterns. A patchwork of rectangles shows dryland fields. Bright green circles, often several hundred metres across, show centre-pivot irrigation, in which a long arm of sprinklers rotates around a central point; they are common along the Orange River in the Northern Cape, where they stand out vividly against the dry land around them. The colour of the same field changes through the year as crops are planted, grow, ripen and are harvested.

The questions for this section are with you now: how water appears in true and false colour, how to recognise a dam, and how to tell forest, plantation, grassland and irrigated farmland apart.

## Subtopic: Land Use and Settlements

Land use is the way people use an area: for housing, industry, mining, farming, transport, conservation or recreation. Satellite images show land use mainly through colour, texture, shape and pattern.

Built-up areas appear grey, pale brown or bluish-grey in true colour, with a fine, busy texture made by roofs, roads and small gardens. Planned suburbs show regular grids or curving street patterns; the central business district of a city often appears as a dense, bright core, and on high-resolution images tall buildings cast long shadows. Informal settlements usually appear as very dense, finely textured areas with few trees and few large open spaces, often on the edges of cities, along railway lines or on land unsuitable for formal building. Large factories and warehouses show as big bright roofs beside roads and railways.

Mining leaves distinctive marks. Around Johannesburg, the old gold mine dumps and tailings dams, made of finely crushed rock left after the gold was extracted, appear as pale yellow, cream or white flat-topped shapes in a long east-west line, tracing the line of the Witwatersrand gold reef. Open-pit mines, such as the great iron ore and copper pits of the Northern Cape and Limpopo, appear as huge terraced holes, and coal mines in Mpumalanga as dark scars.

Transport networks show as lines. Major roads and railways are long and fairly straight, curving gently; airports have unmistakable runways. Harbours show as straight-edged basins of water protected by breakwaters, with quays lined with ships and containers, as at Durban and Saldanha Bay.

Conservation areas often show by contrast: the edge of a national park or game reserve may appear as a sharp boundary where the dense patchwork of farms and settlements on one side meets continuous natural vegetation on the other.

Comparing images of the same place from two dates shows change in land use. Between images years apart, new suburbs appear on what was farmland, informal settlements expand, mine dumps are reprocessed and shrink, and green belts disappear. This is called change detection, and it is one of the most valuable uses of satellite images for planners.

The questions for this section are with you now: how settlements, mines, transport and conservation areas appear, and what change detection means.

## Subtopic: Cloud Patterns and the Error Museum

Weather satellite images are read for cloud patterns, which reveal the weather systems that produce them.

A cold front appears as a long band of cloud, often hundreds of kilometres long, curving like a comma across the ocean south-west of South Africa and moving eastwards. As it passes over the Western Cape in winter, it brings cloud, wind, rain and a drop in temperature, followed by clear, cold air. Watching the band approach on a sequence of images is how forecasters time the warnings.

Thunderstorms appear as bright white, rounded clusters, often bubbling up over the Highveld on summer afternoons and fading by night. Their tops are very high and very cold, which makes them especially bright on infrared images.

Tropical cyclones appear as huge spirals of cloud, sometimes more than five hundred kilometres across, with a clear eye at the centre. In the Southern Hemisphere their spiral turns clockwise, and in summer they occasionally move across the Mozambique Channel towards Mozambique and Madagascar.

Fog and low stratus cloud appear as smooth, flat, grey-white sheets with sharp edges, often lying along the cold west coast, where moist ocean air is cooled over the Benguela Current. On images the fog often fills the coastal valleys while the land behind is clear.

Large areas of clear sky are just as informative. In winter, a high-pressure system usually sits over the interior, giving cloudless skies over most of South Africa while the cold fronts pass to the south.

Images also show what the air carries besides cloud. Smoke from large veld fires and from the burning of sugar cane fields in KwaZulu-Natal appears as grey-blue plumes drifting downwind from a bright source. Dust lifted by strong winds over the dry west and the Kalahari appears as a pale brown haze that blurs the ground beneath it. Plumes from power stations on the Mpumalanga Highveld can sometimes be traced on high-resolution images. Distinguishing smoke and dust from cloud uses the same clues as on the ground: colour, texture, shape and source. Smoke and dust have soft, streaky edges and can be traced back to a point or an area on the ground, while cloud usually has firmer edges and a whiter tone.

Infrared images work by temperature rather than light, so they can be used at night. The rule for reading them: the brighter the white, the colder and usually the higher the cloud top. High, cold cloud is often associated with heavy rain.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: identifying a feature from one clue alone, such as calling every dark patch water; shadows and burnt veld are dark too. Two: mistaking the pale mine dumps of the Witwatersrand for cloud or salt pans; their position along the reef and their flat tops give them away. Three: reading a false-colour image as true colour. Four: forgetting the date, so that winter-brown grassland is described as desert. Five: describing a cold front as a cyclone; a front is a long band, a cyclone a spiral with an eye.

Layout for marks in an identify question: name the feature, give the clues that identify it, and add one sentence of significance. For example: a long curved band of cloud south-west of the country, moving east; a cold front; it will bring rain and cold to the Western Cape.

The questions for this section are with you now: cold fronts, thunderstorms, cyclones and fog on images, how to read infrared images, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: detective clues, water and plants and people, and reading the sky from above.

## Subtopic: Be a Detective

A satellite picture has no labels. Nobody has written dam, forest or city on it. You have to work it out, like a detective.

Detectives use clues. The colour or darkness of something: water is dark, clouds are white, sand is pale. The roughness: a calm dam is smooth, a forest looks bumpy. The shape: people make straight lines, squares and circles, while nature makes wiggly ones. Rivers wiggle; roads do not. The size: a tiny dark dot is a farm dam, a huge dark shape is a big reservoir. The pattern: neat grids are suburbs, a patchwork is farms. And what it is near: a pale patch with roads and railways leading to it is probably a mine.

Before you start, check four things. Which way is north? How much detail does the picture show? When was it taken? Is it normal colour or false colour?

Then start big. Find the sea and the coast first, then the mountains, then the cities, then the small things.

Hold the detective's clues in mind and try the questions for this part: shapes made by people and by nature, and the four checks.

## Subtopic: Water, Plants and People

Water is dark, almost black, because it soaks up light. A muddy river after rain looks brown, and you can see its brown plume spreading into the sea. A dam has a straight edge where the wall is, with arms reaching up the valleys.

Plants are green in normal colour and bright red in false colour. Natural forest looks bumpy and dark. Pine plantations look just as dark, but in neat blocks with straight edges, because people planted them. In the dry Northern Cape you might see bright green circles, each one a farm field watered by a long sprinkler arm that swings round in a circle.

People show up as grey and busy. Suburbs make neat grids. Around Johannesburg there is a long line of pale yellow flat-topped shapes, the old gold mine dumps, running east to west exactly where the gold was found. Roads and railways are long lines. Airports have runways you cannot miss.

And if you compare two pictures of the same place from different years, you can see change: new suburbs, growing settlements, shrinking dams.

Hold the dark water, the red plants and the yellow mine dumps in mind and try this part's questions.

## Subtopic: Reading the Sky from Above

Weather pictures from satellites tell you what the weather is about to do.

A cold front looks like a long white band of cloud, curving like a comma, sweeping in from the ocean towards Cape Town. When it arrives, the Western Cape gets wind, rain and a cold snap.

Summer thunderstorms look like bright white blobs bubbling up over the Highveld in the afternoon.

A tropical cyclone looks like a giant white spiral with a hole in the middle, the eye. In our half of the world it spins clockwise.

Fog on the west coast looks like a smooth, flat grey sheet lying along the shore, made by the cold sea.

And on infrared pictures, which work even at night, the brightest white clouds are the coldest and highest, and often bring the heaviest rain.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Read a satellite image with clues: colour, roughness, shape, size, pattern and what is nearby. Check north, detail, date and colour type first. Water is dark; plants are green, or red in false colour; people make straight lines, grids and circles. Mine dumps near Johannesburg are pale yellow. Cold fronts are long bands, thunderstorms are bright blobs, cyclones are spirals, fog is a smooth sheet.

Hold the detective's clues and the weather shapes, and take the final questions of the lesson: water, land use and cloud patterns.
