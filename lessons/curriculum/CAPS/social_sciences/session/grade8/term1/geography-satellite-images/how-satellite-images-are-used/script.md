# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces satellite images, the third way of representing the Earth's surface after maps and globes. The anchor is the weather report: the swirling white cloud bands moving across southern Africa on a forecast map are satellite images taken a few minutes earlier from far above the equator. The governing idea is that a satellite image is not a drawing but a measurement. Sensors on a satellite record the energy reflected or given off by the surface below, pixel by pixel, and computers turn those measurements into pictures. Because of this, satellite images can show things that maps cannot: what is happening now, what has changed, and even what the human eye cannot see. Understanding how images are made and what resolution means prepares for the next session, on reading information from images, and for remote sensing in Grade 10 to 12 Geography.

## Subtopic: Satellites and Remote Sensing

A satellite is an object that orbits a larger body. The moon is a natural satellite of the Earth; the thousands of machines launched into orbit since the Soviet Union's Sputnik 1 in 1957 are artificial satellites. They serve many purposes: communication, broadcasting, navigation such as the global positioning system, scientific research, and observing the Earth.

Remote sensing means collecting information about something without touching it. A satellite does this by carrying sensors that record electromagnetic energy coming from the Earth's surface. Some of this energy is sunlight reflected by the ground, water, vegetation and clouds. Some is heat given off by the surface itself. Different surfaces reflect and emit energy differently, and that difference is what makes them distinguishable in an image.

Satellite images differ from aerial photographs, which are taken from aircraft flying a few kilometres up, and which this year's third term studies in detail. Satellites fly much higher, cover much larger areas in one image, and pass over the same places again and again on a regular schedule, so they can record change over days, months and decades.

Earth observation from space has a history worth knowing. The first weather satellite images were sent back in 1960. The United States launched the first Landsat satellite in 1972, and the Landsat series has photographed the whole planet continuously ever since, making it the longest record of the Earth's surface from space. Europe's Meteosat weather satellites have watched Africa and Europe since 1977. Today the European Sentinel satellites provide free images of the whole world every few days.

South Africa has a strong place in this field. The South African National Space Agency, known as SANSA, operates a ground station at Hartebeesthoek, west of Pretoria, that receives images from many international satellites and makes them available for national use. South Africa has also built its own satellites, including SumbandilaSat, launched in 2009, and small satellites such as ZACube-2, launched in 2018, which monitors ships along the coast.

The questions for this section are with you now: what remote sensing is, how satellite images differ from aerial photographs, and the South African space agency.

## Subtopic: Two Kinds of Orbit

Satellites that image the Earth use two main kinds of orbit, and the kind of orbit decides what the satellite is good for.

Geostationary satellites orbit about thirty-six thousand kilometres above the equator. At that height, an orbit takes exactly as long as one rotation of the Earth, and the satellite moves in the same direction as the Earth turns, so it stays fixed above the same point on the equator. From there it sees almost a whole hemisphere at once. The Meteosat satellites that watch Africa sit above the equator near zero degrees longitude, in the Gulf of Guinea, and send a new image of the whole of Africa and Europe every fifteen minutes. This makes geostationary satellites ideal for watching weather: cloud bands, cold fronts and tropical cyclones can be followed hour by hour and played as an animation. Their weakness is detail; from so far away, each pixel covers several kilometres on the ground.

Polar-orbiting satellites fly much lower, usually about six hundred to eight hundred kilometres up, in orbits that pass close to both poles. Each orbit takes about one hundred minutes, and while the satellite circles from north to south, the Earth turns beneath it, so each pass covers a new strip of the surface. Over days or weeks the whole planet is covered. Because they are close, polar orbiters record fine detail. Their weakness is that they revisit any one place only every few days, so they cannot track fast-changing weather the way a geostationary satellite can. Landsat and Sentinel are polar orbiters.

Many polar orbiters fly in what is called a sun-synchronous orbit, crossing every place at the same local time on each pass, for example around ten in the morning. This keeps the lighting similar from image to image, so that differences between images show real change on the ground rather than changes in shadows.

The two kinds work together. A weather forecaster watches a storm form over the Southern Ocean on geostationary images, while emergency planners use detailed polar-orbiter images the next day to map which roads and villages were flooded.

The questions for this section are with you now: geostationary and polar orbits, the height and purpose of each, and why both are needed.

## Subtopic: Pixels, Resolution and Colour

A satellite image is made of pixels, the tiny squares from which every digital picture is built. Each pixel records the amount of energy reflected or emitted by one small square of the ground. The size of that square on the ground is the image's spatial resolution.

Resolution decides what can be seen. Landsat images have a resolution of thirty metres, so each pixel covers an area of thirty metres by thirty metres. A soccer field, about one hundred and five metres long and sixty-eight metres wide, covers only about three pixels by two. Fields, dams, suburbs and forests are clear, but individual houses are not. Sentinel-2 images have a resolution of ten metres, so the same soccer field covers about ten pixels by seven, and large buildings become visible. Commercial satellites can achieve resolutions finer than one metre, enough to see individual cars. Weather satellite images, by contrast, may have pixels of one to three kilometres, perfect for clouds but useless for streets. High resolution is not always better: finer pixels mean far more data and smaller areas per image. The right resolution depends on the question.

Time matters too. The revisit time, sometimes called temporal resolution, is how often a satellite images the same place. Geostationary satellites revisit every fifteen minutes; Landsat about every sixteen days; the pair of Sentinel-2 satellites about every five days.

Sensors record more than visible light. They measure separate bands of energy, including blue, green and red light, near-infrared, which our eyes cannot see, and thermal infrared, which is heat. A true-colour image combines the red, green and blue bands, and looks roughly as the Earth would look to an astronaut. A false-colour image assigns visible colours to invisible bands. In the most common false-colour combination, near-infrared is shown as red. Healthy green plants reflect near-infrared very strongly, so vegetation appears bright red, while water, which absorbs near-infrared, appears very dark. False colour therefore makes vegetation, crops and water stand out sharply, and it can show stressed or dying plants before the change is visible to the eye. Thermal images show surface temperature, which reveals active fires, urban heat and the temperature of ocean currents.

Reading an image well therefore starts with three checks before any interpretation: the date and time it was taken, the resolution, and whether it is true or false colour. The date tells you the season, which matters because a grassland that looks brown and bare in a Highveld winter image may be green in summer, and a dam that looks full after the rains may be low at the end of the dry season. The resolution tells you what size of feature you can expect to identify. The colour scheme tells you how to read the colours, because the same green field is green in one image and red in another. Many examination questions on satellite images award a mark simply for noticing one of these three facts in the caption.

The questions for this section are with you now: pixels and spatial resolution, revisit time, and true-colour and false-colour images.

## Subtopic: Uses of Satellite Images and the Error Museum

Satellite images are used wherever information about large areas, or about change over time, is needed.

Weather forecasting: the South African Weather Service uses geostationary images to track cold fronts approaching the Western Cape, thunderstorms building over the Highveld and tropical cyclones in the Mozambique Channel, and to issue warnings.

Disasters: during the April 2022 floods in KwaZulu-Natal, images were used to map flooded areas and landslides. Fire monitoring systems detect active fires from the heat they give off, and SANSA and partner organisations send alerts to fire managers so that veld and forest fires can be fought early.

Water: images showed the shrinking surface of the dams that supply Cape Town during the drought that peaked in 2018, when the city came close to the day its taps would have run dry. Dam levels, wetlands and river channels can be measured across the whole country.

Farming and the environment: false-colour images show crop health and help estimate harvests. Images over decades show the spread of informal settlements and suburbs, the loss of natural vegetation, the spread of invasive alien plants and the erosion of coastlines.

Cities and planning: municipalities compare images from different years to see where new houses, roads and shopping centres have appeared, to plan water pipes, electricity and schools for growing areas, and to identify settlements built on floodplains or steep slopes where people are at risk. The growth of Gauteng's cities since the 1990s, and the spread of settlements along the main roads leading out of them, can be followed year by year on free Landsat images.

Oceans: thermal images measure sea-surface temperature, showing the warm Agulhas Current flowing south along the east coast and the cold water of the Benguela Current welling up along the west coast. Other sensors measure the colour of the sea, which reveals the tiny plants called phytoplankton on which fisheries depend.

Mapping: many of the background images on map apps are satellite images, stitched into a mosaic and kept up to date.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: thinking a satellite image is a map; it shows everything the sensor records, with no symbols, names or boundaries unless they are added. Two: confusing the global positioning system, which uses satellites to tell a receiver where it is, with satellite imaging, which photographs the surface. Three: expecting to see houses on a weather satellite image; its pixels are kilometres wide. Four: reading the red areas of a standard false-colour image as fire or bare ground; they are healthy vegetation. Five: assuming an image is current; every image has a date, and the date matters.

Layout for marks in a describe question: name the type of satellite or image, state its resolution or revisit time where relevant, and give a specific use with an example. For example: a geostationary weather satellite images Africa every fifteen minutes, so forecasters can track a cold front moving towards Cape Town.

The questions for this section are with you now: the main uses of satellite images in South Africa, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: A Camera Very High Up

Imagine a camera so high above the Earth that it can see the whole of Africa in one shot. That is a satellite, a machine circling the Earth with special cameras, called sensors, pointing down.

There are two kinds you need to know. The first kind hangs very, very high, about thirty-six thousand kilometres above the equator. It goes round at exactly the same speed as the Earth spins, so it seems to stay still over one spot. From there it watches the weather all day, taking a new picture of Africa every fifteen minutes. Those are the cloud pictures on the TV weather report.

The second kind flies much lower, about seven hundred kilometres up, zooming from pole to pole. As the Earth turns underneath it, it photographs one strip of land after another. Because it is closer, it sees much more detail, but it only comes back to the same place every few days.

So: the high one watches the weather, and the low one looks at the details.

South Africa has its own space agency, SANSA, with a big station of satellite dishes at Hartebeesthoek near Pretoria, catching pictures from satellites as they pass overhead.

Hold the two cameras in mind and try the questions for this part: which satellite watches the weather, and which sees detail.

## Subtopic: A Picture Made of Dots

Zoom right into any photo on a phone and it breaks into tiny squares. Those are pixels. A satellite picture is the same: millions of little squares, each one showing a patch of ground.

How big a patch? That decides what you can see. On some satellite pictures each square is thirty metres wide, so a whole soccer field is only about six squares. You can see the field, but not the goalposts. On sharper pictures each square is ten metres, and on the very best ones, less than a metre, enough to see a car. On weather pictures each square can be kilometres wide: great for clouds, useless for streets.

Here is the clever part. Satellite cameras can see kinds of light that we cannot. Healthy plants reflect lots of an invisible light called near-infrared. If the computer colours that invisible light red, all the healthy plants glow bright red. That is called a false-colour picture. It looks strange, but it makes farms, forests and gardens jump out, while water looks almost black.

Hold the pixels and the red plants in mind and try this part's questions: what a pixel is, and why plants look red.

## Subtopic: Satellites at Work

Satellites work for South Africa every day.

They watch the weather. When a cold front is heading for Cape Town, forecasters can see it coming across the ocean and warn people.

They watch water. During Cape Town's drought, which was at its worst in 2018, satellite pictures showed the dams shrinking month by month.

They watch fires. Satellites can feel the heat of a veld fire from space and send a warning to fire teams.

They watch floods. After the 2022 floods in KwaZulu-Natal, satellite pictures helped show which areas were under water.

And they help farmers. A false-colour picture shows which parts of a maize field are healthy and which are struggling.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A satellite image is a picture made by sensors on a satellite. Weather satellites stay over one spot very high up and send a picture every fifteen minutes. Lower satellites go from pole to pole and see more detail. Images are made of pixels, and the size of each pixel on the ground decides how much you can see. False colour shows healthy plants as red. South Africa uses satellite images for weather, water, fire, floods and farming.
