Hey — if satellite images sound like space science, here is the calm version.

A satellite is a machine going round the Earth with cameras, called sensors, pointing down.

There are two kinds you need. One hangs very high, about thirty-six thousand kilometres up, above the equator. It moves with the Earth's spin, so it stays over one spot and watches the weather, sending a new picture of Africa every fifteen minutes.

The other flies much lower, about seven hundred kilometres up, from pole to pole. It sees more detail but only comes back every few days.

Every satellite picture is made of pixels, tiny squares. The bigger the patch of ground in each square, the less detail you see. Thirty-metre squares show fields and suburbs but not houses.

Satellites can see light our eyes cannot. Healthy plants reflect lots of near-infrared, so in a false-colour picture they show up bright red.

South Africa uses satellite images for weather forecasts, watching dams, spotting veld fires, mapping floods and helping farmers.
