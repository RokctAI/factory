### subtopic_1 — Latitude and Longitude
**Assistant:** Why was Greenwich chosen as the prime meridian, and could another place have been used?

**Tutor:** Any meridian could have served as zero, because longitude is measured from an agreed line rather than a natural one. That is the important difference from latitude. The equator is fixed by nature: it is the circle halfway between the poles, where the Earth's spin makes it bulge, and every country would arrive at the same equator. There is no natural zero for longitude. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, different countries used their own capitals or observatories as zero: the French used Paris, the Spanish used Cadiz, and many navigators used Greenwich, home of the Royal Observatory near London. This caused confusion on charts and timetables, especially once railways and telegraphs linked countries. In 1884 an international conference in Washington agreed to adopt the meridian through the Royal Observatory at Greenwich as the prime meridian for the whole world. Greenwich won partly because Britain was the leading sea power, and most of the world's shipping already used charts based on Greenwich, so adopting it caused the least disruption. France abstained and kept the Paris meridian on some maps for several more decades. The choice shows that some geographical conventions are human agreements shaped by power and practicality, which connects to this term's History, where Britain's industrial and naval strength is central. Today the reference for the satellite positioning used by phones sits about one hundred metres east of the historic line at Greenwich, a detail of modern measurement, but the principle is unchanged: zero longitude is where the world agreed to put it.

### subtopic_2 — Degrees, Minutes and Writing Coordinates
**Assistant:** Is one minute of longitude the same distance on the ground as one minute of latitude?

**Tutor:** No, and the difference grows as you move away from the equator. Lines of latitude are parallel circles, evenly spaced from the equator to the poles, so one degree of latitude is always about one hundred and eleven kilometres and one minute of latitude about one point eight five kilometres. Lines of longitude are different. They are not parallel; they meet at the poles. At the equator they are furthest apart, and one degree of longitude is also about one hundred and eleven kilometres. But as you move towards either pole, the meridians crowd together. At the latitude of Johannesburg, around twenty-six degrees south, one degree of longitude is about one hundred kilometres. At the latitude of Cape Town, around thirty-four degrees south, it is about ninety-two kilometres. At sixty degrees it is only about fifty-six kilometres, half its equatorial width, and at the poles it shrinks to nothing, because all the meridians meet in a single point. That is why a minute of latitude is used to define the nautical mile, and a minute of longitude is not: only latitude gives a constant distance. For this term the practical consequence is to estimate minutes of latitude and minutes of longitude separately on a map, measuring the gap between parallels for latitude and the gap between meridians for longitude, because on many atlas maps the two gaps are drawn at different lengths. Never assume the grid squares are true squares on the ground; outside the tropics they are narrower east to west than north to south.

### subtopic_3 — Using the Atlas Index
**Assistant:** What if a place in the news does not appear in my atlas index?

**Tutor:** Then work outwards from what the report tells you, and use relative location until you can fix the absolute location. Small towns, villages, informal settlements and new place names are often missing from a school atlas, which only has room for the larger settlements at its scales. First, read the report for clues: the province or country, the nearest large town, a river, a mountain range or a road. A report might say a flood struck a village on the Mthatha River about forty kilometres downstream of the city of Mthatha. Look up Mthatha in the index, find it on the map, find the river, and estimate the position from the distance and direction given, using the scale. You can then give approximate coordinates, saying they are estimated, together with the relative location. Second, check whether the place has another name. South Africa has renamed many towns and cities since 1994, so an older atlas might list Pietersburg rather than Polokwane, Nelspruit rather than Mbombela, or Umtata rather than Mthatha, and a newer atlas might use only the new name. A good index often cross-references old and new names. Third, check spellings, especially for places in other countries, which may be spelled differently in different languages. Finally, remember that the purpose of locating places in the news is not to collect coordinates but to understand why events happen where they do. Even an approximate location, placed correctly in its region, near the right river or coast, tells you a great deal about the climate, the landforms and the people affected.

### subtopic_4 — Places in the News and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why does it matter so much to write latitude before longitude?

**Tutor:** Because a coordinate is only useful if everyone reads it the same way, and swapping the two can move a place thousands of kilometres. Consider Johannesburg at twenty-six degrees twelve minutes south, twenty-eight degrees three minutes east. If someone wrote twenty-eight degrees three minutes first and twenty-six degrees twelve minutes second, a careful reader would see the letters E and S and could sort it out. But if the letters are left off, or if the reader assumes the conventional order, the swap puts the point at twenty-eight degrees south and twenty-six degrees east, which is in the Free State, north-east of Kimberley, roughly two hundred and seventy-five kilometres south-west of Johannesburg. For places where the numbers differ more, the error is greater: swap Cape Town's thirty-three degrees fifty-five minutes south and eighteen degrees twenty-five minutes east and you land in central Mozambique, more than two thousand kilometres away. In real life, coordinates are used by search and rescue teams, pilots, ships, delivery services and emergency services, and a misread coordinate can send help to the wrong place. That is why the convention is strict and why examiners penalise the wrong order even when the numbers are right. Every map system you will meet, from atlas indexes to the global positioning system on a phone to the topographic maps of Grade 10, uses latitude first. Learning the habit now, together with the direction letters and two-digit minutes, means you never have to think about it again.
