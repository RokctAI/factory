### subtopic_1 — One Method for Every Scale
**Assistant:** Why measure from the centre of each town symbol rather than from the closest edges?

**Tutor:** Because the symbol represents the whole settlement, and its centre stands for the town's central point, usually the town centre or the main crossroads. The map maker places the dot there on purpose. Measuring from edge to edge would make the answer depend on how large the dots happen to be printed, which has nothing to do with the real distance. At large scales the difference is small, but at small scales it can be serious. On a regional map at 1:10 000 000, a town dot might be three millimetres across. Measuring from the near edge of one dot to the near edge of another would shorten the reading by about three millimetres in total, one and a half from each dot. Three millimetres on that map is thirty kilometres on the ground, which is nearly half an hour of driving on a national road. On a world map at 1:100 000 000, the same three millimetres would be three hundred kilometres. So the centre-to-centre rule is not fussiness; it keeps the measurement honest. When a town is shown as a shaded area rather than a dot, as large cities often are on local maps, the question will usually name a particular point, such as the town hall or a road junction, and you should measure from that. If it does not, use the centre of the built-up area and say so. Examiners allow a small range of answers for exactly this reason, but the range assumes you measured from the agreed point, and an answer measured from the edges will often fall outside it.

### subtopic_2 — Distances in South Africa at Regional Scales
**Assistant:** How much longer than the straight line is a typical road journey in South Africa?

**Tutor:** It varies, but on long national routes the road is usually between about a tenth and a quarter longer than the straight-line distance, and in mountainous country it can be much more. The figures in this lesson show the pattern. Johannesburg to Cape Town is about one thousand two hundred and sixty kilometres in a straight line and about one thousand four hundred by the N1, roughly eleven percent longer. Johannesburg to Durban is about five hundred kilometres in a straight line and about five hundred and seventy by the N3, roughly fourteen percent longer, because the road must climb down the Drakensberg escarpment through Van Reenen's Pass and wind through the hills of KwaZulu-Natal. On mountain passes the ratio is much higher: a pass that climbs a steep escarpment might cover two or three times the straight-line distance in hairpin bends. Rivers matter too, because roads must reach a bridge, and so do towns, because routes were usually built to link settlements rather than to run straight. That is why geographers sometimes calculate a detour index, which is the road distance divided by the straight-line distance. A detour index near one means a direct route; a high index means a winding one. For Grade 8 the practical point is to read the question carefully. If it asks how far apart two towns are, the straight-line distance is expected. If it asks how far a truck travels or how long a journey takes, the road distance is the right one, measured with string along the route.

### subtopic_3 — Distances Across the World
**Assistant:** If flat world maps distort distances, why do we still use them?

**Tutor:** Because they are useful for many things that a globe does badly, and because the distortion can be managed if we know where it is. A globe has almost no distortion, but you can only ever see half of it at once, it is awkward to carry, it cannot be printed in a book, and it must be very large to show any detail. A flat world map lets us see the whole Earth at a glance, compare continents side by side, and print thematic information such as climate regions or population density. Different projections are chosen for different purposes. Some keep areas correct, so that Africa looks as big as it really is compared with Europe or Greenland; these are good for comparing the sizes of countries or the extent of deserts. Others keep shapes and directions correct over small areas, which made them valuable to sailors steering by compass, even though they greatly enlarge the lands near the poles. Many modern atlases use compromise projections that keep every kind of distortion moderate. So the skill is not to avoid flat maps but to read them knowingly. For distances within South Africa, the distortion on a regional map is negligible. For distances between continents, treat a ruler measurement on a world map as an estimate, mention the projection if the question invites it, and use a globe with string when accuracy matters. Airline route maps often show curved flight paths for exactly this reason: on the flat map the shortest route looks curved, because the map is distorting a straight line on the sphere.

### subtopic_4 — Working Backwards and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Is there a quick way to know whether my distance answer is too big or too small?

**Tutor:** Yes. Keep a few real distances in your head and compare. You do not need exact values, only the right order of size. Within a town, distances are a few kilometres. Between neighbouring towns, tens of kilometres. Johannesburg to Pretoria is about fifty kilometres. Johannesburg to Durban is about five hundred. Johannesburg to Cape Town is about one thousand three hundred in a straight line. South Africa from its north-eastern corner near the Limpopo to Cape Agulhas in the south is roughly one thousand eight hundred kilometres. Johannesburg to London is about nine thousand. The distance around the Earth at the equator is about forty thousand. If an answer for two villages on a 1:50 000 map comes out at thirty-seven kilometres, it is suspicious, because a 1:50 000 sheet only covers an area of about twenty-five by twenty-five kilometres, so no two points on one sheet can be that far apart except corner to corner. If an answer for Johannesburg to Cape Town comes out at one hundred and twenty-six kilometres or twelve thousand six hundred, the decimal point has moved, because the right answer must be close to one thousand three hundred. If a distance between two South African cities is bigger than the distance to London, something has gone badly wrong. Running this check takes five seconds and catches the most common errors: forgetting to convert centimetres into kilometres, moving the decimal point the wrong way, and misreading the scale. It also builds a mental map of South Africa and the world, which is itself one of the aims of Geography.
