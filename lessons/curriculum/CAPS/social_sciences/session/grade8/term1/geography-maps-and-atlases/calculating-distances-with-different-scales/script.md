# Part 1 — Expert

This session applies the three kinds of scale to the most common map calculation of all: the distance between two settlements. The anchor is a single journey seen at four scales, from a 1:50 000 map of two villages in the Eastern Cape, through regional maps of South Africa, to a world map showing Johannesburg and London. The governing idea is that one method works at every scale: measure the map distance accurately in centimetres, convert it to ground distance with the scale, and state the answer in the right unit, checking that it is sensible. The calculation is simple; the marks are lost in measuring carelessly, in converting units wrongly and in forgetting that a straight line is not a road. Distance calculations appear in every Geography mapwork paper up to Grade 12, where they are combined with gradient, area and speed calculations.

## Subtopic: One Method for Every Scale

The method has three steps, and they never change. First, measure the map distance between the two settlements, from the centre of one symbol to the centre of the other, in centimetres to one decimal place. Second, convert the map distance into ground distance using the scale. Third, state the answer with its unit and check that it is reasonable.

The conversion depends on the kind of scale given. With a word scale, multiply the map distance by the ground distance that one centimetre represents. With a ratio scale, there are two equivalent routes. The first is to multiply the map distance by the denominator, which gives the ground distance in centimetres, and then convert to kilometres by dividing by one hundred thousand. The second is to convert the ratio scale into a word scale first and then multiply. With a line scale, mark the map distance on a strip of paper and read the ground distance directly from the bar.

Worked example, ratio scale by the first route. On a 1:250 000 map, two towns are 6,4 cm apart. The ground distance in centimetres is 6,4 × 250 000 = 1 600 000 cm. Converting to kilometres: 1 600 000 ÷ 100 000 = 16 km.

The same example by the second route. 1:250 000 means one centimetre represents two and a half kilometres. The ground distance is 6,4 × 2,5 = 16 km. Both routes must give the same answer, which is itself a useful check.

Worked example, local map. On a 1:50 000 topographic map, two villages are 7,4 cm apart. One centimetre represents half a kilometre, so the distance is 7,4 × 0,5 = 3,7 km, or three thousand seven hundred metres. At large scales like this, answers in metres are often more useful, because a walk of 3,7 kilometres is easier to imagine in metres and minutes than in fractions of kilometres.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three steps, the two routes for a ratio scale, and a local-map example.

## Subtopic: Distances in South Africa at Regional Scales

On regional maps of South Africa, distances between cities run into hundreds of kilometres, and the scales are much smaller.

Worked example one. On a map of South Africa at 1:10 000 000, Johannesburg and Cape Town measure 12,6 cm apart. One centimetre represents one hundred kilometres, so the straight-line distance is 12,6 × 100 = 1 260 km.

Worked example two. On a map at 1:5 000 000, Johannesburg and Durban measure 10 cm apart. One centimetre represents fifty kilometres, so the distance is 10 × 50 = 500 km. On the same map, Bloemfontein and Kimberley measure 2,9 cm apart, which is 2,9 × 50 = 145 km.

These are straight-line distances, sometimes called distances as the crow flies. The distance by road is always longer, because roads follow valleys, avoid mountains and pass through other towns. The N1 from Johannesburg to Cape Town runs about one thousand four hundred kilometres, roughly one hundred and forty kilometres more than the straight line, and the N3 from Johannesburg to Durban runs about five hundred and seventy kilometres, roughly seventy more than the straight line. To measure a road distance on a map, follow the route with string or with the edge of paper, pivoting at each bend, and then measure the straightened length.

Distances lead to travel times. If a bus averages eighty kilometres per hour on the N3, the journey of about five hundred and seventy kilometres takes a little over seven hours: eighty kilometres fit into five hundred and seventy just over seven times, which is about seven hours and eight minutes. In Grade 8 the important point is the link: distance from the map, speed from the question, and time from the division.

Regional maps of the provinces sit between these scales. An atlas map of Limpopo or the Western Cape at 1:2 500 000 shows one centimetre as twenty-five kilometres. On such a map Pretoria and Polokwane measure about 9,6 cm apart, which is 9,6 × 25 = 240 km, close to the true straight-line distance of about two hundred and forty kilometres. The same method, the same three steps, gives a sensible answer at every one of these scales, which is why the method is worth practising until it is automatic. Notice too how the choice of map affects precision. A measurement error of one millimetre is two and a half kilometres on the provincial map and ten kilometres on the national map at 1:10 000 000. When a question needs a precise answer, the larger-scale map is the better tool, and when a question only needs a rough sense of how far, the smaller-scale map is enough.

Distances between South African settlements also help explain patterns studied later in the year. Kimberley lies about four hundred and thirty kilometres in a straight line from Johannesburg and about eight hundred and thirty from Cape Town, which is why the diamond fields needed a long railway to the coast. The gold of the Witwatersrand lay roughly five hundred kilometres from the nearest harbour at Durban, which shaped the building of the railways and the growth of the port. Distance on a map is never only a number; it is often the reason something happened.

The questions for this section are with you now: regional distances between South African cities, straight-line and road distance, and a travel time.

## Subtopic: Distances Across the World

World maps are drawn at scales such as 1:100 000 000, where one centimetre represents one thousand kilometres. On such a map Johannesburg and Nairobi measure about 2,9 cm apart, which is 2,9 × 1 000 = 2 900 km, and Johannesburg and London about 9,1 cm, which is 9,1 × 1 000 = 9 100 km. Measured accurately on the curved Earth, the true straight-line distances are about two thousand nine hundred and about nine thousand and seventy kilometres, so these readings are good.

But world maps carry a warning. The Earth is a sphere, and every flat map of a sphere stretches or squashes some parts, the way the peel of an orange tears and stretches when it is flattened. The way a map maker flattens the globe is called a projection, and every projection distorts something: area, shape, direction or distance. On many world maps, the scale printed on the map is true only along the equator or along certain lines, and distances far from those lines, especially near the poles, are exaggerated. Greenland looks as large as Africa on some world maps, although Africa is about fourteen times larger.

So for long distances, the globe is the better tool. A globe is a scale model of the Earth with almost no distortion. To find the shortest distance between two cities on a globe, stretch a piece of string tightly between them; the string follows the shortest route over the curved surface, called a great circle route. Measure the string against the globe's scale. Airlines fly great circle routes, which is why a flight from Johannesburg to New York crosses the Atlantic far north of where a straight line on a flat map would suggest.

Two more comparisons show the size of the world compared with South Africa. Cape Town to Rio de Janeiro across the South Atlantic is about six thousand kilometres, which on a 1:100 000 000 map is about 6 cm. Johannesburg to Lagos is about four thousand five hundred kilometres, about 4,5 cm on the same map. A whole journey across Africa from Cape Town to Cairo, more than seven thousand kilometres, fits in about seven centimetres on a world map, a reminder of how small a scale a world map really is and how little detail it can show along the way.

Within South Africa, at regional scales, distortion is small enough to ignore, and map measurements are reliable. Across continents, map measurements are estimates, and good answers say so.

The questions for this section are with you now: distances on a world map, why world maps distort distance, and why a globe and string give the shortest route.

## Subtopic: Working Backwards and the Error Museum

Sometimes the question gives the real distance and asks for the map distance, or gives both and asks for the scale.

Map distance from real distance: divide the real distance by what one centimetre represents. Johannesburg and Cape Town are about 1 260 km apart. On a 1:10 000 000 map, where one centimetre represents one hundred kilometres, the map distance is 1 260 ÷ 100 = 12,6 cm.

Scale from map and real distance: divide the real distance in centimetres by the map distance in centimetres. Two towns 30 km apart measure 12 cm on a map. Thirty kilometres is 3 000 000 cm, and 3 000 000 ÷ 12 = 250 000, so the scale is 1:250 000.

Choosing the scale for a question also matters. If an examination gives a map with both a line scale and a ratio scale, use the one the question names. If it names none, the ratio scale gives the more exact calculation, and the line scale gives a quick check.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: measuring from the edge of a town symbol instead of its centre, which can add or subtract several kilometres at small scales. Two: multiplying the map distance by the full denominator and then forgetting to convert centimetres into kilometres, giving an answer one hundred thousand times too large. Three: giving a straight-line distance when the question asked for the distance along a road or railway. Four: trusting a measurement on a flat world map for a long journey near the poles. Five: writing the answer without a unit.

Layout for marks: map distance with unit; scale; calculation shown; answer with unit; for long routes, a note of the method. For example: map distance 12,6 cm; scale 1:10 000 000, so 1 cm represents 100 km; 12,6 × 100 = 1 260 km straight-line distance.

The questions for this section are with you now: working backwards to a map distance and to a scale, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: one trip, four maps, and one habit for every distance.

## Subtopic: Ruler, Multiply, Kilometres

Every distance question is the same three moves. Put the ruler on the map from the middle of one town to the middle of the other. Multiply by what one centimetre stands for. Write the answer with kilometres or metres after it.

The only thing that changes from map to map is what one centimetre stands for. That is what the scale tells you. On a 1:50 000 map, knock five zeros off and one centimetre is half a kilometre. On a 1:250 000 map, one centimetre is two and a half kilometres. On a map of South Africa at 1:10 000 000, one centimetre is a hundred kilometres. On a world map at 1:100 000 000, one centimetre is a thousand kilometres.

So two villages 7,4 centimetres apart on the 1:50 000 map are three point seven kilometres apart, a walk of under an hour. Two towns 6,4 centimetres apart on the 1:250 000 map are sixteen kilometres apart, a short drive.

A good habit is to say the scale out loud in words before you start. One centimetre is half a kilometre. One centimetre is a hundred kilometres. Once you have said it, the multiplying is easy, and you will notice straight away if an answer is silly.

Measure carefully, too. Put the zero of the ruler exactly on the middle of the first town, not on the worn end of the ruler. Read the millimetres. On a map where one centimetre is a hundred kilometres, one millimetre is ten kilometres, so a sloppy reading can put a whole town in the wrong place. Hold the ruler and the five zeros in mind and try the questions for this part: what one centimetre stands for, and two quick distances.

## Subtopic: Same Trip, Bigger Map

Now zoom out to the whole country. On the 1:10 000 000 map of South Africa, Johannesburg to Cape Town measures 12,6 centimetres. Each centimetre is a hundred kilometres, so that is 1 260 kilometres in a straight line.

But nobody drives in a straight line. The N1 bends around mountains and through towns, so the drive is about one thousand four hundred kilometres. A bird could save itself a hundred and forty kilometres. When a question says along the road, use string and follow the bends. When it says straight line, use the ruler.

Zoom out further to the world map. Johannesburg to London measures about nine centimetres. Each centimetre is a thousand kilometres, so that is about nine thousand kilometres, roughly an eleven-hour flight.

Here is the catch with world maps. The Earth is round, and a flat map of a round Earth is like a flattened orange peel: it stretches. So for long trips, a globe with a piece of string pulled tight between the two cities is more accurate. That tight string shows the shortest route, the one aeroplanes actually fly.

Hold the orange peel in mind and try this part's questions: road or straight line, and why a globe beats a flat map for long trips.

## Subtopic: Working Backwards

Sometimes the question turns around. It tells you the real distance and asks how long the line on the map will be. Just divide instead of multiply. Johannesburg to Cape Town is 1 260 kilometres. On a map where one centimetre is a hundred kilometres, divide by a hundred: 12,6 centimetres.

Check that it makes sense: twelve and a half centimetres on a page of South Africa is about half the width of an exercise book, and Johannesburg and Cape Town really do look about that far apart on a school wall map of the country.

And sometimes it asks you to find the scale. Two towns 30 kilometres apart measure 12 centimetres on the map. Turn the 30 kilometres into centimetres by adding five zeros: three million. Share that among the 12 centimetres: two hundred and fifty thousand each. The scale is 1:250 000.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Measure from centre to centre in centimetres. Multiply by what one centimetre stands for, and give the unit. To work backwards, divide. Straight line uses a ruler; road distance uses string. Flat world maps stretch long distances, so a globe and string are better for long trips. Always ask whether the answer makes sense.

Hold the three moves, the string for the road and the string on the globe, and take the final questions of the lesson: a distance, a working-backwards question, and a scale to find.
