# Part 1 — Expert

This session extends map scale from the word and line scales of Grade 7 to the ratio scale used on atlas maps, topographic maps and every map a professional geographer reads. The anchor is the atlas itself: the same page size carrying a map of the whole world, a map of southern Africa and a map of one city, each at a different scale and each showing a different amount of detail. The governing idea is that scale is a ratio between distance on the map and distance on the ground, and that the ratio form, written as one to a number, is the most exact and universal way of stating it, because it works in any unit. Reading a ratio scale, converting it into a word scale or a line scale and back, and comparing scales are all examinable this term, and the ratio scale of one to fifty thousand is the scale of the topographic maps used in Grade 10 to 12 Geography.

## Subtopic: Kinds of Scale in an Atlas

An atlas is a book of maps, and its maps fall into three broad kinds according to how much of the Earth each page shows.

World maps show the whole Earth or a whole continent on one page or a double spread. Their scales are very small, for example one to one hundred million, where each centimetre on the page represents one thousand kilometres on the ground. A city is a dot, a country a coloured shape, and only the largest rivers and mountain ranges appear. World maps are used to show global patterns: climate regions, population distribution, ocean currents, time zones.

Regional maps show a large region such as southern Africa, West Africa or western Europe. A typical scale is one to ten million, where each centimetre represents one hundred kilometres. Now the main towns, national roads, railways, rivers and mountain ranges can be shown, along with provincial and national boundaries.

Local maps show a small area such as a city and its surroundings, a game reserve or a stretch of coast. Scales such as one to two hundred and fifty thousand or one to fifty thousand allow individual suburbs, secondary roads, dams and even large buildings to be shown. In an atlas the local maps are often insets: small maps placed in a corner of a larger map to show a crowded area at a larger scale, such as the Gauteng cities on a map of South Africa.

The pattern across the three kinds is the trade-off met in Grade 7. On a fixed page size, a larger area means a smaller scale and less detail; a smaller area means a larger scale and more detail. World maps are small scale, local maps are large scale, and regional maps lie between. Choosing the right map means choosing the right scale for the question being asked. Planning a flight from Johannesburg to Cairo needs a small-scale map; finding a school in Soweto needs a large-scale one.

An atlas is organised so that the reader can move between these scales. The contents page lists the maps by region and theme, usually starting with the home country and continent and then moving outwards to the rest of the world. Thematic maps of climate, vegetation, population and economy sit beside the physical and political maps. At the back, the index lists every named place with its page number, a grid square or its latitude and longitude, so that a place mentioned in the news can be found in seconds. Every map in the atlas states its scale, and an experienced reader checks it before reading anything else, because the scale decides what the map can and cannot answer.

## Subtopic: Word Scales, Line Scales and Ratio Scales

Every scale states the same relationship, map distance to ground distance, in a different form.

A word scale states it in words: one centimetre represents five kilometres. It is easy to read and calculate with, but it is tied to the units it names and it becomes wrong if the map is enlarged or reduced.

A line scale draws it as a divided bar labelled in ground units. It changes size with the map when copied, so it stays correct, and it can be used without a ruler. Its weakness is precision: reading a fraction of a section by eye is less exact than a calculation.

A ratio scale, also called a number scale or representative fraction, states it as a ratio such as 1:50 000, read as one to fifty thousand. It means that one unit of length on the map represents fifty thousand of the same units on the ground. One centimetre on the map represents fifty thousand centimetres on the ground; one millimetre represents fifty thousand millimetres; one thumb-width represents fifty thousand thumb-widths. Because the units on both sides are the same, they cancel, and the ratio has no units at all. That is its great strength: a map maker in Pretoria and a reader in Tokyo can use the same scale whether they measure in centimetres or inches.

The ratio scale is sometimes written as a fraction, one over fifty thousand, which is why it is also called a representative fraction. In this form it is clear that a ratio scale is a very small number: the map is one fifty-thousandth of the size of the real area in each direction.

The number after the one is called the denominator, and it controls the size of the scale. The smaller the denominator, the larger the scale. A scale of 1:10 000 is larger than 1:50 000, which is larger than 1:1 000 000. On a 1:10 000 map, features are drawn at one ten-thousandth of their real size; on a 1:1 000 000 map, at one millionth, so they are drawn much smaller. South Africa's national topographic maps use 1:50 000, and its orthophoto maps, which combine an aerial photograph with map information, use 1:10 000. Both return in senior Geography.

## Subtopic: Converting Between the Three Kinds of Scale

Conversions depend on three facts about metric units: one metre is one hundred centimetres, one kilometre is one thousand metres, and so one kilometre is one hundred thousand centimetres. To change centimetres into kilometres, divide by one hundred thousand. To change kilometres into centimetres, multiply by one hundred thousand.

Ratio scale to word scale. Take 1:250 000. One centimetre on the map represents two hundred and fifty thousand centimetres on the ground. Dividing by one hundred thousand gives kilometres: 250 000 ÷ 100 000 = 2,5 km. The word scale is one centimetre represents two and a half kilometres. For a larger scale such as 1:50 000, it is often neater in metres: 50 000 ÷ 100 = 500 m, so one centimetre represents five hundred metres, which is half a kilometre.

Word scale to ratio scale. Take one centimetre represents five kilometres. Convert the ground distance into centimetres so that both sides have the same unit: 5 × 100 000 = 500 000 cm. The ratio scale is 1:500 000.

Line scale to ratio scale. Measure the line scale with a ruler. Suppose four centimetres on the line scale are labelled as two kilometres. First find what one centimetre represents: 2 ÷ 4 = 0,5 km. Then convert to centimetres: 0,5 × 100 000 = 50 000 cm. The ratio scale is 1:50 000.

Ratio scale to line scale. To draw a line scale for 1:50 000, note that one centimetre represents half a kilometre, so two centimetres represent one kilometre. Draw a bar, mark it every two centimetres, and label the marks 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4 kilometres. It is usual to add one extra section to the left of zero, divided into tenths, so that small distances can be read.

A useful short cut for ratio scales in centimetres: remove five zeros from the denominator to get the kilometres that one centimetre represents. For 1:250 000, removing five zeros leaves 2,5. For 1:10 000 000, removing five zeros leaves 100, so one centimetre represents one hundred kilometres. The short cut works only because there are exactly one hundred thousand centimetres in a kilometre, so it is a reminder of the method, not a replacement for it.

The questions for this section are with you now: converting ratio scales into word scales, word scales into ratio scales, and line scales into ratio scales.

## Subtopic: Comparing Scales and the Error Museum

Comparing two scales is a common examination question, and there are two ways to do it. The first is to compare denominators: the scale with the smaller denominator is the larger scale and shows more detail. The second is to convert both to word scales and compare what one centimetre represents: the scale on which one centimetre represents less ground is the larger scale.

Worked comparison. Map A has a scale of 1:50 000 and map B a scale of 1:250 000. On map A, one centimetre represents half a kilometre. On map B, one centimetre represents two and a half kilometres. Map A is the larger scale; it shows a smaller area in more detail. A distance of ten kilometres would measure twenty centimetres on map A but only four centimetres on map B, because 10 ÷ 0,5 = 20 and 10 ÷ 2,5 = 4. The same ten kilometres on the ground is drawn five times longer on map A, which matches the ratio of the denominators, because 250 000 ÷ 50 000 = 5.

Choosing a map for a purpose follows from this. A hiker crossing the Drakensberg needs the 1:50 000 topographic map, on which individual paths, streams and huts appear. A truck driver crossing from Durban to Gauteng is better served by a 1:2 500 000 road map, which shows the whole route on one sheet. An atlas world map at 1:100 000 000 is right for comparing the sizes of continents and useless for finding a street.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: thinking 1:250 000 is a larger scale than 1:50 000 because 250 000 is the larger number; the opposite is true. Two: converting 1:50 000 to one centimetre represents fifty kilometres by removing three zeros instead of five. Three: giving a ratio scale with units, such as 1 cm:50 000 km, which mixes units and is wrong. Four: converting a word scale to a ratio without first changing kilometres into centimetres. Five: reading a line scale from its left-hand end instead of from zero.

Layout for marks in a conversion question: state the scale, state the unit conversion used, show the division or multiplication, and give the answer with its unit, or as a ratio with no units. For example: 1:250 000; one kilometre is 100 000 cm; 250 000 ÷ 100 000 = 2,5; one centimetre represents 2,5 km.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing two ratio scales, choosing a map for a purpose, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: the zoom on a phone map, the number after the one, and conversions without fear.

## Subtopic: Same Page, Different Zoom

Open a map app on a phone and pinch the screen. Zoom all the way out and you see the whole world: continents, oceans, and not much else. Zoom in to South Africa and the provinces and big cities appear. Zoom right in and you can read the name of your street and see the shape of your school.

An atlas does the same thing with paper. Its pages are all the same size, but some pages are zoomed right out, showing the whole world, and some are zoomed in, showing one city. Those are world maps, regional maps and local maps.

The more you zoom out, the more area fits on the page, and the smaller everything has to be drawn. That is a small-scale map. The more you zoom in, the less area fits, but everything is drawn bigger with more detail. That is a large-scale map.

So when you pick a map, ask what you need. Want to see how far Cairo is from Cape Town? Zoom out: small scale. Want to find the clinic in your township? Zoom in: large scale.

## Subtopic: The Number After the One

You already know two ways to write a scale. Words: one centimetre represents five kilometres. A line: a little ruler printed on the map.

The third way looks like this: 1:50 000. Say it as one to fifty thousand. It means one of something on the map is fifty thousand of the same thing in real life. One centimetre on the map is fifty thousand centimetres on the ground. One finger-width on the map is fifty thousand finger-widths. It does not matter what you measure with, as long as you use the same thing on both sides. That is why it has no units.

Here is the trick that confuses everyone. The bigger the number after the one, the more the world was shrunk, so the smaller everything is drawn. One to fifty thousand is a large-scale map, zoomed in. One to ten million is a small-scale map, zoomed out. Big number, small scale. Small number, large scale.

Hold the shrinking number in mind and try this part's questions: what 1:50 000 means and which scale is larger.

## Subtopic: Converting Without Fear

To turn a ratio scale into kilometres, you only need one fact: there are one hundred thousand centimetres in a kilometre. That is a one with five zeros.

So take 1:250 000. Knock five zeros off 250 000 and you are left with 2,5. One centimetre on the map is two and a half kilometres on the ground.

Take 1:50 000. Knock five zeros off and you are left with 0,5. One centimetre is half a kilometre, which is five hundred metres.

Going the other way, if the map says one centimetre represents five kilometres, add five zeros to the five: 500 000. The ratio scale is 1:500 000.

Now compare two maps. On a 1:50 000 map, one centimetre is half a kilometre. On a 1:250 000 map, one centimetre is two and a half kilometres. So the first map draws the same road five times longer, with five times more room for detail. It is the larger scale.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Atlases have world, regional and local maps. Scale can be written in words, as a line, or as a ratio like 1:50 000. A ratio scale means one unit on the map is that many of the same units on the ground, so it has no units. Bigger number after the one means smaller scale. To get kilometres from a ratio scale in centimetres, divide by one hundred thousand.

Hold the phone zoom and the five zeros, and take the final questions of the lesson: converting scales and deciding which map is larger scale.
