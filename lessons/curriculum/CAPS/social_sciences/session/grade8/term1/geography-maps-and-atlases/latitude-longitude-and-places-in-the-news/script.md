# Part 1 — Expert

This session gives every place on Earth an exact address. On a street map a grid square is enough; on a globe or an atlas map we need a system that works for the whole planet, and that system is latitude and longitude, measured in degrees and minutes. The anchor is a single headline: a tropical cyclone making landfall on the coast of Mozambique, a place most of us have never visited but can find in seconds with an atlas index and two coordinates. The governing idea is that latitude and longitude are angles measured from two fixed reference lines, the equator and the prime meridian, and that writing them correctly means latitude first, each with its degrees, its minutes and its direction. The skill is examinable this term and is the foundation for locating places on topographic maps in Grade 10, where coordinates are extended to degrees, minutes and seconds.

## Subtopic: Latitude and Longitude

Latitude is the angular distance of a place north or south of the equator. The equator is latitude zero degrees, and the poles are at ninety degrees north and ninety degrees south. Lines of latitude, called parallels, run east to west around the globe, parallel to the equator and to each other, and they become shorter towards the poles. Important parallels have names: the Tropic of Cancer at about 23°26′N, the Tropic of Capricorn at about 23°26′S, the Arctic Circle at about 66°34′N and the Antarctic Circle at about 66°34′S. The Tropic of Capricorn passes through Limpopo, just north of Polokwane, where a roadside marker on the N1 shows the line.

Longitude is the angular distance of a place east or west of the prime meridian. The prime meridian, longitude zero degrees, runs from the North Pole to the South Pole through Greenwich in London, a choice agreed internationally in 1884. Lines of longitude, called meridians, run north to south and meet at the poles. Longitude is measured up to one hundred and eighty degrees east and one hundred and eighty degrees west; the one hundred and eightieth meridian lies on the far side of the Earth, mostly across the Pacific Ocean.

Why angles? Imagine a line from the centre of the Earth to a place. The latitude is the angle between that line and the plane of the equator; the longitude is the angle, around the Earth's axis, between the meridian of the place and the prime meridian. Because they are angles, they work on a sphere of any size, and they never change as long as the place stays put.

Together the parallels and meridians form a grid called the graticule. Any place is fixed by one parallel and one meridian, and where they cross is a unique point on the Earth. South Africa lies between about 22°S on the Limpopo River and about 35°S at Cape Agulhas, the southernmost point of Africa at 34°49′S, and between about 16°E on the Atlantic coast and about 33°E at Kosi Bay. It is therefore entirely in the Southern and Eastern Hemispheres.

Latitude has a strong link to climate, which is why it matters well beyond map reading. Places near the equator receive the sun's rays almost vertically all year and are hot; places at high latitudes receive slanting rays and are cold, with long summer days and long winter nights. The tropics mark the furthest north and south that the midday sun can ever be directly overhead, and the polar circles mark where, on at least one day a year, the sun does not set or does not rise. Longitude has a strong link to time instead: as the Earth turns through fifteen degrees of longitude every hour, places further east see the sunrise earlier. Both links are developed later this term, and both begin with reading the coordinate correctly.

The questions for this section are with you now: definitions of latitude and longitude, the named parallels, and South Africa's position.

## Subtopic: Degrees, Minutes and Writing Coordinates

One degree is a large distance on the ground. Along a meridian, one degree of latitude is about one hundred and eleven kilometres everywhere on Earth. To locate a town accurately, each degree is divided into sixty minutes, written with a single prime mark, so that 1° = 60′. One minute of latitude is about 1,85 km, which is why it is also the length of one nautical mile used at sea and in the air.

A coordinate is written in a fixed order: latitude first, then longitude. Each part gives degrees, then minutes, then the direction letter. Johannesburg lies at 26°12′S 28°03′E. Read it aloud as twenty-six degrees twelve minutes south, twenty-eight degrees three minutes east. Other examples: Cape Town 33°55′S 18°25′E; Pretoria 25°45′S 28°14′E; Bloemfontein 29°05′S 26°10′E; Cairo 30°03′N 31°14′E; London 51°30′N 0°08′W.

Four rules prevent the common errors. First, latitude always comes before longitude. Second, the direction letter is essential: 26°12′S and 26°12′N are more than five thousand eight hundred kilometres apart. Third, latitude can only be N or S and longitude only E or W. Fourth, minutes never reach sixty; sixty minutes make the next degree, just as sixty minutes make the next hour. Writing minutes with two digits, such as 03′ rather than 3′, avoids confusion.

Estimating minutes between printed lines is the practical skill. An atlas map rarely shows every degree; parallels and meridians may be printed every five or every ten degrees. If a town lies exactly halfway between the 25°S and 30°S parallels, it lies at 27°30′S, because halfway across five degrees is two and a half degrees, and half a degree is thirty minutes. If a town lies one-fifth of the way from 28°E to 29°E, it lies at 28°12′E, because one-fifth of sixty minutes is twelve minutes. To estimate, measure the gap between two printed lines with a ruler, measure from one line to the town, and use the fraction. On a map with lines every degree, a gap of 3 cm means each centimetre is twenty minutes, since 60 ÷ 3 = 20.

The questions for this section are with you now: why degrees are divided into minutes, the order and format of a coordinate, and estimating minutes from a map.

## Subtopic: Using the Atlas Index

The index at the back of an atlas lists every named place on its maps alphabetically. A typical entry gives the place name, the country or province, the page of the map on which it appears, a grid reference such as C3 on that page, and the latitude and longitude. For example, an entry might read: Polokwane, Limpopo, South Africa, page 25, C3, 23°54′S 29°28′E.

To find a place with the index, work in four steps. One: look up the name alphabetically. Places with two words, such as Port Nolloth, are listed under the first word, and places with a common prefix may be listed under the main word, so check both. Two: turn to the page given. Three: go to the grid square, using the letters and numbers in the map's margins, exactly as on a street map. Four: use the coordinates to pinpoint the place within the square, reading the parallels and meridians printed on the map.

The index also works in reverse. Given coordinates, such as 19°50′S 34°51′E, find a map that covers that part of the world, locate the parallel and meridian, and read off the place where they cross. In this case it is Beira, the port city on the coast of Mozambique.

The index can help when two places share a name. There is a Richmond in the Northern Cape and another in KwaZulu-Natal, and the index separates them by province and coordinates. It also helps with spelling, with old and new names, and with places too small to find by scanning a crowded map.

A good habit is to check the coordinates against what you know. Any South African town must have a latitude between about 22°S and 35°S and a longitude between about 16°E and 33°E. A coordinate outside those ranges means the index line was misread.

The questions for this section are with you now: what an index entry contains, the four steps for finding a place, and finding a place from its coordinates.

## Subtopic: Places in the News and the Error Museum

Geography happens in real places, and the news is a daily supply of them. The skill this subtopic builds, and keeps building all year, is to take a place named in the news, find it in the atlas, describe its location precisely, and mark it on a map.

Worked example. In March 2019, Cyclone Idai struck the coast of Mozambique near Beira, then moved inland to Zimbabwe and Malawi, causing severe flooding and the deaths of more than a thousand people. The index gives Beira at about 19°50′S 34°51′E. That tells us it lies in the tropics, north of the Tropic of Capricorn, on the Indian Ocean coast, about ten degrees of latitude north of Durban. Its position explains its exposure: tropical cyclones form over the warm waters of the south-western Indian Ocean in summer and track westwards towards Madagascar and Mozambique.

A second example closer to home. In April 2022, extreme rainfall caused floods and landslides in Durban and surrounding parts of KwaZulu-Natal, killing more than four hundred people and damaging the port and thousands of homes. Durban lies at about 29°52′S 31°01′E on the Indian Ocean coast, with steep valleys behind the city that channel run-off.

Describing a place in the news well uses two kinds of location. Absolute location is the coordinate. Relative location describes the place in relation to others: Beira lies on the coast of central Mozambique, about one thousand two hundred kilometres north-north-east of Durban. Both belong in a good answer. Keeping a news map, a blank world or Africa map on which each week's events are numbered and labelled, builds a mental map that makes every later topic easier.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: writing longitude before latitude. Two: leaving out the direction letter. Three: writing 26°75′S, which cannot exist because minutes stop at fifty-nine. Four: giving a longitude as north or south. Five: confusing the place name in the news with another place of the same name.

Layout for marks: name the place, give the coordinates in the correct order and format, and add one sentence of relative location. For example: Beira, Mozambique, 19°50′S 34°51′E, a port on the Indian Ocean coast north of the Tropic of Capricorn.

The questions for this section are with you now: absolute and relative location, describing a place in the news, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: an address for the whole planet, minutes like a clock, and turning headlines into map pins.

## Subtopic: An Address for the Whole Planet

A street map gives a place a square like C4. That works for one suburb, but not for the whole Earth. So geographers wrapped the globe in a net of imaginary lines.

The lines that go around the Earth like belts are lines of latitude. The middle belt is the equator, zero. Move north or south and the number goes up, until you reach ninety at each pole. So latitude answers one question: how far north or south?

The lines that go from pole to pole like the segments of an orange are lines of longitude. The starting line is the prime meridian, zero, which runs through Greenwich in London. Move east or west and the number goes up, all the way to one hundred and eighty on the other side of the world. So longitude answers: how far east or west?

South Africa is south of the equator and east of Greenwich, so every South African address ends in S for latitude and E for longitude.

There are some famous belts too. The Tropic of Capricorn passes through Limpopo, just north of Polokwane. You can stop next to the sign on the N1 and stand on it.

Why bother? Because the lines do work for us. The closer a place is to the equator, the hotter it tends to be, so latitude gives you a clue about the weather before you even get there. And the further east a place is, the earlier the sun rises there, so longitude gives you a clue about the time. Durban, further east than Cape Town, sees the sunrise about fifty minutes earlier.

Hold the belts and the orange segments in mind and try the questions for this part: what latitude and longitude measure, and where South Africa is.

## Subtopic: Degrees and Minutes Like a Clock

One degree of latitude is about one hundred and eleven kilometres, far too big to pin down a town. So we chop each degree into sixty minutes, exactly like an hour on a clock. Halfway through a degree is thirty minutes, like half past.

Now the address. Johannesburg is 26 degrees 12 minutes south, 28 degrees 3 minutes east. Notice the order: latitude first, always. Like saying the street before the town. And never forget the letters. Without the S, you might end up in the northern half of the world.

Reading minutes off a map is like reading a clock without all the numbers. If a town sits halfway between the lines for 28 degrees and 29 degrees, it is at 28 degrees 30 minutes. If it sits one fifth of the way, it is at 12 minutes, because a fifth of sixty is twelve.

And just like a clock never shows seventy-five minutes, a coordinate never has more than fifty-nine minutes.

Hold the clock in mind and try this part's questions: writing an address in the right order, and reading minutes between the lines.

## Subtopic: From Headline to Map Pin

The atlas index is the search engine at the back of the book. Look up the name. It gives you the page, a grid square, and the full address in degrees and minutes. Go to the page, find the square, then use the lines to find the exact spot.

Now use it on the news. In March 2019, Cyclone Idai hit Beira in Mozambique. Look it up: about 19 degrees 50 minutes south, 34 degrees 51 minutes east. On the map you can see it is on the warm Indian Ocean coast, north of the Tropic of Capricorn, right where summer cyclones arrive from the ocean.

Keep a news map. Each week, when a place is in the news, find it, write its address, and stick a numbered pin or dot on a big blank map. By the end of the year you will know the world far better.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Latitude measures north or south of the equator, up to ninety degrees. Longitude measures east or west of the prime meridian at Greenwich, up to one hundred and eighty. Each degree has sixty minutes. Write latitude first, with its letter, then longitude with its letter. The atlas index gives the page, square and coordinates. Every place in the news has an address you can find.

Hold the belts, the clock and the map pins, and take the final questions of the lesson: finding a place in the index, writing its address, and spotting a wrong coordinate.
