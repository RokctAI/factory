### subtopic_1 — Kinds of Scale in an Atlas
**Assistant:** Why does an atlas need world, regional and local maps instead of one very detailed map?

**Tutor:** Because no single scale can serve every purpose on a page of fixed size, and a map that tried would be either too big to use or too crowded to read. Imagine drawing the whole world at the scale of a South African topographic map, one to fifty thousand. The Earth's circumference at the equator is about forty thousand kilometres, and at that scale each kilometre takes two centimetres, so the equator alone would stretch about eight hundred metres of paper, longer than eight soccer fields placed end to end. No book could hold it. Now imagine the opposite: showing every street of Johannesburg on a world map at one to one hundred million. Johannesburg would be smaller than a full stop, and its streets would vanish entirely. So an atlas divides the job. World maps answer global questions: where the deserts are, how ocean currents flow, which countries are most crowded. Regional maps answer questions about one part of the world: which railways link the ports of southern Africa to the mines, where the provincial boundaries run. Local maps and insets answer detailed questions about one place: the layout of Cape Town between the mountain and the sea. A good atlas also helps the reader move between them. The index gives each place a page and a grid square, often with its latitude and longitude, and the reader picks the page whose scale suits the question. That choice is the skill this subtopic builds: before reading any map, check the scale and ask whether it is the right scale for what you need to know.

### subtopic_2 — Word Scales, Line Scales and Ratio Scales
**Assistant:** If a ratio scale works in any unit, why do we nearly always use centimetres?

**Tutor:** Because centimetres are the most convenient unit for measuring on a sheet of paper, and because the metric system makes the conversion to metres and kilometres easy. A school ruler measures in centimetres and millimetres, and most distances on a map are a few centimetres long, so measuring to one decimal place of a centimetre gives a sensible level of accuracy. Then the metric system does the rest: one hundred centimetres in a metre and one thousand metres in a kilometre, so one hundred thousand centimetres in a kilometre. Converting is a matter of moving the decimal point. But the ratio scale itself does not care which unit you use, and that is exactly why it is so useful. A navigator in a country that measures in inches can read a 1:50 000 map and know that one inch represents fifty thousand inches, which is about one thousand two hundred and seventy metres. A pilot might measure in millimetres on a large chart. An engineer might measure a building plan at 1:100 in millimetres, so that each millimetre on the plan is ten centimetres on site. The relationship holds every time because the same unit appears on both sides of the ratio. This is also why a ratio scale must never be written with units attached. Writing 1 cm:50 000 km would mean one centimetre represents fifty thousand kilometres, more than the distance around the Earth, which is obviously wrong. The ratio is pure: one part on the map to so many of the same parts on the ground.

### subtopic_3 — Converting Between the Three Kinds of Scale
**Assistant:** How can I check that my scale conversion is sensible without doing it all again?

**Tutor:** Use a reasonableness check based on what you already know about real distances and common scales. Start from one fixed point that is easy to remember: on a 1:50 000 map, one centimetre represents half a kilometre, so two centimetres represent one kilometre. From that anchor, other common scales follow. A 1:100 000 map is twice as small, so one centimetre represents one kilometre. A 1:250 000 map is five times smaller than 1:50 000, so one centimetre represents two and a half kilometres. A 1:1 000 000 map gives ten kilometres per centimetre, and 1:10 000 000 gives one hundred. If your answer for a 1:250 000 scale comes out as twenty-five kilometres or two hundred and fifty metres, it does not fit the pattern, so you have moved the decimal point the wrong number of places. A second check uses the real world. Johannesburg to Pretoria is roughly fifty kilometres in a straight line. On a regional map at 1:10 000 000, that would be only half a centimetre, which is about right for how close the two cities look on a map of South Africa. If your calculation said fifty centimetres, the cities would be half a metre apart on the page, which cannot be. A third check is to work backwards. If you converted one centimetre represents five kilometres into 1:500 000, multiply the five kilometres back into centimetres and confirm you get five hundred thousand. Examiners award method marks, but a reasonableness check catches the slip that would have lost the answer mark.

### subtopic_4 — Comparing Scales and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why do people so often think that 1:250 000 is a larger scale than 1:50 000?

**Tutor:** Because in everyday life a bigger number usually means more of something, and the instinct carries over. Two hundred and fifty thousand is clearly bigger than fifty thousand, so it feels as though the scale must be bigger too. The way out is to remember that a ratio scale is really a fraction. 1:50 000 is one fifty-thousandth, and 1:250 000 is one two-hundred-and-fifty-thousandth. Just as one fifth of a pizza is a bigger slice than one twenty-fifth, one fifty-thousandth is a bigger fraction than one two-hundred-and-fifty-thousandth. On the 1:50 000 map, the real world has been reduced to a larger fraction of its size, so features are drawn larger. That is what large scale means. Another way to see it is to think about what one centimetre represents. On the 1:50 000 map, it represents half a kilometre. On the 1:250 000 map, it represents two and a half kilometres. If the same centimetre has to carry five times as much ground, everything on that map must be drawn five times smaller, so the map is smaller scale. In examinations this appears as a direct question, which map is the larger scale, and as a hidden one, which map would show more detail or which map covers a larger area. The answer to all three comes from the same reasoning. The smaller the denominator, the larger the scale, the more detail, and the smaller the area covered on the same size of paper. Writing that sentence once in your notes is worth more than memorising examples.
