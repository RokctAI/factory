### subtopic_1 — Revolution and the Tilted Axis
**Assistant:** Why does the axis keep pointing the same way instead of leaning towards the sun all the time?

**Tutor:** Because a spinning object resists changes to the direction of its axis, and nothing pulls the Earth's axis around to follow the sun. A spinning top or a bicycle wheel shows the same behaviour: while it spins fast, its axis holds its direction, which is why a moving bicycle is easier to balance than a still one. The Earth is a gigantic spinning body, and its axis keeps pointing towards the same part of the sky, close to the Pole Star, as it travels around its orbit. The sun's gravity holds the Earth in its orbit, but it pulls on the whole planet, not on the axis in a way that would swing it round each year. So as the Earth moves from one side of the orbit to the other, the fixed axis means that the Southern Hemisphere is tilted towards the sun on one side and away from it on the other. If the axis did turn to follow the sun, one hemisphere would face the sun permanently and there would be no seasons at all, only permanent summer in one half of the world and permanent winter in the other. Over very long periods the axis does slowly wobble, tracing a circle in the sky over about twenty-six thousand years, and the tilt itself varies slightly between about twenty-two and twenty-four and a half degrees over about forty-one thousand years. These slow changes help explain the ice ages, but they are far too slow to notice in a human lifetime. For Grade 8, the working fact is that the axis is tilted at about twenty-three and a half degrees and points the same way all year.

### subtopic_2 — Solstices and Equinoxes
**Assistant:** Why are the solstice dates sometimes the 21st and sometimes the 22nd?

**Tutor:** Because the calendar year and the Earth's actual journey around the sun do not match exactly, and the solstice is a moment in time, not a whole day. The solstice is the instant when the overhead sun reaches its furthest point north or south. That instant might fall at, say, ten in the morning by Greenwich time in one year. The Earth's year is about three hundred and sixty-five days, five hours and forty-nine minutes long, so the following year the same instant arrives roughly six hours later in the day, and the year after that six hours later again. After a few years it has drifted into the next calendar day. The leap year then pulls it back by a whole day. So the solstice wanders back and forth between the 20th, 21st and 22nd of June and December, and the equinoxes between about the 19th and 21st of March and the 22nd and 23rd of September. Time zones add another wrinkle: a solstice that falls late in the evening of 21 December by Greenwich time is already 22 December in Japan, though still 21 December in South Africa if it falls before ten at night by Greenwich time, because South Africa is two hours ahead. The leap year rule is also slightly more complicated than every four years: century years such as 1900 are not leap years unless divisible by four hundred, which is why 2000 was a leap year. This keeps the calendar aligned with the seasons over centuries. For examinations, about 21 December, about 21 June, about 21 March and about 23 September are accepted, and the word about is honest.

### subtopic_3 — The Changing Angle of the Midday Sun
**Assistant:** Does the midday sun ever stand directly overhead anywhere in South Africa?

**Tutor:** Yes, but only in a small strip of the far north. The midday sun can only be directly overhead between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn, because those are the furthest latitudes the overhead sun ever reaches. The Tropic of Capricorn, at about twenty-three and a half degrees south, crosses Limpopo just north of Polokwane. Everywhere north of that line, up to the Limpopo River at about twenty-two degrees south, lies inside the tropics, and there the sun passes directly overhead at midday on two days each year: once in late November or early December as the overhead sun moves south towards the Tropic of Capricorn, and once in January as it moves back north. Musina and the northern part of the Kruger National Park, around twenty-two and a half degrees south, have this experience. On those days a vertical pole casts no shadow at all at midday. Everywhere else in South Africa, from Polokwane southwards, lies outside the tropics, so the midday sun never reaches the vertical. In Johannesburg its highest is about eighty-seven and a half degrees, on the December solstice, close enough that shadows almost vanish but not quite. In Cape Town, at about thirty-four degrees south, it never climbs above about seventy-nine and a half degrees. This is why midday shadows in almost all of South Africa point south all year: the sun is always somewhat north of overhead. Geography teachers in the far north can show learners a shadowless midday, which is a striking way to see the tropics as real lines on the ground.

### subtopic_4 — Why the Angle Matters and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do builders use the angle of the midday sun when designing a house?

**Tutor:** They design overhangs and window positions so that the house is shaded in summer and sunny in winter, which saves energy for cooling and heating. In South Africa the sun at midday is in the northern sky, high in summer and low in winter. A roof overhang on the north side of a house can be sized so that it blocks the high summer sun from the windows but lets the low winter sun slide underneath it into the rooms. The calculation uses exactly the angles in this lesson. In Johannesburg the midday sun is about eighty-seven and a half degrees high in December, so even a short overhang shades a north-facing window completely at midday. In June it is about forty and a half degrees high, so its rays come in at a slant and reach deep into a room, warming the floor and walls, which then release the heat in the evening. In Cape Town the winter sun is lower still, about thirty-two and a half degrees, so winter sunlight reaches even further inside. Architects also avoid large west-facing windows, because the late-afternoon summer sun from the west is low, hot and hard to shade. Solar water heaters and solar panels are angled to face north at roughly the latitude of the place, about twenty-six degrees in Gauteng and thirty-four in Cape Town, so that their surfaces face the sun as squarely as possible over the whole year. Good design with the sun in mind can make a house several degrees warmer in winter without any heater, which matters for comfort and for electricity bills.
