### subtopic_1 — Rotation, Day and Night
**Assistant:** If the Earth spins so fast, why are we not thrown off into space?

**Tutor:** Because gravity holds us far more strongly than the spin pulls us outwards, and because the spin is steady. Every object moving in a circle tends to carry straight on, and something must pull it inwards to keep it on the curve. For a person on the equator, travelling around a circle of about forty thousand kilometres every twenty-four hours, the inward pull needed is tiny: about three-tenths of one percent of the pull of gravity. Gravity supplies that easily, with plenty left over to hold us firmly on the ground. The effect is measurable, though. A person weighed with a very sensitive scale at the equator registers very slightly lighter than at the poles, partly because of the spin and partly because the equator is a little further from the Earth's centre. The second reason is the steadiness. We feel motion only when it changes: when a taxi brakes, swerves or speeds up. The Earth's rotation is extremely regular, so there is nothing to feel. The air moves with the ground, which is why there is no permanent gale blowing from the east. The spin does have visible effects over long distances and times. It deflects winds and ocean currents, so that the great wind belts curve rather than blowing straight from high to low pressure, and it makes storms rotate. It also makes the Earth slightly fatter around the equator than from pole to pole, by about forty-three kilometres in diameter. So the spin is real and powerful in its effects, but on the scale of a human body, gravity wins easily.

### subtopic_2 — Longitude and Time
**Assistant:** Why does a place further east have a later time rather than an earlier one?

**Tutor:** Because the Earth turns towards the east, so eastern places meet each new position of the sun first. Picture sunrise moving across southern Africa. The circle of illumination sweeps from east to west across the land as the Earth turns. It reaches the coast of KwaZulu-Natal first, then the Highveld, then the Karoo, and finally the west coast. Durban sees the sun rise before Cape Town on the same morning. The same is true of midday: the sun crosses Durban's meridian, reaching its highest point there, about fifty minutes before it crosses Cape Town's. So when it is noon by the sun in Durban, it is only about ten past eleven by the sun in Cape Town. Durban is ahead. Extend this around the world. Places east of us, such as Mauritius, India, China and Japan, have already had their noon when ours arrives, so their clocks are ahead of ours. Places west of us, such as Brazil and the Americas, have not yet reached their noon, so their clocks are behind. A useful memory aid: the sun rises in the east, so the east gets the day first and its clocks lead. When you calculate, ask which place is further east, give it the later time, and then work out the difference at fifteen degrees per hour or four minutes per degree. If you ever get an answer in which the western place is ahead, you have added when you should have subtracted, and it is worth checking again before moving on.

### subtopic_3 — Time Zones and South African Standard Time
**Assistant:** Would it not be fairer for South Africa to use two time zones, one for the west?

**Tutor:** It is a reasonable question, and countries make different choices. The argument for two zones is that the sun's position would match the clock more closely in the west. Under one zone based on 30°E, Cape Town's solar noon falls about forty-five minutes after twelve on the clock, so its winter mornings stay dark late, and children in the Western Cape sometimes walk to school before sunrise. A second zone at UTC plus one would bring the clock and the sun closer together there. The arguments against are practical. South Africa's economy is tightly linked across provinces: national broadcasters, airlines, banks, the stock exchange, schools writing national examinations at the same moment, trains and trucks crossing provincial borders. Two zones would mean every timetable, broadcast schedule and exam paper had to state which time it used, and a border running through the Northern Cape or the Eastern Cape would split communities. Countries choose differently. China, which spans about sixty degrees of longitude, uses a single time zone for the whole country, so in its far west the sun can rise after nine in the morning on the clock. The United States, Russia and Australia use several zones. India, about thirty degrees wide, uses one zone at a half-hour offset. Each choice balances convenience against how well the clock matches the sun. South Africa, about seventeen degrees wide, is narrow enough that one zone works reasonably well, and it has kept one standard time since 1903, which is itself a sign that the trade-off is accepted.

### subtopic_4 — The International Date Line and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why does the date line zigzag instead of following the 180° meridian exactly?

**Tutor:** Because the date line is a human agreement, not a natural boundary, and it bends to serve the people who live near it. The hundred and eightieth meridian was chosen as the basis because it lies on the opposite side of the world from Greenwich, mostly over the open Pacific Ocean, where few people live and a sudden change of date causes little trouble. But the meridian does cross land and island groups. It passes through the far east of Russia, through Fiji, and close to many Pacific island nations. If the date line followed it exactly, Russia's easternmost region would be a day apart from the rest of the country, and parts of Fiji would be on different dates from the capital. So the line swings east around Russia's Chukotka peninsula, bends west around the Aleutian Islands of Alaska so that all of Alaska shares the American date, and weaves between island groups so that each country keeps one date. Countries can choose their side for practical reasons. Kiribati, a nation of islands spread across the meridian, moved the line in 1995 so that all its islands share one date, creating a large eastward bulge. Samoa moved to the western side in 2011, skipping 30 December, because most of its trade was with Australia and New Zealand, and being a day behind them meant losing two business days each week. The rule for travellers is simple: westward across the line, add a day; eastward, subtract a day. The exact path matters only to the people who live along it, and they decide it.
