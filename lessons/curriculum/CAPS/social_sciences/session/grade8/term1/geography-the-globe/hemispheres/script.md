# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the hemispheres from Grade 6 and sharpens them into tools for the rest of the term. The anchor is the globe, a scale model of the Earth, cut in two different ways: along the equator into north and south, and along the prime meridian and the hundred and eightieth meridian into east and west. The governing idea is that hemispheres are defined by reference lines, the same lines that define latitude and longitude, so that the coordinate of any place tells us at once which hemispheres it lies in. The hemispheres matter well beyond labelling. Land, people, oceans and climates are distributed very unevenly between them, and the seasons are reversed between north and south, which is the subject of the coming sessions on the Earth's movements. Hemispheres are basic vocabulary in every grade of Geography that follows.

## Subtopic: The Globe and the Four Hemispheres

A globe is a three-dimensional scale model of the Earth. Unlike a flat map, it shows the shapes, sizes, areas and distances of the continents and oceans with almost no distortion, because it keeps the Earth's spherical shape. Its weakness is that only half of it can be seen at a time and it cannot show local detail.

A hemisphere is half of a sphere. The word comes from the Greek for half and sphere. The Earth can be divided into hemispheres in any direction, but two divisions are standard.

The first division uses the equator, the circle of latitude zero degrees, halfway between the North Pole and the South Pole. Everything north of the equator is in the Northern Hemisphere, and everything south of it is in the Southern Hemisphere. Latitudes followed by N are in the Northern Hemisphere; latitudes followed by S are in the Southern Hemisphere.

The second division uses a complete circle made of two meridians: the prime meridian at zero degrees, through Greenwich, and the meridian of one hundred and eighty degrees on the opposite side of the Earth. Everything east of the prime meridian, up to one hundred and eighty degrees, is in the Eastern Hemisphere; everything west of it, up to one hundred and eighty degrees, is in the Western Hemisphere. Longitudes followed by E are in the Eastern Hemisphere; longitudes followed by W are in the Western Hemisphere.

So every place on Earth lies in two hemispheres at once, one from each division. Johannesburg, at 26°12′S 28°03′E, lies in the Southern and Eastern Hemispheres. London, at 51°30′N 0°08′W, lies in the Northern and Western Hemispheres, just. Rio de Janeiro, at about 22°54′S 43°10′W, lies in the Southern and Western Hemispheres. Tokyo, at about 35°41′N 139°41′E, lies in the Northern and Eastern Hemispheres.

The point where the equator and the prime meridian cross, at zero degrees latitude and zero degrees longitude, lies in the Gulf of Guinea off the coast of West Africa, in the open Atlantic Ocean. It is the only point that belongs to no hemisphere at all, or to all four, depending on how one looks at it.

Atlases often show the world as two round hemisphere maps side by side, because a single circle can show only half of the globe without severe stretching. The most common pair shows the Eastern Hemisphere, with Africa, Europe, Asia and Australia, beside the Western Hemisphere, with the Americas. Some atlases instead show the Northern and Southern Hemispheres as two circles centred on the poles, which makes the polar regions and the Southern Ocean easy to see but bends the familiar shapes of the continents. Reading such maps requires checking which lines form the edge of each circle. Many atlases move the east-west split of these two maps to 20°W and 160°E so that Africa and Europe are not cut in two, even though the true dividing lines are the prime meridian and the hundred and eightieth meridian. A learner who knows the true definitions can read either style and spot the difference.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a globe and a hemisphere are, the lines that divide the four hemispheres, and identifying hemispheres from coordinates.

## Subtopic: Continents and Oceans in Their Hemispheres

The seven continents are Africa, Asia, Europe, North America, South America, Australia and Antarctica, and the five oceans are the Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern and Arctic Oceans.

Africa is unique. The equator crosses it, passing through Gabon, the Republic of the Congo, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Uganda, Kenya and Somalia. The prime meridian also crosses it, passing through Algeria, Mali, Burkina Faso and Ghana, where it reaches the coast near the port of Tema. Africa is therefore the only continent that lies in all four hemispheres. Most of Africa's area lies in the Northern Hemisphere, because the continent is much wider in the north, and most lies in the Eastern Hemisphere.

Europe lies entirely in the Northern Hemisphere and mostly in the Eastern, although Portugal, most of Spain, part of France and most of the United Kingdom and Ireland lie west of the prime meridian. Asia lies mostly in the Northern and Eastern Hemispheres, with parts of Indonesia south of the equator. North America lies in the Northern and Western Hemispheres. South America lies mostly in the Southern and Western Hemispheres, with its northern part, in Colombia, Venezuela and the Guianas, north of the equator. Australia lies in the Southern and Eastern Hemispheres. Antarctica lies entirely in the Southern Hemisphere and, because it surrounds the South Pole, it spans every line of longitude, east and west.

The oceans follow a similar logic. The Pacific Ocean, the largest, spans all four hemispheres, because both the equator and the hundred and eightieth meridian cross it. The Atlantic Ocean spans north and south and lies mostly in the Western Hemisphere. The Indian Ocean lies mostly in the Southern and Eastern Hemispheres, washing the coast of KwaZulu-Natal and the Eastern Cape. The Arctic Ocean surrounds the North Pole, and the Southern Ocean surrounds Antarctica.

South Africa lies between two oceans: the Atlantic on the west coast and the Indian Ocean on the south and east coasts. By international agreement the official boundary between them is the meridian through Cape Agulhas, at about 20°E, not the Cape of Good Hope as is often believed.

The questions for this section are with you now: the continents and oceans in each hemisphere, why Africa lies in all four, and where the Atlantic and Indian Oceans meet.

## Subtopic: Unequal Halves: Land, Water and People

The two halves divided by the equator are equal in area but very unequal in what they contain.

The Northern Hemisphere holds about two-thirds of the world's land. It contains all of Europe and North America, nearly all of Asia, most of Africa and a small part of South America. Even so, it is about sixty percent water. The Southern Hemisphere is dominated by ocean: about four-fifths of its surface is water, so much that it is sometimes called the water hemisphere. Its land is Antarctica, Australia, most of South America, the southern third of Africa, and islands.

People are even more unevenly shared. Roughly nine out of ten people live in the Northern Hemisphere, because it has more land, and because the great population centres of China, India, Europe and North America all lie north of the equator. South Africa, Brazil, Indonesia's southern islands, Argentina and Australia hold most of the Southern Hemisphere's people.

The difference has effects on climate. Water heats up and cools down more slowly than land, so the ocean-dominated Southern Hemisphere has milder extremes than the Northern Hemisphere at the same latitudes. No large landmass in the Southern Hemisphere lies between about 56°S, the southern tip of South America, and the Antarctic coast, so the westerly winds and ocean currents of the Southern Ocean circle the globe almost unbroken. That is the source of the cold fronts that bring winter rain to the Western Cape.

Lines of latitude also divide each hemisphere into broad heat zones. The tropical or torrid zone lies between the Tropic of Cancer at about 23°26′N and the Tropic of Capricorn at about 23°26′S, where the midday sun is directly overhead at least once a year. The two temperate zones lie between the tropics and the polar circles at about 66°34′N and 66°34′S. The two frigid zones lie beyond the polar circles. Almost all of South Africa lies in the southern temperate zone, with only the far north of Limpopo north of the Tropic of Capricorn, in the tropical zone.

The questions for this section are with you now: the distribution of land, water and people between the hemispheres, and the heat zones.

## Subtopic: Opposite Seasons and the Error Museum

The most important consequence of the hemispheres for daily life is that the seasons are opposite in the north and the south. When it is summer in the Northern Hemisphere, in June, July and August, it is winter in the Southern Hemisphere. When it is summer in the south, in December, January and February, it is winter in the north. A learner in Johannesburg celebrates the new year in the heat of summer, while a learner in London celebrates it in winter. The reason lies in the tilt of the Earth's axis as it travels around the sun, which is the subject of a later session this term. For now the rule to hold is simple: same date, opposite seasons.

The hemispheres also affect what is seen in the sky. The constellation known as the Southern Cross, Crux, is visible from the Southern Hemisphere and has long been used to find the direction of south at night; it cannot be seen from most of the Northern Hemisphere. Large storm systems turn in opposite directions in the two hemispheres. Tropical cyclones in the Southern Hemisphere, such as those that reach Mozambique, rotate clockwise, while hurricanes in the Northern Hemisphere rotate anticlockwise. This effect is real for systems hundreds of kilometres wide. The common claim that water drains from a basin in opposite directions in the two hemispheres is a myth: a basin is far too small for the effect, and the direction depends on its shape and on how the water was poured.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying that the prime meridian alone divides east from west; the division needs both the prime meridian and the hundred and eightieth meridian. Two: placing South Africa in the Western Hemisphere because it is near the Atlantic. Three: saying Africa lies only in the Southern Hemisphere; most of Africa is north of the equator. Four: placing the meeting of the Atlantic and Indian Oceans at the Cape of Good Hope instead of Cape Agulhas. Five: believing basins drain differently in each hemisphere.

Layout for marks: when asked for the hemispheres of a place, give both, one from each division, with the reason. For example: Johannesburg lies in the Southern Hemisphere because its latitude is south of the equator, and in the Eastern Hemisphere because its longitude is east of the prime meridian.

The questions for this section are with you now: opposite seasons, clues in the sky and in storms, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: an orange cut two ways, the halves you live in, and Christmas in summer.

## Subtopic: Cut the Orange Two Ways

Take an orange and pretend it is the Earth. Cut it around the middle, the way the equator runs, and you have a top half and a bottom half. The top half is the Northern Hemisphere. The bottom half is the Southern Hemisphere. Hemisphere just means half a ball.

Now take a second orange and cut it from top to bottom, through the stalk. One half is east, one half is west. On the Earth, that cut runs along the prime meridian through Greenwich, then comes back up the other side of the world along the hundred and eightieth line in the Pacific. It needs both lines, because a cut through an orange goes all the way around.

So every place lives in two halves at once: one from the first cut and one from the second. You can tell which from its address. An S in the latitude means south. An E in the longitude means east. Johannesburg is S and E, so it lives in the Southern and the Eastern Hemispheres.

A globe is the best place to see all this, because a globe is a little round Earth with nothing stretched.

Hold the two cuts in mind and try the questions for this part: the lines that make each cut, and which halves Johannesburg is in.

## Subtopic: Which Halves Hold What

Now look at what landed in each half. Africa is the only continent cut by both knives. The equator slices through the middle, through Kenya and the Congo, and the prime meridian slices through the west, through Ghana. So Africa is in all four halves.

Australia sits in the south and east. South America is mostly south and west. North America is north and west. Europe and nearly all of Asia are north. Antarctica sits right at the bottom, all south.

Here is the surprise. The top half has most of the land and most of the people, about nine out of every ten people on Earth. The bottom half is mostly sea, about four-fifths water. If you are in South Africa, you live in the quieter, wetter half of the world.

And South Africa sits between two oceans. The Atlantic is on the west coast, at Cape Town and Port Nolloth. The Indian Ocean is on the east coast, at Durban. The official meeting line is at Cape Agulhas, the southern tip of Africa.

Hold the two knives and the uneven halves in mind and try this part's questions: Africa in four halves, and where most people live.

## Subtopic: Christmas in Summer

In South Africa, Christmas comes in the middle of summer. People go to the beach, braai outside and swim. In London, Christmas comes in the middle of winter, with short dark days and sometimes snow. Same day, opposite seasons.

That is the biggest thing the halves do to your life. When the north has summer, the south has winter, and the other way round. The reason is the tilt of the Earth, and you will see exactly how that works in a later lesson this term.

The halves change the night sky too. From South Africa you can see the Southern Cross, a little kite of stars that points towards south. And big storms spin differently. A cyclone heading for Mozambique spins clockwise. A hurricane heading for America spins the other way. But the story about basins draining backwards in the south is a myth. A basin is much too small.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A hemisphere is half the Earth. The equator splits north from south. The prime meridian and the hundred and eightieth meridian together split east from west. Every place is in two hemispheres. South Africa is in the Southern and Eastern. Africa is the only continent in all four. Most land and most people are in the north. Seasons are opposite in north and south.

Hold the two cut oranges and the summer Christmas, and take the final questions of the lesson: naming hemispheres, Africa's four halves, and opposite seasons.
