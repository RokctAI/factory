Hey — if solstices and equinoxes sound complicated, here is the calm version.

The Earth goes all the way round the sun once a year, about three hundred and sixty-five and a quarter days. The extra quarters make a leap year every fourth year.

The Earth leans, about twenty-three and a half degrees, and it keeps leaning the same way all year. So for half the year our bottom half of the world leans towards the sun, and for half it leans away.

Four special days. Around 21 December we lean most towards the sun: our longest day, sun overhead at the Tropic of Capricorn. Around 21 June we lean most away: our shortest day, sun overhead at the Tropic of Cancer. Around 21 March and 23 September the sun is overhead at the equator, and day and night are equal.

To find how high the midday sun is, take ninety and subtract how far you are from where the sun is overhead. Same side of the equator, subtract the latitudes. Opposite sides, add them.

Remember: seasons come from the lean, not from being closer to the sun.
