# Part 1 — Expert

This session completes the study of the Earth's movements by explaining the seasons themselves: the yearly rhythm of long and short days and of warm and cool weather. The anchor is a comparison of three South African cities, Durban, Johannesburg and Cape Town, using their real day lengths at the solstices and their average temperatures in January and July. The governing idea is that the tilt of the Earth's axis produces two effects that work together. In summer a hemisphere has longer days, so the sun heats the ground for more hours, and a higher midday sun, so each hour delivers more energy to each square metre. In winter both effects reverse. Day length, temperature and the seasonal lag are examinable this term, and they lead directly into the climate topic of Term 2.

## Subtopic: Why Day Length Changes

The circle of illumination, the boundary between day and night, always cuts the Earth into two equal halves. But because the axis is tilted, the circle of illumination does not usually pass through the poles, so it cuts each line of latitude unequally.

On the December solstice, the Southern Hemisphere leans towards the sun. Every parallel south of the equator has more than half of its length in the light, so places in the south spend more than twelve hours of each rotation in daylight. Every parallel north of the equator has less than half in the light, so places in the north have days shorter than twelve hours. The further south a place is, the larger the lit part of its parallel and the longer its day. South of the Antarctic Circle, the whole parallel is in the light, and the sun does not set for twenty-four hours. North of the Arctic Circle, the whole parallel is dark, and the sun does not rise.

On the June solstice, everything reverses. The Northern Hemisphere has long days and the Southern Hemisphere short ones, and the polar day and polar night swap poles.

At the equinoxes, the circle of illumination passes exactly through both poles, every parallel is cut exactly in half, and day and night are about twelve hours each everywhere on Earth.

The equator is the special case. It is always cut in half by the circle of illumination, whatever the season, because it is a great circle centred on the Earth's centre. So places on the equator have days of about twelve hours all year round, with almost no seasonal change.

Real figures for South Africa at the solstices, from sunrise to sunset: Durban, about fourteen hours in December and about ten and a quarter hours in June. Johannesburg, about thirteen and three-quarter hours in December and about ten and a half in June. Cape Town, about fourteen and a half hours in December and just under ten in June. The difference between the longest and shortest day is about three and a quarter hours in Johannesburg but about four and a half hours in Cape Town, which is further from the equator.

The questions for this section are with you now: why the tilt makes days longer in summer, the equator as a special case, and polar day and night.

## Subtopic: Day Length Across the World

The pattern can be stated as a rule: the further a place is from the equator, the greater the difference between its summer and winter day lengths.

Musina in far northern Limpopo, at about twenty-two degrees south, swings between about thirteen and a half hours and about ten and three-quarter hours, a difference of under three hours. Cape Town, at about thirty-four degrees south, swings by about four and a half hours. London, at about fifty-one and a half degrees north, swings between about sixteen and two-thirds hours in June and under eight hours in December, a difference of nearly nine hours. Tromsø in northern Norway, at nearly seventy degrees north, has about two months of continuous daylight in summer, when the sun does not set, and about seven weeks in winter when it does not rise. Its people call the summer period the midnight sun and the winter period the polar night.

At the poles themselves, the year becomes a single day and a single night: about six months of continuous daylight, from the March equinox to the September equinox at the North Pole, and about six months of darkness. Research stations in Antarctica, including South Africa's base SANAE IV, experience months without sunrise in the southern winter.

The pattern explains differences in how people live. Near the equator, sunrise and sunset come at almost the same clock time all year, around six in the morning and six in the evening. At high latitudes, summer evenings stay light until late at night and winter days are brief, which shapes work, school and energy use. Many high-latitude countries use daylight saving time to shift summer daylight into the evening.

Day length also changes steadily rather than suddenly. Between the solstices it changes fastest around the equinoxes, by about two minutes a day in Cape Town, and most slowly around the solstices, which is why the longest days of December feel similar for a week or two.

The questions for this section are with you now: the rule linking latitude and day-length range, examples from Musina to the poles, and the midnight sun.

## Subtopic: From Day Length to Temperature

Two effects of the tilt combine to make summer warm and winter cool.

The first is the angle of the midday sun, met in the previous session. In summer the sun climbs high, its rays strike steeply and concentrate their energy, and they pass through less atmosphere. In winter the sun stays low, and the same energy spreads over a larger area.

The second is day length. In summer the sun shines for more hours, so the ground absorbs heat for longer each day and has fewer hours of darkness in which to lose it. In winter the short days and long nights mean less heating and more cooling, so cold nights and frost become common on the interior plateau.

Together these effects produce the seasonal pattern of temperature. Average daily maximum temperatures, rounded, show it clearly. Johannesburg: about twenty-six degrees Celsius in January and about seventeen in July, a difference of nine degrees. Cape Town: about twenty-six in January and about eighteen in July, a difference of eight degrees. Durban: about twenty-eight in January and about twenty-three in July, a difference of only five degrees. Night-time minimums show even bigger contrasts in the interior: Johannesburg averages about fifteen degrees on January nights but only about four on July nights.

Durban's small range has two causes: it is closer to the equator than Cape Town, and it lies beside the warm Indian Ocean, which keeps winters mild. Johannesburg's larger range of night temperatures reflects its position far inland and high above sea level. These factors, latitude, distance from the sea, altitude and ocean currents, are the climate controls studied in Term 2. The seasons supply the yearly rhythm; the climate controls decide how strong it is in each place.

Cloud and rain modify the pattern from day to day. A cloudy summer afternoon on the Highveld, when thunderstorms build after midday, can be cooler than a clear spring afternoon, because the cloud reflects sunlight before it reaches the ground. A clear winter sky lets in strong sunshine by day but also lets heat escape at night, which is why the interior's winter days can be pleasant while its nights are freezing. Over a whole season, however, the averages follow the sun: more hours and a higher angle mean more energy and higher temperatures. That is why geographers use long-term averages, usually thirty years of records, rather than single days when they describe the seasons of a place.

The four seasons in South Africa are usually taken as summer from December to February, autumn from March to May, winter from June to August, and spring from September to November.

The questions for this section are with you now: the two effects that make summer warm, and the temperature figures for three cities.

## Subtopic: The Seasonal Lag and the Error Museum

If the sun's energy is greatest at the December solstice, why are January and February usually hotter than December? And if it is weakest at the June solstice, why is July usually colder than June?

The answer is the seasonal lag. The land, the oceans and the air store heat. After the solstice, the Southern Hemisphere still receives more energy each day than it loses, even though the amount received has begun to fall, so it keeps warming for several weeks. Temperatures peak only when the energy gained each day falls to equal the energy lost. In the same way, after the June solstice the hemisphere goes on losing more heat than it gains for several weeks, so the coldest month is July. The lag is longer near the sea, because water stores more heat and releases it more slowly; in some coastal places February is the warmest month.

A kettle on a stove shows the same idea. Turning the stove down does not instantly cool the water; it keeps heating for a while, and it cools only slowly once the heat is removed.

The seasons shape life in South Africa. Summer-rainfall farmers in the interior plant maize in spring so that it grows through the long, hot, wet summer days. Winter-rainfall farmers in the Western Cape grow wheat through the cool, wet winter. Many trees lose their leaves in autumn and grow new ones in spring, triggered partly by day length. Birds such as swallows migrate south to South Africa for the southern summer and north to Europe for the northern summer, following the long days and the insects they bring. The school year itself has its long holiday in the heat of December.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying that the equator has long days in December; its days are about twelve hours all year. Two: saying that the hottest day of the year must be the longest day. Three: saying that everywhere in the Southern Hemisphere has the same day length on the same date. Four: giving polar day to the North Pole in December. Five: forgetting that day length and sun angle work together; neither alone explains the seasons.

Layout for marks in an explain question: state the tilt, state the two effects with the season, and give an example with figures. For example: in December the Southern Hemisphere is tilted towards the sun, so days are longer, about fourteen and a half hours in Cape Town, and the midday sun is higher; both increase heating, so it is summer.

The questions for this section are with you now: the seasonal lag, seasons in South African life, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Long Days, Short Days

In December you can play outside in Cape Town until well after eight at night, because the sun is up for about fourteen and a half hours. In June it is dark before six, and the sun is up for less than ten hours. That is a difference of about four and a half hours.

Why? Because in December our half of the world leans towards the sun. As the Earth spins, we spend more of each turn on the sunny side. In June we lean away, and we spend more of each turn in the dark.

The further you are from the equator, the bigger the swing. In Musina, near the Limpopo River, the swing is under three hours. In Cape Town it is four and a half. In London it is nearly nine. Right on the equator there is almost no swing at all: about twelve hours of day, every day of the year.

And at the poles it goes crazy. At the South Pole the sun stays up for about six months and then disappears for about six months. One long day and one long night each year.

## Subtopic: Two Reasons It Gets Hot

Summer is hot for two reasons that team up.

Reason one: the sun is high. A high sun is like a torch shining straight down, making a small, hot spot. In winter the sun is low, like a tilted torch, and its heat spreads thin.

Reason two: the days are long. In summer the sun has more hours to heat the ground, and the night is too short for the ground to cool down properly. In winter the opposite: a short day of weak heating, then a long, cold night. That is why Highveld winter mornings can be frosty.

You can see it in the numbers. Johannesburg's average afternoon temperature is about twenty-six degrees in January and about seventeen in July. Durban's is about twenty-eight in January and twenty-three in July: a smaller swing, because the warm sea next to it keeps the winter mild.

In South Africa, summer is December to February, autumn March to May, winter June to August, and spring September to November.

## Subtopic: The Stove That Keeps Heating

Here is a puzzle. The longest, strongest sunshine is around 21 December. So why is January usually hotter? Think of a pot on a stove. When you turn the stove down, the water does not cool at once; it keeps getting warmer for a little while, and then slowly cools. The land and especially the sea are like that pot. They keep warming for weeks after the solstice, so the hottest time comes in January or February. And the coldest time comes in July, after the shortest day in June.

The seasons run a lot of our lives. Maize farmers plant in spring for the hot, rainy summer. Wheat farmers in the Western Cape grow their crops through the wet winter. Trees drop leaves in autumn. Swallows fly all the way from Europe to spend our summer here. Even the long school holiday sits in the middle of summer.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The tilt gives longer days in summer and shorter days in winter. The further from the equator, the bigger the change; the equator stays at about twelve hours. The poles get six months of day and six of night. Summer is hot because the sun is high and the days are long. The hottest and coldest months come a few weeks after the solstices, because land and sea store heat.

Hold the long evenings, the torch and the pot on the stove, and take the final questions of the lesson: day length, the two reasons for summer heat, and the seasonal lag.
