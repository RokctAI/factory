# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains day and night and the world's system of time from a single movement: the rotation of the Earth on its axis. The anchor is a phone call from Johannesburg to a relative in another country, which must be timed so that it does not wake them at three in the morning, and a flight from Johannesburg to London that lands at what looks like an impossible hour. The governing idea is that the Earth turns three hundred and sixty degrees in twenty-four hours, which is fifteen degrees of longitude every hour, so time changes steadily with longitude. From that fact come local time, the standard time zones, South African Standard Time and the international date line. Time calculations are examinable this term and recur in Grade 10 Geography and in everyday life.

## Subtopic: Rotation, Day and Night

The Earth spins on its axis, an imaginary line through the North and South Poles. This spinning is called rotation. The Earth rotates from west to east, which, seen from above the North Pole, is anticlockwise. One full rotation relative to the sun takes twenty-four hours, which is the length of a solar day.

At any moment, the sun lights exactly half of the Earth. The half facing the sun has day; the half facing away has night. The boundary between them is called the circle of illumination, and it moves steadily across the Earth's surface as the planet turns. Places crossing it from the dark side into the light have sunrise; places crossing from the light into the dark have sunset. Because the Earth turns towards the east, the sun appears to rise in the east, move across the sky and set in the west. The sun is not moving across our sky; we are being carried past it.

Rotation is fast. The Earth's circumference at the equator is about forty thousand kilometres, so a point on the equator travels that distance in twenty-four hours, which is about one thousand six hundred and seventy kilometres per hour. At the latitude of Johannesburg, the circle travelled is smaller, and the speed is about one thousand five hundred kilometres per hour. At the poles, the speed is zero; a person standing on the pole simply turns on the spot. We do not feel this motion because everything around us, including the air, moves with us at the same steady speed.

Rotation has other effects besides day and night. It deflects winds and ocean currents, to the left in the Southern Hemisphere and to the right in the Northern, which shapes the direction of the global winds and the spin of cyclones. It makes the Earth bulge slightly at the equator. And it gives every place on Earth a rhythm of light and dark to which plants, animals and people are adapted.

The questions for this section are with you now: the direction and period of rotation, the circle of illumination, and why the sun appears to rise in the east.

## Subtopic: Longitude and Time

Because the Earth turns through three hundred and sixty degrees in twenty-four hours, it turns 360 ÷ 24 = 15 degrees every hour. Divide again, and it turns one degree every four minutes, because 60 ÷ 15 = 4. These two facts connect longitude with time.

Local solar time is set by the sun. When the sun is at its highest point in the sky at a place, it is midday, twelve noon, local solar time, and the sun lies exactly over that place's meridian. Places further east see the sun reach its highest point earlier, because the Earth carries them towards the sun first. So the further east a place is, the later its local time at any instant. The further west, the earlier.

Worked example. Two places are 45° of longitude apart. The time difference is 45 ÷ 15 = 3 hours. If the place further east has local time 15:00, the place further west has 12:00.

Worked example within South Africa. Durban lies at about 31°E and Cape Town at about 18°E, a difference of about thirteen degrees. Thirteen degrees at four minutes per degree is fifty-two minutes. The sun reaches its highest point over Durban nearly an hour before it does over Cape Town. If every town kept its own solar time, Durban's clocks would run about fifty minutes ahead of Cape Town's.

That was the situation in the nineteenth century, when each town set its clocks by the sun. It caused no trouble when travel was slow, but once railways and telegraphs linked towns, timetables became confusing, with every station on its own time. The solution was standard time. In 1884 the same international conference that fixed the prime meridian at Greenwich also laid the foundation for a world system of time based on it. Time at the prime meridian became the reference, known as Greenwich Mean Time and today, in its scientific form, as Coordinated Universal Time, written UTC.

The questions for this section are with you now: fifteen degrees per hour and four minutes per degree, local solar time, and why standard time was needed.

## Subtopic: Time Zones and South African Standard Time

The world is divided into time zones, regions that keep the same standard time. In principle there are twenty-four zones, each fifteen degrees of longitude wide, centred on the meridians that are multiples of fifteen degrees: zero, fifteen, thirty and so on. Each zone differs from its neighbour by one hour. Zones east of Greenwich are ahead of UTC, written as UTC plus one, plus two and so on; zones west are behind, written UTC minus one, minus two. In practice the boundaries zigzag to follow national and provincial borders, so that a country or region is not split, and a few countries use half-hour or quarter-hour offsets: India uses UTC plus five and a half hours.

South Africa uses South African Standard Time, written SAST, which is UTC plus two hours. It is based on the meridian of 30°E, which runs through the eastern part of the country, east of Johannesburg and west of Durban. The whole country uses this one time zone, together with Lesotho and Eswatini, even though South Africa stretches from about 16°E to about 33°E. South Africa does not use daylight saving time, so SAST stays the same all year.

One zone for a wide country has consequences. In Cape Town, about twelve degrees west of 30°E, the sun reaches its highest point about three-quarters of an hour after twelve noon on the clock, varying a little through the year. Mornings in Cape Town are darker and evenings lighter than in Durban at the same clock time. The country accepts this in return for the convenience of one time for business, broadcasting, transport and government.

Calculating time in other zones is a matter of counting offsets. When it is 14:00 SAST in Johannesburg, it is 12:00 UTC at Greenwich, because SAST is two hours ahead. Tokyo uses UTC plus nine, seven hours ahead of South Africa, so it is 21:00 there. New York, in its winter, uses UTC minus five, seven hours behind South Africa, so it is 07:00 there. Some countries, including the United Kingdom and the United States, move their clocks forward one hour in their summer, called daylight saving time, so the difference from South Africa changes during the year. When it is summer in Britain, London is only one hour behind South Africa.

Reading a world time zone map is the practical form of this skill. Such maps colour each zone and label it with its offset from UTC, often along the top or bottom edge. To find the time difference between two places, read both offsets and subtract: Johannesburg plus two and Nairobi plus three differ by one hour, with Nairobi ahead; Johannesburg plus two and São Paulo minus three differ by five hours, with Johannesburg ahead. When the offsets have opposite signs, the difference is the sum of their sizes, because one place is ahead of Greenwich and the other behind it. Checking which place lies further east confirms which one is ahead.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a time zone is, the basis of SAST, and calculating the time in another zone.

## Subtopic: The International Date Line and the Error Museum

If you travel east, you add an hour at each time zone. Keep going all the way round the world and you would add twenty-four hours, arriving home a day ahead of the calendar. Travel west and you would lose a day. Magellan's surviving crew discovered exactly this in 1522, when they returned to Spain after sailing around the world westwards and found their careful ship's log one day behind the date on land.

The solution is the international date line, which runs roughly along the 180° meridian through the Pacific Ocean. It is where the calendar date changes. Crossing it travelling westwards, from the Americas towards Asia, you add one day: Monday becomes Tuesday. Crossing it travelling eastwards, you subtract one day: Tuesday becomes Monday. The line zigzags around island nations so that they are not split between two dates. In 2011 Samoa moved to the western side of the line to align its business days with Australia and New Zealand, and its people went straight from Thursday 29 December to Saturday 31 December, skipping Friday 30 December entirely.

Worked flight example. A flight leaves Johannesburg at 20:00 SAST and flies for eleven hours to London, during the British winter when London uses UTC. Arrival is eleven hours after departure, at 07:00 SAST the next morning. London is two hours behind, so the local arrival time is 05:00. The passenger sees a clock showing five o'clock, though eleven hours have passed.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the sun moves around the Earth; it is the Earth's rotation that makes it appear to. Two: subtracting time when travelling east; going east, clocks go forward. Three: using four minutes per degree and fifteen degrees per hour the wrong way round. Four: forgetting that daylight saving time in other countries changes the difference from SAST. Five: adding a day when crossing the date line eastwards instead of westwards.

Layout for marks: state the longitude difference or the zone offsets, state the rule used, show the calculation, and give the time with am or pm or in twenty-four-hour form, and the day if it changes. For example: Tokyo is UTC plus nine and Johannesburg UTC plus two, so Tokyo is seven hours ahead; 14:00 plus seven hours is 21:00 the same day.

The questions for this section are with you now: the date line, a flight calculation, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: a spinning ball and a torch, fifteen degrees every hour, and the line where tomorrow begins.

## Subtopic: The Ball and the Torch

Put a torch on a table in a dark room and hold a ball in front of it. Half the ball is lit; half is dark. Now spin the ball slowly. A spot on the ball comes round into the light, that is sunrise, crosses the lit side, that is day, then slides back into the dark, that is sunset and night.

That is all day and night are. The sun stays put, and the Earth spins. It spins once every twenty-four hours, from west to east, which is why the sun seems to come up in the east and go down in the west. It is not the sun climbing; it is us being carried round to face it.

And we are going fast. A person on the equator is carried about one thousand six hundred and seventy kilometres every hour. In Johannesburg it is about one thousand five hundred. You never feel it, because the ground, the air and everything around you are moving at the same speed, like a smooth flight where your cooldrink sits still on the tray.

Hold the ball and the torch in mind and try the questions for this part: why we have day and night, and why the sun rises in the east.

## Subtopic: Fifteen Degrees Every Hour

The Earth turns all the way round, three hundred and sixty degrees, in twenty-four hours. Share that out: fifteen degrees every hour. That is the magic number.

So if two cities are fifteen degrees apart, their clocks are one hour apart. Forty-five degrees apart, three hours apart. The city further east is always later, because it turned into the sunlight first. East is ahead.

Durban is further east than Cape Town, so the sun reaches its highest point there almost an hour earlier. Long ago every town set its own clock by the sun, and the trains were a mess. So the world agreed on time zones: big stripes about fifteen degrees wide, each one hour apart, starting from Greenwich in London.

South Africa uses one stripe for the whole country, South African Standard Time, two hours ahead of Greenwich. So when it is two in the afternoon in Johannesburg, it is twelve noon in Greenwich. Tokyo is seven hours ahead of us, so it is nine at night there. New York in its winter is seven hours behind, so it is seven in the morning.

Hold the magic fifteen in mind and try this part's questions: one hour per fifteen degrees, and east is ahead.

## Subtopic: Where Tomorrow Begins

If you kept flying east, adding an hour at every stripe, you would eventually add a whole day. So somewhere the calendar has to jump. That somewhere is the international date line, out in the middle of the Pacific Ocean along the hundred and eightieth line. Cross it going west and you jump forward a day. Cross it going east and you go back a day. Samoa once switched sides and skipped a whole Friday.

Now try a trip. You leave Johannesburg at eight at night and fly eleven hours to London in the British winter. In South African time you land at seven the next morning. But London is two hours behind, so the clock at the airport says five in the morning. You have been flying all night, and it is still early.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Earth spins once in twenty-four hours, west to east, which gives day and night and makes the sun rise in the east. It turns fifteen degrees every hour, one degree every four minutes. East is ahead, west is behind. Time zones are stripes about fifteen degrees wide. South Africa uses one zone, two hours ahead of Greenwich. The date line in the Pacific is where the date changes.

Hold the ball, the torch and the magic fifteen, and take the final questions of the lesson: a time difference, a flight, and the date line.
