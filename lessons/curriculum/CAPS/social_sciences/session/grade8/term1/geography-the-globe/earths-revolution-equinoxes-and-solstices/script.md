# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the second movement of the Earth, its revolution around the sun, and how the tilt of the axis turns that journey into the changing angle of the midday sun. The anchor is a simple observation anyone can make from a school yard in Johannesburg: at midday in December the sun stands almost directly overhead and shadows are tiny, while at midday in June the sun is low in the northern sky and shadows stretch long. The governing idea is that the Earth's axis is tilted and keeps pointing in the same direction in space throughout the year, so the latitude at which the midday sun is directly overhead moves between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn. The solstices, the equinoxes and the midday sun angle are examinable this term, and they are the foundation of the next session on seasons and of the climate topic in Term 2.

## Subtopic: Revolution and the Tilted Axis

Revolution is the movement of the Earth around the sun. One complete revolution takes about three hundred and sixty-five and a quarter days, which is one year. Because a calendar year has a whole number of days, the extra quarter-day is gathered into an extra day every fourth year, the leap year, when February has twenty-nine days. The Earth travels in an orbit, a path around the sun that is very nearly a circle, at an average distance of about one hundred and fifty million kilometres, moving at roughly one hundred and seven thousand kilometres per hour.

The orbit is slightly oval, so the distance varies a little. The Earth is closest to the sun, at about one hundred and forty-seven million kilometres, in early January, and furthest, at about one hundred and fifty-two million kilometres, in early July. The difference is only about three percent, and it is not the cause of the seasons.

The key fact is the tilt. The Earth's axis is not upright relative to the plane of its orbit. It is tilted at about twenty-three and a half degrees from the vertical, or, put the other way, at about sixty-six and a half degrees to the plane of the orbit. More importantly, the axis keeps pointing in the same direction in space all year round. The North Pole always points towards a point in the sky near the Pole Star. As a result, on one side of the orbit the Southern Hemisphere leans towards the sun, and six months later, on the opposite side of the orbit, it leans away.

When a hemisphere leans towards the sun, the sun climbs higher in its sky at midday, its rays strike the ground more steeply and concentrate their energy on a smaller area, and days are longer than nights. When it leans away, the sun stays lower, its rays spread their energy over a larger area, and nights are longer than days. That is the origin of the seasons, developed in the next session.

The numbers twenty-three and a half and sixty-six and a half are not coincidences. The tropics lie at about twenty-three and a half degrees north and south, and the polar circles at about sixty-six and a half degrees, precisely because of the tilt.

The questions for this section are with you now: the period of revolution and the leap year, the angle and direction of the axis, and why distance does not cause the seasons.

## Subtopic: Solstices and Equinoxes

As the Earth travels around its orbit, the latitude at which the midday sun is directly overhead, so that its rays strike the ground at ninety degrees, moves north and south between the two tropics. Four dates mark the turning points.

The December solstice, on about 21 or 22 December, is when the Southern Hemisphere leans furthest towards the sun. The midday sun is directly overhead at the Tropic of Capricorn, about 23,5°S, which passes through Limpopo. It is the summer solstice for South Africa and the longest day of the year in the Southern Hemisphere. South of the Antarctic Circle, the sun does not set at all for twenty-four hours.

The June solstice, on about 21 June, is the reverse. The Northern Hemisphere leans furthest towards the sun, and the midday sun is directly overhead at the Tropic of Cancer, about 23,5°N, which crosses the Sahara, Egypt near Aswan, India and southern China. It is the winter solstice for South Africa, with the shortest day and longest night of the year. South of the Antarctic Circle, the sun does not rise.

The two equinoxes fall between the solstices. On about 21 March and about 23 September, neither hemisphere leans towards the sun, and the midday sun is directly overhead at the equator. Day and night are about twelve hours each everywhere on Earth, which is what equinox means: equal night. In the Southern Hemisphere, the March equinox is the autumn equinox and the September equinox is the spring equinox, the reverse of the names used in the north.

So over a year, the overhead sun travels from the Tropic of Capricorn in December, northwards across the equator in March, to the Tropic of Cancer in June, back south across the equator in September, and to the Tropic of Capricorn again in December. It never goes further north or south than the tropics, which is why the tropics are defined where they are. The exact dates shift by a day from year to year because the year is not a whole number of days.

The questions for this section are with you now: the dates of the solstices and equinoxes, where the midday sun is overhead on each, and the names used in the Southern Hemisphere.

## Subtopic: The Changing Angle of the Midday Sun

The angle of the midday sun is its height above the horizon at midday, measured in degrees from zero at the horizon to ninety directly overhead. It can be calculated from two latitudes: the latitude of the place and the latitude where the sun is overhead that day.

The rule: find the difference in degrees between the two latitudes, then subtract it from ninety. When the place and the overhead sun are in the same hemisphere, the difference is the larger latitude minus the smaller; when they are in opposite hemispheres, the difference is the sum of the two latitudes. The overhead latitude is 23,5°S in December, 0° at the equinoxes, and 23,5°N in June.

Worked examples for Johannesburg, at about 26°S.

December solstice: the sun is overhead at 23,5°S, in the same hemisphere. The difference is 26 − 23,5 = 2,5 degrees. The angle is 90 − 2,5 = 87,5 degrees, nearly vertical.

Equinoxes: the sun is overhead at the equator. The difference is twenty-six degrees, so the angle is 90 − 26 = 64 degrees.

June solstice: the sun is overhead at 23,5°N, in the opposite hemisphere. The difference is 26 + 23,5 = 49,5 degrees. The angle is 90 − 49,5 = 40,5 degrees, low in the northern sky.

Worked examples for Cape Town, at about 34°S. December: the difference is 34 − 23,5 = 10,5 degrees, so the angle is 79,5 degrees. Equinoxes: 90 − 34 = 56 degrees. June: the difference is 34 + 23,5 = 57,5 degrees, so the angle is 90 − 57,5 = 32,5 degrees.

Two patterns appear. First, the midday sun in any one place swings through forty-seven degrees between the solstices, twice twenty-three and a half. Second, the further a place is from the equator, the lower its midday sun at every season: Cape Town's sun is always about eight degrees lower than Johannesburg's, matching the eight-degree difference in latitude. Everywhere in South Africa except the far north of Limpopo, the midday sun is always in the northern part of the sky, so midday shadows always point south, shortest in December and longest in June.

The questions for this section are with you now: the rule for the angle of the midday sun, and the Johannesburg and Cape Town examples.

## Subtopic: Why the Angle Matters and the Error Museum

The angle of the sun controls how much energy each square metre of ground receives. Imagine a torch shining straight down onto a table: it makes a small, bright circle. Tilt the torch and the same light spreads into a larger, dimmer oval. A high midday sun is the straight torch: concentrated energy on a small area. A low midday sun is the tilted torch: the same energy spread over a larger area, so each square metre receives less. A low sun's rays also pass through more atmosphere, which absorbs and scatters some of the energy before it reaches the ground.

That is why Johannesburg's December sun at eighty-seven and a half degrees heats the ground strongly, while its June sun at forty and a half degrees heats it far less, even on a cloudless day. It is also why places near the equator, where the midday sun is always high, are hot all year, and why the polar regions, where it is always low or below the horizon, are cold.

Builders and farmers use these angles. A house in South Africa with its main windows facing north, and with roof overhangs of the right depth, lets in the low winter sun to warm the rooms while the overhang shades out the high summer sun. Solar panels in South Africa face north and are tilted at about the latitude of the place to catch the sun's rays as squarely as possible over the year.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the seasons are caused by the Earth being closer to the sun in summer; the Earth is closest in January, which is winter in the Northern Hemisphere. Two: placing the overhead sun at the Tropic of Capricorn in June. Three: saying day and night are equal on the solstices; that happens at the equinoxes. Four: using the Northern Hemisphere names, calling September the autumn equinox in South Africa. Five: forgetting to add the latitudes when the place and the overhead sun are in opposite hemispheres.

Layout for marks in a sun-angle question: state the latitude of the place, the latitude of the overhead sun on that date, the difference with the reason for adding or subtracting, and the angle as ninety minus the difference. For example: Johannesburg 26°S; 21 June, sun overhead at 23,5°N; opposite hemispheres, so 26 + 23,5 = 49,5; angle 90 − 49,5 = 40,5 degrees.

The questions for this section are with you now: why the angle controls heating, uses of sun angles, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: a tilted spinning top on a long round trip, four special days, and the shadow of a stick at midday.

## Subtopic: A Tilted Top Going Round

Think of a spinning top that is leaning over a little. Now imagine it going in a big circle around a lamp in the middle of the room, while it keeps leaning in exactly the same direction the whole time, say towards the door.

That is the Earth. It spins every day, and it goes all the way around the sun once a year, about three hundred and sixty-five and a quarter days. The quarter days add up, so every fourth year gets an extra day in February: a leap year.

The Earth leans at about twenty-three and a half degrees, and it keeps leaning the same way all year. So for part of the trip, our bottom half of the world leans towards the sun. Six months later, on the other side of the circle, it leans away.

When we lean towards the sun, the sun climbs high at midday and days are long: summer. When we lean away, the sun stays low and days are short: winter.

People often think summer happens because we get closer to the sun. Not true. The Earth is actually closest to the sun in January, and that is winter in Europe. It is the lean that matters, not the distance.

## Subtopic: Four Special Days

On the trip around the sun there are four special days.

Around 21 or 22 December, our half leans furthest towards the sun. At midday the sun is straight overhead at the Tropic of Capricorn, which runs through Limpopo. It is our longest day. That is our summer solstice.

Around 21 June, we lean furthest away. The sun is straight overhead at the Tropic of Cancer, way up in the Sahara and India. It is our shortest day. That is our winter solstice.

In between, around 21 March and 23 September, nobody leans towards the sun. The sun is straight overhead at the equator, and day and night are about twelve hours each everywhere. Those are the equinoxes, which means equal night. For us, March is the autumn one and September the spring one.

So the overhead sun walks back and forth between the two tropics all year, like a person pacing between two walls.

Hold the four days in mind and try this part's questions: which day is our longest, and where the sun is overhead in March.

## Subtopic: The Midday Shadow Test

Stand a stick upright in the school yard and look at its shadow at midday. In December in Johannesburg, the shadow is tiny, because the sun is almost straight overhead, at about eighty-seven and a half degrees. In June, the same stick throws a long shadow pointing south, because the sun is low in the north, at only about forty and a half degrees.

You can work these angles out. Take ninety, and subtract how far you are from where the sun is overhead. In June the sun is overhead at twenty-three and a half degrees north. Johannesburg is twenty-six degrees south. Opposite sides, so add them: forty-nine and a half. Ninety take away forty-nine and a half leaves forty and a half degrees.

Why does it matter? A torch shining straight down makes a small, hot spot. Tilt it, and the light spreads out and gets weaker. High sun, strong heat. Low sun, weak heat. That is the difference between December and June.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Earth goes round the sun once a year, about three hundred and sixty-five and a quarter days. It leans at twenty-three and a half degrees, always the same way. In December the sun is overhead at the Tropic of Capricorn, our longest day. In June it is overhead at the Tropic of Cancer, our shortest day. In March and September it is overhead at the equator, with equal day and night. The angle of the midday sun is ninety minus the distance to where the sun is overhead.

Hold the leaning top, the four days and the stick's shadow, and take the final questions of the lesson: a solstice, an equinox, and a midday sun angle.
