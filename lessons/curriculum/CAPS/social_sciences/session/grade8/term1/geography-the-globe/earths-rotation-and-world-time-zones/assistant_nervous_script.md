Hey — if time zones make your head spin, here is the calm version.

Day and night come from the Earth spinning, once every twenty-four hours, from west to east. The half facing the sun has day; the other half has night. That is why the sun seems to rise in the east.

Now the one number you need. The Earth turns three hundred and sixty degrees in twenty-four hours. That is fifteen degrees every hour. So every fifteen degrees of longitude is one hour of clock time.

East is ahead. Places further east turn into the sunlight first, so their clocks are later.

Time zones are stripes about fifteen degrees wide, starting at Greenwich in London. South Africa uses one stripe for the whole country: South African Standard Time, two hours ahead of Greenwich.

To find the time somewhere else, count the hours between the zones. Tokyo is seven ahead of us. New York in its winter is seven behind.

The date line in the Pacific is where the calendar jumps. Going west, add a day. Going east, take one away.
