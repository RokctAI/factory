### subtopic_1 — The Globe and the Four Hemispheres
**Assistant:** If the Eastern and Western Hemispheres need two meridians, why do people usually mention only the prime meridian?

**Tutor:** Because the prime meridian is the starting point for measuring longitude, so it gets the attention, while the hundred and eightieth meridian lies mostly over the empty Pacific Ocean, far from most people's daily lives. But the logic of a hemisphere needs both. A hemisphere is half a sphere, and a sphere can only be cut into halves by a complete circle running all the way around it. A single meridian is only a half circle, from the North Pole to the South Pole on one side of the Earth. To complete the circle, you must come back up the other side, and the meridian directly opposite zero degrees is one hundred and eighty degrees. Together they form one great circle, and that circle is the boundary between east and west. You can see it on a globe by placing a finger on Greenwich and running it down to the South Pole, then up the far side through the Pacific to the North Pole. The equator is different because it is already a complete circle by itself, so it needs no partner. There is a practical side to this too. The hundred and eightieth meridian is close to the international date line, which is where the calendar date changes. The date line bends around island groups so that countries are not split into two days, but it follows the hundred and eightieth meridian for most of its length. We meet it properly in the next session on time zones, and it will make more sense once you see the meridian as the far half of the east-west boundary.

### subtopic_2 — Continents and Oceans in Their Hemispheres
**Assistant:** Why is most of Africa in the Northern Hemisphere when South Africa is so far south?

**Tutor:** Because of Africa's shape. The continent is very wide in the north and narrows towards the south. North of the equator lies the huge block of North and West Africa, including the Sahara; at its widest the continent stretches roughly seven thousand kilometres from the Atlantic coast of Senegal to the Horn of Africa in Somalia. South of the equator, Africa tapers like the point of a horn, and by the latitude of South Africa it is only about one thousand five hundred kilometres across in places. So although the continent stretches almost as far south of the equator as north of it, about thirty-five degrees south at Cape Agulhas and about thirty-seven degrees north at the coast of Tunisia, much more of its area lies in the north. Roughly two-thirds of Africa's land area lies north of the equator. This has consequences that appear later in the year. Most of Africa's deserts, including the Sahara, lie in the north, while the southern deserts, the Namib and the Kalahari, are smaller. Many of the countries colonised in the scramble for Africa, which is this year's third-term History topic, lie in the wide northern and western parts of the continent. It also means that a learner in South Africa lives near the southern tip of a mostly northern continent, which is one reason South Africa's climate is so strongly influenced by the oceans on three sides. The lesson is a general one about maps: never judge where most of something lies by looking at one end of it.

### subtopic_3 — Unequal Halves: Land, Water and People
**Assistant:** Why does having more ocean make the Southern Hemisphere's climate milder?

**Tutor:** Because water and land respond to the sun very differently. Water needs far more energy to warm it by one degree than rock or soil does, and the sun's heat is mixed downwards through the top layers of the ocean by waves and currents, while on land it stays in a thin surface layer. So in summer the ocean warms slowly, and in winter it cools slowly, releasing stored heat. Land warms quickly in summer and cools quickly in winter. Air takes on the temperature of the surface beneath it, so places dominated by ocean have small differences between summer and winter, and places deep inside large continents have huge differences. The Northern Hemisphere has enormous landmasses, Asia and North America, stretching to high latitudes. In the interior of Siberia, winters can fall below minus forty degrees Celsius while summers climb above twenty-five, a range of more than sixty-five degrees. Nothing like that exists at the same latitudes in the Southern Hemisphere, because there is no large land at those latitudes except Antarctica; between about fifty-six degrees south and the Antarctic coast there is only ocean. South Africa feels the effect too. Cape Town and Durban, beside the sea, have smaller ranges between summer and winter than Johannesburg or Bloemfontein in the interior. This principle, called the effect of distance from the sea, is one of the main factors that control temperature, and it is a full lesson in the second term, comparing Johannesburg and Cape Town. Here it explains why the water hemisphere is the milder half of the world.

### subtopic_4 — Opposite Seasons and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why do cyclones spin clockwise in the south when basins do not?

**Tutor:** Because the effect that makes them spin only matters over very large distances and long times. As the Earth rotates, any air or water moving over its surface is deflected: to the left in the Southern Hemisphere and to the right in the Northern Hemisphere. This deflection, called the Coriolis effect, is very weak. Over a few metres and a few seconds it is far smaller than other influences, such as the shape of a basin, a slight tilt, or a swirl left over from filling it. That is why a basin can drain either way in either hemisphere, and why demonstrations near the equator that claim otherwise are tricks of how the water is poured. A tropical cyclone is a different matter. It is hundreds of kilometres across, and air flows towards its low-pressure centre for days. Over those distances and times, the small deflection adds up. In the Southern Hemisphere, air rushing inwards is turned to the left, which sets the whole system rotating clockwise. In the Northern Hemisphere, deflection to the right produces anticlockwise rotation. Near the equator the effect is zero, which is why tropical cyclones almost never form within about five degrees of the equator. Satellite images, which are the subject of a later lesson this term, show the spiral clearly, and the direction of the spiral is one way to tell which hemisphere an image shows. For Grade 8, hold two points: large storms rotate clockwise in the south and anticlockwise in the north, and the basin story is a myth because a basin is far too small.
