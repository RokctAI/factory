### subtopic_1 — One Man, One Claim
**Assistant:** Was the rule of one man, one claim really fair?

**Tutor:** It was fairer than a system in which the richest could buy everything, but it was fair only within limits, and those limits are important for historians. The rule came from the diggers themselves, many of whom had experienced gold rushes in California and Australia, where similar rules tried to give ordinary men a chance. At the dry diggings in 1870 and 1871, there was no clear government, so diggers' committees made rules, settled disputes and enforced order. By limiting each digger to one claim, later two, they tried to stop speculators and wealthy men from buying up whole mines and to keep the opportunity of finding a fortune open to many. For white diggers without much capital, this was a real protection, and it was popular. However, the rule did not apply equally to everyone. The diggers' committees were made up of white men, and many of them actively campaigned to exclude black claim owners, using accusations of illicit diamond buying and pressing for licence rules that shut black diggers out. So the fair chance was mainly a fair chance for white men. The rule was also open to evasion: wealthier diggers registered claims under the names of relatives, friends or employees. And as the mine deepened, the rule actually became a problem for safety and efficiency, because hundreds of tiny claims at different depths made reef falls and flooding worse. In that sense, the rule could not survive the geology of the mine. A balanced answer would say that the rule expressed a genuine idea of equal opportunity, but that it was limited by race, evaded by the wealthy, and overtaken by the economics of deep mining, which favoured large companies.

### subtopic_2 — Scarcity, Supply and Price
**Assistant:** Why do diamonds cost so much if they are not really that rare today?

**Tutor:** Because their price depends not only on how many exist, but on how many are sold, and on what people believe they are worth, and for a long time the industry managed both. Geologically, gem diamonds are uncommon, since they form deep in the earth under great heat and pressure and are brought to the surface only in rare volcanic pipes. But since the nineteenth century, many deposits have been found in southern Africa, Russia, Canada, Australia and elsewhere, and millions of carats are mined each year. If all of these were sold freely, prices would probably fall. For most of the twentieth century, De Beers controlled the majority of the world's rough diamond sales through its central selling organisation in London. It bought diamonds from its own mines and from other producers, released only as many as the market could absorb at high prices, and stored the rest. When demand fell, as in the Great Depression, it cut production rather than lower prices. It also shaped demand through advertising, promoting the idea that a diamond is a symbol of lasting love and that an engagement ring should contain one. These campaigns, especially from the 1940s, were very successful. So the high price was partly the result of managing supply and creating demand. Today the situation has changed. Russia and Canada sell diamonds independently, De Beers's share of the market is much smaller, and laboratory-grown diamonds, chemically identical to mined ones, are becoming cheaper and more common. That is putting pressure on prices for mined diamonds. In economics, this story is a classic example of how a monopoly can keep prices high by controlling supply, and of what happens when control weakens.

### subtopic_3 — How De Beers Controlled Supply
**Assistant:** How did the Diamond Syndicate help De Beers keep prices high?

**Tutor:** By making sure that diamonds reached the market in a controlled, steady flow rather than in floods that would push prices down. Even after De Beers controlled almost all South African mines from 1888, there was a risk in how diamonds were sold. If the company sold to many different buyers, those buyers might compete, speculate, or dump diamonds when prices wavered, and the market could still swing. In 1890 De Beers made an agreement with a group of leading diamond merchants in London, the Diamond Syndicate. Under this arrangement, the syndicate bought De Beers's production, or an agreed share of it, at agreed prices over a set period. The syndicate members then sold the diamonds to cutters and dealers in centres such as Antwerp and Amsterdam, controlling the quantities and qualities released so that prices stayed stable or rose. If demand fell, the syndicate would buy less, and De Beers would reduce production or hold stocks. Both sides gained: De Beers had a reliable buyer and stable income, and the merchants had a secure supply and steady profits. The arrangement had tensions, especially when new mines outside De Beers's control, such as the Premier Mine, began selling diamonds independently in the early twentieth century. Ernest Oppenheimer later brought the selling system under De Beers's direct control, creating what became the Central Selling Organisation, which operated for most of the twentieth century. From the point of view of historians, the syndicate shows that controlling a market requires control of both production and sales. It also shows how decisions made in London boardrooms affected the jobs and wages of workers in Kimberley, because the quantity the syndicate would buy determined how many people the mines employed.

### subtopic_4 — Effects, the Present and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What is the Kimberley Process, and does it work?

**Tutor:** The Kimberley Process is an international certification scheme designed to stop conflict diamonds, also called blood diamonds, from entering the legitimate diamond trade, and it has had real successes and real weaknesses. Conflict diamonds are rough diamonds sold by rebel movements to finance wars against legitimate governments. In the 1990s, wars in Sierra Leone, Angola, the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Liberia were funded partly by diamond sales, and these wars caused enormous suffering, including the killing and mutilation of civilians and the use of child soldiers. In May 2000, diamond-producing countries in southern Africa, including South Africa, met in Kimberley to discuss the problem. The result was the Kimberley Process Certification Scheme, which began in 2003. Under it, participating countries agree to export rough diamonds only in sealed containers with a government certificate stating that they are conflict-free, and to import them only from other participating countries with such certificates. Governments, the diamond industry and civil society organisations all take part. Supporters say that the scheme helped reduce the share of conflict diamonds in world trade from an estimated several percent in the 1990s to a very small fraction, and that it has helped countries such as Sierra Leone and Angola rebuild their diamond industries legally. Critics argue that its definition of conflict diamonds is too narrow, because it covers only diamonds that fund rebels, not those linked to human rights abuses by governments, violence by security forces or exploitative working conditions. Some civil society groups have left the scheme in protest. So the fair answer is that the Kimberley Process has been an important step, named after the city where South Africa's diamond story began, but that ensuring diamonds are mined and traded ethically remains an ongoing challenge.
