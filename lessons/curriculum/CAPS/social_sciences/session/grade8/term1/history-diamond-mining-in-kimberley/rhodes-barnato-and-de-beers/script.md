# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines how two businessmen, Cecil John Rhodes and Barney Barnato, built companies at the diamond fields and how their contest ended in the formation of De Beers Consolidated Mines in 1888. The anchor is the cheque of 1889 by which De Beers paid for the Kimberley Central Diamond Mining Company, completing the amalgamation. The governing idea is that the diamond monopoly was built through company formation, finance and deal-making by a small number of men with access to capital, and that the wealth and power it created shaped the politics and conquests of southern Africa for decades. Describing the formation of the companies and evaluating their significance is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Cecil John Rhodes and the De Beers Mining Company

Cecil John Rhodes was born in 1853 at Bishop's Stortford in England, the son of a Church of England vicar. As a teenager he suffered from poor health, and in 1870 he was sent to Natal to join his older brother Herbert, who was trying to grow cotton. The cotton farm failed, and in 1871 the brothers went to the diamond fields, where Rhodes took over his brother's claims at the De Beers Mine.

Rhodes showed early business skill. During the 1870s he and his partner Charles Rudd won contracts to pump water out of the flooded mines, which brought steady income when digging was difficult. They used their earnings to buy more claims in the De Beers Mine, a smaller and at first less famous mine about one and a half kilometres from the Kimberley Mine. Rhodes divided his time between Kimberley and Oxford University in England, where he studied on and off from 1873 and finally took his degree in 1881.

In 1880 Rhodes, Rudd and their partners formed the De Beers Mining Company, a joint-stock company, with a capital of two hundred thousand pounds. A joint-stock company raises money by selling shares to investors, who then own part of the company and share its profits. This allowed the company to buy machinery and more claims than any individual could afford. Rhodes steadily bought out other holders in the De Beers Mine until, by 1887, his company owned the whole of it.

Rhodes had political as well as business ambitions. In 1881 he was elected to the Cape Parliament for the constituency of Barkly West, near Kimberley. He believed strongly in the expansion of the British Empire and in what he saw as the superiority of the English-speaking peoples, views that were common among British imperialists of the time and that are rightly criticised today as racist. He dreamed of British control stretching from the Cape to Cairo, and he saw diamond wealth as a means to that end.

Rhodes also had important allies. Alfred Beit, a diamond merchant from Hamburg, was a brilliant financier who became his closest business partner. Through Beit and others, Rhodes gained the support of the Rothschild bank in London, one of the most powerful banks in the world, which would prove decisive.

The questions for this section are with you now: Rhodes's early life and pumping contracts, the formation of the De Beers Mining Company, and his allies.

## Subtopic: Barney Barnato and Kimberley Central

Barney Barnato was born Barnett Isaacs in 1851 in Whitechapel, a poor Jewish district in the East End of London. He left school early, worked at various jobs, and performed as a comedian and boxer in music halls, taking the stage name Barnato. In 1873 he followed his brother Harry to the diamond fields, reportedly arriving with a small amount of money and a box of cigars to sell.

At Kimberley, Barnato began as a kopje-walloper, a dealer who walked from claim to claim buying diamonds from diggers. He was sharp, hard-working and good at judging stones and people. In 1876, when many believed that the blue ground beneath the yellow ground contained no diamonds, Barnato bought claims in the Kimberley Mine, gambling correctly that the blue ground was rich. His gamble paid off spectacularly.

In 1881 he formed the Barnato Diamond Mining Company, and through a series of mergers his interests became part of the Kimberley Central Diamond Mining Company, which by 1887 controlled most of the Kimberley Mine, the richest of the four mines. Barnato was also elected to the Cape Parliament for Kimberley in 1888.

Barnato's life shows that the diamond fields offered unusual opportunities for some newcomers without wealth or connections, provided they were white men. His background was very different from Rhodes's, and some in Kimberley's society looked down on him for it, including with antisemitic prejudice. His story is often told as a rags-to-riches tale, but it should be remembered that the same diamond fields were closing opportunities to black claim owners at the very time Barnato was rising.

By the mid-1880s, Kimberley's diamond industry was dominated by a few big companies, and the two largest were Rhodes's De Beers and Barnato's Kimberley Central. Both were producing diamonds at full speed. With so many diamonds on the market, prices were falling. Both men understood that whoever controlled all the mines could control the supply and therefore the price.

The questions for this section are with you now: Barnato's background and rise, his gamble on blue ground, and the Kimberley Central company.

## Subtopic: The Battle for Control and De Beers Consolidated

The contest between Rhodes and Barnato came to a head in 1887 and 1888. The key was a third company, the French Company, the Compagnie Française des Mines de Diamants du Cap, which owned an important block of claims in the Kimberley Mine. Whoever controlled the French Company would gain a strong position inside Barnato's mine.

Rhodes travelled to London and, with Beit's help, obtained the backing of the Rothschild bank to raise a large sum to buy the French Company. Barnato made a higher counter-offer. Rather than enter a bidding war, Rhodes made a deal: he would sell the French Company to Kimberley Central in exchange for a fifth of Kimberley Central's shares and cash. Barnato agreed, thinking he had won. But Rhodes now owned a large block of Kimberley Central shares, and he and his allies began buying more on the market, pushing the price up. Barnato bought too, to defend his control, but Rhodes had deeper financial backing.

Eventually Rhodes held a majority of Kimberley Central shares, and Barnato agreed to merge. Barnato received a large shareholding in the new company and was made a life governor, a position with special rights and a share of profits, along with Rhodes, Beit and others. On the thirteenth of March 1888 De Beers Consolidated Mines was registered. Some Kimberley Central shareholders challenged the merger in court, so Kimberley Central was wound up and its assets were bought by De Beers; the famous cheque of 1889, for five million three hundred and thirty-eight thousand six hundred and fifty pounds, completed the purchase. Within a few years De Beers also took control of the Dutoitspan and Bultfontein mines.

The trust deed of De Beers Consolidated, the document setting out what the company could do, was unusually broad. Rhodes insisted that it allow the company to do almost anything: not only mine diamonds, but acquire land, build railways, raise armies and govern territory anywhere in Africa. Barnato is said to have remarked that Rhodes wanted to make a new empire. The deed allowed De Beers money to support Rhodes's expansion to the north.

The amalgamation had immediate effects. De Beers controlled about ninety percent of the world's diamond production by the end of the 1880s. It reduced output from the mines to raise prices, closed less profitable operations and laid off many workers. It extended the closed compound system. The company became the most powerful economic force in the Cape.

It is worth seeing the amalgamation from below as well as from the boardroom. For the thousands of African workers in the compounds, and for the white miners and artisans employed by the smaller companies, the merger meant uncertainty. When De Beers cut production to support prices, many lost their jobs, and Kimberley's shops, transport riders and boarding houses lost customers. Some white miners left for the new gold fields of the Witwatersrand, discovered in 1886. African workers were sent home at the end of their contracts and not replaced. Newspapers in Kimberley debated whether the town had been saved or captured by De Beers. One company now decided how many people worked, how much they earned, and how they lived. That concentration of economic power in a single company, closely tied to political power through Rhodes, was something new in southern Africa.

The questions for this section are with you now: the French Company deal, the share contest and the merger of 1888, and the trust deed.

## Subtopic: Power, Legacy and the Error Museum

Rhodes used his wealth and position to pursue political power and territorial expansion. In 1887 his company Gold Fields of South Africa invested in the new Witwatersrand gold fields. In 1889 he obtained a royal charter for the British South Africa Company, which colonised the lands north of the Limpopo that were named Rhodesia after him, today Zimbabwe and Zambia. The company's forces conquered the Ndebele kingdom in 1893 and suppressed the risings of the Ndebele and Shona in 1896 and 1897 with great loss of life. In 1890 Rhodes became Prime Minister of the Cape Colony. His government passed the Glen Grey Act of 1894, which limited African landholding and introduced a labour tax to push men into wage work, and the Franchise and Ballot Act of 1892 raised the property qualification for voting, which reduced the number of black voters. He was forced to resign in 1896 after his involvement in the Jameson Raid, a failed armed attempt to overthrow the government of the Transvaal. He died in 1902 at Muizenberg.

Barnato became one of the richest men in the world and invested heavily in the Witwatersrand gold fields from the late 1880s. He died in 1897, after going overboard from a ship travelling to England.

Rhodes's legacy is debated today. His will established the Rhodes Scholarships at Oxford, which have funded the education of thousands of students, including many from Africa in recent decades. But his imperialism, his racial views and his role in conquest and in policies that dispossessed Africans have led many to criticise his memory. In 2015 students at the University of Cape Town led the Rhodes Must Fall campaign, and a statue of Rhodes was removed from the campus. Historians aim to understand Rhodes in the context of his time and to judge his actions by their consequences for all the people affected.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying Rhodes discovered diamonds; he arrived after the discovery and made his fortune by buying claims and building a company. Two: saying Barnato founded De Beers; Rhodes's company was De Beers, and Barnato's was Kimberley Central. Three: thinking the merger came from friendship; it was a hard-fought contest of finance and shares. Four: forgetting the Rothschild bank's role; finance decided the contest. Five: treating Rhodes's legacy as simple; it includes both the scholarships and conquest and dispossession.

Layout for marks in an essay on the amalgamation: background on falling prices, the two companies and their leaders, the French Company deal and share contest, the merger of 1888, and its effects on prices, workers and politics, ending with a judgement. For example: by buying the French Company with Rothschild backing and then a majority of Kimberley Central shares, Rhodes forced Barnato to merge in 1888, creating a monopoly that controlled about ninety percent of world output and financed Rhodes's expansion north.

The questions for this section are with you now: Rhodes's political career, the legacy debate, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: The Vicar's Son

Cecil John Rhodes was born in England in 1853. He was often sick, so at seventeen he was sent to South Africa to join his brother on a cotton farm in Natal. The farm failed, and in 1871 the brothers went to the diamond fields.

Rhodes was clever with money. When the mines flooded, he and his partner Charles Rudd rented out pumps to get the water out, and they used the money to buy claims in the De Beers Mine. In 1880 they started the De Beers Mining Company, selling shares to raise money. By 1887 they owned the whole De Beers Mine. Rhodes also became a member of the Cape Parliament and dreamed of a British empire from the Cape to Cairo.

## Subtopic: The Comedian from London

Barney Barnato grew up poor in the East End of London. He did comedy and boxing on the stage. In 1873 he arrived at the diamond fields with a box of cigars to sell.

He started by walking from claim to claim, buying diamonds from diggers. Then he took a big gamble: when others thought the hard blue ground had no diamonds, he bought claims in the Kimberley Mine. He was right, and he got rich. His company, Kimberley Central, came to control most of the Kimberley Mine, the richest of all.

Hold the cigar box and the blue ground in mind and try this part's questions: Barnato and Kimberley Central.

## Subtopic: The Big Deal

Too many diamonds made prices fall. Both men knew that whoever owned all the mines could control the price.

Rhodes got help from a huge London bank, the Rothschilds. He bought a company with claims inside Barnato's mine, then swapped it for a big piece of Barnato's company. Then he bought more and more shares until he had most of them. Barnato agreed to join him. In 1888 De Beers Consolidated Mines was born, and in 1889 a cheque for over five million pounds sealed it. De Beers soon controlled about nine out of every ten diamonds in the world.

Rhodes used the money to push north into land that was named Rhodesia, and he became Prime Minister of the Cape. Today some remember his scholarships, and many criticise his conquests and racist ideas.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Rhodes built the De Beers Mining Company from 1880, and Barnato built Kimberley Central, which controlled most of the Kimberley Mine. With Rothschild money, Rhodes bought the French Company, traded it for Kimberley Central shares, bought a majority and forced a merger: De Beers Consolidated Mines, 1888. It controlled about ninety percent of world diamond output and funded Rhodes's expansion north. His legacy is still debated.
