### subtopic_1 — Cecil John Rhodes and the De Beers Mining Company
**Assistant:** Why did Rhodes and Barnato want to merge their companies instead of competing?

**Tutor:** Because competition was driving down the price of diamonds, and both men understood that diamonds are only valuable if they are scarce. Diamonds have few practical uses as gems; their value depends on people believing they are rare and precious. In the early 1870s, the flood of diamonds from Kimberley had already caused prices to fall sharply in Europe. By the mid-1880s, a few large companies were each producing as much as they could to pay for their machinery and dividends, which pushed prices down further. Each company had an incentive to produce more to earn more, but if all did so, all would earn less per diamond. Economists call this a problem of overproduction. The solution, from the producers' point of view, was to bring all the mines under one control so that output could be limited to what the market would buy at a good price. That is a monopoly. Rhodes saw this clearly and pursued it determinedly. Barnato also saw the logic but wanted to be the one in control, which is why the contest was so fierce. There were other gains from amalgamation too: a single company could run the mines more efficiently, avoid duplicating machinery and pumping, coordinate deeper underground mining, and impose a uniform system of labour control, including compounds. For the owners and shareholders, the merger was enormously profitable. For many workers, it meant lay-offs, as De Beers reduced production and closed operations after 1888. For diggers who still hoped to work independently, it meant the end of their dreams. So the merger was a rational decision for the owners, but its benefits were very unevenly shared, which is a theme that appears again and again in the history of South Africa's mineral revolution.

### subtopic_2 — Barney Barnato and Kimberley Central
**Assistant:** How was Barnato able to rise from poverty to great wealth when so many others failed?

**Tutor:** Through a combination of skill, risk-taking, timing and the particular opportunities open to white newcomers at the diamond fields, and a bit of luck. Barnato arrived in 1873 with very little, but he had sharp instincts for trading, gained from his years in the markets and music halls of London's East End. As a kopje-walloper, he walked from claim to claim buying diamonds from diggers and selling them to bigger dealers, learning to judge stones and to bargain. He saved money and watched the market. His big break came in 1876, when many diggers believed that the hard blue ground beneath the yellow ground contained no diamonds and were selling claims cheaply. Barnato, possibly helped by advice from those who had examined the geology, bet that the blue ground was rich and bought claims in the central part of the Kimberley Mine. He was right: the blue ground was the unweathered kimberlite and contained the diamonds all the way down. His claims became enormously valuable. In 1881 he formed a company and floated shares during the share boom, which raised capital and made him rich on paper. He then merged with others to build Kimberley Central. It is important to remember the limits of this rags-to-riches story. Many diggers who took similar risks lost everything. Barnato benefited from being a white man at a time when black claim owners were being excluded from the very same mines. And in the end, he was outmatched by Rhodes, whose access to the Rothschild bank gave him far greater financial power. Barnato's story shows that individual talent mattered, but that structures of race and finance decided who could succeed and how far.

### subtopic_3 — The Battle for Control and De Beers Consolidated
**Assistant:** What was so unusual about the De Beers trust deed?

**Tutor:** It gave a mining company powers that were more like those of a government, and it reflected Rhodes's political ambitions. A trust deed, or company constitution, sets out what a company is allowed to do. Most mining companies' deeds limited them to mining, buying and selling the mineral and related business. Rhodes insisted that the De Beers Consolidated deed be extremely broad. It allowed the company to acquire land and mineral rights anywhere, to engage in any business, to build and operate railways, to lend money to governments, and even to annex territory and maintain armed forces in Africa, as long as the shareholders agreed. Barnato and some other directors were reluctant, because they wanted a company that made money for shareholders, not one that funded political adventures. According to well-known accounts, the negotiations went on through the night, and Barnato finally agreed, commenting that some people have a fancy for one thing and some for another, and that Rhodes had a fancy for making an empire. Rhodes got his way. In practice, De Beers money and credit supported the British South Africa Company's occupation of the land north of the Limpopo in 1890, and Rhodes used the company's resources and his personal fortune to build political influence in the Cape. Historians see the trust deed as evidence that, for Rhodes, mining wealth and empire-building were connected parts of one project. It also shows how private companies could become instruments of colonial expansion, as the British East India Company had been in India. For Africans in the territories affected, the consequences included conquest, loss of land and forced labour. When analysing sources about Rhodes, the trust deed is an important document because it reveals intentions as well as actions.

### subtopic_4 — Power, Legacy and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Should we judge Rhodes by the standards of his own time or ours?

**Tutor:** Historians try to do both, but they keep the two clear and they look at the evidence of what people at the time actually thought. First, understanding the context. Rhodes lived in the age of high imperialism, when many British people believed that their empire was a force for progress and that Europeans were entitled to rule other peoples. His views on race were shared by many of his white contemporaries, so we can explain where they came from. But explaining is not the same as excusing. Second, it is important to recognise that even in his own time, Rhodes was criticised. African leaders such as the Ndebele king Lobengula protested against the deceptions used to obtain mining rights in his country. Some British liberals, missionaries and journalists condemned the violence of the British South Africa Company's conquests and the treatment of African workers. The Jameson Raid was widely condemned, and Rhodes had to resign. So his actions were controversial at the time, not only today. Third, we must look at consequences for all the people affected. His policies brought great wealth to shareholders and some development of railways and towns, and his will funded scholarships that still educate students. But they also brought conquest, deaths in the wars of 1893 and 1896 to 1897, loss of land, forced labour, and laws that reduced African rights, such as the Glen Grey Act. A balanced historical judgement therefore says that Rhodes was a man of his time whose views were common among imperialists, but that his actions caused great harm, were opposed by people who lived through them, and should be judged by their consequences. The Rhodes Must Fall movement is part of an ongoing public discussion about how societies remember such figures.
