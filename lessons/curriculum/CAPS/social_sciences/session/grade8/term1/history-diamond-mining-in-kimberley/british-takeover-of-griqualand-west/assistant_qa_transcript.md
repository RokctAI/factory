### subtopic_1 — The Discovery of Diamonds
**Assistant:** Why did Britain want the diamond fields when it had withdrawn from the interior only twenty years earlier?

**Tutor:** Because the diamonds changed the value of the land completely. In the early 1850s, Britain withdrew from the land between the Orange and Vaal rivers and recognised the Boer republics because ruling the interior was expensive and offered few benefits. The region had no known minerals, few exports and many potential conflicts. After 1867, and especially after the dry diggings were found in 1870 and 1871, the same area became one of the most valuable places in the world. Diamonds were exported through the Cape's ports, bringing customs revenue, trade and investment. If the Orange Free State had controlled the dry diggings, it would have gained a large income, which could have made it richer, better armed and more independent of the Cape and Britain. The British government also had a broader goal at this time, sometimes called confederation: it hoped to unite the colonies and republics of southern Africa under British control, as had been done in Canada in 1867, to make the region cheaper to defend and govern. Control of the diamond fields fitted this plan. There was also a strategic motive: the diamond fields lay along the road to the north through Tswana territory, and Britain wanted to stop the republics from expanding westward and blocking that road. Finally, most diggers were British subjects who wanted British rule. Britain presented annexation as protection of the Griqua, and Waterboer's request gave it a legal justification. Historians generally agree that economic interests were the main driving force, and that the humanitarian language served those interests. This case is an early example of how minerals pulled Britain deeper into southern Africa.

### subtopic_2 — Four Claims to the Diamond Fields
**Assistant:** Was the Keate Award a fair decision?

**Tutor:** Historians have mixed views, and it helps to separate the process from its consequences. The arbitration was agreed to by the Transvaal, Waterboer and the Tswana chiefs, and Robert Keate, the Lieutenant-Governor of Natal, heard evidence from all sides. In that sense it followed a recognised procedure. But there were problems. Keate was a British official, and Britain had an interest in the outcome, so the Transvaal later argued that he could not be impartial. The Transvaal's case was badly prepared; its representatives presented weak evidence and some contradictory documents, while Waterboer's case, prepared by the lawyer David Arnot, was well organised, though some of its documents were later found to be unreliable. Keate ruled against the Transvaal, setting a western border that excluded the land along the Vaal and Harts rivers, and awarded that land to Waterboer and the Tswana chiefs. The decision was so unpopular in the Transvaal that President Pretorius resigned. Importantly, the Keate Award did not decide the ownership of the dry diggings claimed by the Orange Free State, because the Free State refused to take part. Britain annexed those too, which the Free State considered completely unjustified. A land court in 1875 later found that much of Waterboer's claim to the dry diggings was not well founded, which strengthened the Free State's case, and Britain paid compensation. So a balanced answer might say that the arbitration followed a legal form and gave recognition to African and Griqua claims that the republics ignored, but that it was shaped by British interests and that Britain used it as a stepping stone to annex land that had not been part of the arbitration at all.

### subtopic_3 — Annexation and Its Consequences
**Assistant:** If the annexation was in the Griqua's name, why did the Griqua end up losing their land?

**Tutor:** Because annexation transferred power to a colonial government and a market in land that favoured settlers and speculators, and because the Griqua's legal position was weaker than it appeared. After 1871, land in Griqualand West became extremely valuable, not only for diamonds but for farming and grazing to supply the diggings. Settlers, speculators and companies wanted it. The colonial government set up a land court to decide who owned which land. Many Griqua held land according to their own community arrangements and Waterboer's grants, not according to surveyed title deeds of the type colonial courts recognised. When the land court sat in 1875, it rejected many Griqua claims, sometimes because documents were missing or because Arnot and others had issued grants that the court found irregular. At the same time, some Griqua sold land, under pressure from debt or poverty, often for far less than it was worth, and some sales involved deception. Speculators who had bought claims from Waterboer's government also gained land. Within a few years, the Griqua had lost most of their land base and many became labourers or moved away. Their frustration, shared by Tlhaping communities who also lost land, led to the rising of 1878, which colonial forces suppressed. Historians see the Griqua as a clear case of a people whose claims were used to justify annexation and who were then marginalised by it. The episode also shows a broader pattern: when minerals were discovered, the people who lived on or near the land rarely benefited, while those with access to capital, colonial courts and political power did. This pattern repeated on the Witwatersrand after 1886, as you will study next term.

### subtopic_4 — Evidence, Interpretation and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How did the diamond discovery affect African societies far from Kimberley?

**Tutor:** In several important ways, through labour, guns, trade and politics. Diamond digging needed thousands of workers to dig, haul and sort the ground, and from the start most of them were African men who travelled to the diggings for wages. Many came from the Pedi kingdom in the north-east Transvaal, from Basutoland, from Tsonga areas in southern Mozambique and from Tswana communities. They often walked several hundred kilometres, sometimes for weeks, facing danger from robbers and from Boer farmers and officials in the republics who demanded passes or tried to force them into labour. Most stayed at the diggings for a few months and then returned home. In the early 1870s, many used their wages to buy guns, which diamond merchants and shopkeepers in Kimberley sold freely. Tens of thousands of guns passed into African hands in this way. The Pedi under Sekhukhune, for example, used guns acquired through migrant labour to resist the Transvaal, defeating a Transvaal attack in 1876. This alarmed colonial and republican governments and contributed to British efforts to control the region, including the annexation of the Transvaal in 1877 and later wars against the Pedi and the Zulu in 1879. Diamond wages also brought cash into African economies, which some chiefs taxed and used to strengthen their states. In the longer term, the migrant labour system that began at Kimberley drew men away from their homes for long periods, and colonial governments later used taxes and land laws to force men into wage labour. So the discovery of diamonds affected societies hundreds of kilometres away and helped shape the system of migrant labour that became central to South Africa's economy.
