# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains how the discovery of diamonds led Britain to take control of the diamond fields north of the Orange River in 1871. The anchor is the annexation of Griqualand West in October 1871, after a disputed arbitration called the Keate Award. The governing idea is that the discovery turned a remote, contested borderland into one of the most valuable pieces of land in the world, and that Britain, which had withdrawn from the interior in the 1850s because it seemed worthless, now moved to control it, using the claims of the Griqua as its legal justification. Explaining the dispute and the British takeover is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: The Discovery of Diamonds

In 1867 Erasmus Jacobs, the son of a farmer near Hopetown in the northern Cape, found a bright stone near the Orange River. A neighbour, Schalk van Niekerk, passed it on, and it was eventually identified by a geologist in Grahamstown as a diamond of about twenty-one carats. It became known as the Eureka diamond. A carat is a unit of mass for gemstones, equal to one fifth of a gram, so the Eureka weighed a little over four grams. In 1869 van Niekerk bought a much larger stone from a Griqua shepherd for five hundred sheep, ten oxen and a horse; this diamond of about eighty-three carats was named the Star of South Africa, and its discovery convinced the world that South Africa had real diamond wealth.

Diggers soon gathered along the Vaal River, panning and sieving the river gravels for diamonds; these were the river diggings. Then, in 1870 and 1871, diamonds were found away from the rivers, in the dry ground of farms such as Dutoitspan, Bultfontein and Vooruitzicht, which belonged to the brothers De Beer. In July 1871 a party of diggers found diamonds on a small hill on Vooruitzicht called Colesberg Kopje. The rush that followed was called the New Rush, and that site became the Kimberley Mine, today the Big Hole.

These dry diggings were different from the river diggings. Geologists later understood that the diamonds lay in pipes of volcanic rock, called kimberlite after the town, which went deep into the earth. The deeper the diggers went, the more diamonds they found. This was a sign that diamond mining would become a large, long-term industry rather than a short-lived rush.

The rush transformed the area. By 1872, tens of thousands of people lived at the diggings in a sprawling camp of tents and iron shacks: diggers from the Cape, Natal and the republics, from Britain, Europe, the United States and Australia, and African workers from across southern Africa, many walking hundreds of kilometres from the Pedi, Sotho, Tsonga and Tswana areas. The demand for food, fuel, transport and supplies stimulated farming and trade far beyond the diggings.

The discovery mattered for the whole region. For the Cape Colony, which had relied on wool, wine and ostrich feathers, diamonds promised a new source of wealth and customs revenue. For the Boer republics, which were poor, the diamond fields offered a way to pay their debts. For African societies, the diggings offered wages and, in the early years, a chance to buy guns. For Britain, they offered a reason to return to the interior.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Eureka and the Star of South Africa, the river and dry diggings, and the significance of the discovery.

## Subtopic: Four Claims to the Diamond Fields

The diamond fields lay in a borderland where several authorities claimed the land and none had clear, accepted borders.

The Orange Free State claimed the area between the Vaal and the Modder rivers, including the dry diggings, because some of its burghers had bought farms there, and the Free State had been governing parts of it. In 1870 it sent officials to the diggings.

The South African Republic, the Transvaal, claimed land along the Vaal River to the north and west, including some river diggings, and its president, Marthinus Pretorius, granted a monopoly over diamond digging there to a small company, which angered diggers.

Nicolaas Waterboer, leader of the Griqua of Griquatown, claimed that the diamond fields lay within the territory of his people, based on earlier treaties and land grants. He was advised by a Cape lawyer, David Arnot, who argued his case skilfully and who had an interest in British involvement.

Tswana chiefs of the Tlhaping and Barolong also claimed land along the Vaal and Harts rivers.

The diggers themselves added to the confusion. Many of them, especially British subjects, did not want to be governed by the Free State or the Transvaal. In 1870, river diggers at Klipdrift set up their own short-lived Diggers' Republic under a former British sailor, Stafford Parker, to resist the Transvaal's monopoly.

To settle the dispute over the river diggings and the western border of the Transvaal, the parties agreed to arbitration by Robert Keate, the Lieutenant-Governor of Natal. In October 1871 the Keate Award ruled in favour of Waterboer and the Tswana chiefs and against the Transvaal. The Transvaal's President Pretorius was forced to resign after the decision. The Free State, however, refused to accept arbitration over the dry diggings, insisting that they were clearly its territory.

Waterboer, as Arnot had planned, then asked Britain to take him and his people under its protection. The British High Commissioner at the Cape, Sir Henry Barkly, accepted. In October 1871 the diamond fields, including the dry diggings claimed by the Free State, were proclaimed British territory under the name Griqualand West.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four claimants, the Diggers' Republic, and the Keate Award.

## Subtopic: Annexation and Its Consequences

The annexation of Griqualand West was controversial. The Orange Free State protested strongly that Britain had taken land the Free State had governed and on which its citizens held farms. Many people in the Free State and the Transvaal saw the annexation as a British seizure of their mineral wealth disguised as protection for the Griqua. The Free State's president, Johannes Brand, took the case to London. In 1876 Britain agreed to pay the Free State ninety thousand pounds in compensation, without admitting that the Free State's claim had been right. A land court later found that Waterboer's claim to much of the dry diggings had been weak.

Britain's reasons were mainly economic and strategic. The diamond fields were already producing great wealth, and Britain did not want the Boer republics to gain an independent income that could make them strong. The fields also lay on the road to the north, the route into the interior of Africa through Tswana country, which later became known as the Missionaries' Road and which Britain wanted to keep open. Controlling the diamond fields also gave Britain more influence over the labour, trade and guns flowing through the region.

In 1873 Griqualand West became a separate Crown colony, and the camp at New Rush was renamed Kimberley, after the British Colonial Secretary, Lord Kimberley. In 1880 Griqualand West was incorporated into the Cape Colony.

For the Griqua, the outcome was bitter. Although the annexation was justified in their name, Griqua landholders lost much of their land in the following years. The land court of 1875 rejected many Griqua claims, and settlers and speculators bought up farms, sometimes cheaply or through dubious deals. Griqua communities became impoverished. In 1878 Griqua and Tlhaping groups rose in rebellion in the Griqualand West area against land loss and colonial control, and they were defeated. Waterboer himself received a pension. The Griqua had been used to justify annexation and then lost the land.

For the Tswana, annexation meant colonial borders across their land and increasing pressure from settlers and later from the British, who in 1885 declared the southern part of their land the colony of British Bechuanaland and the northern part the Bechuanaland Protectorate, today's Botswana.

The annexation also changed the relationship between Britain and the Boer republics. It increased distrust, which grew further when Britain annexed the Transvaal in 1877, leading to the war of 1880 to 1881 in which the Transvaal regained its independence.

Under British rule, Kimberley grew quickly from a camp into a town. Iron buildings replaced tents, and banks, churches, newspapers, hotels and a hospital appeared. Water was scarce and expensive, fires were frequent, and disease spread in the crowded camp, especially in the hot summer months. The colonial government introduced licences for diggers, rules about the size of claims, and pass regulations for African workers, which you will study in the next session. By the 1880s Kimberley was one of the largest towns in southern Africa, with a mixed population drawn from many countries and regions, and in 1882 it became one of the first towns in the world to have electric street lighting. Diamonds had turned a dry hill on a disputed border into a modern industrial town within ten years, and the money and skills gathered there would later help finance the gold mines of the Witwatersrand.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Free State's protest and compensation, Britain's motives, and what annexation meant for the Griqua and the Tswana.

## Subtopic: Evidence, Interpretation and the Error Museum

Historians have interpreted the annexation in different ways. Imperial historians of the early twentieth century sometimes presented it as Britain protecting the Griqua from Boer expansion. Afrikaner nationalist historians presented it as a theft of Free State territory. More recent historians focus on the economic motives behind British policy and on the experiences of the Griqua, Tswana and African workers, whose voices were often missing from older accounts. Each interpretation selects and emphasises different evidence.

When reading sources on the dispute, consider their origin. Arnot's maps and documents were drawn up to support Waterboer's claim, and some were later shown to be unreliable. Free State documents emphasised its administration of the area. British official correspondence often presented annexation as reluctant and humanitarian, while private letters sometimes reveal the attraction of diamond revenue. A historian compares these sources and asks who gained from each version of events.

A quick calculation helps explain the stakes. A carat is one fifth of a gram, so the Eureka diamond of about twenty-one carats weighed a little over four grams, while the Star of South Africa of about eighty-three carats weighed about sixteen and a half grams. Yet the Star was sold for about eleven thousand pounds, at a time when a farm labourer in Britain earned perhaps thirty pounds a year. That contrast helps explain why governments were prepared to fight over a patch of dry ground.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying diamonds were first discovered at Kimberley; the first find was near Hopetown in 1867. Two: confusing the river diggings with the dry diggings; the deep pipes of the dry diggings made Kimberley a long-term mine. Three: saying the Keate Award gave the diamond fields to Britain; it ruled on the Transvaal's border, in favour of Waterboer and the Tswana, and Britain then annexed the fields at Waterboer's request. Four: believing the Griqua benefited from annexation; most lost their land. Five: saying the Free State accepted the annexation; it protested and was paid ninety thousand pounds in 1876.

Layout for marks in an essay paragraph on the takeover: state the event and date, name the claimants, explain the arbitration and annexation, give Britain's motives, and assess the consequences for at least two groups. For example: in October 1871, after the Keate Award ruled against the Transvaal, Britain annexed the diamond fields as Griqualand West at Waterboer's request; its real motive was control of the diamond wealth, and the Free State received only compensation while the Griqua soon lost their land.

The questions for this section are with you now: interpreting the annexation, reading the evidence, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: a shiny stone, a land with four owners, and Britain moving in.

## Subtopic: A Shiny Stone

It is 1867 on a farm near Hopetown, beside the Orange River. A boy named Erasmus Jacobs picks up a pretty pebble. It is a diamond, about twenty-one carats. Two years later a Griqua shepherd sells an even bigger one, the Star of South Africa, for five hundred sheep, ten oxen and a horse. It is worth a fortune.

People come running. First they dig in the river gravels. Then, in 1871, they find diamonds in the dry ground on a little hill, and it turns out the diamonds go down deep, in pipes of rock. That hill becomes the Kimberley Mine, the Big Hole.

Soon tens of thousands of people live in tents and tin shacks: diggers from everywhere, and African workers who walked hundreds of kilometres for wages.

## Subtopic: Who Owns the Land?

Now the big question: whose land was it? Four sides said it was theirs. The Orange Free State said so. The Transvaal said so. The Griqua leader Nicolaas Waterboer said so, with a clever lawyer, David Arnot, helping him. And Tswana chiefs said so too.

A judge from Natal, Robert Keate, was asked to decide about the Transvaal's border. In 1871 he decided against the Transvaal and for Waterboer and the Tswana.

Then Waterboer asked Britain to protect him, and Britain said yes. In October 1871 Britain took the whole diamond area, including the land the Free State claimed, and called it Griqualand West.

Hold the four hands reaching for the same patch of ground in mind and try this part's questions: the claimants and the Keate Award.

## Subtopic: What Britain Gained

Why did Britain do it? Diamonds. Britain did not want the Boer republics getting rich, and it wanted to control the road to the north.

The Free State was furious and was later paid ninety thousand pounds. The camp was renamed Kimberley in 1873, after a British minister. And the Griqua, whose claim Britain used, soon lost most of their land.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Diamonds were found near Hopetown in 1867, and at the dry diggings, including Colesberg Kopje, in 1871. The Free State, the Transvaal, the Griqua under Waterboer and Tswana chiefs all claimed the land. The Keate Award of 1871 went against the Transvaal, and Britain annexed the fields as Griqualand West in October 1871, mainly for their wealth. The Free State was compensated, and the Griqua lost their land.
