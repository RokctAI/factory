# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains how diamond mining at Kimberley changed from hundreds of individual diggers to a handful of companies and finally a monopoly, and what happened to the black claim owners who had been part of the early diggings. The anchor is the contrast between 1872, when black and coloured men held claims at the diggings, and the late 1880s, when black workers were confined to closed compounds and the industry was controlled by one company. The governing idea is that the move towards monopoly was driven by geology and money, as the mines grew deeper and more expensive, but also by deliberate decisions, laws and pressure from white diggers that excluded black people from ownership and turned them into a controlled labour force. Explaining this process is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Digging the Kimberley Mine

When the New Rush began in 1871, the diggers divided the ground on Colesberg Kopje into claims. A standard claim measured thirty-one by thirty-one Cape feet, about nine and a half metres on each side, so a claim covered roughly ninety square metres. The Kimberley Mine contained around four hundred and seventy claims, and many were divided further into halves, quarters and even smaller pieces, so at one stage the mine had well over a thousand separate holdings. Each digger dug straight down into his claim, and narrow strips of ground were left between the claims as roadways for carts to carry the diamond-bearing ground out of the mine.

The work was simple at first. Diggers and their workers loosened the soil with picks and shovels, carried it out, broke it up and sorted through it on tables, looking for the glint of diamonds. The upper layer, called yellow ground, was soft and weathered and easy to work. As digging went deeper, the claims became a giant pit, the roadways crumbled and collapsed, and diggers had to haul ground up by ropes and buckets on a network of cables stretched from the rim of the mine, which made it look like a huge spider's web.

Below about twenty to thirty metres, the diggers reached the harder blue ground, the unweathered kimberlite. At first some thought it contained no diamonds and despaired, but it proved to be rich. Blue ground had to be spread on open floors and left to weather in the sun and rain for months before it could be broken up, or it had to be crushed by machines.

Depth brought serious problems. The walls of surrounding rock, called reef, kept falling into the pit, burying claims under tons of worthless rubble that had to be removed at great cost. Water seeped into the mine and had to be pumped out. Claims at different depths made the whole operation unstable. These problems needed expensive equipment: steam engines for hauling, pumps for water, and labour to clear reef. Individual diggers with small claims could not afford these costs.

By the end of the 1870s, the Kimberley Mine was about one hundred metres deep. Later it was mined underground through shafts, and when it closed in 1914 the Big Hole was about two hundred and fifteen metres deep and about four hundred and sixty metres across. About two thousand seven hundred kilograms of diamonds had been taken from it, which is roughly fourteen and a half million carats, from more than twenty million tonnes of earth.

A calculation shows how rare diamonds are. If about twenty-two million tonnes of ground produced about two thousand seven hundred kilograms of diamonds, then each tonne of ground produced on average only about one eighth of a gram of diamond, less than one carat. Miners had to move enormous quantities of earth to find a small amount of treasure, which is why scale and machinery mattered so much.

The questions for this section are with you now: claims and how digging worked, yellow and blue ground, and the problems of depth.

## Subtopic: Black Claim Owners and How They Were Pushed Out

In the early years of the diamond fields, ownership was not reserved for white men. At the river diggings and at the dry diggings, some black and coloured men held claims and employed workers of their own. Some were Griqua or coloured men from the Cape and the Free State; others were Africans, including Tswana and Mfengu men from the Cape, who had experience of trading and saving. They bought claims or licences, worked them with family members and hired labourers, and some became prosperous.

Many white diggers resented this. They argued that black claim owners competed with them, and they claimed, without much evidence, that black claim owners were dealing in stolen diamonds. Illicit diamond buying, known as IDB, meaning the buying and selling of diamonds stolen by workers, was a real problem, and white diggers feared that their own workers were stealing diamonds and selling them through black claim owners. But white dealers were also heavily involved in IDB, and the accusation was used as a weapon against black competitors.

White diggers organised diggers' committees and held mass meetings demanding that black people be banned from holding claims. In July 1872 white diggers at New Rush rioted, burning the tents of people they accused of IDB and demanding action from the government. The British administration under Richard Southey did not agree to an open racial ban, partly because British policy and the Cape's tradition did not formally make rights depend on race. Instead, rules were made that had a similar effect. Applicants for digging licences needed certificates of good character signed by magistrates or by a number of existing claim holders, who were mostly white and could refuse. Strict regulations about trading in diamonds made it easy to accuse black claim owners and harder for them to sell their finds. Some black claim owners had their licences refused or not renewed, and others sold up under pressure.

In 1875 white diggers rose in the Black Flag Rebellion, named after the black flag they raised, protesting against Southey's government, which they saw as too sympathetic to black diggers and to large claim holders. Troops were sent from the Cape, and Southey was recalled. After this, the interests of white diggers and the new mining companies became more important to the government.

By the end of the 1870s, black claim ownership at Kimberley had almost disappeared. When companies took over in the 1880s, ownership became a matter of shares and capital, and black people were in practice excluded. The opportunity that the early diggings had offered was closed.

It is worth comparing this with the Cape's political system at the time. The Cape franchise did not exclude men by race, so a black man with enough property could vote. Kimberley's experience shows that formal rules that did not mention race could still be used, through licences, certificates and policing, to produce racial exclusion in practice. Historians call this indirect discrimination, and it is a useful idea for analysing many later laws.

The questions for this section are with you now: black and coloured claim owners, illicit diamond buying and the white diggers' campaign, and how black claim owners were excluded.

## Subtopic: Labour, Passes and Compounds

Tens of thousands of African men came to work at Kimberley. They came from the Pedi kingdom, from Basutoland, from Tsonga areas in southern Mozambique, from Tswana communities and from the eastern Cape, among other places. Most were migrant workers who worked for some months and then returned home. In the early 1870s their wages, sometimes ten to thirty shillings a week, were higher than they could earn elsewhere, and many used them to buy guns, which were sold freely in Kimberley until the government restricted the trade.

Employers wanted to keep wages low and labour plentiful and controlled. In 1872 the authorities introduced pass regulations for African workers: a worker had to carry a pass showing his employer and his contract, and could be arrested if he did not have one or left work without permission. This was one of the origins of the pass laws that later spread across South Africa.

Employers also wanted to prevent theft. Diamonds are small and easy to hide, and workers could swallow them or hide them in their clothes or bodies. From the late 1870s workers were searched at the end of each shift, often in humiliating ways. In 1880 Griqualand West passed strict laws against IDB, and in 1882 the Diamond Trade Act made illicit buying a serious crime, with heavy penalties.

From 1885 the mining companies introduced closed compounds. African workers lived for the whole of their contracts, usually several months, inside a fenced and roofed compound next to the mine, which they could not leave except to go to work through a tunnel or covered passage. Food, clothing and goods were sold inside the compound by the company, often at company prices. At the end of their contracts, workers were held for several days and searched before release, to ensure they had not swallowed diamonds. White workers, by contrast, were not placed in closed compounds; they resisted strongly when the companies tried to search them.

Compounds gave the companies control over theft, over the workforce's time and over wages, and they cut workers off from the town and from other employers. They became a model for the hostels of the gold mines. For African workers, compounds meant isolation from families, restricted freedom and dependence on the company.

Health and safety were serious concerns. Reef falls, accidents with machinery and cables, and diseases in crowded living quarters killed many workers. Pneumonia and scurvy were common, and death rates among African workers in the 1870s and 1880s were very high.

The questions for this section are with you now: migrant labour, the pass system, and why compounds were introduced and what they meant for workers.

## Subtopic: From Many Diggers to a Monopoly, and the Error Museum

Put the pieces together and the movement towards monopoly becomes clear. Depth, reef falls and flooding raised costs beyond what individual diggers could pay. At the same time, the price of diamonds fell in the 1870s because so many were being produced, and an economic depression hit in the late 1870s. Small diggers sold their claims to bigger holders. Rules limiting how many claims one person could own were relaxed in 1876 and later removed, allowing consolidation. From 1880 joint-stock companies were formed, selling shares to raise capital for machinery, and a share-buying frenzy followed, ending in a crash in 1881 that ruined many small investors and strengthened the largest companies.

By the mid-1880s a few companies dominated the four main mines: Kimberley, De Beers, Dutoitspan and Bultfontein. In the Kimberley Mine, the Kimberley Central company, linked to Barney Barnato, was the largest; in the De Beers Mine, the De Beers Mining Company, led by Cecil Rhodes, dominated. In 1888 these rivals merged into De Beers Consolidated Mines, which soon controlled almost all of South Africa's diamond production. That story is the subject of the next session.

The result was a monopoly, a situation in which a single company controls the supply of a product. A monopoly can set prices and wages more easily than many competing producers. It also used its power to shape the labour system, as the compounds show.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying black people were never claim owners at Kimberley; some were, in the early 1870s, before they were pushed out. Two: saying a law simply banned black claim owners by name; exclusion came through licences, certificates, trading rules and pressure. Three: blaming IDB only on black workers; white dealers were heavily involved. Four: saying monopoly came only because of greed; geology, costs and falling prices also drove consolidation. Five: saying white workers were placed in closed compounds; only African workers were.

Layout for marks in an explain question on monopoly: cause, evidence, effect, three times, then a conclusion. For example: deeper mining caused reef falls and flooding that individual diggers could not afford to fix; falling prices and a depression pushed small diggers to sell; the removal of claim limits and the formation of companies after 1880 allowed consolidation; so by 1888 one company controlled almost all production.

The questions for this section are with you now: why monopoly developed, what a monopoly is, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Digging the Hole

Picture a hill in 1871 cut into hundreds of little squares, each about nine and a half metres on a side. Each square is a claim, and each claim has its own digger and workers with picks and shovels.

They dig straight down. Soon the hill is a pit, and the paths between the claims crumble. Ropes and buckets on cables carry the earth up to the edge, so the mine looks like a giant spider's web.

The deeper they go, the harder it gets. Rock walls fall in and bury the claims. Water floods the bottom. Pumps and steam engines are needed, and those cost a lot of money. A small digger cannot pay. So small diggers sell to bigger ones.

## Subtopic: Pushed Out

At first, some black and coloured men owned claims too. They dug, hired workers and some did well.

Many white diggers did not like that. They said, without much proof, that black claim owners were buying stolen diamonds. In 1872 white diggers rioted and burned tents. The government did not pass a law that said no black owners by name, but new rules about licences and character certificates, signed by mostly white people, did the same job. One by one, black claim owners lost their claims or had to sell.

Hold the burning tents and the refused licence in mind and try this part's questions: how black claim owners were pushed out.

## Subtopic: Passes and Compounds

Thousands of African men walked to Kimberley to work for wages, many from far away. Some bought guns with their pay. From 1872 they had to carry a pass, and could be arrested without one.

From 1885 the companies locked African workers inside compounds next to the mine for months at a time. They could only leave to go to work. They were searched before going home, to stop diamond theft. White workers were never locked up like this.

By 1888 one company, De Beers, controlled almost all the mines.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Kimberley Mine began as hundreds of small claims. Depth, rock falls, flooding and falling prices made mining too expensive for small diggers, so claims were combined and companies formed, leading to a monopoly by 1888. Early black and coloured claim owners were pushed out through riots, licence rules and accusations of illicit diamond buying. African workers were controlled by passes from 1872 and closed compounds from 1885.
