# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines how the supply and price of diamonds were regulated, from the diggers' rule of one man, one claim in the early 1870s to the control of world supply by De Beers and the London Diamond Syndicate from 1890. The anchor is the contrast between those two systems: one designed to spread opportunity among many small diggers, the other designed to concentrate control so that output could be limited and prices kept high. The governing idea is that the value of diamonds depends on scarcity, so whoever controls supply controls price, and that the struggle over who should control supply shaped the diamond industry and the lives of the people who worked in it. Explaining the regulation of supply and price is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: One Man, One Claim

When the dry diggings were opened in 1870 and 1871, there was no settled government at the diggings, and the diggers organised themselves through elected diggers' committees. These committees made rules about claims, disputes, and behaviour at the diggings, and the most important rule was one man, one claim, later relaxed to two claims per digger.

The diggers' reasons were partly about fairness and partly about self-interest. A small claim, about nine and a half metres square, was all one digger with a few workers could manage. If wealthy men or companies could buy up many claims, they would dominate the mine and small diggers would be squeezed out. The rule spread the opportunity to get rich among many people and kept the diggings open to newcomers. It also reflected the ideas of many diggers who had come from the gold rushes of California and Australia, where similar rules had been used.

The rule had limits. In practice, it was mainly applied to white diggers, and as the previous session showed, black claim owners faced pressure and rules that excluded them. Some diggers got around the limit by registering claims in the names of relatives or friends. Claims were also divided into halves, quarters and smaller fractions, which complicated ownership.

After Britain annexed Griqualand West in 1871, the colonial government took over the regulation of the diggings. It accepted the claim limit at first, because it was popular with the many small diggers. But as mining deepened and costs rose, the limit became an obstacle to efficient mining. Large holders and investors argued that only big units could afford the machinery to deal with reef falls and flooding. In 1876 the limit was raised to ten claims per holder, and later the restriction was removed entirely, allowing claims to be consolidated without limit. This change opened the way for companies, share selling and eventually monopoly.

The history of the rule shows a conflict between two ideas of how an industry should be organised: many small independent producers, or a few large companies with capital and machinery. At Kimberley, geology, economics and political pressure decided the conflict in favour of the large companies.

Historians note that the conflict over claim limits also shaped politics in Kimberley. Small white diggers formed associations, held mass meetings and pressed the colonial government to protect them against large holders, and their anger was one of the causes of the Black Flag Rebellion of 1875. Large holders and investors, many backed by merchants and banks in Port Elizabeth, Cape Town and London, lobbied in the opposite direction. The colonial government, which needed the revenue that a productive mine would provide, gradually sided with the large holders. In this way, a rule about the size of holdings became a struggle between social classes over who would own the wealth of the diamond fields.

The questions for this section are with you now: the rule of one man, one claim, the reasons for it, and why it was abandoned.

## Subtopic: Scarcity, Supply and Price

To understand why regulating supply mattered, start with the basic idea of supply and demand. The price of a product is set where the amount people want to buy, the demand, meets the amount producers offer for sale, the supply. If supply rises while demand stays the same, the price usually falls. If supply is limited, the price can rise.

Diamonds are a special case. As gemstones, their value depends almost entirely on people's belief that they are rare and precious, and on their use as symbols of wealth and love. Before 1870, the world's diamonds came mainly from India and Brazil, in small quantities, and they were luxury goods for the very rich. Kimberley changed that. Within a few years, South Africa was producing far more diamonds each year than the whole world had produced before, and prices in Europe fell sharply in the early 1870s.

A simple example shows the problem. Suppose a mine sells two thousand carats a month at three pounds a carat; its income is two thousand times three, which is six thousand pounds. If every mine produces more and the price falls to two pounds a carat, the same mine must sell three thousand carats a month just to earn the same six thousand pounds, because three thousand times two is six thousand. Producing more to make up for falling prices pushes prices down even further. This is the trap of overproduction.

The problem returned in the early 1880s. Companies formed during the share boom of 1880 and 1881 produced as much as they could to pay dividends to shareholders, and a world economic depression reduced demand. Prices fell again, many companies failed, and shares collapsed. Diamond producers concluded that unless supply was controlled, the industry would keep swinging between boom and bust.

Theft also affected supply. Diamonds stolen from the mines and sold through illicit channels increased the number of stones on the market without any control. The mining companies estimated that a large share of production was being stolen, which is why controlling theft through searches, IDB laws and closed compounds was also, from their point of view, a way of controlling supply.

## Subtopic: How De Beers Controlled Supply

Once De Beers Consolidated Mines controlled almost all the South African mines from 1888 and 1889, it could do what no group of competing diggers could: limit production. Rhodes and his fellow directors deliberately reduced output to match what the market would buy at good prices. Less profitable mines and workings were closed or slowed, and the workforce was cut. Diamonds not sold immediately could be held in stock rather than released onto the market.

De Beers also organised how diamonds were sold. In 1890 it made an agreement with a group of London diamond merchants known as the Diamond Syndicate. The syndicate agreed to buy the whole of De Beers's output, or an agreed quantity, at an agreed price, and then sold the diamonds to cutters and dealers in a controlled way, releasing only as many as the market could absorb without prices falling. This combination of a single producer and a single selling organisation gave the industry much more stable prices.

Control of supply required control of new discoveries. When large new diamond mines were found outside De Beers's control, they threatened the system. The Premier Mine near Pretoria, opened in 1903, produced the Cullinan diamond in 1905, the largest gem-quality rough diamond ever found, at about three thousand one hundred carats, and for some years the Premier competed with De Beers until an agreement brought it under control. Diamonds were later found in German South West Africa, today Namibia, in 1908. Each time, De Beers worked to buy the new producers or bring them into agreements.

In the twentieth century, Ernest Oppenheimer, founder of the Anglo American Corporation in 1917, became chairman of De Beers in 1929 and built a single central selling system in London that controlled most of the world's rough diamond sales for decades. During the Great Depression of the 1930s, when demand collapsed, De Beers closed mines and held huge stocks of diamonds rather than sell them cheaply. Later, advertising campaigns promoted the idea that a diamond engagement ring was a symbol of lasting love, increasing demand.

The labour system was part of the same structure. Closed compounds, searches and IDB laws prevented diamonds leaking onto the market and kept costs low, while migrant labour on short contracts allowed the companies to expand or cut their workforce as production plans changed.

Notice that each of these tools worked on a different part of the chain from the ground to the buyer: the monopoly controlled digging, the compounds controlled leaks, and the syndicate controlled selling.

The questions for this section are with you now: how De Beers limited production, the Diamond Syndicate, and control of new discoveries.

## Subtopic: Effects, the Present and the Error Museum

The regulation of supply had winners and losers. Shareholders and directors of De Beers gained enormous, stable profits, and the Cape Colony gained revenue. Diamond merchants in London and cutters in Antwerp and Amsterdam benefited from stable prices. On the other side, small independent diggers lost their chance to own claims. African workers, controlled by compounds and passes, saw their wages held down. When production was cut, jobs were lost. And the wealth was concentrated in very few hands, much of it overseas.

Diamonds remain important in southern Africa today. South Africa, Botswana and Namibia are major producers, and in Botswana diamonds, mined through a partnership between the government and De Beers, have helped fund schools, roads and health services since independence. Internationally, De Beers no longer controls the market as completely as it once did, because new producers such as Russia and Canada sell their diamonds independently, and laboratory-grown diamonds now compete with mined ones. In 2003 the Kimberley Process, named after a meeting held in Kimberley in 2000, began certifying rough diamonds to stop the trade in conflict diamonds, which had funded wars in countries such as Sierra Leone and Angola.

Kimberley itself shows the long arc of this story. The Kimberley Mine closed in 1914, and the Big Hole is now a museum and heritage site. De Beers continued to mine in the area for most of the twentieth century, and the city remained the company's South African headquarters for many years. As the old mines were worked out, jobs declined, and today the Northern Cape economy depends more on farming, other minerals, tourism and government services. The pattern is a familiar one in mining towns: great wealth flows out while the deposit lasts, and the town must find a new future when it ends. Historians and economists ask how much of the wealth of the diamond fields stayed in the places and with the people who produced it, and the answer for Kimberley is: much less than left.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying one man, one claim was a government law from the start; it began as a diggers' rule. Two: thinking diamond prices are set only by how useful diamonds are; gem prices depend on scarcity and belief. Three: believing more production always means more profit; overproduction lowered prices. Four: saying De Beers sold diamonds directly to the public; it sold through the London syndicate. Five: forgetting that controlling theft and labour was part of controlling supply.

Layout for marks in an explain question on price regulation: define supply and demand, explain why diamonds depend on scarcity, describe the overproduction of the 1870s and 1880s, then explain the two main tools of control, the monopoly limiting output and the syndicate controlling sales, with a sentence on who benefited. For example: because diamond prices depend on scarcity, overproduction caused prices to fall; De Beers, controlling almost all South African output from 1888, cut production, and from 1890 sold through the London Diamond Syndicate, keeping prices high for shareholders while workers' wages were held down.

## Subtopic: A Fair Share for Each Digger

At the start, the diggers made their own rules. The most important one was one man, one claim. Each digger could have only one small square of the mine, later two. That way, rich people could not buy the whole mine, and everyone had a chance.

But it was mostly fair for white diggers. Black diggers were pushed out by other rules. And as the mine got deeper and more dangerous, small claims could not pay for pumps and engines. In 1876 the limit went up to ten claims, and later there was no limit at all. Big holders and companies bought up the claims.

## Subtopic: Too Many Diamonds

Why are diamonds expensive? Because they are rare. If there were diamonds everywhere, nobody would pay much for them.

After Kimberley opened, there were suddenly far more diamonds than ever before, and prices fell. Here is the trap: if your diamonds sell for less, you dig up more to earn the same money, and that makes prices fall even more. That happened in the 1870s and again in the early 1880s, and many companies went broke.

## Subtopic: One Hand on the Tap

Once De Beers owned almost all the mines in 1888, it could turn the tap down. It dug fewer diamonds, closed some workings, and kept extra diamonds in storage. From 1890 a group of London merchants, the Diamond Syndicate, bought the diamonds and let them out slowly. Prices stayed high.

Stopping theft was part of it too, so the companies used compounds and searches. Owners got rich; small diggers lost out; workers' wages stayed low.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The diggers' rule of one man, one claim tried to spread opportunity but was abandoned as mining deepened: limits rose to ten claims in 1876 and then ended. Diamond prices depend on scarcity, so overproduction in the 1870s and 1880s made prices fall. De Beers, with almost all the mines from 1888, cut output, and from 1890 the London Diamond Syndicate controlled sales. Compounds and IDB laws helped control supply. Today the Kimberley Process fights conflict diamonds.
