### subtopic_1 — What Is a Revolution?
**Assistant:** Why does the Haitian Revolution matter so much if it happened in a small Caribbean colony?

**Tutor:** Because it proved, against everything the slave-owning world believed, that enslaved people could free themselves, defeat European armies and found their own nation. Saint-Domingue, the French colony on the western part of the island of Hispaniola, was in 1789 the richest colony in the world, producing a huge share of the sugar and coffee consumed in Europe. Its wealth rested on about half a million enslaved Africans, worked brutally on plantations, outnumbering the white colonists by more than ten to one. In 1791 the enslaved people rose in revolt. Over thirteen years of war they fought French planters, a British invasion and Spanish forces, and finally a large army sent by Napoleon to restore slavery. Toussaint Louverture, who had been born into slavery, became the outstanding leader until he was captured by the French and died in prison in France in 1803. Jean-Jacques Dessalines led the final victory, and on 1 January 1804 Haiti declared independence, the first black republic and the second independent nation in the Americas after the United States. The revolution sent shock waves through every slave society. Slave owners in the Caribbean, the United States and Brazil feared similar uprisings, and the abolition movement in Britain gained evidence that enslaved people would not accept their condition. Haiti paid a heavy price: France later forced it to pay a huge indemnity to compensate former slave owners in exchange for recognition, a debt that crippled its economy for generations. For this term, Haiti matters because it reminds us that the people at the bottom of the Atlantic economy were not passive; they were historical actors who changed the world.

### subtopic_2 — The Agricultural Revolution
**Assistant:** Was enclosure good or bad for Britain?

**Tutor:** It depends on whose experience you measure, and a good historical answer weighs both sides with evidence. From the point of view of food production and the national economy, enclosure was largely a success. Compact private farms let landowners use the new methods of the agricultural revolution: crop rotations, the seed drill, drainage and selective breeding. Yields of grain and the weight of animals rose, and Britain could feed a population that grew from about six million in England and Wales around 1750 to nearly eighteen million by 1851. Without that extra food, the industrial towns could not have grown. From the point of view of the rural poor, enclosure was often a disaster. Families who had farmed a few strips and grazed a cow on the common lost both. Those who could prove a legal right to land sometimes received a small plot in compensation, but many cottagers had used the common by custom without legal title and received nothing. Fencing and hedging were expensive, so small farmers often sold up. People who had been partly independent became wage labourers, dependent on a landowner or on the parish for relief, and many moved to towns. Contemporary writers and poets lamented the loss of the commons, and there were protests and occasional riots. Historians also note who decided: enclosure was carried out by Acts of Parliament, and Parliament was dominated by landowners, so the people who gained most made the rules. A balanced paragraph would state that enclosure increased food production and helped supply labour for industry, but at the cost of the independence and security of many poor rural families.

### subtopic_3 — The Domestic System
**Assistant:** Was life under the domestic system better than life in the factories that replaced it?

**Tutor:** In some ways yes, in others no, and historians warn against imagining the pre-industrial cottage as a happy rural paradise. Under the domestic system, families worked together in their own homes and could arrange their own hours. Nobody stood over them with a watch, and there was no factory bell. They often had a garden or a strip of land and perhaps a cow on the common, which gave some food security. Skills were passed from parents to children. When factory workers later described their lives, many looked back on that independence with longing. But the cottage had its own hardships. Earnings were low, and the merchant who owned the wool and set the piece rate held the real power; when trade was poor he simply put out less work. Cottages were small, damp and dark, with the loom taking up much of the space, and the whole family, including young children, worked long hours when orders came in. Illness, a bad harvest or a fall in prices could push a family into debt. So the change to factories was not simply from good to bad. What the factories took away above all was control: control over time, pace and conditions. The factory worker had to arrive at a set hour, work at the pace of the machine, obey overseers and follow rules backed by fines and dismissal. That loss of control, together with the crowded towns in which the factories stood, is why the early factory period is remembered as so harsh. When you compare the two systems in an essay, compare them under clear headings: hours, pay, control, family life and living conditions.

### subtopic_4 — Life and Trade Before Industry and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why did the Industrial Revolution start with cotton rather than with something like food or iron?

**Tutor:** Because cotton combined a huge demand, a production bottleneck that machines could break, and a raw material that could be supplied in growing quantities. Demand came first. Light, washable, brightly printed cotton cloth was hugely popular in Britain and in overseas markets, including West Africa, where it was traded, and the colonies. For much of the eighteenth century the finest cotton cloth came from India, imported by the East India Company, and British producers were desperate to compete. Then there was the bottleneck. Weaving a piece of cloth required the thread of several spinners, so spinning held everything back. Cotton fibre turned out to be well suited to mechanical spinning: it is strong and could be drawn out and twisted by rollers and spindles. The spinning jenny of the 1760s let one worker spin many threads at once, and Arkwright's water frame of 1769 produced strong thread by water power in purpose-built mills. Once spinning was mechanised, weaving became the bottleneck, which encouraged the power loom. Finally, supply could grow. Raw cotton came first from the Caribbean and then, in enormous quantities, from plantations in the southern United States worked by enslaved people, especially after the cotton gin of 1793 made it quicker to remove seeds. Food production could not be mechanised the same way at the time, and iron, though vital, became revolutionary only once steam engines and coke smelting created mass demand. So cotton led, and the factory system it created became the model that other industries followed. It is a reminder that the first factories were tied directly to the Atlantic slave economy.
