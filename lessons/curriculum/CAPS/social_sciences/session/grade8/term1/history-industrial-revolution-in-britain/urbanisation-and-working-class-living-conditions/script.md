# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the living conditions of the working class in Britain's industrial towns during the first half of the nineteenth century and the responses to them. The anchor is a court in central Manchester in the 1840s: a narrow yard enclosed on all sides by houses, entered through a tunnel from the street, with a single privy and a pump serving dozens of families, as described by the writer Friedrich Engels and by the government's own investigators. The governing idea is that the towns grew far faster than anyone planned for, and that housing, water, sewerage and help for the poor were left largely to private builders and to a harsh Poor Law, so that disease and poverty became the defining experiences of working-class urban life until public action slowly changed them. Using primary sources such as reports and statistics to describe and explain these conditions is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Overcrowded Housing

Industrial towns grew at astonishing speed. Thousands of families arrived every year from the countryside, and from Ireland, especially after the famine of the 1840s. They needed homes close to the mills, because working days were long and there was no cheap transport. There were no national building regulations and little town planning, so builders and landlords put up houses as cheaply and densely as possible to earn the highest rents.

The typical result was the back-to-back house. Rows of houses were built sharing a back wall as well as side walls, so each house had windows and a door on one side only. There was no through draught to clear smoke and smells, no back yard and no garden. Many had one room downstairs and one up. Houses were built around narrow courts, enclosed yards reached through a passage from the street, which trapped dirt and stale air.

The poorest families lived in cellars, below street level, damp, dark and often flooded when it rained. In the 1840s an inquiry found tens of thousands of people living in cellars in Liverpool alone. Lodging houses rented out space by the night, with several people to a bed.

Overcrowding was extreme. Large families shared one or two rooms, often taking in lodgers to help pay the rent. Engels, the son of a German cotton manufacturer who lived in Manchester and published The Condition of the Working Class in England in 1845, described districts where people lived among heaps of refuse, with pigs kept in the courts and filth running down the alleys. Writers such as Charles Dickens brought similar scenes to middle-class readers in novels.

Factory chimneys added to the misery. Coal smoke from mills and from thousands of household fires covered the towns in soot, blackened buildings and damaged lungs, and the rivers that ran through the towns were used both as drains and as sources of water.

Not every working family lived in a cellar or a court. Skilled workers with regular wages could rent better terraced houses, and some employers, such as Arkwright at Cromford and later Titus Salt at Saltaire, built decent homes for their workers. But for the unskilled and the irregularly employed, overcrowded and unhealthy housing was the norm.

The questions for this section are with you now: why towns were overcrowded, the features of back-to-back houses, courts and cellars, and why housing was so poor.

## Subtopic: Dirt, Water and Disease

Overcrowding became deadly because of the lack of clean water and sewerage. Most working-class streets had no piped water inside houses. Families carried water from a shared pump or standpipe, often available for only a short time each day, or bought it from carts. Water companies drew much of their supply from rivers into which sewage was poured.

Sanitation was primitive. A single privy, a toilet over a pit called a cesspool, might serve twenty or more households. Cesspools overflowed into yards, cellars and wells. Nightsoil men emptied them by hand and carted the waste away, but not often enough. Streets were unpaved, undrained and covered in rubbish and animal dung.

These conditions spread disease. Cholera, a disease carried in water contaminated with human waste, reached Britain in 1831 and returned in great epidemics in 1848 to 1849, when more than fifty thousand people died in England and Wales, and again in 1853 to 1854 and 1866. Typhoid also spread through dirty water and food. Typhus, carried by lice, flourished in overcrowded, unwashed housing. Tuberculosis, a lung disease spread through the air, thrived in damp, crowded rooms and was the biggest killer of all. Infant mortality was terrible: in some working-class districts, a large share of children died before the age of five.

In 1842 Edwin Chadwick, a civil servant, published his Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population of Great Britain. Using evidence collected from all over the country, he showed that disease was linked to dirt, poor drainage and bad water, and that the poor died much younger than the rich. His most quoted figures came from Manchester, where the average age at death was thirty-eight for professional men and their families, twenty for tradesmen, and seventeen for labourers. These averages were pulled down by the many deaths of babies and young children, but they showed a staggering inequality.

The questions for this section are with you now: water supply and sanitation, the main diseases and how they spread, and the findings of the Chadwick report.

## Subtopic: Public Health Reform

Change came slowly, because many people believed that government should not interfere in private property or local affairs, and because ratepayers objected to the cost of drains and water works. It took evidence, epidemics and persistence.

The first Public Health Act of 1848, passed during a cholera epidemic, set up a General Board of Health and allowed towns to create local boards of health to provide clean water, drains and sewers. But it was permissive: towns could choose whether to act, and many did not.

Scientific understanding changed too. Many people, including Chadwick, believed disease was caused by bad air, called miasma, rising from filth. In 1854, during a cholera outbreak in Soho in London, the doctor John Snow mapped the deaths and showed that they clustered around a single water pump in Broad Street. When the pump handle was removed, the outbreak in that area declined. Snow argued that cholera spread through contaminated water, not foul air. His argument took years to be accepted, but it was later confirmed by the germ theory of disease, developed by Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch in the 1860s to 1880s.

In the hot summer of 1858, the stench of sewage in the River Thames beside Parliament, known as the Great Stink, finally pushed the government to act in London. The engineer Joseph Bazalgette built a vast network of intercepting sewers that carried waste away from the city, completed over the following years. London's last major cholera outbreak was in 1866, in an area not yet connected to the new system.

In 1875 a new Public Health Act made it compulsory for local authorities to provide clean water, drainage and sewage disposal, to appoint medical officers of health and to deal with nuisances. In the same year, the Artisans' Dwellings Act allowed councils to clear slums. Slowly, from the 1870s, death rates in towns began to fall.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Public Health Acts of 1848 and 1875, John Snow's discovery, and the Great Stink.

## Subtopic: Poverty, the Workhouse and the Error Museum

Poverty was a constant threat to working families. Wages were low and irregular. Illness, injury, old age, the death of a breadwinner, a trade slump or a large family could push a household into destitution, and there was no unemployment insurance, no state pension and no health service.

For centuries the poor had received help from their parish under the Poor Laws, paid for by local taxes called poor rates. Sometimes this help was given as money or food to people living in their own homes, called outdoor relief. Ratepayers complained that the cost was rising and that relief encouraged idleness. In 1834 Parliament passed the Poor Law Amendment Act. Parishes were grouped into unions, each of which built a workhouse. Able-bodied people seeking help were, in principle, to receive it only by entering the workhouse.

The workhouse was designed on the principle of less eligibility: conditions inside had to be worse than those of the poorest independent labourer outside, so that only the truly desperate would apply. Inmates wore uniforms. Families were separated, with husbands, wives and children housed in different wings. Food was plain and monotonous. Inmates did hard, dull work such as breaking stones, crushing bones for fertiliser or picking apart old ropes, called oakum picking. Rules were strict, and many people felt entering the workhouse was a deep disgrace. In 1845 a scandal at the Andover workhouse revealed that starving inmates had fought over scraps of rotting meat on the bones they were crushing. Charles Dickens attacked the system in Oliver Twist, which famously shows a workhouse boy asking for more.

In practice, outdoor relief continued in many areas, especially in industrial towns where trade slumps could throw thousands out of work at once and no workhouse could hold them all. But the fear of the workhouse shaped working-class life for generations, and it encouraged workers to form friendly societies and trade unions to protect themselves.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: believing all working families lived in cellars. Two: thinking cholera spread through bad air; Snow showed it spread through water. Three: treating the 1848 Public Health Act as compulsory; it was permissive until 1875. Four: describing the workhouse as a place of work for wages. Five: reading Chadwick's average age at death of seventeen as meaning that most labourers died at seventeen; high child deaths pulled the average down.

Layout for marks in a source-based question on conditions: identify what the source shows, use one figure or detail from it, explain the cause, and comment on the source's origin. For example: Chadwick's 1842 report shows an average age at death of seventeen for Manchester labourers, because of overcrowding, polluted water and poor drainage; as a government report based on wide evidence it is useful, though Chadwick wanted to prove the need for reform.

The questions for this section are with you now: causes of poverty, the 1834 Poor Law and workhouse conditions, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Twelve People, One Room

Imagine your family arrives in Manchester in 1840 with almost nothing. The mill needs workers, so you need a home close by, because the shift starts at six and you will be walking. There are thousands of other families doing exactly the same thing.

The builders know it. They put up rows of houses as fast and cheaply as possible, back to back, so each house shares its back wall with another house. That means windows on one side only, no back yard, no fresh air blowing through. One room down, one room up. Your family of seven moves in, and to pay the rent you take in a lodger or two.

If you are even poorer, you rent a cellar under the street. It is dark and damp, and when it rains, water runs in.

Outside, the houses crowd around a narrow yard. The air is thick with coal smoke from the mill chimneys and every kitchen fire. Everything is covered in soot.

Not everyone lived like this. Skilled workers with good wages could afford better houses. But for most unskilled families, this was home.

Hold the crowded room in mind and try the questions for this part: back-to-back houses, cellars, and why builders built this way.

## Subtopic: Dirty Water, Deadly Disease

Now look at the yard. There is one toilet, a hole over a pit, for maybe twenty families. The pit overflows into the yard. There is one tap, and it only runs for an hour a day. The water comes from the river, the same river the sewage pours into.

You can guess what happened. Diseases spread fast. Cholera, carried in dirty water, killed tens of thousands of people in great waves, including more than fifty thousand in England and Wales in 1848 and 1849. Typhus spread through lice. Tuberculosis spread through coughs in damp, crowded rooms. Many babies did not live to see their fifth birthday.

In 1842 a man called Edwin Chadwick collected evidence from all over the country and found that in Manchester labourers' families died, on average, at seventeen, while wealthy families died at thirty-eight. That average is so low because so many babies and children died.

Then, in 1854, a London doctor called John Snow put dots on a map for every cholera death and found they gathered around one water pump. Take the handle off, and the deaths slowed. Dirty water was the killer. Sewers and clean water followed, slowly, and by the 1870s town death rates began to fall.

Hold the one toilet and the one pump in mind and try this part's questions: how disease spread, and what Snow discovered.

## Subtopic: The House Nobody Wanted to Enter

If your father fell ill, or the mill closed, or your parents grew too old to work, what then? No unemployment money, no pension, no free hospital.

From 1834 the answer was the workhouse. The government made the rule that poor people who could work should only get help inside a workhouse, and that life there had to be worse than the worst job outside. So families were split up as they walked in: men one way, women another, children another. Everyone wore a uniform, ate the same plain food every day, and did boring, hard work like breaking stones or picking apart old ropes. People were ashamed to go in, and terrified of ending up there.

Charles Dickens wrote about it in Oliver Twist, where a hungry boy dares to ask for more food.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Industrial towns grew too fast. Builders put up cheap back-to-back houses, and the poorest lived in cellars. One toilet and one tap served many families, so cholera, typhoid, typhus and tuberculosis spread, and the poor died much younger than the rich. Chadwick's report of 1842 and John Snow's map of 1854 led to clean water and sewers, made compulsory in 1875. Poor people seeking help after 1834 faced the workhouse, designed to be harsh.

Hold the crowded room, the deadly pump and the workhouse door, and take the final questions of the lesson: housing, disease and the Poor Law.
