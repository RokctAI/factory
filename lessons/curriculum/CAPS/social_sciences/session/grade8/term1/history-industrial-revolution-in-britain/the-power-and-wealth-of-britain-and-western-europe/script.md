# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines how the Industrial Revolution turned Britain, and later other countries of Western Europe, into the world's leading economic and military powers, and how that power was connected to the rest of the world. The anchor is the Great Exhibition of 1851, held in the Crystal Palace in London, where Britain displayed its industrial achievements to millions of visitors. The governing idea is that industrial wealth and global power reinforced each other: factories needed raw materials and markets overseas, and the navy, empire and banks that secured them were paid for by industrial wealth. That cycle brought great prosperity to some and great suffering to others, and it set the stage for the colonisation of Africa. Explaining the sources of Britain's power and its consequences is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: The Workshop of the World

By the middle of the nineteenth century, Britain had become the first industrial nation. Around 1860, a country with only about two percent of the world's population produced roughly half of the world's iron, coal and cotton cloth. Its factories turned out textiles, machinery, tools, rails, locomotives and ships, much of it for export. The phrase workshop of the world described this position well.

Several foundations made this possible. Britain had large reserves of coal and iron ore close together, often near the coast or rivers. It had a growing population that supplied workers and customers. Farming had become more productive, so fewer people were needed on the land and food could feed the growing towns. The country had a stable government, laws that protected property and contracts, and a network of banks that lent money to businesses. Its inventors and engineers, from James Watt with the steam engine to Richard Arkwright with the water frame, turned ideas into working machines. And Britain already had a large overseas trade and colonies before industrialisation began.

Transport tied it together. Canals, built mainly between 1760 and 1830, carried heavy coal cheaply. The Liverpool and Manchester Railway opened in 1830, the first inter-city railway with steam locomotives, and by 1850 Britain had about ten thousand kilometres of track. Railways cut the cost and time of moving goods and people, created demand for iron, coal and engineering, and employed hundreds of thousands of workers. Steamships, built of iron and later steel, crossed oceans faster and more reliably than sailing ships. The electric telegraph, and from 1866 a successful cable under the Atlantic, let merchants and governments send messages across the world in minutes instead of weeks.

The Great Exhibition of 1851 was a celebration of all this. Prince Albert, the husband of Queen Victoria, supported the idea, and the Crystal Palace was built of prefabricated iron and about three hundred thousand panes of glass. About fourteen thousand exhibitors showed goods from around the world, but Britain's machinery was the star. The exhibition made a profit that was used to found museums and colleges in London.

Industrial wealth changed British society. A large middle class of factory owners, merchants, bankers, engineers, doctors and clerks grew rich and gained political influence. The Reform Act of 1832 gave many of them the vote. Cities grew rapidly; by 1851, for the first time in any large country, more British people lived in towns than in the countryside. At the same time, as earlier sessions showed, many working people lived in crowded, unhealthy conditions, and the benefits of growth reached them only slowly. By the later nineteenth century, real wages for many workers were rising, but inequality remained very great.

Historians debate why Britain was first rather than France, the Netherlands or China. Some stress coal: Britain's easily reached coal gave cheap energy that other places lacked. Some stress high wages: because British labour was relatively expensive and coal cheap, it paid to invent machines that replaced workers. Others stress institutions such as Parliament, patents and banks, or the profits and raw materials of empire and the Atlantic trade, including the trade in enslaved Africans. A strong answer recognises that several factors worked together.

The questions for this section are with you now: why Britain became the workshop of the world, transport and communication, and the Great Exhibition.

## Subtopic: Trade, Money and the Navy

Factories needed raw materials that Britain could not produce, and they produced far more goods than Britain could buy. Overseas trade solved both problems. Raw cotton came mostly from the southern United States, where it was grown by enslaved African Americans until slavery was abolished there in 1865; later more came from India and Egypt. Wool came from Australia and, from the middle of the century, from the Cape Colony and Natal. Sugar, tea, coffee, timber, rubber and palm oil came from colonies and trading partners in the Caribbean, Asia and Africa. In return, Britain exported textiles, iron goods, machinery, coal and later railway equipment to the world.

Britain moved towards free trade, meaning trade without high taxes, called tariffs, on imports. The Corn Laws, which had kept the price of grain high to protect landowners, were repealed in 1846 after a campaign by the Anti-Corn Law League of manufacturers, who wanted cheap food for workers and open markets for their goods. Free trade suited the world's leading industrial power, because British goods were cheaper than anyone else's and could compete anywhere.

London became the financial centre of the world. Its banks, insurance companies and stock exchange raised money for railways, mines, ports and governments on every continent. The British pound was backed by gold, and much of the world's trade was paid for in pounds. Investment overseas earned interest and dividends that flowed back to Britain. By 1914 British investors owned a huge share of all foreign investment in the world, including in South African gold mines.

The Royal Navy protected this trading system. After the defeat of Napoleon in 1815, Britain's navy was larger than the next two or three navies combined. It guarded sea routes, coaling stations and ports such as Gibraltar, Cape Town, Aden, Singapore and Hong Kong. The Cape mattered because, until the Suez Canal opened in 1869, the sea route from Britain to India went around the southern tip of Africa. The navy also enforced Britain's ban on the slave trade, which Parliament had passed in 1807, by patrolling the coast of West Africa.

The empire grew alongside industry. India came under the control of the British East India Company and, after 1858, the British government directly. Australia, New Zealand, Canada and the Cape were settled by British emigrants. By the early twentieth century the British Empire covered about a quarter of the world's land and ruled about a quarter of its people.

Think about the cycle this created. Profits from factories paid for ships, guns and loans overseas. Ships, guns and loans opened new sources of raw materials and new markets. Raw materials and markets made the factories more profitable. Each part of the system strengthened the others. A historian calls this a self-reinforcing process, and it explains why the gap between industrial Europe and the rest of the world grew so quickly during the nineteenth century.

The questions for this section are with you now: raw materials and markets, free trade and the City of London, and the role of the Royal Navy.

## Subtopic: Western Europe Industrialises

Britain tried to keep its industrial secrets, and until 1825 skilled workers were forbidden to emigrate and until 1843 many machines could not legally be exported. But knowledge spread. Engineers, workers and machines found their way abroad, and other countries copied and improved British methods.

Belgium was the first country on the continent to industrialise, from the 1820s, using its coal and iron and building a state railway network from 1835. France industrialised more slowly, with strengths in textiles, luxury goods and later steel and cars. Germany, united as one country in 1871, grew fastest in the later nineteenth century. Its Ruhr valley became a great centre of coal and steel, and firms such as Krupp made steel and weapons on a huge scale. By 1900 Germany produced more steel than Britain and led the world in the new chemical and electrical industries. Outside Europe, the United States grew into the world's largest industrial economy by about 1890.

This spread is sometimes called the Second Industrial Revolution, from about 1870. It was based on steel, chemicals, electricity, oil and the internal combustion engine rather than only on coal, iron and cotton. Science and research laboratories became more important.

Competition between industrial powers grew sharper. Each country wanted secure supplies of raw materials and protected markets for its goods. Many turned away from free trade and raised tariffs. Industrial power also meant military power: factories produced rifles, artillery, steel warships and railways that moved armies quickly. Rivalry between Britain, Germany and France for trade, colonies and naval strength became one of the long-term causes of the First World War, which you study in Term 4.

This rivalry also drove the race to colonise Africa from the 1880s. European powers wanted African raw materials such as rubber, palm oil, cotton, copper, gold and diamonds, new markets, places to invest, and strategic bases, and they had the industrial tools to conquer: steamships to travel up rivers, quinine to reduce deaths from malaria, railways and the telegraph, and the Maxim machine gun, invented in 1884. At the Berlin Conference of 1884 to 1885, they agreed rules for dividing up Africa among themselves without African rulers present.

Industrialisation did not spread evenly within Europe either. Southern and eastern Europe, including Spain, Italy and Russia, industrialised later and more patchily, and millions of their people emigrated to the Americas in search of work. The map of industrial Europe in 1900 had a core in Britain, Belgium, northern France and western Germany, surrounded by regions that were still mostly farming. This pattern of core and periphery is a useful idea for Geography as well as History.

The questions for this section are with you now: how industry spread to Belgium, France and Germany, the Second Industrial Revolution, and rivalry between the powers.

## Subtopic: The Cost to the World and the Error Museum

The wealth and power of Western Europe had costs that were paid elsewhere. India had been one of the world's great producers of fine cotton cloth. British machine-made textiles, helped by colonial policies that favoured British goods and taxed Indian ones, undercut Indian hand-weavers, and India became mainly an exporter of raw cotton and an importer of British cloth. The cotton that fed Lancashire's mills was grown, until 1865, by enslaved people in the American South. Profits from the earlier Atlantic slave trade and from slave plantations in the Caribbean had helped build ports such as Liverpool and Bristol and provided capital for some industrial enterprises, although historians debate how large that contribution was.

In Africa, industrial power made colonial conquest possible, and colonies were organised to send raw materials to Europe rather than to build their own industries. In southern Africa, as you will study, British capital and engineering developed the diamond fields at Kimberley from 1871 and the gold mines of the Witwatersrand from 1886, and Natal's sugar plantations used indentured labourers from India from 1860. The wealth produced flowed largely to investors in Britain and to the mine owners, while black workers were paid low wages and controlled by pass laws and compounds.

The industrial world also changed the environment. Burning coal released smoke and gases that blackened cities and, as we now know, began the build-up of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere that drives climate change today.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying Britain was rich only because of its inventors; coal, capital, trade, empire, institutions and labour all mattered. Two: believing industrialisation brought immediate wealth to all British people; workers' gains came slowly. Three: forgetting that raw cotton was grown by enslaved people. Four: assuming industry stayed in Britain; Belgium, France, Germany and the United States followed, and Germany and the United States overtook Britain in some industries. Five: treating colonisation as unrelated to industrialisation; the demand for raw materials and markets, and industrial weapons and transport, drove it.

Layout for marks in a paragraph on Britain's power: a topic sentence stating Britain's position, two or three causes with evidence, a link to the wider world, and a judgement. For example: by 1860 Britain, with about two percent of the world's population, produced about half its iron and coal, because it combined cheap coal, inventions, capital and a protected global trade guarded by the Royal Navy; this power rested partly on raw materials from colonies and enslaved labour, and it led to further colonisation.

The questions for this section are with you now: the costs of industrial power to India, the Americas and Africa, links to South Africa, and the error museum.

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: the workshop of the world, how trade and the navy kept it running, and who paid the price.

## Subtopic: Britain the Workshop

Imagine walking into a huge glass building in London in 1851. It sparkles in the sun. Inside are steam engines, spinning machines, locomotives and goods of every kind. This is the Great Exhibition in the Crystal Palace, and Britain is showing off.

Why was Britain so far ahead? It had lots of coal and iron close together. It had inventors who built machines that did the work of many people. It had banks to lend money, a stable government, and a growing number of workers and customers. Canals and then railways moved goods quickly and cheaply; the first big passenger railway, between Liverpool and Manchester, opened in 1830.

The result: around 1860 this one small country, with about two people in every hundred in the world, made about half of the world's iron and coal. People called Britain the workshop of the world. Factory owners, bankers and merchants became rich, though many workers stayed poor for a long time.

## Subtopic: Ships, Money and Empire

Factories needed things Britain did not grow, like cotton, and they made more than Britain could buy. So Britain traded across the world. Cotton came from the United States, where enslaved people grew it until 1865. Wool came from Australia and later from the Cape and Natal. Britain sent back cloth, iron goods and machines.

Britain's banks in London lent money all over the world, and the pound was the money of world trade. The Royal Navy, the strongest in the world, guarded the sea routes and ports, including Cape Town on the way to India. By the early 1900s the British Empire covered about a quarter of the world.

Other countries caught up. Belgium went first in Europe, then France, then Germany, whose steel industry passed Britain's by 1900. The United States grew bigger still. These powers became rivals for trade and colonies.

## Subtopic: Who Paid the Price

Wealth in one place often meant hardship in another. India's skilled weavers lost their work when cheap British cloth flooded in. Enslaved people in America grew the cotton for Britain's mills.

Industrial power also made it easier for Europe to conquer Africa. Steamships, medicine against malaria, railways and machine guns gave Europeans a huge advantage. From 1884 they divided Africa among themselves. In South Africa, British money and machines opened the diamond mines of Kimberley and the gold mines of Johannesburg, and Natal's sugar farms brought workers from India. The riches mostly went to owners and investors overseas, while black workers were paid little.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Coal, iron, inventions, banks, transport and trade made Britain the workshop of the world by about 1860. Free trade, the City of London and the Royal Navy spread and protected its power, and the empire grew to about a quarter of the world. Belgium, France, Germany and the United States followed. The costs fell on India, on enslaved people and on Africa, where industrial power made colonisation possible.
