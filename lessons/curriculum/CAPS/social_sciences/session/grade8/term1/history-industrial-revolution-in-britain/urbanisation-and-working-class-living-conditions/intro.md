In 1842 a government report found that in Manchester the average age at death of a professional man was thirty-eight, and of a labourer only seventeen. Today we find out why. We walk into the working-class districts of Britain's industrial towns: back-to-back houses built as cheaply as possible, cellars where whole families lived below the street, one toilet shared by dozens of households, and water drawn from polluted rivers. We see how cholera, typhoid and tuberculosis spread, and how the investigations of Edwin Chadwick and the detective work of John Snow pushed the government into public health reform. Then we turn to poverty, and to the workhouse, the institution designed by the Poor Law of 1834 to be so unpleasant that only the desperate would enter it.
