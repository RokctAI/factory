### subtopic_1 — Early Resistance: Luddites and Peterloo
**Assistant:** Were the Luddites just people who were afraid of new technology?

**Tutor:** No, and this is one of the most common misunderstandings in this topic. The Luddites were skilled workers, mostly framework knitters in the Midlands, croppers in Yorkshire who finished woollen cloth, and weavers in Lancashire. They had spent years learning their trades, and their skill gave them a decent income and a sense of independence. What they objected to was the way particular machines were being used. In the Midlands, for example, knitters attacked wide frames that produced cheap, badly made stockings, and employers who used unapprenticed workers and paid in goods instead of money. In Yorkshire, croppers attacked shearing frames that did the work of several skilled men. Their letters and the targets they chose show that they were selective: they often smashed the frames of employers who cut wages and left alone the frames of those who paid fairly. The context matters too. Britain was at war with France, trade was disrupted, food prices were very high, and there was mass unemployment in some districts. Workers had no vote and unions were illegal under the Combination Acts, so peaceful bargaining and political pressure were closed to them. Machine-breaking was a form of collective bargaining by riot, as one historian called it: a way of putting pressure on employers when no other method existed. That does not mean the Luddites were right about everything, or that machines could have been stopped. In the long run, machines did make goods cheaper for everyone. But it does mean that the modern use of the word Luddite, to mean someone who is simply scared of technology, does not fit the historical evidence. A good answer explains their real grievances.

### subtopic_2 — Building Unions: From Illegal to Legal
**Assistant:** Why were the Tolpuddle labourers punished if trade unions were already legal in 1834?

**Tutor:** Because the authorities found another law to use, which is exactly why the case caused such anger. After the Combination Acts were repealed in 1824 and a new law was passed in 1825, it was no longer a crime simply to form or join a union. But many unions still used ceremonies when new members joined, including an oath of loyalty and secrecy, partly because members feared being sacked if employers found out. In 1834 the Tolpuddle labourers held such a ceremony. Local landowners and magistrates were alarmed, because there had been serious rural unrest in southern England a few years earlier, known as the Swing Riots, when labourers burned barns and broke threshing machines. The authorities wanted to stop unions spreading among farm workers. They used the Unlawful Oaths Act of 1797, which had been passed after a naval mutiny and was meant for that kind of danger. The trial was quick, and the judge passed the maximum sentence of seven years' transportation, saying he wanted to set an example. Many people saw this as a clear misuse of the law against peaceful working men who had harmed no one. The Grand National Consolidated Trades Union organised a march of tens of thousands in London, and petitions carried around eight hundred thousand signatures. Under this pressure, the government granted pardons in 1836, although it took time for the men to be brought back from Australia. The lesson historians draw is that legal rights on paper did not mean real freedom for workers, because the authorities could use other laws against them. A well-argued answer would explain this gap between legality and practice.

### subtopic_3 — Chartism: Workers Demand the Vote
**Assistant:** If Parliament rejected all three petitions, why do historians not call Chartism a failure?

**Tutor:** Because historians judge a movement both by what it achieved at the time and by its long-term influence, and Chartism's long-term influence was very great. In the short term, it certainly failed. Parliament rejected the petitions of 1839, 1842 and 1848 by large majorities, the Newport Rising was crushed, many leaders were imprisoned or transported, and after 1848 the movement declined. Reasons for the failure include the fact that Parliament was controlled by landowners and wealthy men who had no wish to share power, the divisions between moral force and physical force Chartists, the weakness of organisation in some areas, the government's firm use of police, soldiers and the courts, and economic recovery in the late 1840s and 1850s, which reduced discontent. The fake signatures in 1848 also damaged its reputation. But look at what came later. The property qualification for members of Parliament was abolished in 1858. The Reform Acts of 1867 and 1884 gave the vote to many more working men, and by 1918 virtually all men over twenty-one could vote. The secret ballot was introduced in 1872. Payment of members of Parliament began in 1911. Constituencies became more equal through redistribution. Only annual parliaments were never adopted. Chartism also trained thousands of working people in political organisation, public speaking and newspaper writing, and many later became leaders of trade unions and the early Labour movement. So the best answer is balanced: Chartism failed to achieve its aims in its own time, but its ideas became the foundation of British democracy. This kind of judgement, separating short-term and long-term results, is useful in many history essays.

### subtopic_4 — New Unions, Legacy and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why were the matchgirls' and dockers' strikes seen as such important victories?

**Tutor:** Because they showed that unskilled, poorly paid workers, who had been thought impossible to organise, could act together and win. Before the late 1880s, most strong unions were made up of skilled craftsmen whose skills made them hard to replace and whose good wages allowed them to pay union subscriptions. Unskilled workers, by contrast, could easily be replaced, were often hired by the day, and earned too little to build strike funds. The matchgirls at Bryant and May in east London were mostly young women and girls. They worked long hours, suffered fines and deductions from their low wages, and handled white phosphorus, which caused phossy jaw, a disease that rotted the jawbone. When the journalist Annie Besant published an article about their conditions in 1888, the company tried to make the workers sign a statement denying it. Instead, about fourteen hundred of them walked out. With public support and publicity, they won the end of fines and other improvements within about three weeks, and formed a union. The following year, around a hundred thousand dock workers in London struck for sixpence an hour and for a minimum of four hours' work at a time. Led by Ben Tillett, John Burns and Tom Mann, they held peaceful marches through the city and won wide public sympathy. Donations, including about thirty thousand pounds from workers in Australia, kept them going. After five weeks the employers gave in. These victories inspired new unions of gas workers, general labourers and others, and union membership grew rapidly. They also strengthened the push for working people to have their own political party, which led to the Labour Party.
