# Part 1 — Expert

This session prepares the ground for the study of the Industrial Revolution by asking two questions: what do historians mean by a revolution, and what was Britain's economy like before industrialisation? The anchor is a weaving family in a Yorkshire village around 1750, working at a loom in their own cottage on wool supplied by a merchant, with a small strip of land outside and a common field for their cow. The governing idea is that revolutions are periods of fundamental change, and they come in different kinds: some are sudden and political, others are gradual and economic, but all transform how people live. The Industrial Revolution can only be understood against the world it replaced. The vocabulary of revolution and the features of the pre-industrial economy are examinable this term and recur in Grade 10 History.

## Subtopic: What Is a Revolution?

In everyday speech, a revolution is a sudden, violent overthrow of a government. Historians use the word more broadly. A revolution is a fundamental change in the way something is done or organised, a change so deep that life afterwards is very different from life before. It can happen quickly or over decades.

Political revolutions change who holds power and how a country is governed. The American Revolution, from 1775 to 1783, ended British rule over thirteen colonies in North America and created the United States, a republic with a written constitution, though it left slavery in place. The French Revolution, which began in 1789, overthrew the absolute monarchy of Louis XVI, proclaimed the rights of man and of the citizen, and set off wars across Europe; its ideas of liberty, equality and fraternity spread around the world. The Haitian Revolution, from 1791 to 1804, was the only successful revolt by enslaved people that created an independent state. Led at first by Toussaint Louverture, the enslaved population of the French colony of Saint-Domingue defeated French, Spanish and British forces, and in 1804 declared the independent nation of Haiti.

Economic and social revolutions change how people produce goods, earn a living and live together. The agricultural revolution changed farming methods in Britain during the eighteenth century. The Industrial Revolution changed manufacturing, transport and work from about 1750. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, many people speak of a digital or information revolution brought by computers, the internet and mobile phones.

Scientific revolutions change how people understand the natural world, such as the shift in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries to explaining nature through observation, experiment and mathematics.

These kinds of revolution influence each other. New scientific understanding made new machines possible. The agricultural revolution released workers and food for the industrial towns. The political revolutions in America and France spread ideas of rights and representation that industrial workers later used to demand the vote and better conditions.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of revolution, examples of political revolutions with their dates, and the difference between political and economic revolutions.

## Subtopic: The Agricultural Revolution

Before the Industrial Revolution, Britain was a farming country. Around 1750 most people lived in villages and worked on the land. Many villages still used the open-field system inherited from the Middle Ages. The land around the village was divided into a few huge fields, each split into long narrow strips. A family farmed scattered strips in different fields, so that good and poor land was shared. One field in three was left fallow each year to recover. Villagers also had rights to the common land, where they grazed animals, collected firewood and gathered food. The system was fair in some ways but inefficient: time was wasted walking between strips, animals grazing together spread disease, and it was hard for any farmer to try new methods.

During the eighteenth century, farming changed in ways that together are called the agricultural revolution. Jethro Tull's seed drill, developed early in the century, planted seeds in neat rows at the right depth instead of scattering them by hand, so less seed was wasted. The four-field rotation, associated with Norfolk and with Charles Townshend, known as Turnip Townshend, grew wheat, turnips, barley and clover in turn. Clover put goodness back into the soil and turnips fed animals through the winter, so no field needed to lie fallow and more animals survived to spring. Robert Bakewell bred sheep and cattle selectively, choosing the best animals as parents, and produced much heavier animals for meat.

These methods worked best on large, compact, privately owned farms. So landowners enclosed the open fields and common land, fencing or hedging them into separate farms. Enclosure was carried out through thousands of Acts of Parliament between about 1750 and 1850. Farms became more productive and food supplies increased, which allowed the population to grow. But many poorer villagers lost their strips and their rights to the common, and with them their independence. Some became labourers on the new farms for low wages; many moved to the growing towns in search of work, becoming the workforce of the new factories.

The questions for this section are with you now: the open-field system, the new methods of the agricultural revolution, and the effects of enclosure.

## Subtopic: The Domestic System

Before factories, most goods were made by hand, at home or in small workshops. The most important manufacture was cloth, made from wool and, increasingly, cotton and linen.

Much cloth was produced through the domestic system, also called the putting-out system or cottage industry. A merchant, sometimes called a clothier, bought raw wool or cotton and put it out to families in the surrounding villages. The children and women carded the fibre, combing it straight, and spun it into thread on a spinning wheel. The men usually wove the thread into cloth on a handloom in the cottage. The merchant collected the finished cloth, paid the family by the piece, and took it to be finished and sold. Families often combined this work with farming their land.

The domestic system had real advantages for workers. The family worked together at home and set their own hours, so they could stop to tend the crops at harvest. Children learned skills from their parents. Families were not supervised by an overseer. But it had disadvantages too. Hours could be very long when merchants demanded more. Pay was low and uncertain, and depended on the merchant, who held the power because he owned the raw material and controlled sales. Cottages were cramped, dark and damp, with the loom filling one room. And production was slow: a single weaver needed the yarn of several spinners, so spinning was always the bottleneck.

In the towns, skilled craftsmen such as blacksmiths, shoemakers, tailors and carpenters worked in small workshops with a few apprentices, and many trades were regulated by guilds that controlled training and prices.

The shortage of yarn is the key to what happened next. Because weavers were always waiting for thread, any machine that could spin faster would be immensely profitable. That is why the first great inventions of the Industrial Revolution, the spinning jenny and the water frame, attacked spinning first, and why the factory system began in the cotton industry.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the domestic system worked, its advantages and disadvantages, and why spinning was the bottleneck.

## Subtopic: Life and Trade Before Industry and the Error Museum

The pre-industrial economy ran on limited sources of energy: the muscles of people and animals, water wheels on fast rivers, windmills, and wood for fuel. Coal was mined and used in some places, but without steam engines its use was limited. This shortage of power set a ceiling on how much could be produced.

Transport was slow and costly. Roads were mostly unpaved, rutted and muddy in winter. Goods travelled by packhorse, by wagon or, where possible, by river and along the coast. Moving heavy goods such as coal far overland could double or treble their price. Turnpike roads, maintained by tolls, and the first canals in the 1760s began to improve this, but in 1750 a journey from London to Manchester still took several days.

Population was small by later standards: England and Wales had roughly six million people around 1750, most of them rural. Towns were small, apart from London, which had several hundred thousand people and was Britain's great centre of trade and government. Most people produced much of their own food, and markets and fairs in country towns linked villages to wider trade.

Society was arranged in a clear order of rank. At the top stood the aristocracy and the gentry, families who owned most of the land, lived on rents and dominated Parliament and local government. Below them came merchants, lawyers, clergy and prosperous farmers, a middle rank whose wealth was growing. The great majority were labourers, servants, small farmers, cottagers and craftsmen. Only a small minority of men could vote, and no women could, because the right to vote depended on owning property. Power and land went together, which is why the landowners in Parliament could pass the enclosure acts that benefited themselves. The Industrial Revolution would challenge this order by creating two large new groups: wealthy factory owners who demanded political influence, and a huge working class who eventually demanded the vote and better conditions.

Britain was nevertheless becoming a trading nation. Its merchants traded across the world, including the Atlantic trade in enslaved people and plantation goods studied earlier, and the East India Company traded with India and Asia, importing cotton cloth that British spinners and weavers could not yet match in quality or price. Overseas trade built up merchant wealth and created demand for manufactured goods.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: thinking every revolution is a sudden political event; the agricultural and industrial revolutions took decades. Two: saying the Haitian Revolution was led by France; it was a revolution by enslaved people against French rule. Three: describing the domestic system as factory work done at home; there were no machines driven by power and no overseers. Four: treating enclosure as good for everyone; it raised food production but took land and common rights from poorer villagers. Five: imagining Britain in 1750 as already industrial; most people still lived and worked on the land.

Layout for marks in a describe-and-explain question: describe the feature, give one example with a date or name, and explain one consequence. For example: under the domestic system, merchants put out wool to families who spun and wove at home; spinning was slow, so the demand for yarn encouraged the invention of spinning machines.

The questions for this section are with you now: energy, transport and population before industry, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: big change in a short time, farming that got smarter but left villagers out, and a spinning wheel by the fire.

## Subtopic: Big Change, Short Time

A revolution is a change so big that life afterwards is completely different from life before.

Some revolutions are about who is in charge. In America, from 1775, the colonists threw out British rule and made a new country. In France, from 1789, the people overthrew their king. In Haiti, from 1791, enslaved people rose up against their French masters, defeated armies from France, Spain and Britain, and in 1804 created their own free country. That had never happened before anywhere.

Other revolutions are about how people live and work, and they can take longer. Farming changed so much in eighteenth-century Britain that we call it the agricultural revolution. Then making things changed so much that we call it the Industrial Revolution. Today, phones and the internet have changed life so much that people talk about a digital revolution.

So a revolution does not always mean fighting in the streets. It means a huge change, and often one kind of revolution helps cause the next.

Hold the big change in mind and try the questions for this part: kinds of revolution, and what happened in Haiti.

## Subtopic: Smarter Farming, Fewer Farmers

Picture a village in England around 1750. Around it lie a few huge fields, cut into long thin strips like a striped blanket. Your family farms a few strips here and there. Every year one field rests and grows nothing. And there is the common, open land where everyone's cow can graze and everyone can collect firewood.

Then farmers got smarter. A machine called the seed drill planted seeds in neat rows. Farmers stopped resting fields and grew turnips and clover in rotation, which fed the soil and fed the animals through winter. They bred bigger sheep and cows.

But those new ideas worked best on big private farms. So the rich landowners fenced off the strips and the common land and made them into their own farms. This was called enclosure. Farms grew more food than ever, and the population grew. But many poor families lost their strips and their common. With no land to farm, many walked to the towns to look for work. Those families would become the workers in the new factories.

Hold the striped blanket and the fences in mind and try this part's questions: what enclosure was, and who lost out.

## Subtopic: The Spinning Wheel by the Fire

Before factories, cloth was made at home. A merchant would bring a bag of wool to a cottage. The children combed it, the mother spun it into thread on a spinning wheel by the fire, and the father wove it into cloth on a big wooden loom. The merchant came back, collected the cloth and paid by the piece. That was the domestic system.

It had good points. The family worked together, at home, at their own pace, and could stop to help with the harvest. It had bad points too. The pay was low, the merchant had all the power, the cottage was crowded and dark, and it was slow. One weaver needed the thread of several spinners, so weavers were always waiting.

Everything else was slow too. Power came from people, horses, water wheels and windmills. Roads were muddy. Goods crawled along on packhorses and wagons. Most people still lived in villages.

That waiting weaver is the key to what comes next. Anyone who could build a machine to spin faster would make a fortune. That is exactly how the Industrial Revolution began.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A revolution is a fundamental change in how things are done. Political revolutions happened in America, France and Haiti. The agricultural revolution brought new farming methods and enclosure, which pushed many villagers towards the towns. Before industry, cloth was made at home under the domestic system, power came from muscles, water and wind, and transport was slow.

Hold the cottage loom and the waiting weaver, and take the final questions of the lesson: revolutions, enclosure, and the domestic system.
