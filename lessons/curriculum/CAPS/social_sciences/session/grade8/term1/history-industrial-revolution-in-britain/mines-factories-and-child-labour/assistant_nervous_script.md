Hey — if this topic feels upsetting, that is understandable. Here is the calm version.

In the early 1800s, many children worked long days in cotton mills and coal mines. In the mills, piecers joined broken threads and scavengers crawled under running machines to sweep up fluff. The days were twelve to fourteen hours, the air was hot and dusty, the machines had no guards, and late or sleepy children were fined or hit.

In the mines, trappers as young as five or six opened and closed doors in the dark, and hurriers pushed tubs of coal through low tunnels. Mines could explode, flood or collapse.

Owners used children because they were cheap and easy to control, and families needed the money.

Reformers like Richard Oastler, Michael Sadler and Lord Shaftesbury collected evidence, and the law changed step by step. 1833: no children under nine in textile mills, and inspectors. 1842: no women, girls or boys under ten underground. 1847: a ten-hour day in the mills.

Today South Africa's Constitution protects children from exploitative work.
