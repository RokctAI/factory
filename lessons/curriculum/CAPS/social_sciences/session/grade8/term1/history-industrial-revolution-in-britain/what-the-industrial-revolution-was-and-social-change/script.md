# Part 1 — Expert

This session defines the Industrial Revolution and surveys the social changes it brought to Britain between about 1750 and 1900. The anchor is Cromford, a village in Derbyshire where Richard Arkwright opened a water-powered cotton spinning mill in 1771, built houses for its workers, and ran it on shifts timed by a bell, a small model of the factory society that would spread across Britain. The governing idea is that the Industrial Revolution was not one invention but a connected set of changes, in machines, power, organisation and transport, each feeding the others, and that these economic changes produced equally deep social changes: in population, where people lived, how society was divided and how people experienced time and family. Defining the Industrial Revolution and explaining its social effects are examinable this term and prepare for the study of industrialisation in South Africa.

## Subtopic: Machines, Power and the Factory System

The Industrial Revolution can be defined as the change from an economy based on farming and handicraft to one based on machine manufacture in factories, powered by water and then steam, with rapid growth in output, trade and towns. Four connected changes define it.

The first was machinery. In the cotton industry, the spinning jenny of the 1760s, the water frame of 1769 and Samuel Crompton's spinning mule of 1779, which combined the best of both, multiplied the output of each spinner many times over. The power loom of 1785 did the same for weaving, though it took until the 1820s and 1830s to replace most handloom weavers.

The second was power. Water wheels drove the first mills, which is why they stood on fast rivers in the hills of Derbyshire and Lancashire. Steam engines, made efficient by James Watt and his partner Matthew Boulton from the 1770s, allowed factories to be built anywhere coal could be delivered, especially in towns. Manchester, which had hardly any mills in 1780, had dozens of steam-powered mills by the 1820s and became known as Cottonopolis.

The third was the factory system. Instead of putting work out to cottages, owners brought workers, machines and power together in one building under supervision. Work was divided into simple repeated tasks, each worker minding a machine or one stage of the process. The owner provided the building, the machines and the raw material, and paid wages by time or by output. The factory system was more efficient, and it allowed owners to control quality, pace and hours. Arkwright's mill at Cromford, opened in 1771, is often called the first modern factory.

The fourth was the spread of these methods across industries. By the early nineteenth century, pottery, as at Josiah Wedgwood's works, engineering, brewing and paper-making were also organised in factories, and engineers were building machines to make other machines.

The result was a rapid rise in output. British imports of raw cotton grew from a few million pounds in weight in the 1760s to hundreds of millions of pounds by the 1830s, and cotton cloth became Britain's leading export.

The questions for this section are with you now: a definition of the Industrial Revolution, the key machines in cotton, and the features of the factory system.

## Subtopic: Coal, Iron, Canals and Railways

Coal was the fuel of the Industrial Revolution. It fired the boilers of steam engines, smelted iron, heated homes and was turned into gas for lighting streets. British coal output rose from roughly five million tonnes a year around 1750 to about fifty million by the middle of the nineteenth century, and continued rising. Coal mines went deeper, using steam engines to pump out water and to haul coal and miners up the shafts.

Iron was the material. Abraham Darby's use of coke instead of charcoal in 1709 freed iron-making from dependence on Britain's shrinking forests, and Henry Cort's puddling process of 1784 made good wrought iron cheaply in large quantities. Iron went into machines, steam engines, tools, bridges, ships and, above all, rails. The Iron Bridge over the River Severn, opened in 1781, became a symbol of the age.

Transport tied it all together. In 1761 the Duke of Bridgewater's canal carried coal from his mines to Manchester and halved the price of coal there. A canal boom followed, and by the 1830s a network of several thousand kilometres of canals linked the industrial districts with ports and with each other. Heavy, bulky goods such as coal, iron, pottery and grain could be moved cheaply and smoothly.

Then came the railways. George Stephenson's locomotives ran on the Stockton and Darlington Railway from 1825, and the Liverpool and Manchester Railway, opened in 1830, was the first to carry both passengers and goods between two cities entirely by steam. A railway mania followed in the 1840s, and by 1850 Britain had about ten thousand kilometres of railway. Railways carried goods faster and more cheaply than canals, created huge demand for iron, coal and labour, allowed fresh food to reach towns, made travel possible for ordinary people, and even forced the country to adopt standard railway time, the beginning of the timekeeping discussed in the Geography lessons on time zones.

Steamships followed on the seas, and by the second half of the century iron and then steel ships driven by steam were carrying goods and people to every part of the empire, including southern Africa.

The questions for this section are with you now: why coal and iron mattered, the contribution of canals, and the effects of the railways.

## Subtopic: Population, Towns and New Classes

The economic changes transformed society. The population of England and Wales grew from about six million in 1750 to about nine million in 1801, eighteen million in 1851 and over thirty-two million in 1901. Better food supplies from the agricultural revolution, earlier marriage, and later some improvements in public health all played a part.

People moved to where the work was. This movement from the countryside to towns is called urbanisation. In 1851, for the first time, more people in Britain lived in towns than in the countryside; Britain was the first country in the world where this happened. Manchester grew from about twenty-five thousand people around 1770 to over three hundred thousand by 1850. Birmingham, Leeds, Liverpool, Glasgow and many smaller towns grew just as fast. The next session studies the conditions in these towns.

Society was reshaped into new classes. The old order was based on land: aristocrats and gentry at the top, labourers at the bottom. Industry created two large new groups. The industrial middle class included factory and mine owners, merchants, bankers, engineers, managers and professionals such as doctors and lawyers. They grew wealthy and confident, believed in hard work and free trade, and wanted political power to match their wealth. The working class included the men, women and children who worked for wages in factories, mines, workshops, docks and railways, and as servants. They owned no property apart from their labour, and depended entirely on wages that were often low and uncertain.

Political power changed slowly. In 1832 the Great Reform Act gave the vote to more middle-class men and gave seats in Parliament to the new industrial towns, which had previously had none. Working men gained the vote only in stages, through further Reform Acts in 1867 and 1884. Women gained the vote only in the twentieth century, a story that returns in Term 4.

Working people did not wait quietly. Between 1838 and 1848 the Chartist movement collected millions of signatures for a People's Charter that demanded the vote for all men, a secret ballot, payment for members of Parliament so that working men could afford to serve, and equal electoral districts. Parliament rejected the Charter three times, but most of its demands became law over the following seventy years. Chartism showed that the new working class could organise on a national scale, a theme taken further in the session on trade unions and labour resistance.

The questions for this section are with you now: population growth, the meaning of urbanisation and the 1851 milestone, and the new middle and working classes.

## Subtopic: Time, Work and Family, and the Error Museum

The factory changed the experience of work itself. Under the domestic system, the family set its own pace. In the factory, the machine and the clock set the pace. Workers had to arrive when the bell rang, often at six in the morning, and work until the evening, commonly twelve to fourteen hours a day, six days a week, with short breaks for meals. Lateness, talking or slowing down were punished with fines deducted from wages, or with dismissal. Historians call this the imposition of time discipline.

Work moved away from the home. Fathers, mothers and children might all work, but now in different places and under different bosses, so the family no longer worked as a unit. Women and children made up a large share of the workforce in early textile mills, because they could be paid less and were thought to be more easily controlled; children were valued for their small hands and their ability to move under machines. Their experience is the subject of a later session.

Living standards are debated by historians. Optimists point out that over the long term, especially after about 1850, real wages rose, cheap cotton clothing, soap, tea and sugar became widely available, and life expectancy eventually improved. Pessimists point to the terrible conditions in the early industrial towns, the long hours, the dangers of factories and mines, child labour, and evidence that in the first decades of industrialisation the health and height of working people actually declined. Many historians conclude that the gains came mostly in the second half of the nineteenth century, and that the first generations of industrial workers paid a heavy price.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: defining the Industrial Revolution as a single invention such as the steam engine. Two: forgetting water power, which drove the first factories. Three: describing the middle class as poor because it was not aristocratic. Four: saying all working men could vote after 1832; the 1832 Act enfranchised mainly middle-class men. Five: claiming either that industry made everyone better off at once or that it made no one better off.

Layout for marks in an essay-style explanation: introduce your argument, give one paragraph each on economic change and social change with dates and examples, weigh benefits and costs, and conclude. For example: the Industrial Revolution transformed Britain's economy through machines, steam, factories and railways, and transformed its society through rapid urbanisation and the creation of new classes, but its benefits reached working people only slowly.

The questions for this section are with you now: time discipline, changes to family work, the standard of living debate, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: From Muscles to Steam

Imagine making a T-shirt from scratch in 1750. Someone spins the cotton into thread on a wheel by hand, one thread at a time. Someone weaves it on a wooden loom, throwing the shuttle by hand. It takes days.

Now jump to 1850. In a huge brick building in Manchester, one person minds a machine with hundreds of spindles spinning at once, while a steam engine in the basement, burning coal, turns long iron shafts that drive every machine on every floor. The cloth pours out. That building is a factory.

Four things changed. Machines did the work hands used to do. Power came from water wheels at first, then steam engines that could go anywhere coal could reach. Workers, machines and power were brought together in one building under a boss: the factory system. And transport got faster: first canals that carried coal smoothly on barges, then railways from 1825 and 1830 that carried everything faster than a horse.

Coal fed the engines. Iron built the machines, the bridges and the rails. Each change pushed the next one along.

Hold the T-shirt that took days and the factory that pours out cloth, and try the questions for this part: the four big changes, and why coal and iron mattered.

## Subtopic: The Great Move to Town

The factories needed workers, and people needed work. Families who had lost their land in the villages packed up and walked to the towns. So many people moved that towns exploded. Manchester went from a small town of about twenty-five thousand people to a city of over three hundred thousand in less than a hundred years. In 1851, for the first time ever anywhere in the world, more people in Britain lived in towns than in the countryside.

The population grew fast too: about six million in England and Wales in 1750, about eighteen million a hundred years later.

Society split in a new way. On one side were the people who owned the factories, mines and banks, the middle class, who grew rich and wanted a say in government. On the other were the people who worked for wages, the working class, who owned nothing but their ability to work.

The rich factory owners got the vote first, in 1832. Working men had to wait until 1867 and 1884, and women longer still.

## Subtopic: The Factory Clock

At home, a weaving family worked at its own pace. In the factory, a bell rang at six in the morning and you had better be there. The machines set the speed. Talk, arrive late or slow down, and money was taken from your wages, or you lost your job. Days were twelve to fourteen hours long, six days a week.

The family no longer worked together. Father might be in a mine, mother in a mill, children in another part of the mill, each with a different boss.

Was life better or worse? Historians argue. In the long run, by the late 1800s, wages rose, and cheap clothes, soap, tea and sugar reached ordinary homes. But the first generations of factory workers had a very hard time, with long hours, danger, child labour and dirty, crowded towns.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Industrial Revolution was the change to machines, water and steam power, factories and faster transport, from about 1750. Coal and iron fed it; canals and railways carried it. Population boomed and people moved to towns, and by 1851 most British people lived in towns. New classes formed: a rich middle class and a large working class. The factory clock changed work and family life, and the benefits reached workers only slowly.

Hold the factory bell, the packed road to town and the rising cloth, and take the final questions of the lesson: definitions, social change and the living standards debate.
