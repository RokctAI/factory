# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines working conditions in Britain's factories and mines during the Industrial Revolution, with a focus on the children whose labour was central to both. The anchor is the evidence given to two great parliamentary inquiries: the Sadler Committee on factory children in 1832, and the Children's Employment Commission on mines in 1842, whose report included drawings of children dragging coal tubs through tunnels too low for an adult to stand. The governing idea is that child labour was not new, since children had always worked on farms and in cottages, but that the factory and the mine changed its scale, its pace and its dangers, and that it took evidence, campaigning and law to limit it. Analysing these sources and explaining the reforms are examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Work in the Mills

The textile mills set the pattern of factory work. A typical working day in the 1820s and 1830s ran from about six in the morning to seven or eight at night, twelve to fourteen hours, six days a week, with short breaks for meals that were sometimes eaten while the machines kept running. At busy times, hours could be longer still.

Mills were hot and humid, because cotton thread breaks less in warm damp air, and the air was filled with cotton dust and fluff that workers breathed all day, damaging their lungs. The noise of hundreds of machines was deafening. Floors were slippery with oil. Above all, the machines were unguarded: belts, shafts, gears and spindles moved constantly, and a loose sleeve, a strand of hair or a moment of tiredness could cause terrible injuries, the loss of fingers or hands, or death.

Discipline was strict. Overseers walked the floors enforcing the rules. Workers were fined for arriving late, for talking, for leaving a window open, for whistling or for spoiling work, and fines could take a large share of a week's wage. Children who fell behind or fell asleep were often beaten, sometimes with a leather strap.

Women and children made up a large part of the early mill workforce. In some cotton mills in the 1830s, children and young people under eighteen were around a third or more of all workers. Owners employed them because their wages were much lower than men's, because they were thought easier to control, and because their small size suited certain jobs. Some children were pauper apprentices, sent from workhouses to mills, where they lived in apprentice houses and worked in return for food and lodging until they were adults.

Children did two jobs especially. Piecers walked up and down the spinning machines, leaning over to join the threads that broke, many times an hour; one estimate suggested a piecer might walk many kilometres in a day. Scavengers crawled under the moving machines to sweep up the cotton waste, while the machines kept working above them.

The questions for this section are with you now: hours, conditions and discipline in the mills, and the jobs of piecers and scavengers.

Accidents were part of daily mill life. Machines were not fenced, so loose hair, sleeves and aprons could be caught in moving belts and shafts. Children who crawled under running machines to gather loose cotton, the work of the scavengers, risked crushed fingers and worse. Injured workers often received no compensation and could simply lose their place. Long hours made accidents more likely, because tired children lose concentration late in a shift. Reformers used accident records, alongside the testimony of doctors, to argue that the length of the working day was not only a question of fairness but a question of physical safety. Later laws required dangerous machinery to be fenced, which shows how evidence slowly turned into rules. When the 1833 Act created paid inspectors, only four were appointed for the whole country, among them Leonard Horner, whose reports became valuable evidence for historians. Four inspectors for thousands of mills meant that many owners broke the law without being caught.

## Subtopic: Work in the Mines

Coal mines were even more dangerous. As demand for coal rose, mines went deeper, and work underground was dark, cramped, wet and hot or bitterly cold.

The dangers were many. Firedamp, the gas methane, collected in the workings and could explode when it met a naked flame, killing dozens or hundreds of men and boys at once. Chokedamp, a mixture of gases with too little oxygen, could suffocate miners after an explosion. Roofs collapsed. Water flooded the workings. Cages and ropes in the shafts failed. Miners' lungs filled with coal dust. The Davy safety lamp, invented in 1815, reduced the risk of igniting firedamp, but explosions continued, partly because mines were pushed deeper and into more dangerous seams.

Children worked underground from a very young age. Trappers, sometimes only five or six years old, sat alone in the dark for twelve hours or more, opening and closing wooden trapdoors to let coal tubs pass while keeping the air flowing through the mine. Hurriers pushed or pulled tubs of coal along tunnels from the coal face to the shaft. Where tunnels were low, they crawled on hands and knees, sometimes harnessed to the tub with a belt and a chain passing between their legs. In parts of Scotland, women and girls called bearers carried heavy baskets of coal on their backs up long ladders to the surface.

In 1838 a sudden storm flooded part of the Huskar Colliery near Barnsley in Yorkshire, drowning twenty-six children, the youngest seven years old. The disaster shocked the public and helped lead to the Children's Employment Commission, which investigated conditions in mines. Its report of 1842 was illustrated with drawings of children and women at work underground, and the pictures caused outrage, partly because of the dangers and partly because many middle-class readers were shocked to see women and girls working half-clothed alongside men.

The questions for this section are with you now: the dangers of coal mining, the work of trappers, hurriers and bearers, and the Huskar disaster.

## Subtopic: The Campaign and the Laws

Many people defended child labour. Families depended on children's wages to survive, and some parents sent children to work willingly or of necessity. Owners argued that shorter hours would make their mills unprofitable and their goods uncompetitive, that work kept children out of mischief, and that government had no right to interfere between employers and workers. Some even argued that factory children were healthier than children in the countryside.

Reformers disagreed. In 1830 Richard Oastler, a Yorkshire land agent, wrote a famous letter comparing the treatment of factory children to slavery, at a time when the campaign against slavery in the colonies was at its height. Michael Sadler, a member of Parliament, chaired a committee in 1832 that took evidence from factory workers about beatings, deformities and exhaustion. Lord Ashley, later the Earl of Shaftesbury, led the campaign in Parliament for decades. Factory workers themselves organised short-time committees demanding a ten-hour day.

The laws came step by step. The Factory Act of 1833 applied to textile mills. It banned the employment of children under nine; limited children aged nine to thirteen to nine hours a day; limited young people aged thirteen to eighteen to twelve hours a day; required two hours of schooling a day for children under thirteen; and, crucially, appointed four factory inspectors to enforce it. Four inspectors for thousands of mills was far too few, but enforcement had begun.

The Mines Act of 1842 banned all women and girls, and boys under ten, from working underground, and appointed an inspector of mines. The Factory Act of 1844 required dangerous machinery to be fenced and reduced the hours of children under thirteen. The Ten Hours Act of 1847 limited women and young people in textile mills to ten hours a day, which in practice often reduced men's hours too, because the mills could not run without them.

Later laws extended protection to other industries and trades, including the climbing boys forced up chimneys by chimney sweeps, finally protected by an act of 1875. The Education Act of 1870 provided elementary schools across the country, and from 1880 schooling was compulsory up to the age of ten, which drew children out of full-time work.

The questions for this section are with you now: arguments for and against child labour, the reformers, and the main acts of 1833, 1842, 1844 and 1847.

## Subtopic: Evidence, Legacy and the Error Museum

Historians know so much about child labour because Parliament collected evidence. The reports of the Sadler Committee and the Children's Employment Commission contain the words of children, parents, overseers, doctors and owners. These are powerful primary sources, but they must be read critically. Sadler's committee was set up to support a reform bill, and its witnesses were chosen to show the worst conditions; owners later complained that it was one-sided. The mines commission's drawings were vivid but sometimes dramatised. Mill owners' own evidence, on the other hand, tended to play down the problems. Comparing sources from different sides and checking them against statistics, such as accident and death records and the factory inspectors' reports, gives the most reliable picture.

Child labour did not end in the nineteenth century, and it has not ended worldwide today. The International Labour Organization estimates that around one hundred and sixty million children are in child labour across the world, many in agriculture, mining and domestic work. In South Africa, the Constitution of 1996 gives every child the right to be protected from exploitative labour practices and from work that is inappropriate for their age or that puts their well-being, education, health or development at risk. The Basic Conditions of Employment Act forbids the employment of children under fifteen. These rights descend in part from the struggles of the nineteenth century.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying that child labour began with the Industrial Revolution; children had always worked, but industry changed the scale and danger. Two: believing the 1833 Factory Act covered all industries; it covered textile mills only. Three: saying the Mines Act banned all children from mines; it banned boys under ten underground and all females underground. Four: treating Sadler's evidence as completely neutral. Five: assuming that laws immediately ended abuses; enforcement was weak for years.

Layout for marks in a paragraph on reform: a topic sentence stating the change, two or three laws with dates and their main provisions, one point about enforcement or opposition, and a concluding sentence on significance. For example: between 1833 and 1847 Parliament gradually limited child labour; the 1833 Factory Act banned children under nine from textile mills and appointed inspectors, the 1842 Mines Act removed women and young boys from underground work, and the 1847 Act set a ten-hour day, though enforcement remained weak.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading inquiry evidence critically, child labour today and children's rights in South Africa, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: a day in the mill, a day down the pit, and how the law changed.

## Subtopic: A Day in the Mill

It is half past five in the morning in 1830, and an eight-year-old girl is walking through the dark to the cotton mill. The bell rings at six. If she is late, money comes off her wages.

Inside it is hot and wet and so loud you have to shout. White fluff floats in the air and gets into her nose and throat. Her job is to be a piecer: she walks up and down beside the great spinning machine, and every time a thread snaps she leans in and twists it together, quick, before the machine moves on. Her little brother is a scavenger. He crawls under the machines while they are still running to sweep up the fluff. If he lifts his head at the wrong moment, the machine can hurt him badly.

The machines have no guards. Workers lose fingers, and some lose their lives. If a child falls asleep, the overseer may hit them with a strap. She works until seven or eight at night, six days a week.

Why children? Because they were paid much less than adults, they did as they were told, and they were small enough to fit under the machines. And their families needed every penny.

Hold the snapping threads and the moving machines in mind and try the questions for this part: the jobs children did, and why owners used them.

## Subtopic: Down the Pit

Now go underground. A six-year-old boy sits alone in a pitch-black tunnel for twelve hours. His job is to be a trapper: when he hears a coal tub coming, he pulls open a wooden door, then shuts it again so the fresh air keeps flowing through the mine. That is all, all day, in the dark.

Older children are hurriers. They push and pull heavy tubs of coal through tunnels too low to stand up in, crawling on their hands and knees, sometimes with a chain fixed to a belt round their waist. In Scotland, women and girls carried baskets of coal on their backs up tall ladders.

And the mine could kill. Gas could explode. Roofs could fall. Water could flood in. In 1838 a storm flooded a Yorkshire mine called Huskar and twenty-six children drowned, the youngest only seven.

When a government report in 1842 printed drawings of children dragging coal underground, people across Britain were shocked.

Hold the trapper in the dark in mind and try this part's questions: trappers, hurriers and the dangers of the mine.

## Subtopic: How the Law Changed

Some people said children's wages kept families alive, and that the government should not interfere. Others fought hard to change things: Richard Oastler, who compared factory children to slaves, Michael Sadler, who collected children's stories in Parliament, and Lord Shaftesbury, who campaigned for years. Workers organised too.

Bit by bit, the law changed. In 1833, no children under nine in textile mills, shorter hours for older children, some schooling, and the first inspectors to check. In 1842, no women or girls underground, and no boys under ten. In 1844, dangerous machines had to be fenced. In 1847, a ten-hour day for women and young people in the mills. From 1880, schooling was compulsory to age ten.

Today South Africa's Constitution protects every child from exploitative work, and the law forbids employing children under fifteen. That protection grew from struggles like these.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Mills had long hours, hot dusty air, unguarded machines and harsh discipline; children worked as piecers and scavengers. Mines had explosions, floods and collapses; children worked as trappers and hurriers. Reformers used evidence to win laws: the Factory Act of 1833, the Mines Act of 1842, the Factory Act of 1844 and the Ten Hours Act of 1847. Evidence from inquiries must be read critically.

Hold the piecer, the trapper and the steps of the law, and take the final questions of the lesson: jobs, dangers and reforms.
