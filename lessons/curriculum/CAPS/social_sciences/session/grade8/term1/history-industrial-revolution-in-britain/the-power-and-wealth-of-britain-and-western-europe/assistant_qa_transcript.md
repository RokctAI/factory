### subtopic_1 — The Workshop of the World
**Assistant:** Why did Britain industrialise before richer and bigger countries such as China or France?

**Tutor:** Historians still debate this, and the best answer combines several factors rather than choosing just one. A few centuries ago, parts of China, India and Western Europe had similar levels of wealth, and China in particular had great cities, advanced crafts and a huge market. So why was Britain first? One important factor is coal. Britain had large coal deposits that were easy to reach and close to iron ore, water transport and growing cities, so energy was cheap. China's main coal deposits were far from its most developed regions. A second factor is wages and prices. British wages were relatively high and coal was cheap, so it paid British businesses to invent machines that replaced expensive labour with cheap energy. A third factor is institutions: Parliament had limited the power of the monarch, property and contracts were protected by law, patents rewarded inventors, and banks and joint-stock companies helped raise money. A fourth is the scientific and practical culture of the time, with societies where engineers, scientists and businessmen met and shared ideas. A fifth is trade and empire. Britain controlled large overseas markets, and profits from the Atlantic economy, including the slave trade and sugar plantations, added to its wealth, while colonies supplied raw materials such as cotton. France faced revolution and long wars between 1789 and 1815, which disrupted its economy, and much of its coal was harder to use. Germany was divided into many states until 1871. In an essay, you would explain several of these factors, show how they worked together, and acknowledge that historians weigh them differently. That is a stronger answer than claiming one single cause.

### subtopic_2 — Trade, Money and the Navy
**Assistant:** Why did Britain support free trade when other countries used tariffs to protect their industries?

**Tutor:** Because free trade suited the country that was already ahead. A tariff is a tax on imported goods, which makes foreign goods more expensive and protects home producers from competition. In the early nineteenth century, Britain itself used tariffs, most famously the Corn Laws, which taxed imported grain to keep prices high for British landowners. Manufacturers, organised in the Anti-Corn Law League led by Richard Cobden and John Bright, argued that cheap imported food would let workers live on lower wages, would let other countries earn money by selling grain to Britain, and would let those countries buy more British manufactured goods. After the Irish famine made the case for cheap food urgent, Prime Minister Robert Peel repealed the Corn Laws in 1846, and over the next twenty years most other tariffs were removed. For Britain, free trade made sense because its factories produced cheaper and better goods than anyone else; in an open market, British goods would win. For countries that were just starting to industrialise, such as Germany and the United States, the situation was different. Their new industries could not yet compete with Britain's, so they used tariffs to protect them while they grew. Economists call this the infant industry argument. Later in the century, as competition increased, many countries raised tariffs, and some people in Britain began to argue for protection too. The debate between free trade and protection continues today, including in South Africa, which uses tariffs on some imports, such as clothing and certain foods, to protect local industries and jobs. A good answer explains who gained and who lost from each policy.

### subtopic_3 — Western Europe Industrialises
**Assistant:** How did Germany manage to overtake Britain in steel production by 1900?

**Tutor:** Through a combination of rich resources, national unity, new technology, banking and state support. After Germany was united in 1871 under Prussian leadership, it had a large internal market without internal customs barriers, a growing population of more than fifty million by 1890, and rich coal in the Ruhr and Silesia. It also took the iron ore of Lorraine from France after the war of 1870 to 1871. Germany industrialised later than Britain, which, oddly, gave it some advantages. Its new steelworks used the latest processes, such as the Gilchrist-Thomas process of 1879, which allowed the phosphorus-rich iron ores of Lorraine to be made into good steel. British firms, with older plants that still made profits, were slower to invest in new methods. German banks worked closely with industry, lending large sums for long periods and helping firms grow into giant companies such as Krupp and Thyssen. Germany also invested heavily in technical schools and universities and in research laboratories, which gave it a lead in the chemical and electrical industries. The government protected industry with tariffs from 1879 and built railways that served industrial needs. By around 1900 Germany produced more steel than Britain, and by 1914 it produced roughly twice as much. This shift mattered politically. German steel built a navy that challenged Britain's, and the naval race between the two countries was one of the tensions that led to the First World War. In history, then, economic change and international rivalry are closely linked, as you will see in Term 4.

### subtopic_4 — The Cost to the World and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How was South Africa connected to Britain's industrial wealth in the nineteenth century?

**Tutor:** In several ways, both before and after the discovery of minerals. First, the Cape was strategically important. Britain took the Cape from the Dutch in 1795 and again, permanently, in 1806, mainly to protect the sea route to India, which was the most valuable part of the empire. Second, the colonies of the Cape and Natal supplied raw materials to British industry. Wool from merino sheep became the Cape's leading export by the middle of the century, feeding the woollen mills of Yorkshire. Natal grew sugar from the 1850s, and from 1860 its planters brought indentured labourers from India because they could not get the labour they wanted locally. Third, South Africa became a market for British manufactured goods, from cloth and tools to guns and, later, railway equipment. Fourth, and most dramatically, the discovery of diamonds near Kimberley in 1867 and 1871 and gold on the Witwatersrand in 1886 drew in huge amounts of British capital, engineers and machinery. Deep-level gold mining needed expensive equipment, and companies raised money on the London stock exchange. Profits and dividends flowed back to investors in Britain and Europe. The mines also needed large numbers of workers, and the mine owners and colonial governments created systems of migrant labour, pass laws and compounds that kept black workers' wages low and controlled their movement. Fifth, British desire to control the gold fields contributed to the South African War of 1899 to 1902. So the story of the Industrial Revolution is not only British history; it is also part of South African history, and you will see these links again in the sessions on diamonds and gold.
