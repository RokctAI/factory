### subtopic_1 — Work in the Mills
**Assistant:** Why did some parents send their young children to work in such dangerous places?

**Tutor:** Because for many working families, children's wages were the difference between getting by and going hungry, and because work by children was a normal part of life in the society they came from. Wages for unskilled adult workers in the early nineteenth century were low and irregular, and families were often large. A father's wage might not cover rent, food and fuel, especially when he was ill, injured or laid off in a slump. Every shilling a child brought home mattered. The alternative might be the workhouse, which, after 1834, meant splitting the family up. Child labour was also familiar. In the countryside, children had always worked: scaring birds from crops, minding animals, helping with the harvest, spinning and carding wool in the cottage. Parents who had worked from a young age themselves did not always see factory work as unusual, though the factory's pace, hours and dangers were new. In some trades, parents worked alongside their children and even hired them as their own assistants; adult spinners often employed and supervised piecers who were their own children or relatives. There was little alternative for children's time: before 1870 there was no national system of elementary schools, and schools that existed often charged fees. Some parents did resist and campaigned for shorter hours, joining short-time committees. So the historian's answer is not that parents did not care, but that poverty and a lack of choices pushed them. That is why the reformers' solution included schools and limits on hours, not only bans, and why reducing child labour went together with rising adult wages later in the century.

### subtopic_2 — Work in the Mines
**Assistant:** Why did the mines report's drawings cause more outrage than earlier written evidence?

**Tutor:** Because pictures reached people's feelings and imaginations in a way that columns of testimony did not, and because they showed things that many readers found shocking for more than one reason. The Children's Employment Commission reported in 1842 with drawings made from evidence gathered underground. They showed small children harnessed to coal tubs with chains, crawling through tunnels no higher than a table, trappers sitting alone in darkness, and women and girls carrying heavy baskets up ladders. Most middle-class and upper-class Britons had never been inside a mine. The drawings made a hidden world visible overnight, and they were reproduced in newspapers and pamphlets. Part of the outrage was about cruelty and danger to children. But historians also point out that much of the shock came from Victorian ideas about women and respectability. Readers were appalled to see women and girls working in the dark beside men, sometimes stripped to the waist in the heat, which offended their beliefs about how women should behave and where they belonged. That explains why the Mines Act of 1842 banned all women and girls from underground work, while it allowed boys from the age of ten to continue. It also explains why many women who lost their jobs, and whose families lost their wages, did not welcome the law. Some continued to work in secret for a time. For source analysis, the drawings are a good example: they are powerful evidence of conditions, but they were made for a purpose, they were selected, and their impact depended on the values of the people who looked at them. A strong answer mentions both what they show and how they were used.

### subtopic_3 — The Campaign and the Laws
**Assistant:** If the 1833 Factory Act had only four inspectors, did it actually achieve anything?

**Tutor:** Yes, though less than its supporters hoped, and its greatest achievement was a principle rather than an immediate change. Four inspectors for several thousand textile mills spread across England, Scotland, Wales and Ireland meant that any particular mill might go years without a visit. Owners could and did break the law, and early fines were small. There was also a practical problem: the law depended on knowing children's ages, and before the compulsory registration of births began in 1837, many children had no birth certificate, so an eight-year-old could be passed off as nine. Some owners also ran children in relays, which made hours hard to check. But the act did several important things. It established that Parliament could and would limit the hours of work in private businesses, overturning the idea that the state must leave employers and workers alone. It created a professional inspectorate, which grew over time, and the inspectors wrote detailed reports that gave Parliament evidence for further laws. Inspectors such as Leonard Horner became powerful voices for reform. The act's schooling requirement, though poorly enforced at first, began the link between limiting child labour and educating children. And it set a model that was extended to mines in 1842, to machinery safety in 1844, to the ten-hour day in 1847, and gradually to other industries. Historians describe the 1833 act as the foundation of factory regulation in Britain. In an essay, a balanced judgement would say that its immediate impact was limited by weak enforcement, but its long-term significance was great because it established state responsibility for working conditions.

### subtopic_4 — Evidence, Legacy and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Does child labour still happen in the world today?

**Tutor:** Yes, on a large scale, though it has fallen considerably over recent decades. The International Labour Organization, the United Nations body for work, estimated in its report of 2021 that about one hundred and sixty million children worldwide were in child labour, roughly one in ten of the world's children, and that about half of them were in hazardous work that endangers their health, safety or development. Most child labour today is not in factories like Manchester's mills but in agriculture, on family farms and plantations, and in domestic work, small workshops, street selling and mining, including small-scale mines that dig for gold, cobalt and other minerals. The causes are recognisable from the nineteenth century: poverty, families who depend on children's earnings, the lack of affordable schools, and employers who profit from cheap, compliant workers. The solutions are recognisable too: laws setting minimum ages and limiting hazardous work, inspectors to enforce them, free and compulsory schooling, and rising adult wages that remove the need for children to earn. South Africa has strong protections. The Constitution of 1996, in the section on children's rights, protects every child from exploitative labour practices and from work that is inappropriate for their age or that puts their education, health or development at risk. The Basic Conditions of Employment Act makes it a crime to employ a child under fifteen, and limits the work of older children. Some children in South Africa still work, especially on farms and in households, and organisations monitor this. Studying the Industrial Revolution helps us recognise child labour where it still exists and understand why the rights we have today were hard won.
