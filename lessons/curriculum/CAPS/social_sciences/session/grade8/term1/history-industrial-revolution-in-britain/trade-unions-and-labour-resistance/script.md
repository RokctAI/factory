# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines how workers in industrial Britain organised to resist exploitation, and how governments and employers responded. The anchor is the story of the Tolpuddle Martyrs, six labourers punished in 1834 for forming a union, whose release after a national protest campaign became a symbol of the right to organise. The governing idea is that workers had little power on their own, because any single worker could be dismissed and replaced, but that together, through unions, strikes, petitions and political movements, they gradually won legal rights, better conditions and a voice in government. The process was slow and often violent, and it was resisted at every step. Explaining the forms of resistance and their results is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Early Resistance: Luddites and Peterloo

Early industrial workers faced a hard situation. Wages were set by employers, hours were long, and when trade was bad, workers were laid off with no support except the parish or the workhouse. Skilled hand workers, such as hand-loom weavers and framework knitters, saw their earnings collapse as machines and factories produced goods more cheaply. A hand-loom weaver who earned a good living in the 1790s might earn only a fraction of that by the 1830s.

The first widely known resistance came from the Luddites, between 1811 and 1816. They were skilled textile workers in Nottinghamshire, Yorkshire and Lancashire who broke into workshops at night and smashed knitting frames and new machines, especially those used to make cheap, poor-quality goods or to replace skilled workers. They took their name from a mythical leader called Ned Ludd or General Ludd, in whose name they sent threatening letters to employers. Today the word Luddite is used to mean someone who simply hates new technology, but historians point out that this is unfair to them. The Luddites were not against all machines; they objected to machines being used to cut wages, employ unskilled workers and destroy their trades, at a time of high food prices caused by war with France.

The government responded with force. In 1812 Parliament made machine-breaking a crime punishable by death, and thousands of soldiers were sent to the disturbed districts, more, at one point, than were fighting in Spain against Napoleon. In 1813, after a mass trial at York, more than a dozen men were hanged and others were transported to Australia. Transportation meant being shipped to a penal colony on the other side of the world, usually never to return.

Working people also campaigned peacefully for political change, because they had no vote. In the early nineteenth century, only a small minority of men could vote, and big new industrial towns such as Manchester had no member of Parliament of their own. On the sixteenth of August 1819, a crowd of around sixty thousand people gathered peacefully at St Peter's Field in Manchester to hear the speaker Henry Hunt call for the reform of Parliament. Many were dressed in their best clothes and came with their families. The local magistrates ordered the yeomanry, a mounted volunteer force, to arrest Hunt; the horsemen rode into the dense crowd with sabres drawn, and regular cavalry followed. Around eighteen people were killed, including women and a child, and hundreds were injured. Newspapers called it the Peterloo Massacre, a bitter joke comparing it with the Battle of Waterloo four years earlier. The government did not punish the magistrates; instead it passed the Six Acts, which restricted public meetings and radical newspapers.

A historian evaluating Peterloo uses sources from both sides. Eyewitnesses and radical newspapers described an unprovoked attack on unarmed people. The magistrates and government claimed they feared a riot or revolution. Comparing the sources, most historians conclude that the crowd was peaceful and the response was a grave overreaction. Peterloo became a lasting symbol of the struggle for democratic rights.

It is worth asking why the authorities were so frightened. The French Revolution had begun in 1789, and its violence, including the execution of the king, terrified Britain's landowners and governments for a generation. Any large gathering of working people calling for change looked to them like the first step towards revolution. This fear explains much of the harshness of the period: the Combination Acts, the hanging of Luddites, the cavalry at Peterloo and the Six Acts. Understanding this context does not excuse the violence, but it helps explain it, which is the historian's task.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Luddites and their aims, the government response, and the Peterloo Massacre.

## Subtopic: Building Unions: From Illegal to Legal

A trade union is an organisation of workers who join together to bargain with employers over wages, hours and conditions, and to support members who are sick, injured or out of work. Its power comes from unity: one worker who refuses to work can be replaced, but if all the workers in a trade refuse together, which is called a strike, the employer must negotiate.

Small craft societies of skilled workers had existed for a long time. But in 1799 and 1800, Parliament passed the Combination Acts, which made it illegal for workers to combine, meaning to join together, to demand higher wages or shorter hours. The acts were passed in the shadow of the French Revolution, when the government feared that any organisation of workers could lead to revolution. Unions survived in secret or disguised as friendly societies, which were clubs that collected small weekly payments to support members in sickness and to pay for funerals.

After a campaign led by Francis Place, a London tailor, the Combination Acts were repealed in 1824. A wave of strikes followed, and in 1825 a new law allowed unions to exist and bargain over wages and hours, but made many of their methods, such as intimidation and picketing, risky under the law. Unions were now legal, but weak.

In 1834, the reformer Robert Owen helped form the Grand National Consolidated Trades Union, which aimed to unite workers of all trades in one great union. It claimed hundreds of thousands of supporters, but it collapsed within months, weakened by employers who locked out union members and forced workers to sign a pledge, called the document, promising not to join a union.

The same year came the case of the Tolpuddle Martyrs. In the village of Tolpuddle in Dorset, farm labourers led by George Loveless, a Methodist preacher, formed a friendly society to resist cuts in their wages, which had fallen to seven shillings a week. Joining a union was not itself illegal, so the authorities used an old law against swearing secret oaths, because new members had sworn an oath of loyalty. Six men were convicted in March 1834 and sentenced to transportation to Australia for seven years. The punishment caused national outrage. A huge demonstration marched through London, and a petition of around eight hundred thousand signatures was presented to Parliament. In 1836 the men were pardoned, and they eventually returned to England. The Tolpuddle Martyrs became heroes of the trade union movement.

Before a union could be strong, workers had to overcome real obstacles. Many feared losing their jobs, since an employer could dismiss anyone known to be a union member and blacklist them so that no other employer would hire them. Workers in different trades often saw themselves as separate, and skilled workers sometimes looked down on unskilled labourers. Wages were too low for many workers to afford regular union contributions. Unions in this period were therefore strongest among skilled craftsmen, such as engineers, carpenters and printers, who were hard to replace.

Employers had powerful weapons of their own. A lockout was the employer's version of a strike: the owner closed the works and refused to let anyone work until they gave up their union. Blacklists circulated the names of activists. The document forced workers to choose between their union and their wages. Magistrates, who were often employers or landowners themselves, could be expected to side with masters. Against all this, a union's strike fund, built up from members' weekly pennies, was what allowed strikers' families to eat; when the fund ran out, strikes usually collapsed. This is why unions that lasted were the ones that built strong funds and careful organisation.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a trade union is, the Combination Acts and their repeal, and the Tolpuddle Martyrs.

## Subtopic: Chartism: Workers Demand the Vote

The Reform Act of 1832 gave new industrial towns members of Parliament and extended the vote to more middle-class men, but most working men still could not vote, because the right depended on owning or renting property of a certain value. Many workers felt betrayed, since they had campaigned for reform. In 1834 the New Poor Law made poor relief harsher, sending the poor into workhouses where families were separated. Workers concluded that only a Parliament elected by working men would pass laws in their interests.

In 1838 the London Working Men's Association, with William Lovett as a leader, drew up the People's Charter, which gave the movement its name: Chartism. The Charter had six points. First, a vote for every man over twenty-one. Second, a secret ballot, so that voters could not be bullied by landlords or employers. Third, no property qualification for members of Parliament, so that working men could stand. Fourth, payment of members of Parliament, so that working men could afford to serve. Fifth, equal constituencies, each with about the same number of voters. Sixth, annual elections for Parliament, every year.

Chartism became the first mass working-class political movement in the world. It held huge outdoor meetings, often by torchlight, published newspapers such as the Northern Star, edited by Feargus O'Connor, and organised petitions to Parliament. The first petition in 1839 carried about one and a quarter million signatures. The second, in 1842, carried more than three million. Parliament rejected both by large majorities.

Chartists disagreed about methods. Moral force Chartists such as Lovett believed in persuasion, education and peaceful petitions. Physical force Chartists argued that the government would only give way to the threat of force. In November 1839 several thousand men marched on the town of Newport in Wales; soldiers fired on them, and around twenty were killed. The leaders were sentenced to death, a sentence changed to transportation. In 1842, during an economic slump, a wave of strikes spread across the industrial north, linked to Chartist demands; some strikers removed the plugs from factory boilers to stop production, so these are called the Plug Plot riots.

The last great Chartist effort came in 1848, a year of revolutions across Europe. A mass meeting was held on Kennington Common in London, and a third petition was delivered. The organisers claimed nearly six million signatures, but clerks found far fewer genuine ones, and many were fake, which brought ridicule. Chartism faded after 1848. Yet within seventy years five of the six points became law: the secret ballot in 1872, the end of the property qualification in 1858, payment of members in 1911, and votes for all men in stages through 1867, 1884 and 1918, with more equal constituencies over the same period. Only annual parliaments never came. Historians therefore describe Chartism as a failure in the short term but a success in the long term.

The Chartists also left a model of organisation. Local branches met weekly, collected subscriptions, ran reading rooms and Sunday schools, and sent delegates to national conventions. Women took part too, forming female Chartist associations, collecting signatures and speaking at meetings, though the Charter itself asked only for votes for men. These habits of meeting, voting, keeping minutes and electing leaders trained a generation of working people in democracy, and many former Chartists later became trade union leaders, local councillors and campaigners for co-operative shops. A movement can fail in its main aim and still change a society.

The questions for this section are with you now: the six points of the Charter, moral and physical force, and why Chartism failed in the short term and succeeded in the long term.

## Subtopic: New Unions, Legacy and the Error Museum

From the 1850s, skilled workers built strong, careful unions, known as new model unions. The best known was the Amalgamated Society of Engineers, founded in 1851. Members paid high weekly subscriptions, which funded benefits for sickness, accidents, unemployment and funerals, and the unions preferred negotiation to strikes. In 1868 the Trades Union Congress, the TUC, was founded in Manchester to bring unions together. The Trade Union Act of 1871 gave unions full legal recognition and protected their funds, and in 1875 peaceful picketing was made legal.

Unskilled workers organised later, in the new unionism of the late 1880s. In 1888 the matchgirls at the Bryant and May factory in London went on strike. The young women worked long hours for low pay, were fined for small offences, and handled white phosphorus, which caused a horrible bone disease of the jaw called phossy jaw. Encouraged by the journalist Annie Besant, who wrote about their conditions, about fourteen hundred workers struck and won better terms within weeks. In 1889 the London dockers struck for a wage of sixpence an hour, called the docker's tanner. Supported by donations, including a large sum from Australian workers, they won after five weeks. These victories showed that even unskilled, low-paid workers could organise successfully. In 1900 unions helped found the Labour Representation Committee, which became the Labour Party in 1906, so that working people could elect their own members of Parliament.

The right to organise travelled to South Africa, though in a society divided by race. In 1919 Clements Kadalie founded the Industrial and Commercial Workers' Union, the ICU, which at its height in the 1920s had tens of thousands of black members. In 1946 about seventy thousand black mineworkers struck for higher wages and were driven back to work by police. The Durban strikes of 1973 revived black trade unions, and in 1985 the Congress of South African Trade Unions, COSATU, was formed and played a major role in the struggle against apartheid. Today section 23 of the Constitution gives every worker the right to form and join a trade union and to strike.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the Luddites hated all machines; they opposed machines used to cut wages and destroy skilled trades. Two: saying the Tolpuddle Martyrs were punished for joining a union; legally they were convicted of swearing secret oaths. Three: listing votes for women as a point of the Charter; the Charter asked for votes for men. Four: calling Chartism a complete failure; five of its six points later became law. Five: confusing the Combination Acts, which banned unions, with their repeal in 1824.

Layout for marks in a paragraph on workers' resistance: a topic sentence naming the form of resistance, the date and the people involved, what they wanted, how the authorities responded, and a final sentence on the result or significance. For example: in 1834 six Dorset labourers were transported for swearing a union oath; a national campaign with a petition of about eight hundred thousand signatures won their pardon in 1836, and the Tolpuddle Martyrs became a lasting symbol of the right to organise.

The questions for this section are with you now: new model unions and the TUC, the matchgirls and dockers, unions in South Africa, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: machine breakers and a massacre, the long fight for unions, and the Chartists and the great strikes.

## Subtopic: Machine Breakers and a Massacre

Picture a skilled weaver in 1812. For years he has earned a decent living with his hands. Now factory machines make cloth so cheaply that his pay has crashed, and bread costs more than ever because of the war with France. He has no vote and no union. What can he do?

Some workers went out at night with hammers and smashed the new machines. They called themselves Luddites, after a made-up leader, General Ludd. They were not against every machine. They were against machines being used to take their jobs and cut their wages. The government sent thousands of soldiers and made machine-breaking punishable by death. Some Luddites were hanged.

Other people tried peaceful protest. In August 1819 about sixty thousand people, many in their Sunday best with their children, gathered in a field in Manchester to ask for the vote. Horsemen with swords charged into the crowd. About eighteen people died and hundreds were hurt. People called it Peterloo.

The people in power were afraid of a revolution like the one in France, so they hit hard. But the anger of Peterloo was never forgotten.

Hold the hammer and the field in Manchester in mind and try the questions for this part: the Luddites and Peterloo.

## Subtopic: The Fight for Unions

A union is simple: workers stand together. One worker who complains can be sacked. But if every worker stops work together, that is a strike, and the boss has to listen.

At first unions were against the law. From 1799 the Combination Acts banned workers from joining together for better pay. In 1824 those laws were scrapped, and unions became legal, though still weak. Bosses fought back. They locked workers out and made them sign a promise never to join a union.

Then came Tolpuddle, a small village in Dorset. In 1834 a group of farm workers, earning just seven shillings a week, formed a union to stop their pay being cut again. They had sworn an oath when they joined, and a judge used an old law against secret oaths to send six of them to Australia for seven years. People across Britain were furious. Around eight hundred thousand signed a petition. Two years later the men were pardoned and came home. They became heroes.

Hold the six men of Tolpuddle in mind and try this part's questions: what a union is, and how unions became legal.

## Subtopic: Chartists and Great Strikes

Most working men still had no vote, so in 1838 they wrote a list of six demands called the People's Charter. A vote for every man over twenty-one. A secret ballot. No need to own property to become a member of Parliament. Pay for members of Parliament. Equal-sized voting areas. An election every year.

Millions signed huge petitions: over a million in 1839 and over three million in 1842. Parliament said no each time. Some Chartists wanted to stay peaceful; others were ready to fight, and at Newport in Wales soldiers shot marchers dead. Chartism faded after 1848, but later five of its six demands became law.

Later in the century, even poorly paid workers won strikes. The matchgirls in 1888, poisoned by phosphorus at work, walked out and won. The London dockers in 1889 won sixpence an hour. Unions went on to start the Labour Party.

In South Africa, workers built unions too: the ICU in 1919, the big mineworkers' strike in 1946, and COSATU in 1985. Today the Constitution protects every worker's right to join a union and to strike.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Luddites smashed machines that cut wages; the authorities answered with soldiers and the gallows. Peterloo in 1819 killed about eighteen peaceful protesters. The Combination Acts banned unions until 1824. The Tolpuddle Martyrs were transported in 1834 and pardoned in 1836. The Chartists' six points failed at first but mostly became law later. The matchgirls and dockers showed that unskilled workers could win, and South Africa's Constitution now protects the right to organise.

Hold the hammer, the oath and the six points, and take the final questions of the lesson: resistance, unions and reform.
