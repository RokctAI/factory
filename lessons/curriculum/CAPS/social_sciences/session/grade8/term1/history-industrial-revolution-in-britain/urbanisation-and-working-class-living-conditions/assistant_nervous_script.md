Industrial towns grew much faster than anyone planned. Families needed homes near the mills, so builders put up cheap back-to-back houses, sharing walls on three sides, with windows on one side only. Families crowded into one or two rooms, and the poorest lived in damp cellars.

One toilet and one tap served many families, and the water often came from rivers full of sewage. So diseases spread: cholera and typhoid through dirty water, typhus through lice, tuberculosis through coughs in damp rooms.

Edwin Chadwick's report in 1842 showed the poor died much younger than the rich. John Snow's map in 1854 showed that cholera came from a dirty water pump. Clean water and sewers came slowly, and became compulsory in 1875.

If a family fell into poverty after 1834, help came through the workhouse. It was made deliberately harsh: families separated, uniforms, boring hard work. People feared it.
