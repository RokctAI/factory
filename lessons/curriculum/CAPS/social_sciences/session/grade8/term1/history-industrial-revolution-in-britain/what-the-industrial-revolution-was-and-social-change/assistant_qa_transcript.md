### subtopic_1 — Machines, Power and the Factory System
**Assistant:** Why is the steam engine not enough on its own to explain the Industrial Revolution?

**Tutor:** Because the steam engine only became important when it was combined with other changes, and several key developments happened before it was widely used. The first factories, including Arkwright's mill at Cromford in 1771, ran on water power. The great cotton spinning inventions of the 1760s and 1770s were driven by hand or by water wheels. So industrial production had already begun to grow before steam took over. Steam made the factory system mobile and much larger: mills no longer had to sit beside fast streams in the hills and could be built in towns where coal, workers and markets were close together, which is why Manchester grew so dramatically. But steam engines themselves depended on other changes. They needed cheap iron, which depended on coke smelting and puddling. They needed coal, which depended on deeper mines, which in turn depended on steam pumps, a circle of cause and effect. They needed engineers and precise machine tools to build them, and capital to buy them. Their fuel had to be moved cheaply, which depended on canals and later railways, which themselves ran on steam. And they were worth installing only because there was a huge demand for the goods they helped produce, at home and in overseas markets. Historians therefore describe the Industrial Revolution as a system of connected changes that reinforced each other. When an examination asks for the causes or features of the Industrial Revolution, the strongest answers show these connections, for example how coal, iron, steam and railways each made the others more useful, rather than naming the steam engine as a single cause.

### subtopic_2 — Coal, Iron, Canals and Railways
**Assistant:** Why did railways change ordinary people's lives so much?

**Tutor:** Because they changed the speed, cost and reach of almost everything people did. Before railways, the fastest land travel was a horse-drawn coach at perhaps fifteen kilometres an hour on good roads, and most people walked. A journey of three hundred kilometres took days and was too expensive for working families. By the 1840s trains travelled at fifty kilometres an hour or more, and the 1844 Railway Act required companies to run at least one cheap train a day on each line, so ordinary people could travel. Day trips to the seaside became possible for factory workers, and seaside resorts grew. Railways carried fresh milk, meat, fish and vegetables into the cities quickly, improving diets. They carried coal cheaply to homes, so more families could heat their houses. Newspapers and letters travelled overnight across the country, and the penny post of 1840 depended on fast trains. Railways also changed time itself. Each town had kept its own local time by the sun, which made timetables chaotic, so the railway companies adopted a single standard time based on Greenwich, and by 1880 it became the legal time for the whole of Great Britain. This is the same problem and the same solution studied in Geography this term. Building the railways employed huge numbers of labourers, called navvies, often in dangerous conditions, and created demand for iron, coal and engineering. Railways also spread beyond Britain: by the 1860s they were being built in the Cape and Natal, and later they carried diamonds, gold, workers and troops across southern Africa. Few inventions touched as many lives so quickly.

### subtopic_3 — Population, Towns and New Classes
**Assistant:** How did the new middle class differ from the old landowning class?

**Tutor:** They differed in where their wealth came from, what they valued and what they wanted from government. The old landowning class, the aristocracy and gentry, drew their wealth from land and rents, often inherited over generations. Their status came from birth and family, and they dominated Parliament, the army, the church and local government. They tended to favour policies that protected farming, such as the Corn Laws, which from 1815 kept the price of imported grain high and so protected landowners' rents. The industrial middle class drew its wealth from business: factories, mines, trade, banking, engineering and the professions. Many were self-made, or the children of craftsmen and small merchants, and they valued hard work, thrift, respectability, education and individual effort, ideas often linked with religious groups such as the Methodists and Quakers. They wanted government to stay out of business, to allow free trade so that their goods could be sold abroad and food imported cheaply, which would keep wages down and workers fed. Above all, they wanted political power to match their wealth. The Great Reform Act of 1832 gave them much of it. In 1846 their campaign succeeded in repealing the Corn Laws, a landmark victory of industrial interests over landed ones. Over time the two classes mixed: rich industrialists bought country estates, and landowners invested in railways and mines. For working people, the middle class could be both opponent and ally: many factory owners resisted limits on working hours, while other middle-class reformers campaigned against child labour and for public health. History essays reward recognising that variety within a class.

### subtopic_4 — Time, Work and Family, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** If wages eventually rose, why do historians still describe the early industrial period as so harsh?

**Tutor:** Because averages over a century hide what particular groups of people experienced at particular times, and the evidence shows that the first generations bore very high costs. Historians who study real wages, meaning wages measured against what they could buy, find that for working people in Britain they rose only slowly, if at all, between about 1780 and the 1830s, and rose clearly only after about 1850. During that early period, the population was growing fast, many families had lost land and common rights, and handloom weavers, once well paid, saw their earnings collapse as power looms replaced them. Hours in factories were long, often fourteen hours a day, and children as young as six or seven worked. Other evidence points the same way. Studies of the heights of army recruits suggest that people born in industrial towns in the early nineteenth century were on average shorter than those born earlier, a sign of poor nutrition and disease in childhood. Life expectancy in the worst industrial towns, such as Manchester and Liverpool in the 1840s, was far below that of rural areas, partly because of overcrowding, polluted water and diseases such as cholera and typhoid. So when historians say that industrialisation eventually raised living standards, they also say that for decades many people lived worse lives than their parents. The harshness was not inevitable; it reflected choices about wages, housing, working hours and public health, which is why the reforms and the labour movements studied later this term mattered so much. In an essay, give both the long-term gains and the short-term costs, with evidence and dates.
