### subtopic_1 — Doing History: Sources and Assessment
**Assistant:** How can a source be biased and still be useful to a historian?

**Tutor:** Because a historian is not only asking what happened but also what people believed, wanted and argued about, and a biased source is excellent evidence for that. Take two sources about a cotton mill in the 1830s. One is a pamphlet written by a mill owner arguing that factory children are well fed, healthy and grateful for their work. The other is the evidence given by a former child worker to a parliamentary committee, describing fourteen-hour days and beatings. The owner's pamphlet is clearly biased: he had money and reputation at stake, and he was writing to persuade Parliament not to limit working hours. As evidence of what life was really like for the children, it is weak. But as evidence of how mill owners defended themselves, what arguments they used and what they wanted the public to believe, it is very strong. The worker's testimony may also be shaped by its setting, since witnesses were sometimes chosen to make a case for reform, but it is evidence of experiences that the owner had every reason to hide. A careful historian reads both, notes who wrote each and why, looks for points where they agree, which are more likely to be true, and checks them against other kinds of evidence, such as medical reports, wage records or the factory inspectors' reports that began after 1833. In a source-based question, the marks for usefulness and reliability come from exactly this reasoning: name the author, the date and the purpose, say what the source is useful for, and say what it cannot tell us.

### subtopic_2 — Wealth from the Slave Trade
**Assistant:** Why do historians disagree about how much the slave trade paid for industrialisation?

**Tutor:** Because the question requires measuring something very hard to measure, and because the answer depends on what is counted. The disagreement began in earnest with Eric Williams, a historian from Trinidad who later became his country's first prime minister. In his book Capitalism and Slavery, published in 1944, he argued that the profits of the slave trade and the Caribbean plantations provided much of the capital that financed Britain's industrialisation, and that Britain abolished the slave trade and slavery once they had become less profitable, rather than mainly for moral reasons. Later historians tested his claims with numbers. Some calculated the profits of slaving voyages and found them to be lower than once believed, perhaps around ten percent on average, and compared them with Britain's total investment, concluding that they were only a modest share. Others answered that this counts too narrowly. They point out that the whole Atlantic economy depended on enslaved labour: the cotton that fed Manchester's mills came mainly from slave plantations in the United States, the sugar trade supported shipping, banking, insurance and refining, and the colonies were markets for British manufactured goods. Recent research tracing the wealth of slave owners, using the compensation records of 1834, when Britain paid about twenty million pounds to former slave owners and nothing to the people who had been enslaved, has shown how widely that wealth spread through British society. So historians argue about the size of the contribution, but the evidence that slavery was woven into Britain's economy is strong. Weighing such a debate, and using evidence on both sides, is exactly what a good History essay does.

### subtopic_3 — What the Industrial Revolution Was and Why Britain
**Assistant:** If Britain had all these advantages, why did the Industrial Revolution not happen earlier?

**Tutor:** Because the advantages had to come together, and several of them only matured in the eighteenth century. Coal had been mined in Britain for centuries, but it became essential only when wood for fuel grew scarce and when new methods allowed coal, turned into coke, to be used for smelting iron, which Abraham Darby achieved early in the eighteenth century. Steam engines existed from about 1712, but Thomas Newcomen's engines wasted so much fuel that they were practical only at coal mines, where fuel was cheap. James Watt's separate condenser, patented in 1769, and his later engines that could turn wheels made steam useful for factories, and that took until the 1770s and 1780s. The agricultural changes that fed more people with fewer farm workers, such as new crop rotations and enclosure of land, built up over the century. Canals were dug in large numbers from the 1760s, making it cheap to move heavy coal. Overseas trade, including the Atlantic trade in enslaved people and plantation goods, reached its peak in the eighteenth century, supplying capital and raw cotton. And the demand for cheap cotton cloth, at home and abroad, gave inventors a powerful reason to mechanise spinning and weaving. Each of these developments made the others more useful. Historians call this a combination of factors reaching a turning point, and it is a good model for any question about causes: not one cause, and not a list of causes in isolation, but causes that worked together at a particular time. A strong essay explains how the causes connected.

### subtopic_4 — The Industrial Revolution Reaches Southern Africa
**Assistant:** Did southern Africa become industrial in the same way as Britain?

**Tutor:** No. Southern Africa industrialised later, faster and in a very different setting, and the differences shaped South Africa's history. In Britain, industrialisation grew over about a century from textiles, and its workforce was mostly British people moving from the countryside to towns. They suffered terrible conditions, which we study in the coming lessons, but they were citizens who could, over time, organise trade unions and win the vote. In southern Africa, industrialisation arrived suddenly, through mining, after the discovery of diamonds in 1867 and gold in 1886, and it arrived under colonial rule. The capital came largely from Britain and Europe, and the profits flowed largely to mine owners and shareholders there. The workforce was overwhelmingly black. Most black workers came as migrants, recruited on contracts, often from rural areas where their land was being taken or reduced, and sometimes from neighbouring territories such as Mozambique. They were housed in closed compounds, controlled by pass laws, paid far less than white workers, and denied political rights. White workers, many of them skilled immigrants, held the better-paid jobs. Indian indentured workers in Natal were bound by five-year contracts. So the same forces, machines, capital and the search for profit, produced a different kind of society: one divided by race in work, wages, housing and rights. This is why historians speak of the mineral revolution in South Africa as a turning point that laid the foundations of later segregation and apartheid. Comparing Britain and southern Africa is one of the main threads running through this term and the next.
