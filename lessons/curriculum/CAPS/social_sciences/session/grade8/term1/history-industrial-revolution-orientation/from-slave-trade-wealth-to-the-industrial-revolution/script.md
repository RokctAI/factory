# Part 1 — Expert

This session opens the History of Grade 8 with the tools of the historian and then frames the term's topic, the Industrial Revolution in Britain and southern Africa from 1860. The anchor is a single bale of cotton in a Manchester mill around 1820: grown on a plantation in the American South by enslaved people of African descent, shipped across the Atlantic, spun and woven by steam-powered machines, and sold as cloth across the world, including in the Cape Colony. The governing idea is that the Industrial Revolution did not happen in Britain alone. It drew on wealth and raw materials from across the world, including wealth connected to the slave trade and slavery, and it reached outwards again in a search for raw materials, markets and labour that transformed southern Africa after 1860. Source skills and the structure of a historical argument are examinable in every History task this year and are the core of History in Grade 10 to 12.

## Subtopic: Doing History: Sources and Assessment

History is the study of the past based on evidence. Historians do not simply collect facts; they ask questions, find evidence, judge how trustworthy it is, and build an explanation that others can check.

The evidence comes from sources. A primary source was created at the time being studied, by people who took part in or witnessed events: a letter, a diary, a government report, a photograph, a newspaper article, a painting, a ship's log, a law. A secondary source was created later, usually by someone who studied primary sources: a history textbook, an article by a historian, a documentary. Both are useful. Primary sources bring us close to the past; secondary sources help explain it.

Every source must be questioned. Who created it, when and why? Was the author in a position to know? Did the author have a reason to exaggerate, leave things out or present one side favourably? This is the question of reliability. A source that favours one point of view is said to be biased, but a biased source can still be useful, because it reveals what its author believed or wanted others to believe. A factory owner's report on working conditions and a child worker's testimony to a government inquiry describe the same mill very differently, and a historian learns from both.

Grade 8 History is assessed through three kinds of task. Source-based questions give one or more sources and ask what they say, what they mean, how they compare, and how useful or reliable they are. A paragraph asks for a short argument: a topic sentence that answers the question, supporting evidence in a logical order, and a concluding sentence. An essay, introduced from the second term, is a longer argument with an introduction that states a clear line of argument, several body paragraphs each developing one point with evidence, and a conclusion. In the first term the formal test has fifty marks, made up of source-based questions and a paragraph.

The questions for this section are with you now: primary and secondary sources, reliability and bias, and the three kinds of History task.

## Subtopic: Wealth from the Slave Trade

In Grade 7 we studied the transatlantic slave trade, and it is the starting point for this term, because the wealth it generated is part of the story of how Britain became the first industrial nation.

From the sixteenth century to the nineteenth, European traders transported enslaved Africans across the Atlantic to work on plantations and in mines in the Americas and the Caribbean. Historians estimate that about twelve and a half million Africans were forced onto ships, and that more than one and a half million died during the crossing known as the Middle Passage, in crowded, disease-ridden holds. Those who survived were sold into lifelong slavery, as were their children.

British ships played a leading part, especially in the eighteenth century. The trade is often described as triangular. Ships left Liverpool, Bristol or London carrying manufactured goods such as cloth, guns, metal goods and alcohol to the coast of West Africa, where they were exchanged for captives. The ships then crossed the Atlantic, and the captives were sold in the Caribbean or the Americas. The ships returned to Britain carrying plantation produce: sugar, tobacco, rum and, later, cotton.

Profits flowed into Britain from several directions: from the voyages themselves, from the plantations owned by British people in the Caribbean, from the banks and insurers that financed and insured the voyages, and from the industries that made the trade goods and processed the plantation crops. Liverpool grew from a small port into a major city on this trade. Some of this wealth was invested in land, banks, canals and new industries.

For Africa the trade was a catastrophe. It removed millions of young men and women in their most productive years, encouraged wars and raids to capture people for sale, and disrupted farming, crafts and political life in large parts of West and West-Central Africa, while the goods received in exchange did little to build lasting wealth. These losses are part of the background to the scramble for Africa studied in the third term.

Historians debate how important this wealth was. In 1944 the Trinidadian historian Eric Williams argued that profits from slavery and the slave trade were central to financing the Industrial Revolution. Other historians have argued that these profits were a smaller share of Britain's total investment. Many now agree that slavery was not the only cause of industrialisation, but that the Atlantic economy built on enslaved labour supplied capital, raw cotton and markets that mattered a great deal, especially for the cotton industry.

Britain abolished its slave trade in 1807 and slavery in most of its colonies through an act of 1833, which took effect in 1834. At the Cape, where enslaved people brought from Indonesia, India, Madagascar and Mozambique had worked since the 1650s, emancipation came on 1 December 1834, followed by a period of so-called apprenticeship that ended in 1838.

The questions for this section are with you now: the triangular trade, the Middle Passage, the ways slavery created wealth in Britain, and the debate about its importance.

## Subtopic: What the Industrial Revolution Was and Why Britain

The Industrial Revolution was the change from making goods by hand at home or in small workshops to making them by machines in factories, powered first by water and then by steam. It began in Britain around the middle of the eighteenth century and spread to Western Europe and North America in the nineteenth century.

The change began in textiles. A series of inventions made spinning and weaving faster: the spinning jenny of James Hargreaves in the 1760s, the water frame of Richard Arkwright in 1769, which led to the first water-powered spinning mills, and the power loom of Edmund Cartwright in 1785. Then came steam. Steam engines had been used to pump water out of mines since early in the eighteenth century, but James Watt's improvements from the 1760s and 1770s made them efficient enough to drive factory machines. Factories no longer needed to stand beside fast rivers; they could be built in towns near coal. Coal fuelled the engines and was used to smelt iron, and cheap iron made more machines, bridges, ships and rails. The first public steam railway, the Stockton and Darlington, opened in 1825, and the Liverpool and Manchester Railway in 1830.

Why Britain first? Historians point to a combination of factors. Britain had large deposits of coal and iron ore, often close together. An agricultural revolution had made farming more productive, so fewer people were needed on the land and more food fed growing towns. Britain had capital to invest, including wealth from overseas trade and slavery. It had colonies and trading networks that supplied raw materials such as cotton and bought finished goods. It had a powerful navy that protected its shipping, a relatively stable government, banks, and a culture that rewarded invention, protected by patents. No single factor was enough; it was the combination that mattered.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of the Industrial Revolution, the key inventions in textiles, steam and transport, and the factors that made Britain first.

## Subtopic: The Industrial Revolution Reaches Southern Africa

Britain's factories needed raw materials, markets and, eventually, minerals, and that need reached southern Africa. Britain had taken the Cape from the Dutch, permanently from 1806, partly to protect its sea route to India. In the 1840s it annexed Natal. By 1860 the Cape exported wool to British textile mills, and Natal was developing sugar plantations.

From 1860 the story accelerates, and that is why this term's topic starts there. In 1860 the first indentured labourers from India arrived in Natal to work on the sugar plantations, a story we study in detail. In 1867 diamonds were found near the Orange River, and by 1871 the great diamond rush at what became Kimberley had drawn tens of thousands of people. Britain annexed the diamond fields in 1871. Mining needed machines, capital and huge numbers of workers, and it brought the methods of industrial capitalism to southern Africa: companies, shares, wage labour and, eventually, monopoly. In 1886 gold was discovered on the Witwatersrand, which is the subject of Term 2.

The changes in southern Africa were not a copy of those in Britain. In Britain, the workers of the industrial towns were mostly British people who had moved from the countryside. In southern Africa, industrialisation took place under colonial rule, and the labour system that developed relied on black workers who were increasingly controlled through migrant labour, contracts, compounds and pass laws, while their land and political independence were being taken. Indian indentured workers, too, were brought under contracts that limited their freedom. Understanding this difference is central to understanding South Africa's twentieth century.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: treating every source as equally reliable. Two: describing the slave trade as a two-way exchange of goods; it was a trade in human beings. Three: saying the slave trade alone caused the Industrial Revolution, or that it played no part; the evidence supports a contribution among several causes. Four: dating the Industrial Revolution to the twentieth century. Five: assuming industrialisation in southern Africa followed the same path as in Britain.

Layout for marks in a paragraph: a topic sentence that answers the question directly, three or four supporting points with evidence, and a concluding sentence. For example: Britain industrialised first because of a combination of factors; it had coal and iron, capital from trade including the slave trade, colonies for raw materials and markets, and inventors whose machines transformed textile production.

The questions for this section are with you now: why southern Africa was drawn into the industrial world, the key dates of 1860, 1867 and 1886, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: history as detective work, following the money, and the long road from Manchester to Kimberley.

## Subtopic: History as Detective Work

A detective arrives after the event. Nobody can rewind time, so the detective collects clues and asks who left them and why. Historians work the same way.

Their clues are called sources. A primary source comes from the time itself: a letter, a photograph, a newspaper, a diary, a law. A secondary source was made later by someone studying the past, like your textbook.

A good detective never believes a witness without asking questions. Who wrote this? Were they there? Did they have a reason to tell only one side? A factory owner might say his workers were happy. A child working in his mill might say she was beaten for falling asleep. Both are clues. The detective learns from both, and from the difference between them.

This year you will show your detective skills in three ways. Answering questions about sources. Writing a paragraph: say your answer in the first sentence, back it up with evidence, finish strongly. And from next term, writing an essay, which is a longer argument built from several paragraphs.

Hold the detective's questions in mind and try the questions for this part: primary and secondary sources, and how to test a witness.

## Subtopic: Following the Money

Last year you studied the slave trade. Now follow the money, because it leads straight into this term.

British ships sailed to West Africa with cloth, guns and metal goods, and traded them for captured African people. The ships carried those people across the Atlantic in terrible conditions, and many died on the way. Survivors were sold into slavery on plantations in the Caribbean and the Americas. Then the ships came home loaded with sugar, tobacco and cotton. Three journeys, one triangle, and money made at every corner.

That money built Liverpool. It filled banks. Some of it went into canals, factories and machines. Historians argue about how much it mattered. Some say it was the key that started British industry. Others say Britain had many other sources of money. Most agree it was one important piece among several, especially for the cotton mills, which ran on cotton grown by enslaved people.

Britain ended its slave trade in 1807 and slavery in its colonies in 1834. At the Cape, enslaved people were freed on 1 December 1834, though they had to keep working for their former owners as apprentices until 1838.

Hold the triangle in mind and try this part's questions: the three journeys, and the argument about the money.

## Subtopic: Machines, Coal and a Long Road South

Around 1750, most cloth in Britain was spun and woven by hand at home. A hundred years later, it was made by machines in giant factories, driven by steam engines burning coal. That change is the Industrial Revolution. New machines spun thread faster than a hundred hands. James Watt's steam engine drove them. Railways carried coal, cotton and people faster than any horse.

Britain went first because it had everything together: coal and iron in the ground, money to invest, colonies to supply cotton and buy cloth, a strong navy, and inventors.

But factories are hungry. They need raw materials and customers, and later, minerals. That hunger reached all the way to southern Africa. Sugar plantations in Natal brought workers from India from 1860. Diamonds were found near Kimberley in 1867. Gold was found on the Witwatersrand in 1886. Each discovery pulled southern Africa deeper into the industrial world, under British rule, and with hard rules for the black workers who did the digging.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Historians use primary and secondary sources and test them for reliability. The slave trade carried millions of Africans across the Atlantic and made wealth in Britain that helped, along with other causes, to fund industry. The Industrial Revolution was the change to machines, steam and factories, starting in Britain around 1750. From 1860, its demand for sugar, diamonds and gold transformed southern Africa.

Hold the detective, the triangle and the hungry factory, and take the final questions of the lesson: sources, the slave trade, and the dates that bring industry to southern Africa.
