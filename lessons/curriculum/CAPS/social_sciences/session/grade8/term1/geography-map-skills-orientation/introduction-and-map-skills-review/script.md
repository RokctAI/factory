# Part 1 — Expert

This session opens Grade 8 Social Sciences and sets the foundation for the term's map work. Social Sciences in Grade 8 is two disciplines, Geography and History, taught side by side every term and assessed separately. The first half of the session lays out what the year covers and how marks are earned. The second half revises the Grade 7 map skills that the rest of the term builds on: reading a local street map, finding a place with a grid reference and an index, using word and line scales, telling small-scale maps from large-scale maps, and calculating real distances. The governing idea is simple and examinable: a map is a reduced, organised picture of the real world, and the scale is the key that lets us translate between the two. These skills return in Grade 10 Geography, where every examination includes a mapwork paper.

## Subtopic: What Grade 8 Social Sciences Covers

Geography studies the Earth's surface, the patterns we see on it and the processes that create them. It asks where things are, why they are there, and how people and environments affect each other. History studies change over time. It asks what happened, why it happened, what the results were, and how we know, using evidence from the past.

The Grade 8 year has a clear shape. In the first term, Geography covers maps and globes: scale, distance, latitude and longitude, the movements of the Earth, time zones and satellite images. History covers the Industrial Revolution in Britain and the beginning of industrial change in southern Africa from 1860, including indentured labour in Natal and the diamond fields of Kimberley. In the second term, Geography studies climate regions, beginning with the factors that make one South African town hotter, colder, wetter or drier than another. History follows the mineral revolution: the gold mines of the Witwatersrand, migrant labour, the defeat of African kingdoms, the Union of South Africa in 1910 and the Natives Land Act of 1913. In the third term, Geography studies settlement, from rural villages to the land use zones of cities and the causes and problems of urbanisation. History studies the scramble for Africa, the Berlin Conference of 1884 and a case study of the Ashanti kingdom in present-day Ghana. In the fourth term, Geography studies transport and trade, including South Africa's harbours, railways and roads and the daily journeys of commuters. History studies the First World War, from its causes to the Treaty of Versailles, including South Africa's part at Delville Wood and the sinking of the SS Mendi.

Each term ends with formal assessment. In Geography, the first term ends with a map skills test of 50 marks. The second term ends with a controlled test of 75 marks covering the first two terms. The third term's Geography task is a project of 50 marks, introduced early in the term and handed in near the end. The year ends with an examination that covers the third and fourth terms. History is assessed alongside: a test of 50 marks in the first term built on source-based questions and a paragraph, a controlled test in the second term that adds an essay, a test in the third term, and the end-of-year examination.

Three answer formats appear again and again. A map skills question asks for a measurement, a calculation or a reading, and the marks go to the working and the unit as well as the answer. A source-based question gives a written extract, a photograph, a cartoon or a table and asks what it shows, what it means and how useful or reliable it is. A paragraph or an essay asks for an argument: a clear opening statement, supporting facts in a logical order, and a conclusion.

The questions for this section are with you now: what Geography and History each study, the themes of the four terms, and the main kinds of assessment.

## Subtopic: Local and Street Maps and Grid References

A map is a drawing of part of the Earth's surface seen from directly above, reduced in size and simplified. Every map leaves things out; what it chooses to show depends on its purpose. A street map of a suburb shows streets, their names, schools, shops, clinics, places of worship, parks and the police station, because its purpose is to help people find their way around a small area.

Most street maps are divided into a grid of squares to make places easy to find. Along the top or bottom edge, the columns are labelled with letters, A, B, C and so on. Down the side, the rows are labelled with numbers, 1, 2, 3 and so on. Each square therefore has a unique name made of one letter and one number, such as C4. The convention is to give the letter first, then the number. To find square C4, move along the columns to C and then down the rows to 4; the square where they meet is C4.

Street maps usually come with an index, an alphabetical list of street names and important places with the grid square where each is found. To find Mandela Avenue, look it up in the index, read its square, perhaps E2, then go to E2 on the map and search only that square. A long street may cross several squares, so the index may give more than one.

Street maps also use symbols, and every symbol is explained in the key, sometimes called the legend. A small cross might show a church, a red H a hospital, a flag a school. Colours carry meaning too: green for parks and sports fields, blue for water, grey or pink for built-up land. The key is the first thing to read on any map, because a symbol without its key is only a shape.

Direction on a street map is given by the compass. Most maps are drawn with north at the top, and a north arrow confirms it. From the four cardinal points, north, east, south and west, come the intermediate points north-east, south-east, south-west and north-west, giving eight directions; sixteen-point directions such as north-north-east add finer detail. To describe the direction of one place from another, imagine standing at the second place and looking towards the first. If the school is above and to the right of the station on a map with north at the top, the school is north-east of the station.

The questions for this section are with you now: how grid squares are named, how an index is used, and how to give the direction of one place from another.

## Subtopic: Word Scales, Line Scales and Small and Large Scale

Every map is smaller than the area it shows, and the scale tells us by how much. Scale is the relationship between a distance on the map and the same distance on the ground. Without it, a map can show where things are but not how far apart they are.

A word scale states the relationship in words, for example one centimetre represents two kilometres. This means that every centimetre measured on the map stands for two kilometres in reality. Word scales are easy to read but they have a weakness: if the map is enlarged or reduced on a photocopier, the word scale stays the same while the map changes size, so it becomes wrong.

A line scale, also called a linear or bar scale, is a ruler drawn on the map. It is divided into equal sections, each labelled with the real distance it represents, often with a zero part way along and one section to the left of zero divided into smaller units for more accurate readings. To use a line scale, measure the map distance with the edge of a sheet of paper or a piece of string, then lay it along the line scale and read off the real distance. The great strength of a line scale is that it is enlarged or reduced with the map, so it always stays correct.

Scales also tell us what kind of map we are looking at. A large-scale map shows a small area in great detail: a street map of a suburb or a plan of a school, where individual buildings and street names can be shown. A small-scale map shows a large area with little detail: a map of South Africa, of Africa or of the whole world, where a city is only a dot. The names seem backwards until we remember that the scale describes how large the features are drawn. On a large-scale map, a house is drawn large enough to see. On a small-scale map, the whole of Johannesburg is drawn small enough to fit on a page beside Cape Town.

One way to hold this: the larger the area shown on a page of the same size, the smaller the scale and the less detail it can carry. A page showing one suburb is large scale; a page showing the whole country is small scale. This term the third kind of scale, the ratio or number scale, will be added to these two.

The questions for this section are with you now: the difference between a word scale and a line scale, why a line scale survives photocopying, and how to tell a large-scale map from a small-scale map.

## Subtopic: Calculating Distances and the Error Museum

Distance on the ground is found in three steps: measure, convert, state. First, measure the distance between the two places on the map in centimetres, as accurately as possible, to one decimal place. Second, convert the map distance to a real distance using the scale. Third, state the answer with its unit, usually kilometres or metres.

Worked example one, a straight line. On a map with the word scale one centimetre represents two kilometres, the school and the clinic are 6,5 cm apart. Each centimetre stands for two kilometres, so the real distance is 6,5 × 2 = 13 km. The school is 13 kilometres from the clinic in a straight line.

Worked example two, a smaller map. On a street map, one centimetre represents 250 metres. The station is 4,8 cm from the library. The real distance is 4,8 × 250 = 1 200 metres, which is 1,2 kilometres, because one kilometre is 1 000 metres.

Worked example three, a curved route. Roads and rivers rarely run straight, so a ruler alone gives the wrong answer. Lay a piece of string or the edge of a sheet of paper along the road, marking each bend, then straighten it and measure. Suppose the road from the farm to the town measures 9,2 cm on a map where one centimetre represents five kilometres. The road distance is 9,2 × 5 = 46 km. A ruler laid straight from farm to town might show only 7 cm, which is 35 kilometres, the distance as the crow flies. Both are correct answers to different questions, so always check whether the question asks for the straight-line distance or the distance along the route.

Worked example four, a line scale. A line scale shows each section of two centimetres representing one kilometre. A measured map distance of 7 cm therefore represents 7 ÷ 2 = 3,5 km. When a line scale is used, the first step is always to work out what one centimetre represents.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: measuring in millimetres and multiplying as if they were centimetres, giving an answer ten times too large. Two: forgetting the unit, so that 13 becomes a number with no meaning. Three: using a straight ruler for a winding road. Four: confusing large scale with large area. Five: trusting the word scale on an enlarged photocopy.

Layout for marks: write the map distance with its unit, write the scale, show the multiplication, and give the real distance with its unit. For example: map distance 6,5 cm; scale 1 cm represents 2 km; real distance 6,5 × 2 = 13 km.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three steps of a distance calculation, a straight-line and a curved-route example, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: the year as a road trip, a map of the walk to school, and the ruler that turns centimetres into kilometres.

## Subtopic: Two Subjects, One Way of Thinking

Think of Social Sciences as two lenses for looking at the same world. Geography is the lens that asks where. Where is the river, where do people live, why is it hot here and cold there? History is the lens that asks when and why. What happened here a hundred years ago, why did it happen, and what did it change?

This year the two lenses take you on a road trip through four stops. In the first term, Geography gives you the map and the globe, the tools for finding anywhere on Earth and knowing what time it is there, while History takes you to the smoky factories of Britain and the diamond diggings at Kimberley. In the second term, Geography explains why Cape Town gets winter rain and Durban gets summer rain, and History goes underground with the gold miners of Johannesburg. In the third term, Geography walks through villages and cities, and History watches European powers carve up Africa. In the fourth term, Geography follows trucks, trains and ships, and History goes to the trenches of the First World War.

At each stop there is a check. In Geography the first one is a map skills test. History asks you to read sources, which are pieces of evidence from the past such as letters, photographs and cartoons, and to write paragraphs and later essays. The good news: these are skills, and skills grow with practice.

Keep the four stops in mind and try the questions for this part: what each subject studies and what comes in each term.

## Subtopic: Your Walk to School as a Map

Picture your walk to school from above, as a bird would see it. Your house, the corner shop, the taxi rank, the soccer field, the school gate. Now draw it, leaving out everything you do not need, and you have made a map.

A street map does the same for a whole suburb. To help you find things, it is cut into squares like a chessboard. Letters run along the top, numbers down the side. Every square has a name: letter first, then number. If the clinic is in C4, go along to C, down to 4, and look only there.

If you only know the street name, use the index at the back. It is like the contacts list on a phone: find the name, read the square, go straight there.

Little pictures on the map are symbols, and the key tells you what each one means. Always read the key first, just like you would check what each button does before using a new remote.

Directions work like a compass. North is at the top. Between north and east is north-east, and so on, all the way round. To say where the school is from the shop, stand at the shop in your mind and look towards the school.

Hold your walk to school in mind and try this part's questions: naming a grid square, using an index, and giving a direction.

## Subtopic: From Ruler to Real Kilometres

A map is the world shrunk down to fit on paper. The scale tells you how much it was shrunk. If the scale says one centimetre represents two kilometres, every centimetre on your ruler stands for two kilometres of real road.

So measure, multiply, and say the unit. School to clinic measures 6,5 centimetres. Each centimetre is two kilometres, so that is 13 kilometres. Done.

Roads bend, though. For a bendy road, lay a piece of string along it, follow every curve, then straighten the string against your ruler. That is how far the taxi actually drives, which is always further than the straight line.

There are two kinds of scale you already know. A word scale says it in words. A line scale is a little ruler printed on the map, and it is clever: if someone enlarges the map on a photocopier, the little ruler grows too, so it stays right. The word scale does not grow, so it becomes wrong.

And there are big-scale and small-scale maps. A map of your suburb shows each house, so it is large scale. A map of the whole world shows a city as a dot, so it is small scale. Big detail, large scale. Big area, small scale.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Geography asks where and why there, History asks when and why then. Find places with grid squares, letter first, and with the index. Read the key first. Distance means measure, multiply by the scale, and give the unit. Use string for curves. A line scale survives photocopying. Large scale means small area and lots of detail.

Hold the walk to school and the string on the bendy road, and take the final questions of the lesson: a distance calculation, a curved route, and large or small scale.
