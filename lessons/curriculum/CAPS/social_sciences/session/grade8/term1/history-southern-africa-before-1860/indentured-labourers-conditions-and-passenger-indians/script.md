# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the living and working conditions of indentured Indian labourers in Natal, the arrival of passenger Indians who came as free traders, and the response of the colonial state and white settlers to both. The anchor is the Wragg Commission of 1885 to 1887, a commission of inquiry that heard evidence from labourers, employers and officials and documented both abuses and improvements. The governing idea is that indenture was a system of controlled labour in which the law favoured employers, that labourers resisted it in many ways, and that once Indians became free residents and traders, settlers who had wanted their labour turned against their presence. Describing conditions and explaining the settler response is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Life and Work on the Sugar Estates

Most indentured labourers were sent to the sugar estates of the Natal coast, from the Tugela River in the north to the Umzimkulu in the south. A typical estate had cane fields, a mill where the cane was crushed and the juice boiled into sugar, and living quarters for the workers.

Labourers usually lived in barracks, long rows of rooms built of wood, iron or brick, sometimes with earth floors. A single room might house a family, or several single men. Sanitation was often poor, with few latrines and limited clean water, which encouraged diseases such as dysentery and typhoid. Hookworm, malaria in some areas, and chest diseases were common. Estates were required to provide medical care, but many had no resident doctor, and hospital facilities were basic. Death rates in the early decades were high, especially among children.

The working day in the fields began at dawn and ended at dusk, often nine to ten hours or more, six days a week. During the crushing season, from about May to December, mill workers worked much longer hours, sometimes through the night in shifts. Field work meant planting, weeding, trenching and, above all, cutting cane with a cane knife, then loading it for transport to the mill. The work was heavy and the coastal heat and humidity made it exhausting.

Discipline was strict. Overseers, often called sirdars when they were themselves Indian, supervised gangs of workers. Employers deducted wages for days missed, even through illness, and some withheld wages or rations as punishment. Although the law forbade it, some employers and overseers beat labourers. Labourers needed a pass to leave the estate, and anyone found without one could be arrested. Leaving without permission was treated as desertion, a criminal offence punishable by fines or imprisonment, and the time spent in jail could be added to the contract.

Rations were provided in place of part of the wage: rice, dhal, ghee or oil, salt, and sometimes dried fish. Many labourers grew vegetables and kept chickens or goats near the barracks to add to their diet. The quality and quantity of rations varied widely from estate to estate, and complaints about poor food were among the most common.

Not every estate was the same. Some employers were known for fair treatment, and some labourers re-indentured with the same employer by choice. Others were notorious for abuse. Conditions also improved somewhat over the decades as a result of complaints, inquiries and pressure from the government of India. A balanced account recognises this variation while making clear that the system as a whole gave employers great power over workers.

Labourers who worked for other employers had different experiences. Those on the Natal Government Railways laid track and worked in yards and stations, often far from towns. Those in the Durban Corporation swept streets and removed refuse. Those on the coal mines of northern Natal, from the 1880s, faced underground dangers. Domestic servants in settler households were isolated and dependent on the goodwill of a single family.

The questions for this section are with you now: housing and health, the working day and discipline, and differences between estates.

## Subtopic: Women, Families and Resistance

Indentured women had particular burdens. Women were indentured for field work, but often at lower wages and with smaller rations than men, and some were not indentured at all but accompanied husbands, which left them dependent. After a day in the fields, women did most of the cooking, fetching water and caring for children. Because there were far fewer women than men, at first about forty for every hundred, women faced pressure, harassment and sometimes violence, and disputes over marriage and relationships were common. Marriages performed according to Hindu or Muslim rites were not at first recognised by colonial law unless they were registered, which caused problems for inheritance and children's legitimacy.

Children of indentured families grew up on the estates. Many began working young, weeding fields or scaring birds. Schooling was very limited; a few mission and community schools taught children of indentured families, but most received little education until the community built its own schools.

Labourers resisted the system in many ways, and historians have studied these forms of resistance. Some resistance was everyday: working slowly, pretending to be ill, doing careless work, or taking unauthorised time off. Some labourers deserted, walking away from estates to look for better work elsewhere or to reach Durban. Others made formal complaints to magistrates and to the Protector of Indian Immigrants, sometimes walking long distances as a group to do so. In some cases, gangs refused to work together, which was a kind of strike. Tragically, some labourers took their own lives; the Wragg Commission noted a high rate of suicide among indentured Indians, which it linked to the hardships of the system.

Labourers also resisted by keeping their culture alive. They built small temples and shrines on the estates, celebrated festivals such as Deepavali and the Muharram processions, and kept their languages, music and food traditions. In doing so they created communities that could support one another.

Complaints reached the government of India, which in 1866 suspended emigration to Natal. Natal responded with new laws and the appointment of a full-time Protector, and emigration resumed in 1874. In 1872 the Natal government appointed a commission to investigate conditions. The larger Wragg Commission of 1885 to 1887 took extensive evidence and recommended reforms, including better medical care and stricter supervision of employers, some of which were adopted.

The questions for this section are with you now: the experiences of women and children, forms of resistance, and the role of the Indian government and commissions of inquiry.

## Subtopic: Passenger Indians and Free Indians

From the 1870s a second stream of Indian immigrants arrived in Natal. They paid their own passages, as ordinary passengers on ships, and were therefore called passenger Indians. Most were traders and their assistants, many of them Muslims and Hindus from Gujarat in western India, some coming via Mauritius. They were not bound by indenture and were free to live and work where they chose.

Passenger Indians opened shops in Durban, especially around Grey Street, and in towns and rural trading posts across Natal, and later moved into the Transvaal and the Cape. They sold goods to indentured and free Indians, to Africans, and to whites, often at lower prices than white traders because they kept costs low and offered credit. Some became wealthy merchants with businesses importing rice, spices and cloth from India. Others worked as clerks, interpreters, teachers and later lawyers.

Meanwhile, the number of free Indians, those who had completed their indentures, grew steadily. Many became market gardeners, renting or buying small plots around Durban and Pietermaritzburg and supplying the towns with vegetables and fruit. Others became fishermen, hawkers, waiters and skilled workers. By the 1890s, people of Indian origin in Natal outnumbered white settlers.

Many white settlers, especially white traders and artisans, saw this as a threat. They had wanted Indian labour for the plantations, but they did not want Indians as competitors in trade and skilled work, or as voters. The Natal government, which gained responsible government in 1893, passed a series of discriminatory laws. In 1895 an annual tax of three pounds was imposed on every ex-indentured Indian who did not re-indenture or return to India, including their children once they reached a certain age; this was a heavy burden, equal to several months' wages for a labourer. In 1896 Indians were effectively removed from the voters' roll. In 1897 the Immigration Restriction Act made new immigrants pass a test in a European language, and the Dealers' Licences Act allowed town officials to refuse trading licences, which was used against Indian traders. In the Transvaal, Law 3 of 1885 had already restricted where Indians could own property and live.

Indians organised in response. In 1893 a young lawyer named Mohandas Gandhi came to Natal to work on a legal case for a passenger Indian firm. That year, he was thrown off a train at Pietermaritzburg station because he refused to leave a first-class compartment reserved for whites. In 1894 he and local merchants founded the Natal Indian Congress to petition against discriminatory laws. Gandhi later developed the method of satyagraha, non-violent resistance, in South Africa. In 1913, Indian workers, including indentured labourers on the sugar estates and coal mines, went on strike and marched in protest against the three-pound tax, and in 1914 the tax was abolished.

The questions for this section are with you now: who the passenger Indians were, the growth of free Indian communities, and the laws passed against Indians in Natal.

## Subtopic: Sources, Legacy and the Error Museum

Historians reconstruct indentured life from several kinds of sources. Ships' lists and the Protector's registers record each labourer's name, number, age, caste, village and employer; today many South African Indian families use these records to trace their ancestors. Commission reports contain the testimony of labourers, employers, doctors and officials. Newspaper reports, court records and petitions add detail. Oral histories collected from descendants preserve family memories. Each source has limits: official records were written by people with power over labourers, and labourers' testimony was often given through interpreters and in front of employers.

Indenture's legacy is visible in South Africa today. The country's Indian community, with its temples, mosques, churches, schools, businesses, languages and food, grew from the labourers and passenger traders of the nineteenth century. Many Indian South Africans took part in the struggle against apartheid, from the passive resistance campaign of 1946 to the formation of the United Democratic Front in 1983. Every year, the sixteenth of November is remembered as the anniversary of the Truro's arrival.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: confusing indentured Indians with passenger Indians; the first were contract labourers, the second paid their own way as traders. Two: saying that Gandhi came to Natal as an indentured labourer; he came as a lawyer for a trading firm. Three: saying that the three-pound tax applied to all Indians; it applied to ex-indentured Indians who stayed without re-indenturing. Four: assuming that all estates had the same conditions; they varied, though the system favoured employers. Five: saying that labourers accepted the system passively; they resisted through complaints, desertion, work refusals and cultural survival.

Layout for marks in a describe question on conditions: organise by category, with housing, work, health, discipline and pay, give a specific detail for each, and finish with a judgement. For example: labourers lived in crowded barracks with poor sanitation; worked dawn to dusk, longer in the crushing season; suffered dysentery and hookworm; needed passes and were punished for desertion; and earned ten to fourteen shillings a month with rations; overall the system gave employers great control.

The questions for this section are with you now: sources on indenture, the legacy of Indian immigration, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: A Day on the Estate

It is before sunrise on a sugar estate on the Natal coast. A man and a woman wake in one small room of a long row of rooms called the barracks. There is one tap for many families and a few toilets that are not clean.

By dawn they are in the cane fields, swinging cane knives in the heat. They work until it gets dark, six days a week. In the crushing season, the mill runs through the night. If they are sick and miss a day, money comes off their pay. If they leave the estate without a pass, they can be arrested and jailed.

The pay is small: ten shillings a month to start. They get rice, dhal, oil and salt, and they grow vegetables beside the barracks. Many people get sick, and many children die young. Some employers were fair. Others were cruel.

## Subtopic: Pushing Back

Labourers did not simply accept this. Some worked slowly or said they were sick. Some ran away. Some walked together to the magistrate to complain. Some groups refused to work. And they kept their culture alive with small temples, festivals, music and food.

Women had it hardest: lower pay, field work, then cooking and children, and danger from men because there were so few women.

Their complaints reached India. In 1866 India stopped sending workers to Natal until things improved, and inquiries looked into the abuses.

## Subtopic: Traders, Taxes and Gandhi

From the 1870s other Indians came who paid for their own tickets. We call them passenger Indians. Most were traders from Gujarat, and they opened shops in Durban and across Natal. Meanwhile, labourers who finished their contracts became farmers, fishermen and workers.

Many white settlers did not like the competition. From 1895, ex-indentured Indians who stayed had to pay a heavy tax of three pounds a year. Indians lost the vote, and new laws made it harder to come in or get a trading licence.

A young lawyer, Mohandas Gandhi, was thrown off a train in Pietermaritzburg in 1893. In 1894 he helped start the Natal Indian Congress. In 1913 thousands of Indian workers went on strike, and in 1914 the tax was scrapped.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Indentured labourers lived in crowded barracks, worked long days in the cane, earned little, needed passes and could be jailed for leaving; women carried extra burdens. They resisted through complaints, desertion and work refusals, and India suspended emigration from 1866 to 1874. Passenger Indians came as traders from the 1870s. Settlers responded with the three-pound tax and other laws, and the Natal Indian Congress, founded in 1894, fought back.
