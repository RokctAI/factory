### subtopic_1 — Life and Work on the Sugar Estates
**Assistant:** If the law said employers had to provide medical care, why were death rates so high?

**Tutor:** Because the law set a standard that was often not met, and because the conditions that caused illness were built into the system. The regulations required employers to provide medical attention and, in larger groups, hospital facilities, and the colonial government appointed medical officers to visit estates. But estates were spread out, medical officers were few, and many estates had no resident doctor. Sick labourers sometimes had to walk long distances to be seen, and some employers regarded illness with suspicion, treating it as an excuse to avoid work and deducting wages for days missed. That made labourers reluctant to report sick until they were very ill. The root causes of disease lay in living conditions. Crowded barracks spread infections such as tuberculosis and measles. Poor sanitation and contaminated water spread dysentery and typhoid. Hookworm, a parasite that enters through bare feet, was common where latrines were inadequate. Long hours of heavy work in heat and humidity, combined with rations that were sometimes poor in quality or quantity, weakened people's resistance. Newly arrived labourers were also exposed to diseases unfamiliar to them, and some areas had malaria. Infants and young children were especially vulnerable, because mothers returned to field work soon after giving birth and clean water and nutritious food were scarce. Over time, under pressure from the government of India and after inquiries such as the Wragg Commission, conditions improved somewhat: more hospitals were built, medical inspection increased and sanitation was addressed on some estates. A historian would point out that the gap between the regulations and real conditions is a common feature of systems in which the people affected have little power to enforce their rights.

### subtopic_2 — Women, Families and Resistance
**Assistant:** Why did the government of India have so much influence over conditions in Natal?

**Tutor:** Because it controlled the supply of labour that Natal's planters depended on. Indentured emigration from India was not a private arrangement between recruiters and planters. It was regulated by the government of India, which, though itself British, had its own officials and interests. It licensed recruiters, set rules for the depots, ships and contracts, and decided which colonies could receive emigrants. It could stop emigration to any colony whose treatment of Indians was considered unacceptable. Officials in India had several motives. Some genuinely wished to protect Indian subjects. Others were concerned about the reputation of British rule, especially after the uprising of 1857, and about criticism from educated Indians and from humanitarian groups in Britain who compared indenture to slavery. When complaints came from Natal, the government of India acted. It suspended emigration in 1866, and planters, facing a labour shortage, pressed the Natal government to improve the system so that it would be resumed, which happened in 1874. Later, the government of India's concerns grew to include the treatment of free Indians in Natal, such as the three-pound tax and trading restrictions. Indian political leaders in India, including Gopal Krishna Gokhale, who visited South Africa in 1912, raised these issues. Finally, in 1911, the government of India stopped indentured emigration to Natal altogether. This case shows that a colony's labour policies could be influenced by decisions made in another part of the empire, and that the people affected used every available channel, including petitions to India, to improve their situation.

### subtopic_3 — Passenger Indians and Free Indians
**Assistant:** Why did white settlers in Natal turn against Indians when they had asked for Indian labour?

**Tutor:** Because they wanted workers, not neighbours, competitors or voters, and that contradiction sat at the heart of Natal's policy. Sugar planters, who were influential, wanted a steady supply of indentured labourers whose movements and wages could be controlled. But when labourers finished their contracts and stayed, they became free residents with the right to live, work and trade. Many became market gardeners and supplied Durban's markets cheaply. Passenger Indian traders opened stores across the colony and often undersold white shopkeepers, partly because they kept their costs low and extended credit to customers. White traders, artisans and small farmers saw this as unfair competition and campaigned against it. There was also racial prejudice. Many settlers believed in white superiority and wanted Natal to be a white man's country politically, even though whites were a minority. When Natal received responsible government in 1893, which meant settlers controlled the colony's government, they moved quickly. Indians had been able to qualify for the vote under the same property rules as whites, and settlers feared Indian voters might one day outnumber them, so the franchise was changed in 1896 to exclude them. The three-pound tax of 1895 aimed to push ex-indentured Indians either to re-indenture or to leave. The Immigration Restriction Act and the Dealers' Licences Act of 1897 restricted new arrivals and trade. Planters, interestingly, often opposed measures that might stop the labour supply, showing that settlers were not united. For historians, this episode shows how economic interests and racial ideas combined to shape discriminatory laws, a pattern that recurs throughout South African history.

### subtopic_4 — Sources, Legacy and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How did Gandhi's experience in South Africa shape his later struggle in India?

**Tutor:** In South Africa Gandhi developed the ideas and methods that he later used to lead India's independence movement, but it is important to see his time in South Africa in its full context. He arrived in Natal in 1893 as a young, shy lawyer to work on a case for the trading firm of Dada Abdulla. The humiliation of being removed from a first-class train compartment at Pietermaritzburg made him determined to fight racial discrimination. He stayed for most of the next twenty-one years. In 1894 he helped found the Natal Indian Congress, which wrote petitions, organised meetings and published a newspaper, Indian Opinion, from 1903. In the Transvaal, from 1906, he led resistance to a law requiring Indians to register and carry passes. Here he developed satyagraha, which means truth-force: deliberate, non-violent breaking of unjust laws and acceptance of punishment, to appeal to the conscience of opponents and the public. Thousands of Indians, including women, burned registration certificates, crossed provincial borders without permits and went to jail. In 1913 the campaign broadened to include indentured and ex-indentured workers striking on sugar estates and coal mines against the three-pound tax, and a march from Natal into the Transvaal. The Indian Relief Act of 1914 abolished the tax. Gandhi left for India in 1914 and used these methods on a much larger scale. Historians also note limits: in his early years in South Africa, Gandhi focused on Indian rights and sometimes expressed prejudiced views about Africans, which he later moved away from. His legacy in South Africa includes the tradition of non-violent mass resistance, which influenced later campaigns, including the Defiance Campaign of 1952.
