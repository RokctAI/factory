### subtopic_1 — India as a British Colony
**Assistant:** Was indenture just another name for slavery?

**Tutor:** No, but it shared some of slavery's harsh features, and that is why historians have argued about it for decades. Start with the differences. A slave was owned as property for life, and the children of enslaved people were born into slavery. An indentured labourer signed a contract, in theory voluntarily, for a fixed period, usually five years in Natal. They were paid a wage, although a very low one, and given rations and housing. The law gave them certain rights, such as medical care and the right to complain to the Protector of Indian Immigrants, and at the end of the contract they were free to leave their employer, to re-indenture, to take another job, or to return to India. Their children were not automatically bound. Now the similarities. Many recruits did not fully understand what they were signing, and some were deceived. Once in Natal, a labourer could not leave the estate without a pass, and one who left without permission could be arrested and imprisoned as a deserter. Absence from work, even through illness, could lead to wage deductions and fines. Some employers used physical punishment, and complaints to magistrates often failed because magistrates sympathised with employers. Housing and medical care were frequently inadequate. The historian Hugh Tinker wrote about the whole Indian indenture system around the world under the title A New System of Slavery to stress these features. Other historians point to the many labourers who completed their contracts, stayed, and built prosperous lives. A balanced answer says that indenture was legally different from slavery and offered a path to freedom, but in practice it was coercive and often brutal, and it was designed to give employers control over workers.

### subtopic_2 — Sugar and the Labour Problem in Natal
**Assistant:** Why did people in India agree to travel so far to work in a place they had never seen?

**Tutor:** Because for many of them, conditions at home were desperate, and because recruiters made Natal sound like an opportunity. In the second half of the nineteenth century, large parts of rural India were very poor. Many families farmed tiny plots or worked as labourers for landlords, and high land revenue demands and moneylenders' interest pushed many into debt. Droughts led to famines, and the great famine of 1876 to 1878 in southern India killed millions of people, especially in the Madras region, which is one reason why so many recruits for Natal came from there. Artisans such as weavers had lost work to factory-made British goods. Some people faced pressures within their communities: young people in conflict with their families, widows with few prospects, or people of low caste looking for escape from discrimination. Recruiters, paid for each person they signed up, promised good wages, light work and a chance to save money and return home wealthy. Some told the truth; others exaggerated or lied about the distance and the nature of the work, and a few tricked or pressured vulnerable people. Officials tried to check that emigrants understood the contract, but this was not always effective. It is also important to recognise that many emigrants made rational decisions. They weighed the risks of staying in poverty against the risks of a long journey and hard work, and decided to go. Some had relatives or neighbours who had gone before. In this sense, they were not simply victims; they were people making difficult choices within limited options. Historians try to recover both sides of this picture from emigration registers, ships' lists, letters and later oral histories.

### subtopic_3 — Recruitment, the Contract and the Voyage
**Assistant:** What was the Protector of Indian Immigrants supposed to do, and did it work?

**Tutor:** The Protector was a colonial official appointed to safeguard the rights of indentured labourers, and the answer to whether it worked is: only partly. The Indian government agreed to allow emigration to Natal on condition that labourers would be protected, so Natal created the office of Protector of Indian Immigrants. The Protector registered new arrivals, kept records of each labourer's employer and contract, inspected estates, heard complaints of ill-treatment, unpaid wages or poor rations, and could take employers to court or, in serious cases, remove labourers from an abusive employer. He also reported to the government on conditions, deaths and births. In practice, the system had serious weaknesses. The Protector's office was small and the estates were many and spread out. Labourers who wanted to complain had to leave the estate, which without a pass could get them arrested as deserters, so complaining was risky. Many labourers did not speak English, and interpreters were not always available or trustworthy. Some Protectors were sympathetic to the planters, who were powerful in Natal's politics. Commissions of inquiry, such as the one in 1872 and the Wragg Commission of 1885 to 1887, investigated conditions after complaints, including complaints from the Indian government, and recommended improvements, some of which were adopted. Records from the Protector's office are now valuable historical sources, because they contain labourers' own complaints in their words, translated. A historian would conclude that the Protector gave labourers some protection that slaves never had, but that the system was weighted in favour of employers, and that real improvements often came only when the Indian government threatened to stop emigration.

### subtopic_4 — Interpreting Indenture and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why did so many indentured Indians stay in Natal rather than going home?

**Tutor:** For a mixture of practical, economic and personal reasons. By the time a labourer had served five years of indenture and lived another five years in Natal, they had been away from India for ten years or more. Many had married in Natal and had children born there, for whom India was a distant, unfamiliar place. Some had lost contact with family at home, or knew that they would return to the same poverty and debt they had left. Others feared the long sea journey. Some were concerned that crossing the sea had affected their caste status, which might make it hard to be accepted back in their villages. At the same time, Natal offered opportunities. Ex-indentured Indians, often called free Indians, became market gardeners on the edges of Durban and Pietermaritzburg, supplying the towns with vegetables and fruit. Others became fishermen, hawkers, small shopkeepers, waiters, clerks, interpreters, railway workers and skilled tradesmen. They built temples, mosques, churches and schools, and established community organisations. By around 1900, people of Indian origin in Natal outnumbered the white population. Not everyone stayed: a significant minority did take the free passage home, and some who returned came back to Natal again. White settlers, who had wanted Indian labour but not Indian residents or competitors, responded with hostility, and the Natal government introduced measures to push ex-indentured Indians to leave or re-indenture, such as an annual tax of three pounds from 1895. That conflict is the subject of the next session. The decision of so many to stay is the origin of South Africa's Indian community today, which is part of the nation's history and identity.
