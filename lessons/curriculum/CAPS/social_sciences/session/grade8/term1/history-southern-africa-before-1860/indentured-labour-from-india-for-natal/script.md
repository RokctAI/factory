# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains why, from 1860, the British colony of Natal brought labourers from India under contracts of indenture to work on its sugar plantations. The anchor is the arrival of the ship Truro in Durban on the sixteenth of November 1860, followed ten days later by the Belvedere from Calcutta, the first of more than three hundred and eighty voyages that brought about one hundred and fifty-two thousand indentured Indians to Natal by 1911. The governing idea is that indenture connected three histories studied this term: the British Empire in India, the growth of sugar as a plantation crop after the end of slavery, and the struggle over African labour in Natal. Explaining the reasons for indentured labour is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: India as a British Colony

India was the largest and most valuable part of the British Empire. British involvement began with trade. The East India Company, a private company founded in London in 1600, set up trading posts at Madras, Bombay and Calcutta in the seventeenth century, buying Indian cotton cloth, spices, indigo and tea. As the Mughal Empire weakened in the eighteenth century, the Company used its own armies, made up largely of Indian soldiers called sepoys, to take control of territory. The victory at Plassey in Bengal in 1757 is often taken as the beginning of Company rule. By the early nineteenth century the Company governed most of India directly or through Indian princes who accepted its authority.

Company rule brought great changes. The Company collected land revenue, a tax on land, which in many areas was set so high that farmers fell into debt and lost their land. As you learnt in the session on Britain's power, Indian hand-woven cloth was undercut by machine-made British textiles, and many weavers lost their livelihoods. India was turned into a supplier of raw materials such as raw cotton, indigo and opium, and a market for British manufactured goods.

In 1857 a great uprising broke out, beginning with a mutiny of sepoys at Meerut and spreading across northern India. British writers called it the Indian Mutiny; many Indian historians call it the First War of Independence. It was crushed with great violence on both sides. In 1858 the British government abolished the East India Company's rule and took over India directly, a period known as the British Raj. In 1877 Queen Victoria was proclaimed Empress of India.

For the story of indenture, several features of British India matter. Poverty and debt were widespread in rural areas, especially in regions with dense populations and frequent droughts. Famines struck repeatedly; the famines of the late 1870s killed millions. Because India and Natal were both British colonies, a labourer could be moved from one to the other under British law, with the governments of India and Natal agreeing the rules. The Indian government regulated emigration and could, and eventually did, stop it.

The two ports from which labourers left for Natal reflect the two regions they came from. From Madras on the south-east coast came speakers of Tamil and Telugu. From Calcutta in the north-east came speakers of Hindi and Bhojpuri, many from the districts of present-day Uttar Pradesh and Bihar. Most were Hindu, while a minority were Muslim and some were Christian. Their languages, festivals, foods and religious practices became part of South African life, from Diwali to the temples and mosques of Durban.

The questions for this section are with you now: how India became a British colony, the uprising of 1857 and British rule, and conditions in India that encouraged emigration.

## Subtopic: Sugar and the Labour Problem in Natal

Natal became a British colony in 1843. Its settlers looked for a crop that would make the colony pay. The humid, frost-free coastal belt suited sugar cane, which had been grown on slave plantations in the Caribbean, Brazil and Mauritius. The first commercial sugar was produced in Natal in the early 1850s, and by the end of the decade planters had invested in land, mills and machinery.

Sugar is labour-intensive. Cane must be planted, weeded and cut by hand, and once it is cut it must be crushed quickly in the mill before the sugar content falls, so the harvest needs many workers at the same time, working long hours. Planters needed a large, reliable workforce that they could keep for the whole season and pay as little as possible.

They did not find it among the African population of Natal, and the reasons are important. Most Zulu-speaking people in Natal had access to land in the locations and on mission and Crown land, and could support themselves by farming and herding. In Zulu society, cultivating crops was largely women's work, while men looked after cattle, so long contracts of field work for low wages had little appeal. Men who did work for wages usually did so for short periods to earn money for specific needs, such as paying the hut tax introduced in 1849, or buying cattle for lobola, and then returned home. Shepstone's policy of governing Africans through chiefs in locations also limited the pressure on them to work for settlers. Planters complained of a labour shortage, but it was really a shortage of workers willing to accept the wages and conditions on offer.

Natal's planters therefore looked to the solution other sugar colonies had used after the abolition of slavery. Mauritius had brought indentured labourers from India since 1834, and British Guiana, Trinidad and Jamaica had followed. In 1859 Natal's Legislative Council passed laws to allow the importation of Indian labourers, with the colonial government paying part of the costs. The Indian government agreed, on condition that the labourers were protected by regulations.

Indenture also served the planters in another way. A labourer bound by contract could not leave to work for someone else, and could be punished by law for desertion or refusing to work. This gave employers control that wage labour alone did not.

The decision also shaped the population of Natal permanently. By 1904 Natal had about one hundred thousand people of Indian origin, more than its white population. That fact would later fuel anti-Indian politics among settlers who wanted Indian labour but not Indian neighbours, a contradiction you will explore in the next session.

The questions for this section are with you now: why sugar needed many workers, why planters did not find enough local labour, and the decision to import Indian labour.

## Subtopic: Recruitment, the Contract and the Voyage

Indenture was a contract that bound a labourer to work for a particular employer for a fixed number of years in return for passage, wages, food, housing and medical care. In Natal, the first contracts were for three years; from 1864 they were for five years. Wages started at ten shillings a month in the first year and rose by one shilling a month each year, so in the fifth year a labourer earned fourteen shillings a month. Rations of rice, dhal, ghee, salt and other food were supplied. After five years of indenture and five more years living in Natal, a labourer was entitled to a free return passage to India. In the early years, a labourer could instead claim a grant of Crown land of equal value, though this option was soon restricted and then ended.

Recruitment was done by licensed recruiters and their agents, who toured villages and towns and spoke to people at markets, railway stations and temples. Many recruits were poor rural people: landless labourers, small farmers in debt, weavers and artisans who had lost their trades, people escaping famine, and some who wanted to leave family conflict or caste restrictions. Some recruits were well informed and chose to go, hoping to save money and return. Others were misled about the work, the distance or the conditions, and complaints of deception and even kidnapping were made against some recruiters. Recruits were taken to depots in Madras or Calcutta, where they were examined by doctors, the contract was explained, and, in theory, a magistrate checked that they were going willingly.

The Indian government required that a certain proportion of emigrants be women, eventually forty women for every hundred men, to avoid communities made up only of men. Families with children also travelled, and children were registered and later could be indentured themselves.

The voyage across the Indian Ocean from Madras or Calcutta to Durban took around four to eight weeks by sailing ship, and later less by steamship. Ships carried a surgeon, and rules set space, food and water for each passenger. Even so, conditions below deck were crowded, and diseases such as cholera, dysentery and measles could spread; some voyages saw many deaths. People of different castes, regions and religions were thrown together on the ship, and many historians note that the shared hardship of the voyage created strong bonds, called jahaji bhai, ship brotherhood, between those who travelled together.

On arrival in Durban, labourers were registered by the Protector of Indian Immigrants, given a number, and allocated to employers. Most went to sugar estates on the coast, but others were sent to the Natal Government Railways, the Durban Corporation, coal mines, hotels and private households.

The questions for this section are with you now: the terms of the indenture contract, how recruitment worked, and the voyage and arrival in Natal.

## Subtopic: Interpreting Indenture and the Error Museum

Historians have debated how to describe indenture. Some, such as the historian Hugh Tinker, called it a new system of slavery, because labourers could be punished for leaving, were often confined to estates, and in many cases were overworked and abused. Others point out that indenture differed from slavery: it was a contract for a limited time, labourers were paid wages, the law gave them some rights, they could complain to the Protector, and at the end they were free. Many ex-indentured Indians chose to stay in Natal, became market gardeners, hawkers, fishermen and workers, and built new lives. A balanced view recognises both the harsh, coercive side of indenture and the agency of people who used the system to escape poverty and who built a community despite it.

When studying sources, compare the voices. Official reports by Natal's government and the Protector tended to present the system as fair. Petitions and complaints by labourers, which survive in the archives, reveal abuses. Accounts by Indian leaders and later historians bring other perspectives. As with all sources, ask who wrote it, why, and what they might have left out.

The numbers can be checked. Between 1860 and 1911, about one hundred and fifty-two thousand indentured labourers came to Natal. Most were adults between about eighteen and thirty years old, though children travelled with their families. Indenture to Natal ended in 1911, when the Indian government stopped it because of criticism of the treatment of Indians in South Africa.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying that the first Indians came to Natal as traders; the first arrivals in 1860 were indentured labourers. Two: confusing indentured labourers with slaves; indenture was a contract for a fixed term with wages, even though it was coercive. Three: saying that Africans in Natal were lazy; they had land and chose not to accept low wages and long contracts. Four: believing all recruits were tricked; many chose to go, though some were deceived. Five: saying all Indians returned at the end of their contracts; most stayed.

Layout for marks in an explain-why answer: state the reason, give the evidence, and link it to the result, three times. For example: sugar needed many workers at harvest; most Africans in Natal had land and did not need low-paid contract work; and Mauritius had already used Indian indentured labour; so in 1859 Natal passed laws to import Indian labourers, and the first arrived on the Truro in November 1860.

The questions for this section are with you now: debates about indenture and slavery, reading the sources, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: India Under the British

India is far away from Natal, but in the 1800s both were ruled by Britain. It started with a trading company, the East India Company, which came to buy Indian cloth and spices and slowly took control of the country with its armies. After a big uprising in 1857, the British government took over India directly.

Life became very hard for many villagers. Land taxes were high, families fell into debt, weavers lost work to cheap British cloth, and terrible famines killed millions. Some people were ready to travel far away for a chance to earn money.

## Subtopic: Sugar Needs Hands

Natal's settlers found that sugar cane grew well on the warm coast. But sugar needs lots of workers, especially at harvest, when the cane must be cut and crushed quickly.

The planters wanted Zulu men to do this work for low pay on long contracts. Most said no. They had land of their own to farm and cattle to look after, and they only worked for wages for short periods when they needed money. The planters called this a labour shortage, but really it was a shortage of people willing to accept their low pay.

So the planters copied Mauritius, another sugar island, which already brought workers from India. In 1859 Natal passed a law, and in November 1860 the first ship, the Truro, arrived in Durban.

## Subtopic: The Contract and the Journey

A worker signed a contract, an indenture, to work for one employer for five years. The pay was ten shillings a month, going up by one shilling each year, plus food and a place to sleep. After ten years in Natal, a worker could get a free ticket home.

Recruiters went to villages and told people about the work. Some people chose to go, hoping to save money. Others were misled. They sailed for weeks from Madras or Calcutta in crowded ships, and some fell ill or died on the way. People who shared the voyage became like family to each other.

Pocket summary of the lesson. India was a British colony where poverty, debt and famine pushed people to leave. Natal's sugar planters needed many workers, and most Africans in Natal had land and refused low-paid contracts. So from 1860 Natal brought about one hundred and fifty-two thousand indentured labourers from India, on five-year contracts with low wages, until 1911. Indenture was not slavery, but it was harsh and coercive.
