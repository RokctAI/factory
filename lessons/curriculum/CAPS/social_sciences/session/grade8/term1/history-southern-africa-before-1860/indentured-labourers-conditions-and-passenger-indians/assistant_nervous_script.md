On Natal's sugar estates, indentured labourers lived in crowded barracks with poor toilets and water. They worked dawn to dusk, six days a week, and even longer in the crushing season. Pay started at ten shillings a month, plus rice, dhal, oil and salt. They needed a pass to leave the estate and could be jailed for leaving. Many got sick. Women had extra burdens.

Labourers resisted: they complained, ran away, refused to work together and kept their culture alive. India stopped sending workers from 1866 to 1874 because of complaints.

From the 1870s, passenger Indians came as traders, paying their own way.

Settlers passed unfair laws, like a three-pound yearly tax from 1895. Gandhi and the Natal Indian Congress, from 1894, fought back, and the tax ended in 1914.
