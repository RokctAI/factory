# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the political map of southern Africa in about 1860, just before the mineral discoveries began to transform the region. The anchor is a single map showing the British colonies of the Cape and Natal, the Boer republics of the Orange Free State and the South African Republic, and the independent African states that still controlled most of the land: the Zulu, Swazi, Basotho, Pedi, Tswana, Xhosa, Mpondo, Venda and others. The governing idea is that around 1860 power in southern Africa was divided among many states of different kinds, none of which controlled the whole region, and that land, labour, cattle and trade were at the centre of their relationships. Locating these states and explaining their origins is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: The British Colonies: the Cape and Natal

The Cape Colony was the oldest European settlement. The Dutch East India Company had set up a refreshment station at the Cape in 1652, and over the following century and a half Dutch-speaking settlers spread inland, taking land and cattle from the Khoekhoe and fighting the San, many of whom were killed or forced into labour. Enslaved people were brought from the East Indies, India, Madagascar and East Africa. Britain took the Cape in 1795, returned it briefly to the Dutch, and took it again permanently in 1806, mainly to protect the sea route to India.

Under British rule the Cape changed in important ways. The slave trade was banned from 1807, and slavery itself ended in 1834, with a period of apprenticeship until 1838. British settlers arrived in the eastern Cape in 1820. Along the eastern frontier, the Cape fought a series of wars against Xhosa chiefdoms over land and cattle, nine wars in all between 1779 and 1879. In 1847 the British annexed the land between the Keiskamma and Kei rivers as a separate territory called British Kaffraria; the name used an old term that is now regarded as offensive, and historians use it only as the historical name. It was joined to the Cape in 1866.

In 1853 the Cape received representative government, which meant an elected parliament. The vote was given to men who owned property worth at least twenty-five pounds or earned a certain wage, regardless of race. In practice most voters were white, but some black and coloured men did qualify. This qualified non-racial franchise was unusual in southern Africa and became important later.

Natal was the second British colony. British traders had settled at Port Natal, later called Durban, from 1824 with the permission of the Zulu king Shaka. Voortrekkers arrived in 1837 and 1838 and, after defeating the Zulu at the Battle of Ncome, also called Blood River, in December 1838, set up the Republic of Natalia. Britain annexed Natal in 1843, and most of the Voortrekkers moved back across the Drakensberg. Natal became a separate colony in 1856. Its African population was far larger than its settler population. Under Theophilus Shepstone, the colonial official in charge of African affairs, Africans were settled in locations, or reserves, governed through chiefs under customary law, an approach that later influenced segregation policies across southern Africa. In the 1850s settlers began growing sugar along the coast, which leads to the next session's story of indentured labour from India.

The two colonies were not equal partners with London. Each had a governor appointed by Britain, and the British government decided matters of war and peace with neighbouring states. Britain wanted its colonies to be cheap to run and secure, and it often hesitated to take on new territory because of the cost. This hesitation explains why Britain sometimes annexed land and then withdrew, as it did with the Orange River Sovereignty.

The questions for this section are with you now: the origins of the Cape Colony and Natal, the Cape franchise, and British policy on the frontier.

## Subtopic: The Boer Republics

From 1835, several thousand Dutch-speaking farmers and their families, along with their coloured servants and dependants, left the eastern Cape in what became known as the Great Trek. The trekkers, later called Voortrekkers, were unhappy with British rule for several reasons: the end of slavery and the compensation they considered unfair, the shortage of land, insecurity on the eastern frontier, and laws such as Ordinance 50 of 1828, which gave Khoekhoe and other free people of colour equality before the law. They wanted to govern themselves and to keep control over labour and land.

The trekkers moved onto the highveld, a region that had been disrupted by wars and displacements in the 1820s and 1830s. They fought Mzilikazi's Ndebele, who in 1837 and 1838 moved north across the Limpopo into what became Matabeleland, in today's Zimbabwe. They fought the Zulu in Natal. They made agreements with some African rulers and fought others, and they claimed large areas of land.

Britain at first tried to follow them. In 1848 it annexed the land between the Orange and Vaal rivers as the Orange River Sovereignty. But ruling the interior was expensive and caused conflict, so Britain withdrew. Under the Sand River Convention of 1852, Britain recognised the independence of the trekkers north of the Vaal River, who formed the Zuid-Afrikaansche Republiek, the South African Republic, often called the Transvaal. Under the Bloemfontein Convention of 1854, Britain recognised the independence of the Orange Free State between the Orange and Vaal rivers. Both conventions included promises about arms: Britain agreed not to sell guns to African societies north of the Orange River, which strengthened the republics.

The republics were small in white population and loosely governed. The Orange Free State had a constitution, an elected Volksraad, or parliament, and a president. The South African Republic was divided among rival leaders at first and took time to unite; Pretoria was founded in 1855 and became its capital. In both republics, citizenship and the vote were reserved for white men, and the constitution of the South African Republic stated there would be no equality between black and white in church or state. Africans living within the territory the republics claimed were often required to provide labour, and there were cases of children captured in raids being forced into so-called apprenticeship, which critics described as a form of slavery.

The republics' borders were not fixed. They claimed much land that African rulers also claimed and still occupied, and conflict over land continued for decades, especially between the Orange Free State and Moshoeshoe's Basotho, and between the South African Republic and the Pedi.

The questions for this section are with you now: why the Voortrekkers left the Cape, the Sand River and Bloemfontein conventions, and how the republics were governed.

## Subtopic: Independent African Kingdoms

Around 1860, most of the land and people of southern Africa were still under African rulers. Several powerful kingdoms had emerged or grown during the early nineteenth century, a time of upheaval that some older books call the Mfecane, or Difaqane in Sesotho. Historians now debate that term, because older accounts blamed all the turmoil on the Zulu, while newer research shows that drought, competition for land, the slave trade from Delagoa Bay and raids from the Cape frontier also played a part.

The Zulu kingdom lay between the Thukela and Phongolo rivers, north of Natal. Shaka built it into a powerful state in the 1810s and 1820s with age-regiment armies. In 1860 it was ruled by King Mpande, while his son Cetshwayo, who had defeated his brother in a civil war in 1856, held real power and became king in 1872.

The Swazi kingdom, north of the Zulu, was built by Sobhuza I and strengthened by his son Mswati II, who ruled from about 1840 to 1868 and gave the Swazi people their name. Swaziland, today Eswatini, survived as a kingdom into the present.

The Basotho kingdom was built by Moshoeshoe from his mountain fortress at Thaba Bosiu, which was never captured. He gathered refugees from the wars of the 1820s, used diplomacy as much as war, and invited French missionaries in 1833. In the 1850s and 1860s he fought the Orange Free State over the fertile land west of the Caledon River. In 1868 he asked Britain to take the Basotho under its protection to prevent further losses; this is how Lesotho came to exist as a separate state.

The Pedi kingdom of Sekwati and later Sekhukhune, in the area of the Steelpoort River, resisted the South African Republic. The Tswana chiefdoms, such as the Ngwato, Kwena and Ngwaketse, lay to the west. In the eastern Cape, Xhosa chiefdoms east of the Kei remained independent, although the cattle-killing movement of 1856 and 1857, in which many followed the prophecies of the young woman Nongqawuse that killing cattle and destroying crops would bring renewal, led to the deaths of tens of thousands from starvation and left the region open to colonial control. The Mpondo kingdom in the east, the Venda in the far north, and the Gaza kingdom founded by Soshangane in southern Mozambique completed the picture.

There were also Griqua states. The Griqua were people of Khoekhoe, slave and settler descent who had organised themselves under their own leaders. Andries Waterboer's Griqua at Griquatown, and Adam Kok III's Griqua at Philippolis, held land north of the Orange River. Adam Kok's people later moved east in 1861 and 1862 to land called Griqualand East. Waterboer's land became central to the diamond story.

The questions for this section are with you now: the major African kingdoms around 1860, Moshoeshoe's diplomacy, and the Griqua states.

## Subtopic: Relations on the Map and the Error Museum

The states on the 1860 map were connected in many ways. Trade ran between them: ivory, hides, cattle and grain moved towards the coast, and guns, cloth, beads and metal goods moved inland. Missionaries, traders and hunters travelled widely. Workers from African societies, such as the Pedi and the Basotho, walked to the Cape and Natal to work on farms and in towns to earn money for guns and cattle, an early form of migrant labour.

Relations were also full of conflict. Land was the main issue: settlers wanted farms, and African rulers wanted to keep the land their people depended on. Cattle were central to wealth and status for almost every society on the map, so cattle raids and cattle seizures were frequent causes and results of war. Labour was another issue, since colonial and republican farmers wanted African workers. Guns mattered more and more; whoever had firearms and horses had an advantage.

Diplomacy was common too. Moshoeshoe sent messengers and letters to governors and presidents. The Zulu kings made agreements with Natal. Treaties drew boundaries that were often misunderstood or broken, partly because African and European ideas about land differed: many African rulers saw land as held for the community and granted for use, while settlers saw it as private property that could be bought and sold.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: drawing the 1860 map with South Africa as one country; it did not exist until 1910. Two: showing the interior as empty land; African kingdoms and chiefdoms occupied most of it. Three: confusing the Sand River Convention of 1852, which recognised the Transvaal, with the Bloemfontein Convention of 1854, which recognised the Orange Free State. Four: saying the Cape franchise was for whites only; it was non-racial but based on property and income. Five: blaming all the upheavals of the early nineteenth century on the Zulu alone.

Layout for marks in a map question: give the map a title and date, a key, a compass direction and a scale or approximate distances, then label each colony, republic and kingdom with its name and, where useful, a date. In a paragraph describing the map: name the two colonies and their capitals, the two republics and the dates of their recognition, and at least four African kingdoms with their rulers, then conclude that power was divided among many states.

The questions for this section are with you now: trade, conflict and diplomacy between states, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: the British colonies, the Boer republics, and the African kingdoms.

## Subtopic: Two British Colonies

Put a map of southern Africa in front of you, but forget the borders of today. In 1860 there was no South Africa yet.

In the south-west lies the Cape Colony, the oldest settlement. The Dutch arrived in 1652, and the British took over for good in 1806 because the Cape was on the sea road to India. Slavery ended in 1834. In 1853 the Cape got its own parliament, and men who owned enough property could vote, whatever their colour, though most voters were white.

On the east coast lies Natal, a British colony from 1843, with Durban as its port and Pietermaritzburg as its capital. Far more Africans than settlers lived there. Settlers started growing sugar on the coast in the 1850s.

On the eastern frontier of the Cape, the colony fought many wars with Xhosa chiefdoms over land and cattle.

Hold the two British colonies, one on each coast, in mind and try the questions for this part: the Cape and Natal.

## Subtopic: Two Boer Republics

From 1835 thousands of Dutch-speaking farmers left the Cape with their wagons. We call this the Great Trek. They did not like British rule, the end of slavery, or laws that made free people of colour equal before the law, and they wanted land and to govern themselves.

They set up two republics. North of the Vaal River was the South African Republic, also called the Transvaal; Britain accepted it in 1852. Between the Orange and the Vaal was the Orange Free State; Britain accepted it in 1854. Only white men could vote in them.

Hold the two rivers, the Orange and the Vaal, in mind and try this part's questions: the Great Trek and the two republics.

## Subtopic: African Kingdoms Everywhere

Now colour in most of the map, because most of the land was still ruled by African kings and chiefs. The Zulu kingdom under Mpande, with Cetshwayo rising. The Swazi kingdom under Mswati. The Basotho under Moshoeshoe on his mountain, Thaba Bosiu, which no enemy ever captured. The Pedi in the north-east. The Tswana in the west. Xhosa chiefdoms beyond the Kei. The Mpondo, the Venda, and the Griqua with their own leaders.

These states traded, sent messengers, made treaties and fought, mostly over land and cattle. Guns and horses became more and more important.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Around 1860 southern Africa was divided among many states: the British Cape Colony and Natal; the Boer republics of the Transvaal and the Orange Free State, recognised in 1852 and 1854; and independent African kingdoms such as the Zulu, Swazi, Basotho and Pedi that held most of the land. Land, cattle, labour and guns shaped their relations. There was no single South Africa until 1910.

Hold the many colours on the 1860 map, and take the final questions of the lesson: colonies, republics and kingdoms.
