### subtopic_1 — The British Colonies: the Cape and Natal
**Assistant:** Why is it wrong to show the interior of southern Africa as empty land on old maps?

**Tutor:** Because it was not empty, and the idea of empty land was used to justify taking it. For centuries, southern Africa had been home to many societies. San hunter-gatherers had lived there for tens of thousands of years. Khoekhoe herders had lived in the western and southern regions for about two thousand years. Bantu-speaking farming communities, ancestors of the Zulu, Xhosa, Sotho, Tswana, Pedi, Venda, Tsonga and others, had lived across the eastern and northern regions for well over a thousand years, growing crops, keeping cattle, smelting iron and trading, as sites such as Mapungubwe show. In the early nineteenth century, some areas of the highveld were temporarily less populated because of war, drought and displacement, and some settler writers later used this to claim that the Voortrekkers had moved into vacant land. Historians call this the myth of the empty land. Research shows that most land was used, claimed or temporarily abandoned by people who returned, and that settlers often took land that African communities were living on or had good reason to regard as theirs. The myth mattered because it was used to justify dispossession, and later apartheid governments repeated it in school textbooks. Today it is important for historians to check whose voices a map or book includes. Maps drawn by colonial surveyors often marked only settler farms and towns, while African homesteads, grazing land and chiefdoms were left out or labelled vaguely. When you draw the 1860 map, label the African kingdoms and chiefdoms clearly, because they controlled most of the land and most of the people. That is a matter of accuracy, not opinion.

### subtopic_2 — The Boer Republics
**Assistant:** Why did Britain recognise the Boer republics instead of ruling the interior itself?

**Tutor:** Mainly because of cost and priorities. In the 1840s and 1850s, Britain's main interest in southern Africa was the Cape and its naval base at Simon's Town, which protected the sea route to India. The interior had no known minerals and few obvious economic attractions, while ruling it meant paying for soldiers, officials and wars with African kingdoms and with the trekkers themselves. When Britain annexed the Orange River Sovereignty in 1848, it had to fight the trekkers at the Battle of Boomplaats and then became entangled in conflict between the Sovereignty and Moshoeshoe's Basotho. At the same time, Britain was fighting a costly war on the eastern Cape frontier from 1850 to 1853. Officials in London concluded that the interior was not worth the expense. The Sand River Convention in 1852 and the Bloemfontein Convention in 1854 allowed Britain to withdraw while keeping influence through agreements, including a promise not to arm African societies north of the Orange River. Some historians see this as abandonment of African allies such as Moshoeshoe, who had earlier made treaties with Britain. The policy changed later. After diamonds were discovered in 1867 and especially after gold in 1886, the interior became extremely valuable, and Britain took a much more active role: annexing Griqualand West in 1871, annexing the Transvaal in 1877, and eventually fighting the South African War of 1899 to 1902. So British policy depended on what the land was worth to Britain at the time. A historian's answer would show this change over time and connect it to economic interests.

### subtopic_3 — Independent African Kingdoms
**Assistant:** How did Moshoeshoe manage to keep the Basotho kingdom together when so many others lost their land?

**Tutor:** Through a combination of a strong defensive base, generous leadership, skilled diplomacy and timely use of outside protection. In the 1820s, at a time of wars and displacement on the highveld, Moshoeshoe moved his people to Thaba Bosiu, a flat-topped mountain with steep cliffs and springs of water, which no enemy ever managed to capture. He welcomed refugees from other groups, gave them cattle and land, and allowed their leaders to keep some authority, which built a large and loyal following. He avoided unnecessary wars, sometimes sending cattle as gifts to stronger neighbours to keep the peace. In 1833 he invited French missionaries of the Paris Evangelical Missionary Society, who acted as advisers and helped him write letters to colonial governments, giving him a way to argue his case. Against the Orange Free State, which claimed the fertile land west of the Caledon River, the Basotho fought several wars in the 1850s and 1860s. They won some battles, but by 1866 they had lost a great deal of arable land and faced famine. In 1868, realising that the Basotho could not hold out alone, Moshoeshoe asked Britain to annex his country. He chose British protection because it seemed the lesser danger and would stop the Free State taking everything. Basutoland was annexed in 1868, and the Treaty of Aliwal North in 1869 fixed boundaries that left much of the lowland to the Free State. Basutoland survived as a separate territory and became independent Lesotho in 1966. Moshoeshoe died in 1870 and is remembered as a wise and practical leader.

### subtopic_4 — Relations on the Map and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why was land understood so differently by African societies and by settlers?

**Tutor:** Because the two systems rested on different ideas about who could own land and what owning it meant. In most African societies in southern Africa, land belonged to the community and was held in trust by the chief or king on behalf of the people. A chief allocated land to households for homesteads and fields, and grazing land was shared. People had secure rights to use land as long as they lived there and were loyal members of the community, but land was not normally a commodity to be bought and sold. When a chief allowed newcomers to settle, he was usually granting them permission to use land as his subjects, not selling it to them forever. Settler law, coming from Dutch and British traditions, treated land as private property. A farmer could hold a title deed giving him exclusive rights, fence the land, and sell it or pass it to his heirs. When settlers made agreements with chiefs, they often believed they had bought land outright, while the chief believed he had granted use rights or made an alliance. Some agreements were also made under pressure or with people who had no authority to give the land away. These different understandings caused many disputes, and colonial and republican governments usually enforced the settler interpretation through their courts and armies. Later laws such as the Natives Land Act of 1913 built on these foundations. Understanding the difference helps historians read treaties critically. When you see a treaty in a source, ask who made it, under what conditions, in what language, and how each side understood it. The answer often explains why conflict followed.
