# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the defeat of Germany in 1918 and the Treaty of Versailles of 1919. The anchor is the treaty's main terms, often remembered as guilt, reparations, armed forces and territory, together with the League of Nations. The governing idea is that Germany was defeated by a combination of military failure, the arrival of American troops, the naval blockade and collapse at home, and that the treaty was shaped by the different aims of the victors; Germans saw it as a harsh dictated peace, and historians still debate whether it was too harsh, too lenient or a reasonable compromise. Describing the end of the war and the terms of the treaty, including its effect on South Africa, is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: The Defeat of Germany in 1918

At the start of 1918, Germany seemed to have a chance of victory. Russia had left the war after its revolution, and the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk in March 1918 gave Germany control of huge areas in the east. Germany could move about fifty divisions to the Western Front. Its leaders, Hindenburg and Ludendorff, knew that they had to win quickly, before large numbers of American troops arrived.

On 21 March 1918, Germany launched the Spring Offensive, also called the Ludendorff Offensive. Using new tactics with specially trained stormtroopers who moved fast, bypassing strong points, the Germans broke through Allied lines and advanced further than at any time since 1914, coming within artillery range of Paris. But the attacks cost Germany its best soldiers, its troops outran their supplies, and exhausted men sometimes stopped to loot Allied food stores, which showed how hungry they were. By July the offensive had failed.

The Allies then counter-attacked. On 8 August 1918, at the Battle of Amiens, British, Australian, Canadian and French forces, using tanks, aircraft and artillery together, broke through the German lines. Ludendorff called it the black day of the German army. In the Hundred Days Offensive that followed, the Allies pushed the Germans back steadily and broke through the strong defences of the Hindenburg Line.

American troops arrived in growing numbers, about ten thousand a day by mid-1918, and by November there were nearly two million in France. They brought fresh energy, supplies and the certainty that the Allies' strength would keep growing.

Germany's allies collapsed one after another: Bulgaria signed an armistice at the end of September, the Ottoman Empire at the end of October and Austria-Hungary at the beginning of November.

At home, Germany was collapsing too. The British naval blockade had caused severe shortages of food, fuel and raw materials. Hundreds of thousands of German civilians died of hunger-related illness during the war, and the influenza pandemic of 1918 added to the misery. Strikes and protests spread. At the end of October, sailors at Wilhelmshaven and Kiel mutinied rather than sail out on a final hopeless attack, and revolution spread to cities across Germany. On 9 November 1918 the Kaiser abdicated and fled to the Netherlands, and Germany became a republic.

The new German government agreed to an armistice, a ceasefire. It was signed in a railway carriage near Compiègne early on 11 November and came into force at 11 a.m. that day.

The cost of the war was enormous. About nine million soldiers were killed, and millions of civilians died from fighting, hunger and disease. Millions more were wounded, many disabled for life. Every year on 11 November, Remembrance Day, people in many countries, including South Africa, keep two minutes of silence at 11 a.m. to remember those who died.

## Subtopic: The Peace Conference and the Big Three

The Paris Peace Conference opened in January 1919. Representatives of more than thirty countries attended, including South Africa, represented by Louis Botha and Jan Smuts. The defeated countries, including Germany, were not allowed to take part in the negotiations. Separate treaties were made with each defeated country; the treaty with Germany was the Treaty of Versailles.

The most important decisions were made by the leaders of the main victorious powers, often called the Big Three: Georges Clemenceau, prime minister of France; David Lloyd George, prime minister of Britain; and Woodrow Wilson, president of the United States. The Italian prime minister, Vittorio Orlando, also took part, making a Big Four, but he had less influence.

Each had different aims. Clemenceau wanted security for France and revenge. France had suffered the most destruction on the Western Front, with about 1.4 million soldiers killed and large areas of the north devastated. It had been invaded by Germany twice in fifty years. Clemenceau wanted Germany to be weakened so that it could never attack France again, and to pay for the damage.

Lloyd George was in a more difficult position. Many British voters wanted Germany punished; in the 1918 election some politicians promised to make Germany pay. But Lloyd George also wanted Germany to recover so that it could trade with Britain again, and he feared that a harsh peace would cause bitterness and future war, or push Germany towards communist revolution as in Russia. He wanted to protect Britain's navy and empire, and he was pleased that the German navy and colonies would be taken away.

Wilson wanted a fair and lasting peace based on his Fourteen Points, announced in January 1918. These included self-determination, the right of nations to rule themselves; disarmament; freedom of the seas; open diplomacy instead of secret treaties; and a League of Nations to settle disputes peacefully. The United States had joined late and suffered less, so Wilson was more willing to be lenient towards Germany.

The final treaty was a compromise that fully satisfied none of them. It was harsher than Wilson wanted, less harsh than Clemenceau wanted, and Lloyd George was left defending it at home. Germany was presented with the terms in May 1919 and given little choice: if it refused, the war might start again. The treaty was signed on 28 June 1919 in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, the same room in which the German Empire had been proclaimed in 1871.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Paris Peace Conference, the Big Three and their aims.

## Subtopic: The Terms of the Treaty of Versailles

The Treaty of Versailles contained hundreds of articles. Its main terms can be remembered under four headings, with the League of Nations as a fifth.

Guilt. Article 231, called the war guilt clause, stated that Germany and its allies accepted responsibility for causing all the loss and damage of the war. Germans found this deeply humiliating, because they did not believe that they alone had caused the war.

Reparations. Because of Article 231, Germany had to pay reparations, compensation for war damage, to the Allies. The total was set in 1921 at 132 billion gold marks, a huge sum, to be paid in instalments over many years. Germany also had to hand over ships, coal, machinery and livestock.

Armed forces. The German army was limited to one hundred thousand men, and conscription was banned. Germany was not allowed tanks, an air force or submarines, and its navy was limited to six battleships and a small number of other ships. The Rhineland, the area of Germany bordering France, Belgium and the Netherlands, was demilitarised: no German troops or fortifications were allowed there, and Allied troops occupied it for up to fifteen years.

Territory. Germany lost about thirteen percent of its territory in Europe and about a tenth of its population. Alsace and Lorraine were returned to France. The Saar, a coal-rich region, was placed under the League of Nations for fifteen years, with France controlling its coal mines. Eupen and Malmedy went to Belgium and North Schleswig to Denmark after a vote. West Prussia and Posen were given to the new state of Poland, creating a strip of land called the Polish Corridor that separated East Prussia from the rest of Germany. Danzig became a free city under the League. Union between Germany and Austria, called Anschluss, was forbidden.

Colonies. Germany lost all its overseas colonies. They became mandates, territories governed by Allied countries on behalf of the League of Nations, which were supposed to prepare them for self-government. German South West Africa was given to South Africa as a mandate; Smuts had argued strongly for this. German East Africa went mainly to Britain as Tanganyika, Kamerun and Togoland were divided between Britain and France, and Germany's Pacific islands went to Japan, Australia and New Zealand.

The League of Nations, an international organisation to keep peace, settle disputes and encourage cooperation, was set up as part of the peace treaties. Smuts contributed ideas to its design. Germany was not allowed to join at first, and the United States Senate refused to approve the treaty, so the United States never joined.

## Subtopic: Judging the Treaty, Calculation and the Error Museum

How did people react? Germans of almost all political views were outraged. They called it a Diktat, a dictated peace, because they had not been allowed to negotiate. Many Germans did not believe that their army had been defeated, because German troops were still on foreign soil in November 1918, and a myth spread that the army had been stabbed in the back by politicians, socialists and others at home. The government that signed the treaty, the new democratic Weimar Republic, was blamed for it, which weakened democracy in Germany. Many French people thought the treaty was too lenient, while some British observers, such as the economist John Maynard Keynes, argued that the reparations were too harsh and would damage the whole European economy.

Within Germany, the treaty's terms were felt in everyday life. Families in the lost territories had to choose whether to stay under a new government or move to Germany. Unemployed former soldiers resented the tiny army. Many Germans saw the war guilt clause as an insult to the memory of the two million German soldiers who had died. These feelings were used by nationalist politicians throughout the 1920s and 1930s.

Historians continue to debate the treaty. Some argue that it was too harsh, humiliated Germany and helped Adolf Hitler and the Nazis to gain support by promising to overturn it, contributing to the Second World War. Others argue that it was not harsh enough to stop Germany from recovering, yet harsh enough to make Germans bitter. Others point out that Germany remained the largest and potentially strongest country in central Europe, that the treaty Germany imposed on Russia at Brest-Litovsk had been even harsher, and that the failure to enforce the treaty in the 1930s mattered more than its terms. A balanced answer explains these different views.

A calculation. The assassination at Sarajevo took place on 28 June 1914, and the treaty was signed on 28 June 1919, so 1919 minus 1914 is exactly 5 years later. The armistice was signed on 11 November 1918. From 11 November 1918 to 28 June 1919 is 229 days, about seven and a half months.

The error museum. Exhibit one: Germany helped write the treaty. False: Germany was not allowed to negotiate. Exhibit two: the war ended with the Treaty of Versailles on 11 November 1918. False: the fighting ended with the armistice on 11 November 1918; the treaty was signed on 28 June 1919. Exhibit three: the United States joined the League of Nations. False: the Senate rejected the treaty, and the United States never joined. Exhibit four: South Africa took no part in the peace. False: Botha and Smuts attended and signed, and South Africa received German South West Africa as a mandate.

## Subtopic: How Germany Lost

Plain words now. In March 1918 Germany made one last big attack, and it nearly worked. But it ran out of men and food. In August the Allies, with tanks and planes, hit back hard. American soldiers kept arriving.

At home, Germans were starving because of the British blockade. Sailors refused to fight, the Kaiser fled, and on 11 November 1918 at 11 o'clock the fighting stopped.

## Subtopic: Making the Peace

In 1919 the winners met in Paris. The Big Three were Wilson of the United States, Lloyd George of Britain and Clemenceau of France. Clemenceau wanted Germany weak. Wilson wanted a fair peace. Lloyd George was in between. Germany was not allowed to argue; it just had to sign.

Botha and Smuts signed the treaty for South Africa.

Louis Botha and Jan Smuts were there for South Africa.

## Subtopic: The Treaty

Germany had to accept the blame, pay huge sums, keep only a small army with no tanks, planes or submarines, and give up land and all its colonies. South Africa got German South West Africa to govern. The League of Nations was set up to keep peace.

Pocket summary of the lesson. In 1918 the German Spring Offensive failed, the Allies struck back from August, American troops arrived, the blockade starved Germany, its allies collapsed, sailors mutinied, the Kaiser fled and the armistice began at 11 a.m. on 11 November. At Paris, the Big Three made the Treaty of Versailles, signed on 28 June 1919: war guilt, reparations, a small army, lost land and colonies, and a League of Nations. Germans called it a Diktat, and historians still debate it.
