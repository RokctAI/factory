# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the changing role of women in the British workplace during the First World War. The anchor is the munitions factory, where hundreds of thousands of women made the shells and bullets that the army needed. The governing idea is that total war created a huge demand for labour, which opened jobs to women that had been closed to them, and that women's work was vital to the war effort and changed attitudes, but that many changes were temporary, women were paid less than men, and most lost their wartime jobs after 1918. Describing women's wartime work and evaluating how far it changed their position is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Women's Work Before the War and the Demand for Labour

Before 1914, many British women already worked for wages, especially working-class women. The largest group worked in domestic service, as maids, cooks and cleaners in other people's homes, for long hours and low pay. Many others worked in textile mills, in dressmaking and in shops. Middle-class women could work as teachers, nurses or clerks and typists, a growing area of work, but they were usually expected to leave paid work when they married. Many professions, such as law, were closed to women, and women were paid much less than men for similar work.

Society expected women's main role to be in the home, as wives and mothers, an idea sometimes described as separate spheres: public life and work for men, home and family for women. Women did not have the right to vote in national elections, as you will study in the next lesson.

The war changed this situation. Millions of men left their jobs to join the armed forces, first as volunteers and then, from 1916, as conscripts. At the same time, the war created an enormous demand for weapons, ammunition, uniforms, food and transport. Total war is a war in which a country uses all of its people and resources to win, so that the home front becomes as important as the fighting front.

In 1915 there was a shell crisis, when British newspapers reported that the army on the Western Front did not have enough artillery shells. The government created a Ministry of Munitions, led by David Lloyd George, to increase production. Factories needed workers, and women were the largest available source of labour.

At first, some employers and trade unions resisted employing women in skilled jobs. Unions feared that employers would use women as cheap labour to lower men's wages and that men would not get their jobs back. Agreements were made allowing dilution, in which skilled jobs were broken down into simpler tasks that less experienced workers, including women, could do, on the understanding that the changes would last only for the war.

Many employers were surprised by how quickly women learned new skills. Training schemes taught women to operate machines, read technical drawings and inspect finished shells for faults. Some managers reported that women were especially careful in precision work. Others remained doubtful, and women often faced mockery and hostility from male workers who saw them as a threat.

In 1915 the suffragette leaders Emmeline and Christabel Pankhurst, with government support, organised a march in London in which tens of thousands of women demanded the right to serve, under the slogan of women's right to work.

The questions for this section are with you now: women's work before the war, total war, the shell crisis and dilution.

## Subtopic: Munitionettes and Industrial Work

The biggest change was in munitions, the making of weapons and ammunition. Women munitions workers were nicknamed munitionettes. By 1918, about nine hundred thousand women worked in munitions factories, and they produced the great majority of the shells and bullets used by the British army.

Munitions work was hard and dangerous. Women worked long shifts, often twelve hours a day, six or seven days a week, including night shifts. They filled shells with explosives such as TNT, a chemical that turned their skin yellow, so they were nicknamed canaries. TNT poisoning could damage the liver and other organs, and some women died from it. Some babies born to women who worked with TNT had yellow-tinged skin at birth.

Explosions were a constant danger. In December 1916, an explosion at the Barnbow munitions factory near Leeds killed thirty-five women. In January 1917, a huge explosion at a factory in Silvertown in east London killed seventy-three people and destroyed hundreds of homes. News of such disasters was often censored during the war to protect morale.

Munitions work was usually better paid than domestic service, and many women welcomed the money and independence. Factories also offered companionship, and some provided canteens, nurseries and welfare supervisors. Many munitions factories formed women's football teams that played to large crowds and raised money for charity. The most famous, Dick, Kerr Ladies from Preston, drew a crowd of more than fifty thousand people to a match on Boxing Day 1920.

Women also worked in engineering, shipbuilding, chemical works and other heavy industries that had been almost entirely male. They worked as mechanics, welders and crane operators. In transport, women became bus conductors, tram drivers, railway porters and ticket collectors. In offices, banks and the civil service, the number of women clerks grew rapidly. Women worked as police officers for the first time, in women's police patrols.

Photographs and posters from the time are useful sources about women's war work, but they must be used with care. Many official photographs were taken to encourage recruitment and boost morale, so they show smiling, tidy women in clean factories, rather than the exhaustion, noise and danger that workers described in letters and interviews. Government reports on women's health, factory records and the memories of women recorded later in life give a fuller picture.

Overall, the number of women in paid work in Britain rose from about 5.9 million in 1914 to about 7.3 million in 1918. Many of the new workers were women who moved from domestic service and other low-paid jobs into better-paid war work.

The questions for this section are with you now: munitions work, its dangers and rewards, and women in industry and transport.

## Subtopic: Women on the Land, in Uniform and in Nursing

Women also worked on farms, served in the armed forces in non-combat roles, and cared for the wounded.

The land. German submarines attacked ships bringing food to Britain, and by 1917 food was running short. In 1917 the Women's Land Army was formed to recruit women to work on farms, ploughing, planting, harvesting, milking cows and caring for animals. Land girls wore practical clothing such as breeches, which shocked some people at the time. Many more women did farm work locally without joining the Land Army.

The armed forces. In 1917 the Women's Army Auxiliary Corps, the WAAC, was formed, followed by the Women's Royal Naval Service, the WRNS, in 1917 and the Women's Royal Air Force in 1918. Women served as cooks, clerks, telephone operators, drivers, mechanics and storekeepers, freeing men to fight. Tens of thousands served, some of them in France, close to the front. They did not fight, but some were killed by shelling and air raids.

Nursing and medicine. Professional nurses served in the Queen Alexandra's Imperial Military Nursing Service, and tens of thousands of volunteers served in Voluntary Aid Detachments, known as VADs, in hospitals in Britain and abroad. The First Aid Nursing Yeomanry, the FANY, drove ambulances and ran field hospitals. Women doctors, who had often been refused by the British army, set up their own hospitals; the Scottish Women's Hospitals, founded by Dr Elsie Inglis, served in France, Serbia and Russia. The British nurse Edith Cavell, who ran a nursing school in occupied Belgium, helped about two hundred Allied soldiers escape; she was arrested and executed by the Germans in October 1915, and her death was used widely in British propaganda.

Class made a difference to women's experience. Middle-class and upper-class women were more likely to serve as VADs or in the auxiliary forces, often as volunteers who could afford to work for little or no pay, while working-class women were more likely to work in munitions and industry for wages. Some wealthy women used their money and influence to set up hospitals and charities. Historians therefore warn against speaking of women as if they all had the same war.

Women at home also volunteered in huge numbers: knitting socks and scarves for soldiers, raising money, running canteens for troops and caring for refugees from Belgium. Many women ran households alone while their husbands were away, managing family money and making decisions in new ways. Millions of women also lost husbands, sons, brothers and fiancés, and lived with grief for the rest of their lives.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Women's Land Army, women in the auxiliary forces and women in nursing and medicine.

## Subtopic: After the War, Calculation and the Error Museum

What happened to women's work when the war ended in November 1918? For many women, the answer was disappointing.

As soldiers returned, most women in munitions and many in industry and transport lost their jobs. Munitions factories closed, and agreements with trade unions had promised that men would get their jobs back. In 1919 Parliament passed the Restoration of Pre-War Practices Act, which required employers to restore the working practices of before the war, pushing women out of many skilled jobs. Women who did not leave were sometimes accused of taking jobs from returning soldiers. Many women went back to domestic service or to the home, and the marriage bar, which forced women in jobs such as teaching and the civil service to resign when they married, continued.

Pay. Women were usually paid less than men, even for similar work, although the gap narrowed during the war. Equal pay was not achieved.

Lasting changes. Despite this, the war did bring some lasting changes. Fewer women returned to domestic service, and more worked in offices, shops and light industry. Women had proved that they could do skilled and heavy work, which changed some attitudes. In 1918 women over thirty who met a property qualification gained the vote, as you will see in the next lesson, and in 1919 the Sex Disqualification (Removal) Act opened some professions, such as law, to women. Women's clothing became more practical, and many young women enjoyed greater social freedom.

Women's experiences in other countries were similar. In France and Germany, women also worked in munitions factories, on farms and in transport, and in Germany women organised protests over food shortages. In South Africa, women raised funds, knitted for soldiers and served as nurses, and the war increased the number of women working in some industries. Comparing countries shows that total war changed women's work wherever it was fought.

Historians debate how far the war changed women's position. Some stress the new opportunities and confidence. Others stress that most changes were temporary, and that older ideas about women's role returned quickly. A balanced answer recognises both.

A calculation. The number of women in paid work rose from about 5.9 million in 1914 to about 7.3 million in 1918. The increase was 7.3 minus 5.9, which is 1.4 million. As a percentage of the 1914 figure, 1.4 divided by 5.9 is about 0.24, or about 24 percent.

The error museum. Exhibit one: no women worked before the war. False: millions did, mainly in domestic service, textiles, shops and teaching. Exhibit two: women were paid the same as men in the war. False: they were usually paid less. Exhibit three: women kept their wartime jobs after 1918. False: most women in munitions and many in industry lost them. Exhibit four: women fought in the trenches in the British army. False: they served in non-combat roles, though some were killed near the front.

The questions for this section are with you now: women's work after the war, the debate about change, the employment calculation and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: new jobs for women; shells, farms and uniforms; and when the men came home.

## Subtopic: New Jobs for Women

Plain words now. Before the war, many women worked as maids, in mills or in shops. When men went to fight, factories needed workers. The army was short of shells, so women were needed fast.

At first, some bosses did not believe women could do factory work, but women learned quickly and proved them wrong.

Unions worried that women would be paid less and take men's jobs. They agreed that the changes were only for the war.

## Subtopic: Shells, Farms and Uniforms

Hundreds of thousands of women made shells in munitions factories. It was dangerous: TNT turned their skin yellow, and explosions killed many. But the pay was better, and many enjoyed their new freedom.

Some women joined the police for the first time.

Women also drove buses, worked on farms in the Land Army, served in uniform as clerks and drivers, and nursed the wounded.

## Subtopic: When the Men Came Home

When the war ended, most women lost their factory jobs. Women were still paid less than men. But some things changed for good: fewer women went back to being maids, some professions opened up, and some women got the vote in 1918.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The war took men away and created a huge need for workers, so women moved into munitions, industry, transport, farms, offices, the auxiliary forces and nursing. Munitions work was dangerous but better paid. Women were paid less than men, and after 1918 most lost their wartime jobs. Some changes lasted: new attitudes, new kinds of work and, for some women, the vote.
