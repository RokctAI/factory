# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the campaign for women's suffrage in Britain, the leadership of Emmeline Pankhurst, and how the First World War contributed to some women gaining the vote in 1918. The anchor is the contrast between the peaceful suffragists and the militant suffragettes, and the Representation of the People Act of 1918. The governing idea is that the vote was won through decades of campaigning by different groups using different methods, and that women's war work strengthened the case and created the political moment for reform, though full equality in voting came only in 1928; historians debate how much credit belongs to each factor. Explaining the campaign and the results of 1918 and 1928 is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Suffragists: The Peaceful Campaign

Suffrage means the right to vote in political elections. In the nineteenth century, voting in Britain was limited. Reform acts in 1832, 1867 and 1884 gradually extended the vote to more men, but even after 1884 about four in ten adult men could not vote, and no women could vote in elections for Parliament.

Arguments against votes for women were common. Opponents claimed that women's place was in the home, that women were too emotional or not well enough educated to make political decisions, that politics concerned matters such as war and empire which women did not understand, and that most women did not want the vote. Some women also opposed women's suffrage, and an Anti-Suffrage League was formed in 1908.

Supporters argued that women paid taxes and obeyed laws, so they should help to make them; that women were as capable as men; that laws on issues such as education, health, work and marriage affected women directly; and that women could already vote in some local elections and serve on school boards, showing that they could take part in public life.

In 1897 several women's suffrage societies joined together to form the National Union of Women's Suffrage Societies, the NUWSS, led by Millicent Fawcett. Its members were called suffragists. They used peaceful, lawful methods: petitions, public meetings, leaflets, letters to newspapers, lobbying members of Parliament and peaceful marches. The NUWSS grew into the largest suffrage organisation, with more than fifty thousand members by 1914, and it built support among Liberal and Labour politicians.

The campaign was not only for wealthy women. In the north of England, women textile workers organised their own petitions and campaigns, and some working-class women spoke at factory gates and trade union meetings. These radical suffragists linked the vote to better wages and working conditions. Their stories were often left out of older histories that focused on famous leaders, but they show that support for women's suffrage was broad.

The suffragists believed that by being patient, reasonable and law-abiding, they would show that women deserved the vote and would gradually win over public opinion and Parliament. Several bills to give women the vote were debated in Parliament, and some passed early stages, but none became law before the war. Critics within the movement felt that decades of peaceful campaigning had achieved too little.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of suffrage, arguments for and against votes for women, and the peaceful suffragists.

## Subtopic: Emmeline Pankhurst and the Suffragettes

Emmeline Pankhurst was born in Manchester in 1858 into a family that supported political reform. She married Richard Pankhurst, a lawyer who supported women's rights, and worked in local government, where she saw the poverty and hardship faced by many women. Frustrated by the slow progress of peaceful campaigning, in 1903 she founded the Women's Social and Political Union, the WSPU, in Manchester, with her daughters Christabel and Sylvia among its early leaders. Its motto was Deeds, not words.

In 1906 a newspaper, the Daily Mail, called WSPU members suffragettes, intending to mock them, but they proudly adopted the name.

At first the WSPU interrupted political meetings, held rallies and marched. When the government still refused to act, the WSPU turned to increasingly militant methods: chaining themselves to railings, smashing windows of shops and government buildings, cutting telegraph wires, attacking post boxes, and later setting fire to empty buildings and churches. Emmeline Pankhurst argued that women had been driven to these actions because peaceful methods had failed, and she was imprisoned many times.

Hunger strikes. From 1909, imprisoned suffragettes went on hunger strike, refusing food to protest against being treated as ordinary criminals rather than political prisoners. To prevent them from dying and becoming martyrs, the government ordered force-feeding, in which women were held down and fed through tubes forced into their noses or throats, a painful and dangerous process that shocked many people. In 1913 Parliament passed the Prisoners (Temporary Discharge for Ill-Health) Act, nicknamed the Cat and Mouse Act. It allowed hunger strikers to be released when they became weak and then re-arrested once they had recovered, like a cat playing with a mouse.

On 4 June 1913, Emily Wilding Davison, a WSPU member, stepped onto the course at the Epsom Derby horse race and was struck by Anmer, the king's horse. She died of her injuries four days later. Her exact intention, whether to attach a suffrage flag to the horse or something else, is still debated, but her funeral in London was attended by thousands of suffragettes and watched by huge crowds.

Suffragettes were skilled at publicity. They wore the colours of purple, white and green, sold their newspaper on street corners, held huge rallies in Hyde Park and organised processions with banners. Photographs of women being arrested or carried away by police appeared in newspapers across the world. The government also watched them closely: the police used hidden cameras to photograph suffragettes, among the first uses of surveillance photography in Britain.

The effect of militancy is debated. Supporters believed it brought publicity and showed women's determination. Critics, including many suffragists, argued that violence turned public opinion and politicians against votes for women and allowed opponents to claim that women were unfit to vote. The movement also divided: Sylvia Pankhurst worked with working-class women in the East End of London and later split from the WSPU.

The questions for this section are with you now: Emmeline Pankhurst, the WSPU, militant tactics, hunger strikes and the Cat and Mouse Act.

## Subtopic: The War and the Vote

When war broke out in August 1914, the suffrage movement changed course.

Emmeline and Christabel Pankhurst stopped all militant action and gave their full support to the war. The government released suffragette prisoners in an amnesty. The WSPU campaigned for men to enlist and, as you saw in the last lesson, organised the women's right to serve march in 1915 with government funding. Its newspaper was renamed Britannia, and the Pankhursts strongly attacked pacifists and anyone they saw as unpatriotic. Emmeline Pankhurst travelled to the United States and Russia to support the Allied cause.

The truce did not mean that women had given up. Suffrage organisations continued to meet, publish and keep in contact with sympathetic politicians, ready to press their case when the time came.

Not all suffrage campaigners agreed. Sylvia Pankhurst opposed the war and worked to help poor women and families in the East End. The NUWSS under Millicent Fawcett supported the war effort in practical ways, such as relief work, while continuing to argue for the vote, and some of its members opposed the war and joined peace movements.

Several factors created the opportunity for reform during the war. First, women's war work in munitions, transport, farming, nursing and the auxiliary forces showed their contribution and capability, and changed the minds of some opponents. Second, the voting system needed reform anyway: under the old rules, many soldiers fighting overseas would lose the right to vote because they had not lived at the same address for the required time. Politicians wanted all soldiers to have the vote, and once the system was being changed, it was difficult to leave women out. Third, by 1916 the Liberal Herbert Asquith, a long-time opponent of women's suffrage, had been replaced as prime minister by David Lloyd George, who was more sympathetic. Fourth, a Speaker's Conference, a meeting of members of Parliament from all parties, recommended in 1917 that some women should get the vote.

The Representation of the People Act was passed in February 1918. It gave the vote to all men over 21, and to men over 19 who had served in the war. It gave the vote to women over 30 who met a property qualification, or whose husbands did. About 8.4 million women gained the vote. The age limit of 30 was set partly so that women would not become a majority of voters, since many men had died in the war.

Later in 1918 another act allowed women over 21 to stand as members of Parliament. In the December 1918 election, Constance Markievicz was elected but, as an Irish republican, refused to take her seat. In 1919 Nancy Astor became the first woman to sit in the House of Commons.

The questions for this section are with you now: the suffrage movement during the war, the reasons for reform and the Representation of the People Act of 1918.

## Subtopic: Weighing the Causes, Calculation and the Error Museum

Why did some women get the vote in 1918? Historians weigh several factors.

The long campaign of the suffragists built support among politicians and the public over decades and kept the issue alive. The suffragettes brought enormous publicity and showed women's determination, though their violence may also have hardened opposition before 1914. Women's war work demonstrated their value and gave politicians a reason to change their minds without seeming to give in to militancy. The need to reform the voting system for soldiers created the political opportunity. Changes in political leadership helped. Most historians conclude that no single factor was enough, and that the war was a turning point that built on decades of campaigning.

Notice also that many of the women who did the most war work, young working-class women in munitions factories, were under thirty or did not meet the property qualification, so they did not gain the vote in 1918.

Women's votes changed politics gradually. In the years after 1918, Parliament passed laws on matters of concern to many women, such as the Sex Disqualification (Removal) Act of 1919, which opened professions such as law, and laws improving the rights of mothers. Historians debate how much these changes were due to women voters.

Equal voting rights came ten years later. The Representation of the People (Equal Franchise) Act of 1928 gave the vote to all women over 21, on the same terms as men. Emmeline Pankhurst died on 14 June 1928, a few weeks before the act became law.

Compare South Africa. White women in South Africa gained the vote in 1930, partly to increase the white vote and reduce the weight of the few black and coloured voters in the Cape. Most black South African women and men could vote in national elections only in 1994. New Zealand, in 1893, was the first self-governing country to give women the vote.

A calculation. In 1918 the minimum voting age for most men was 21, but for women it was 30, a difference of 30 minus 21, which is 9 years. Equal voting came in 1928, which is 1928 minus 1918, or 10 years later. About 8.4 million women gained the vote in 1918, out of an electorate of about 21.4 million, so women were about 8.4 divided by 21.4, which is about 0.39, or 39 percent of voters.

The error museum. Exhibit one: all British women got the vote in 1918. False: only women over 30 who met a property qualification. Exhibit two: all suffrage campaigners used violence. False: the suffragists of the NUWSS used peaceful methods. Exhibit three: all suffragettes supported the war. False: Sylvia Pankhurst opposed it. Exhibit four: women got the vote only because of the war. False: the war built on decades of campaigning.

The questions for this section are with you now: weighing the causes of reform, the 1928 act, comparisons, the calculations and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Asking Nicely

Plain words now. Suffrage means the right to vote. Before 1918, no women in Britain could vote for Parliament. Some people said women belonged at home.

The suffragists, led by Millicent Fawcett, campaigned peacefully with petitions, meetings and marches. They believed patience would win.

## Subtopic: Deeds, Not Words

Emmeline Pankhurst started the WSPU in 1903. Its members, the suffragettes, used louder methods: smashing windows, setting fires and hunger strikes in prison. The government force-fed them, then passed the Cat and Mouse Act. Emily Wilding Davison died after running in front of the king's horse in 1913.

Suffragettes wore purple, white and green so people would notice them.

Some people think this helped the cause; others think it hurt it.

## Subtopic: Votes at Last

When war came, the Pankhursts stopped the violence and supported the war. Women's war work showed what they could do. In 1918, women over 30 with property got the vote, and all men over 21. In 1928, all women over 21 got the vote, just after Emmeline Pankhurst died.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Suffragists under Millicent Fawcett campaigned peacefully; suffragettes in Emmeline Pankhurst's WSPU used militant deeds, faced force-feeding and the Cat and Mouse Act. In 1914 the WSPU stopped militancy and backed the war. War work, the need to reform votes for soldiers and new leaders led to the 1918 act: women over 30 with property. Equal votes came in 1928. In South Africa, white women voted from 1930 and all adults from 1994.
