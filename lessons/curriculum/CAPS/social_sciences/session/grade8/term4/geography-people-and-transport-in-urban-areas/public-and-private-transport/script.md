# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines how people travel in urban areas, comparing public transport, namely buses, trains and minibus taxis, with private transport, namely cars, bicycles and walking. The anchor is the daily journey to work in a South African city. The governing idea is that the way people travel depends on income, distance, cost, safety, time and the services available, and that apartheid planning, which placed many black South Africans far from city centres, still shapes long and costly commutes today. Describing the forms of urban transport and their advantages and disadvantages is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Public Transport: Buses and Trains

Public transport is transport that is available to the general public, usually for a fare, on set routes. In South Africa, the main forms are buses, trains and minibus taxis.

Buses. Municipal bus services such as Metrobus in Johannesburg, Golden Arrow in Cape Town and buses in Tshwane and eThekwini run on fixed routes and timetables. Large commuter bus companies, such as Putco in Gauteng, carry workers from distant townships and former homelands to the cities. Many commuter bus services are subsidised by the government, which lowers the fare. Bus rapid transit systems, such as Rea Vaya in Johannesburg, A Re Yeng in Tshwane and MyCiTi in Cape Town, use special bus lanes so that buses avoid traffic jams.

Advantages of buses: they carry many people at once, so the cost per passenger is low; they reduce the number of cars on the road; and they are fairly safe. Disadvantages: they follow fixed routes and timetables, so passengers may wait or walk to a stop; they can be slow in traffic unless they have their own lanes; and services may not reach every area.

Trains. Metrorail commuter trains, run by PRASA, operate in Gauteng, Cape Town, eThekwini and the Eastern Cape. The Gautrain is a fast rail link between Johannesburg, Pretoria and O. R. Tambo International Airport. Advantages of trains: they carry very large numbers of people, they avoid road traffic and they are among the cheapest forms of travel for long commutes. Disadvantages: they only run where there are tracks, stations may be far from homes and workplaces, and in recent years many services have been unreliable because of cable theft, vandalism and broken trains, with overcrowding and safety concerns.

How does a bus rapid transit system work in practice? Passengers buy a card, load money onto it and tap it at a station or on the bus. Stations are often in the middle of the road, with raised platforms level with the bus floor, so people can board quickly, including people in wheelchairs and parents with prams. Trunk routes along the main roads use large buses in dedicated lanes, and smaller feeder buses bring passengers from the surrounding suburbs to the trunk stations. The aim is a service that is fast, safe, frequent and predictable, so that people can plan their day.

Public transport also matters for the city as a whole. One full bus can replace dozens of cars, and one full commuter train can replace hundreds. Fewer cars mean less congestion, less air pollution and less need for parking. That is why cities invest in public transport even when it does not make a profit: the benefit is shared by everyone who uses the roads and breathes the air.

The questions for this section are with you now: buses and trains as public transport in South African cities.

## Subtopic: Minibus Taxis

Minibus taxis are the most used form of public transport in South Africa. Most South Africans who use public transport to get to work or school travel by taxi. A taxi is usually a minibus carrying up to about fifteen passengers, owned privately and run by an owner or a driver who belongs to a taxi association.

The industry grew rapidly in the 1980s, when apartheid controls on transport were relaxed and black entrepreneurs started their own businesses. It filled gaps that buses and trains did not serve, especially between townships and city centres.

Advantages of minibus taxis: they run on many routes, including into townships, informal settlements and rural areas; they run frequently and from early in the morning; they can stop almost anywhere along a route; and they provide work for drivers, owners, rank marshals, mechanics and car-wash businesses. Hand signals are used across the country to show a driver where a passenger wants to go.

Disadvantages: fares can be higher than subsidised buses and trains, and most taxis are not subsidised in the same way. Some taxis are old or overloaded. Unsafe driving and accidents are a concern, and competition between associations over routes has at times led to violence. The government's Taxi Recapitalisation Programme has helped owners replace old minibuses with newer, safer vehicles.

Taxi ranks are often next to train stations, bus terminals and shopping centres, so passengers can change from one mode to another. In this way the taxi industry links the whole public transport system together.

Taxi ranks are busy places of work. Rank marshals organise queues, make sure taxis leave in turn and help passengers find the right vehicle. A taxi usually waits until it is full before it leaves the rank, which means passengers may wait during quiet times but travel quickly once the vehicle departs. Fares are collected by passing money forward from passenger to passenger, and someone sitting near the front often helps the driver count change. This shows how much the system depends on trust and cooperation among strangers.

The industry is a major part of the economy. It employs hundreds of thousands of people directly and supports many more in related businesses such as fuel stations, panel beaters, insurance and food sellers at ranks. Because most taxis are not formally subsidised, owners must cover fuel, maintenance, insurance and vehicle repayments from fares. When the fuel price rises, fares usually rise soon afterwards, and that affects the budgets of millions of households.

The questions for this section are with you now: the minibus taxi industry, its advantages and disadvantages.

## Subtopic: Private Transport: Cars, Bicycles and Walking

Private transport is transport owned or controlled by the traveller, such as a private car, a motorcycle or a bicycle. Walking is also a form of private transport, and for millions of South Africans it is the main way to travel.

Cars. Advantages: a car offers comfort, privacy and freedom to travel at any time and to any place, door to door, and it can carry shopping and children easily. Disadvantages: cars are expensive to buy, insure, fuel and maintain; most carry only one or two people, so they fill the roads quickly and cause congestion; they need parking; and they produce air pollution and greenhouse gases. Car ownership is much higher among wealthier households, so private car use reflects inequality in South African cities.

Bicycles. Advantages: they are cheap, need no fuel, produce no pollution and give exercise; in busy streets a bicycle can be faster than a car over short distances. Disadvantages: cycling can be dangerous where there are no cycle lanes, distances in South African cities are often long, the weather and hills can make it hard, and bicycles can be stolen.

Walking. Many people, especially learners and low-income workers, walk long distances because they cannot afford fares. Walking is free and healthy, but it takes time and can be unsafe along busy roads without pavements or in poorly lit areas at night.

Why do different people choose differently? Choice depends on income, the distance to work, what transport is available nearby, the cost of fares, travel time, safety and comfort. A worker living far from the city may need two or three different modes, for example walking to a taxi rank, then a taxi to a train station and a train to the city.

Non-motorised transport is the name planners give to walking and cycling. Many South African cities now include pavements, cycle lanes and safe crossings in their plans, because these modes are cheap, healthy and clean. However, much of the road network was designed mainly for cars, with wide fast roads and few safe places for pedestrians. As a result, pedestrians make up a large share of the people killed on South African roads. Better street design, lighting and lower speeds near schools and shops can save lives.

Motorcycles and scooters are another private option. They are cheaper to run than cars and move through traffic easily, and many delivery workers in cities use them. Their riders, however, are much less protected in crashes. E-hailing services, where a passenger books a private car through a cellphone application, sit between private and public transport: the car is privately owned, but anyone can hire it for a trip.

The questions for this section are with you now: private transport by car, bicycle and on foot, and why people choose different modes.

## Subtopic: Apartheid Geography, Calculation and the Error Museum

Why are commutes in South African cities so long? Part of the answer lies in history. Under apartheid, the Group Areas Act of 1950 set aside separate residential areas for different race groups. Black South Africans were moved to townships far from city centres and from the jobs in industry, shops and offices. Some were placed in former homelands, even further away, and travelled long distances daily to work. The government subsidised buses and trains to carry workers over these distances.

Since 1994, the law no longer separates people, but the shape of the cities has changed slowly. Many people still live far from where they work, and new low-cost housing has often been built on cheap land at the edge of cities. As a result, many workers spend hours travelling and a large share of their income on transport.

A calculation. A worker pays a taxi fare of R25 each way, travelling to and from work 22 days a month. The monthly cost is 25 times 2 times 22, which is R1 100. If the worker earns R5 500 a month, the share spent on transport is 1 100 divided by 5 500, which is 0.2, or one fifth, which is 20 percent of income.

The error museum. Exhibit one: public transport means only government buses. False: it includes trains and minibus taxis, which are privately owned but carry the public for a fare. Exhibit two: everyone in a city can choose to drive. False: many households do not own a car, and walking or public transport is the only option. Exhibit three: long commutes are only about personal choice. False: apartheid planning placed many people far from jobs, and this pattern remains. Exhibit four: a R25 fare costs R25 a month. False: travelling both ways on 22 days costs R1 100.

Spatial planning since 1994 has tried to change this pattern. Cities aim to build housing closer to jobs, along public transport routes, and to bring jobs, shops and services into townships. Ideas such as transit-oriented development encourage higher-density housing near stations and bus rapid transit routes, so that more people can walk to public transport. Progress has been slow, because land in central areas is expensive and much housing has already been built far away. Understanding this history helps explain why transport is such an important issue for fairness in South African cities, and why the cost of commuting is a big part of household budgets.

Time is another cost. A worker who travels two hours each way spends twenty hours a week commuting, time that could be spent with family, studying or resting. Long trips also start very early, so people leave home in the dark.

The questions for this section are with you now: apartheid geography and commuting, the fare calculation and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: buses, trains and taxis; cars, bikes and feet; and why the trip is long.

## Subtopic: Buses, Trains and Taxis

Plain words now. Public transport is for everyone who pays a fare. Buses run on set routes. Some, like Rea Vaya and MyCiTi, have their own lanes so they skip traffic. Trains carry huge numbers of people, but only where there are tracks, and many have been late or cancelled.

Bus systems often use a card that you tap when you get on. Taxis leave the rank once they are full.

Minibus taxis are the most used. They go almost everywhere, even into townships, and stop almost anywhere. But fares can be high and some taxis are unsafe.

## Subtopic: Cars, Bikes and Feet

Private transport is your own way of travelling. A car is comfortable and goes door to door, but it costs a lot, and many cars with one person each cause traffic jams and pollution.

Walking and cycling are called non-motorised transport. They need safe pavements and lanes.

A bicycle is cheap and clean, but roads without cycle lanes can be dangerous. Many people walk, often far, because they cannot pay fares.

## Subtopic: Why the Trip Is Long

Under apartheid, many black South Africans were made to live far from city jobs. Today many people still live far away, so trips are long and costly. A R25 taxi each way for 22 days costs R1 100 a month.

Today cities try to build homes closer to jobs and near bus and train routes. This takes time, because land near the centre costs a lot. Long trips also cost time: some people leave home in the dark and get back in the dark.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Public transport: buses, trains and minibus taxis, which carry most commuters. Private transport: cars, bicycles and walking. People choose by cost, distance, time, safety and what is available. Apartheid placed many people far from jobs, so commutes are long and fares take a big share of income.
