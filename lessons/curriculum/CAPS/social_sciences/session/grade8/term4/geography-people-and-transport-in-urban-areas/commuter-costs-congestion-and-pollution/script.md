# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines three problems of transport in South African cities: the cost of commuting to households, traffic congestion and the pollution caused by vehicles. The anchor is the morning peak hour on a busy urban highway. The governing idea is that these problems are linked: long distances and poor public transport raise costs and push people into cars and taxis, more vehicles cause congestion, and congestion and vehicle exhaust cause air pollution, noise and lost time, with the poorest commuters often bearing the heaviest burden. Describing the causes and effects of these problems is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: The Cost of Commuting

A commuter is a person who travels regularly between home and work or school. The cost of commuting includes money, time and effort.

The money cost. For households that use public transport, the cost is the fare for every trip, often for more than one mode, such as a taxi to the station and then a train. For car owners, the cost includes fuel, maintenance, insurance, licence fees, parking, tolls on some roads and the repayments on the car itself. When the price of fuel rises, taxi fares usually rise soon after, and the cost of running a car rises immediately.

Studies of South African households have shown that many low-income workers spend a large share of their income on transport, often well above one tenth, and for some more than a fifth. Transport planners generally regard spending more than about one tenth of household income on transport as a heavy burden. The burden falls hardest on the poorest households, who often live furthest from jobs because of apartheid planning and the location of low-cost housing on the edges of cities.

The time cost. Many commuters spend two, three or more hours a day travelling. This lost time cannot be used for family, rest, study or earning extra income. Early starts mean that children may be left alone in the mornings, and tiredness affects health and work.

The cost to employers and the economy. When workers arrive late because of delays, businesses lose productive time. Transport costs also affect where businesses choose to locate and how much they must pay workers.

Consider two households in the same city. The first lives in a suburb close to the CBD, owns a car and spends perhaps forty minutes a day travelling. Its transport costs are high in rand, but they are a small share of a large income. The second lives in a township or settlement thirty or forty kilometres away, uses a taxi and a train, and spends three hours a day travelling. Its transport costs are lower in rand, but they are a large share of a small income, and the time cost is far greater. Comparing households in this way shows why geographers look at transport costs as a share of income rather than only as an amount of money.

School learners are commuters too. Many children walk long distances to school or travel in scholar transport, which can be crowded and unsafe. Government scholar transport programmes in some provinces carry learners in rural areas who live far from the nearest school.

Some commuters respond by walking part of the way, sharing lifts, or choosing a cheaper but slower mode. Others rent a room in the city during the week and return home at weekends, which separates families. In every case, high commuting costs reduce the money and time available for other needs such as food, school costs and savings.

## Subtopic: Traffic Congestion

Traffic congestion happens when there are more vehicles on a road than it can carry smoothly, so traffic slows down or stops. In South African cities, congestion is worst during peak hours, roughly from six to nine in the morning and from four to seven in the afternoon, when most people travel to and from work and school.

Causes of congestion. First, the number of vehicles has grown faster than road space, as more households buy cars. Second, most cars carry only one or two people, so they use a lot of road space per person. Third, many jobs are concentrated in the CBD and a few business nodes such as Sandton, so traffic converges on the same roads at the same time. Fourth, unreliable trains push commuters onto roads. Fifth, accidents, breakdowns, roadworks, broken traffic lights during load shedding, and taxis or buses stopping in traffic lanes can block roads. Sixth, poor road design and narrow old streets in city centres cannot cope with modern volumes. Seventh, heavy trucks on urban highways, especially near ports, add to the load.

Effects of congestion. Commuters lose time, sometimes hours each week. Fuel is wasted because vehicles idle and stop and start. Businesses lose money when deliveries and workers are late. Drivers become stressed and frustrated, which can lead to road rage and accidents. Emergency vehicles such as ambulances and fire engines are delayed. Air pollution increases because idling engines keep burning fuel.

Congestion has a cost that can be estimated. If a commuter loses forty minutes a day in traffic, over 22 working days that is more than fourteen hours a month, almost two full working days. Multiply that by hundreds of thousands of commuters in a city, and the lost time adds up to millions of hours each year. Economists use figures like these to show governments that investment in public transport and better roads can pay for itself through time saved.

Congestion also spreads. When a highway is blocked, drivers turn into side streets, so residential roads fill with fast traffic that was never meant to be there. Local residents then suffer noise, danger to children and more pollution near their homes.

Congestion is also a matter of fairness. Bus and taxi passengers who are stuck in traffic created mostly by private cars lose time without having caused the problem. Dedicated bus lanes are one way to give public transport priority, so that the people who use road space efficiently are rewarded.

Interpreting a traffic graph. If a graph shows the number of vehicles per hour on a road through the day, it usually has two peaks, one in the morning and one in the late afternoon, with lower traffic at midday and at night. These are the rush hours.

The questions for this section are with you now: the causes and effects of traffic congestion.

## Subtopic: Air Pollution, Noise and Health

Vehicles that burn petrol and diesel release exhaust gases and tiny particles into the air. In busy cities, road transport is one of the main sources of air pollution, together with industry, power stations and the burning of coal and wood in homes.

Pollutants from vehicles. Carbon monoxide is a poisonous gas produced when fuel burns incompletely. Nitrogen oxides irritate the lungs and help form smog. Fine particles, especially from diesel engines, are so small that they can be breathed deep into the lungs. Carbon dioxide is a greenhouse gas that adds to climate change. Old, poorly maintained vehicles release more pollution than new, well-kept ones.

Smog is a brown haze that forms over cities when pollutants are trapped near the ground. On the highveld in winter, cold air near the ground can be trapped under a layer of warmer air, which is called a temperature inversion. Pollution cannot rise and spread out, so it builds up, which is why Gauteng often has hazy, polluted air on winter mornings.

Effects on health. Air pollution can cause and worsen asthma, bronchitis and other breathing problems, and it increases the risk of heart disease. Children, the elderly and people who already have illnesses are most at risk. People who live, work or sell goods next to busy roads and taxi ranks breathe more polluted air.

Noise pollution. Constant traffic noise from engines, hooters and brakes can cause stress, disturb sleep and make it hard to concentrate in schools and homes near highways.

Measuring air quality. Monitoring stations in many South African cities measure the amount of pollutants in the air, and the results are compared with national air quality standards. Some areas, such as the Vaal Triangle and the Highveld in Mpumalanga, have been declared air quality priority areas because pollution from industry, power stations, mines, households and vehicles together is so high. Cleaner fuels with less sulphur, regular vehicle testing and moving to electric vehicles and electric trains are some ways to reduce transport pollution.

Other environmental effects. Oil and rubber from vehicles wash off roads into rivers when it rains. Large areas of land are covered by roads and parking, which increases runoff and can add to flooding. Greenhouse gases from transport contribute to climate change, which affects the whole world.

The questions for this section are with you now: air pollution, smog, noise and the health effects of urban transport.

## Subtopic: Linking the Problems, Calculation and the Error Museum

The three problems are linked in a cycle. Long distances between homes and jobs raise commuting costs. Where public transport is poor or unreliable, people who can afford it buy cars. More cars cause congestion. Congestion wastes fuel and increases pollution, and slows buses and taxis too, which makes public transport less attractive, so even more people try to drive. The cycle can also be seen in a single street. A busy road with a taxi rank, many cars and no trees will be noisy, polluted and stressful for the people who live, trade and walk there. The same street with a bus lane, wide pavements, trees and fewer cars will be quieter, cleaner and safer. Geographers studying a city often map where problems are worst, using traffic counts, travel time surveys, air quality readings and interviews with commuters, so that solutions can be targeted where they are most needed.

Breaking this cycle requires better public transport, better land-use planning and fair road use, which is the subject of the next lesson.

A calculation. A car travels 30 kilometres to work and 30 kilometres home on 22 working days a month. That is 30 times 2 times 22, which is 1 320 kilometres a month. If the car uses 8 litres of fuel per 100 kilometres, it uses 1 320 divided by 100, times 8, which is 105.6 litres. At R22 a litre, the fuel alone costs 105.6 times 22, which is R2 323.20 a month, before insurance, maintenance or parking. If four colleagues share one car, each pays about a quarter, R580.80.

The error museum. Exhibit one: congestion only wastes time. False: it also wastes fuel, increases pollution, costs businesses money and delays emergency services. Exhibit two: smog is just fog. False: smog is a haze of pollutants, and it harms health. Exhibit three: the cost of a car is only the fuel. False: insurance, maintenance, licence fees, parking and repayments add much more. Exhibit four: pollution affects everyone equally. False: people living and working beside busy roads and ranks, and children and the elderly, are affected most.

When you write about these problems, avoid blaming individual commuters. Most people choose the best option open to them given where they live, what they earn and what services exist. A worker who drives alone may have no reliable bus or train; a family in a distant township did not choose apartheid planning. The problems are mainly the result of how cities have been planned and built, so the solutions are mainly about planning too: where homes and jobs are located, and what transport links them. This is a balanced, evidence-based way to answer.

The questions for this section are with you now: the cycle linking cost, congestion and pollution, the fuel calculation and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: paying to get to work, being stuck in traffic, and dirty air and noise.

## Subtopic: Paying to Get to Work

Plain words now. Getting to work costs money and time. Bus, train and taxi riders pay fares, often for two or more trips. Car owners pay for fuel, insurance, repairs and parking.

A rich family may pay a lot for its car, but that is a small part of what it earns. A poor family may pay less in rand, but that is a big part of what it earns.

The poorest people often live furthest away, so they pay the most of their wages and spend the longest time on the road. Some leave home in the dark and get back in the dark.

## Subtopic: Stuck in Traffic

Traffic jams happen when there are too many cars for the road. They are worst in the morning and late afternoon. Many cars carry only one person. Accidents, roadworks and broken traffic lights make it worse.

Forty minutes lost every day adds up to more than fourteen hours a month.

Jams waste time and fuel, make people stressed and slow down ambulances. Buses and taxis get stuck too.

## Subtopic: Dirty Air and Noise

Car and truck exhaust makes the air dirty. In winter on the highveld, cold air traps the smoke and fumes near the ground, so a brown haze called smog forms. Cleaner fuels, testing old vehicles and electric trains and cars can make the air cleaner.

Dirty air causes asthma and other lung problems. Traffic noise disturbs sleep and learning.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Commuting costs money and time, and hits the poorest hardest. Congestion comes from too many vehicles, mostly cars with one person, at peak hours, and it wastes time and fuel. Exhaust causes air pollution, smog and noise, which harm health. The three problems feed one another, so better public transport and planning are needed.
