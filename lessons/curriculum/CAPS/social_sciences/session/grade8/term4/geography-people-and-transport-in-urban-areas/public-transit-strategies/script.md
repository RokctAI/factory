# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines strategies that cities use to improve public transport and reduce congestion, cost and pollution: rapid transport systems, subsidies, dedicated bus and cycle lanes, park-and-ride schemes and car-free zones, together with supporting measures. The anchor is South Africa's bus rapid transit systems and the Gautrain. The governing idea is that cities can break the cycle of cost, congestion and pollution by making public transport faster, cheaper, safer and more reliable than driving, and by planning streets and land use for people rather than only for cars. Describing and evaluating these strategies is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Rapid Transport Systems

A rapid transport system is a public transport system designed to move large numbers of people quickly and reliably, usually on its own routes, separated from ordinary traffic. Examples include metro and underground railways, light rail, fast commuter rail and bus rapid transit.

Bus rapid transit, or BRT, uses large buses in dedicated lanes, with stations where passengers pay before boarding and step onto the bus at floor level. South African examples include Rea Vaya in Johannesburg, the first full BRT in the country, which began in 2009; MyCiTi in Cape Town; A Re Yeng in Tshwane; Go George in George; and systems planned or partly built in other cities, such as eThekwini, Rustenburg and Polokwane. Large trunk buses run on main routes, and smaller feeder buses bring passengers from nearby areas to the trunk stations.

Advantages of BRT: it is much cheaper and quicker to build than an underground railway; buses avoid traffic jams in their own lanes; passengers can board quickly; services are frequent and predictable; and the stations and buses are designed to be safe and accessible for people with disabilities. Disadvantages: dedicated lanes take road space from cars, which some drivers resent; systems are costly to run and often need subsidies; routes may not reach every area; and some projects faced conflict with the taxi industry, whose routes they overlapped. Many cities have therefore involved taxi operators as shareholders or operators in BRT companies.

The Gautrain is a fast rail system that opened in stages from 2010, in time for the FIFA World Cup. It links Johannesburg, Sandton, Pretoria and O. R. Tambo International Airport, with feeder buses at the stations. It is fast, clean, safe and reliable, and has drawn many car owners out of their cars on the busy Johannesburg to Pretoria route. However, its fares are higher than other public transport, so it is used mainly by middle- and higher-income commuters, and it covers only a small part of Gauteng.

Around the world, cities such as Bogotá in Colombia and Curitiba in Brazil showed that BRT can carry very large numbers of passengers at a fraction of the cost of an underground railway. South African planners studied these examples before building Rea Vaya and MyCiTi. Lessons included the importance of frequent services, safe stations, good feeder routes and involving existing transport operators from the start.

Rail-based rapid transport, such as a metro or light rail, can carry even more people than BRT, but it costs far more to build and takes many years. For most South African cities, upgrading existing Metrorail lines and building BRT corridors has been seen as more affordable than building new metro systems.

Rapid transport works best where many people travel along the same corridor, for example from large residential areas to a CBD, and where homes and jobs are built close to the stations.

## Subtopic: Subsidies and Making Public Transport Affordable

A subsidy is money paid by the government to lower the price of a service or to keep it running. In transport, subsidies help pay for buses and trains so that fares are lower than the full cost of providing the service.

Why subsidise public transport? First, many workers, especially in low-income households living far from jobs, cannot afford the full cost of travel, and subsidies make it possible for them to get to work. Second, cheaper public transport encourages people to leave their cars at home, which reduces congestion and pollution for everyone. Third, good public transport supports the economy by connecting workers to jobs. Fourth, it is a way of correcting the unfair spatial pattern left by apartheid, which placed many people far from opportunities.

In South Africa, the national government provides grants to subsidise commuter bus contracts, Metrorail through PRASA, and the building and running of BRT systems. Many cities also offer reduced fares for pensioners, learners and people with disabilities, and some offer cheaper off-peak fares to spread passengers through the day.

Challenges of subsidies. Subsidies cost a great deal of public money, which must be balanced against other needs such as health, housing and education. If they are not well managed, money can be wasted. The minibus taxi industry, which carries most commuters, has historically received little direct operating subsidy, so many of the people who most need help do not benefit. Proposals to subsidise taxis or integrate them into planned networks have been discussed for years.

Subsidies can also be targeted. Instead of lowering every fare, a city can give cheaper fares to those who need help most, such as learners, pensioners and the unemployed seeking work, or to people travelling from the furthest areas. Targeting makes public money go further, but it requires a system to check who qualifies, which itself costs money to run.

Integrated fares and tickets are another way to make travel cheaper and simpler. With one card, a commuter can change from a feeder bus to a trunk bus, or from a bus to a train, without paying a full new fare. Many cities around the world use such systems, and South African cities are developing them.

## Subtopic: Bus Lanes, Cycle Lanes, Park and Ride and Car-Free Zones

Cities also change how road space is used, to give priority to people who travel efficiently.

Dedicated bus lanes are lanes reserved for buses, and in some places taxis, during some or all hours. They let public transport move quickly past traffic jams, which makes it more attractive than driving. Cars that use these lanes illegally can be fined. Some cities also allow high-occupancy vehicles, cars carrying several people, to use special lanes, which encourages lift clubs.

Cycle lanes are lanes marked or separated for bicycles. They make cycling safer, encourage people to cycle short distances instead of driving, and reduce pollution. Cape Town, Johannesburg, Tshwane and other cities have built cycle lanes and pedestrian paths, often alongside BRT routes. To work well, cycle lanes must be connected into a network, well lit and protected from cars, and there must be safe places to lock bicycles.

Park and ride is a scheme in which drivers leave their cars in a large parking area on the edge of the city, or at a station, and complete their journey by bus or train. This reduces the number of cars entering the city centre, while still allowing people from areas with poor public transport to use it for part of their trip. Gautrain stations, for example, have large parking areas for this purpose.

Car-free zones are streets or areas where cars are banned, permanently or at certain times, so that only pedestrians, cyclists and sometimes public transport may use them. Pedestrian malls in city centres and open-streets days, when roads are closed to cars for community events, show how streets can be shared. Car-free zones reduce noise and pollution, make streets safer and can help local shops by bringing more people on foot. Some businesses fear losing customers who arrive by car, so planning must include deliveries and access for emergency vehicles.

Safe walking is part of every strategy, because almost every public transport trip begins and ends on foot. Wide pavements, safe crossings, street lighting and shelters at stops make it easier and safer to reach a bus or train. Universal access, which means designing stations, buses and pavements for wheelchair users, older people and parents with prams, ensures that improvements serve everyone.

Other strategies include traffic light coordination, so that green lights follow one another along a main road; congestion charges or tolls in busy areas; flexible working hours and working from home, which spread or reduce peak-hour travel; and planning cities so that homes, schools, shops and jobs are close together.

## Subtopic: Evaluating Strategies, Calculation and the Error Museum

No single strategy solves urban transport problems. The best results come from a package of measures that work together: fast, reliable rapid transport on main corridors; feeder services, including taxis, that link neighbourhoods to the corridors; affordable fares through subsidies and integrated tickets; priority lanes for buses and safe lanes for cyclists and pedestrians; park and ride at the edges; and land-use planning that brings homes and jobs closer together.

Strategies also need time and public support. People change their travel habits slowly, and a new system may be quiet at first until people trust it. Clear information, such as maps, timetables and real-time arrival updates on cellphones, helps build trust. If a service is cut or becomes unreliable, passengers quickly return to cars and taxis, so long-term funding and good management are essential.

When you evaluate a strategy, ask: Who benefits? Who pays? Does it reduce congestion and pollution? Is it affordable for low-income commuters? Does it serve the areas with the greatest need? Is it safe and reliable? What problems might it create?

Consider an example of a package at work. Along a BRT corridor, a city can run frequent trunk buses in dedicated lanes, connect feeder buses and taxi routes to the stations, build safe walkways and cycle lanes to each station, offer park and ride at the outer end, and allow new apartments, shops and offices near the stations. Each measure supports the others: more people living near the stations means more passengers, more passengers make the service worth running often, and frequent services attract even more passengers. This is the opposite of the cycle of cost, congestion and pollution studied in the previous lesson.

A calculation. A lane of road carries about 1 800 cars an hour, with an average of 1.2 people per car. That moves 1 800 times 1.2, which is 2 160 people an hour. A dedicated bus lane carries 60 buses an hour with 80 passengers each, which moves 60 times 80, which is 4 800 people an hour. The bus lane moves 4 800 divided by 2 160, which is about 2.2 times as many people as the car lane, in the same road space.

The error museum. Exhibit one: bus lanes waste road space because fewer vehicles use them. False: although fewer vehicles use them, each bus carries many people, so the lane can move more people. Exhibit two: subsidies are free money. False: subsidies are paid from public funds, so they must be managed well. Exhibit three: one new BRT line will end all traffic jams. False: a package of measures and better land-use planning is needed. Exhibit four: car-free zones only harm businesses. False: they often bring more people on foot to local shops, although deliveries must be planned.

The questions for this section are with you now: evaluating transit strategies, the lane capacity calculation and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: fast buses and trains, cheaper fares, and lanes, parking and car-free streets.

## Subtopic: Fast Buses and Trains

Plain words now. Rapid transport moves lots of people fast. Bus rapid transit buses, like Rea Vaya, MyCiTi and A Re Yeng, have their own lanes, so they skip the traffic. You pay before you get on, and you step straight onto the bus.

The Gautrain is a fast train between Johannesburg, Pretoria and O. R. Tambo airport. It is quick and safe, but its fares are higher than other public transport.

Many other cities around the world use fast buses like these. Most South African cities chose buses because new railways cost much more.

These systems take road space from cars and cost a lot to run.

## Subtopic: Cheaper Fares

A subsidy is government money that makes fares cheaper. It helps poor workers get to work, and it gets people out of cars. Pensioners and learners often pay less.

Some cities give cheaper fares only to those who need them most, like learners and pensioners.

But subsidies use public money, so they must be spent carefully. Taxis carry the most people, but have received little of this help.

One card for buses and trains makes changing easier and cheaper.

## Subtopic: Lanes, Parking and Car-Free Streets

Every trip starts and ends on foot, so safe pavements, crossings and lights matter too.

Bus lanes let buses pass traffic. Cycle lanes make cycling safe. With park and ride, you leave your car at the edge of the city and take a bus or train in. Car-free zones are streets with no cars, so they are quieter, cleaner and safer.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Rapid transport: BRT buses in their own lanes and fast trains like the Gautrain. Subsidies: government money for cheaper fares. Bus and cycle lanes: priority for people who travel efficiently. Park and ride: car to the edge, then public transport. Car-free zones: streets for people. Use them together, and plan homes near jobs. A bus lane can move about twice as many people as a car lane.
