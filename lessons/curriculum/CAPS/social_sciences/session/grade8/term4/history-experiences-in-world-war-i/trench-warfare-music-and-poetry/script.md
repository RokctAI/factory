# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the experience of soldiers in the trenches of the First World War, and how that experience was expressed in songs and poetry. The anchor is the Western Front between 1914 and 1918, a line of trenches from the Belgian coast to Switzerland. The governing idea is that new weapons made defence stronger than attack, producing a war of trenches, stalemate and huge casualties, and that songs and poems are valuable historical sources that show how soldiers' attitudes changed from early enthusiasm to bitterness about the reality of war. Describing trench conditions and analysing a war poem as a source are examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Why the Trenches? Stalemate on the Western Front

In August 1914 the war began as a war of movement. German armies swept through Belgium into France, following the Schlieffen Plan. In September 1914, French and British forces stopped the German advance at the Battle of the Marne, east of Paris. The Germans withdrew to higher ground and dug in. Each side then tried to outflank the other by moving north, in what was called the race to the sea, until the lines reached the coast of Belgium.

By the end of 1914, a continuous line of trenches ran for about seven hundred kilometres from the North Sea coast to the Swiss border. This was the Western Front. For more than three years, it moved very little. This situation, in which neither side can defeat the other or make real progress, is called stalemate.

Why did stalemate happen? The main reason is that new weapons made defence much stronger than attack. Machine guns could fire hundreds of bullets a minute, cutting down soldiers advancing across open ground. Barbed wire slowed attackers, holding them in the open. Artillery, large guns firing explosive shells from kilometres behind the lines, could destroy anything above ground, so soldiers had to dig in to survive. Defenders in trenches were protected, while attackers had to cross open ground under fire.

Generals tried many ways to break the stalemate. They used huge artillery bombardments lasting days before an attack, hoping to destroy the enemy's wire and trenches; but the bombardments warned the enemy that an attack was coming, churned the ground into mud and often failed to cut the wire. They tried new weapons. Germany first used poison gas on a large scale at Ypres in Belgium in April 1915, releasing clouds of chlorine; later both sides used phosgene and mustard gas, and soldiers were issued with gas masks. Britain first used tanks at the Somme in September 1916; early tanks were slow and often broke down, but by 1918 they were more effective. Aircraft were used for scouting, photographing enemy lines and later for fighting and bombing.

The great battles of the Western Front, such as Verdun and the Somme in 1916 and Passchendaele in 1917, killed and wounded hundreds of thousands of men on each side, often for gains of only a few kilometres. Movement returned only in 1918, with a German offensive in spring and an Allied counter-offensive from August that pushed the Germans back until the armistice.

## Subtopic: Life in the Trenches

A trench system had several lines. The front-line trench faced the enemy. Behind it were support trenches and, further back, reserve trenches. Communication trenches ran from front to back, allowing men, food, ammunition and messages to move without crossing open ground. Trenches were dug in a zigzag pattern, so that an enemy who entered one section could not fire straight along the whole trench, and so that a shell burst would be contained.

Trenches had a fire step, a raised ledge on which soldiers stood to fire or keep watch over the top; a parapet of sandbags facing the enemy; duckboards, wooden slats laid on the muddy floor; and dugouts, holes cut into the sides or underground for shelter. In front of the trench were belts of barbed wire. The land between the two front lines, which could be anywhere from a few dozen metres to several hundred metres wide, was called no man's land.

Conditions were terrible. In wet weather, trenches filled with water and mud. Standing in cold water for days caused trench foot, in which the feet swelled and could become infected, sometimes requiring amputation. Rats, feeding on food scraps and dead bodies, were everywhere. Lice lived in clothing and caused itching and trench fever. The smell of mud, latrines, chloride of lime and decaying bodies was overwhelming. Food was often tinned, such as bully beef and hard biscuits, and was cold by the time it reached the front.

Daily routine. Soldiers stood to at dawn and dusk, when attacks were most likely, then cleaned weapons, repaired trenches, carried supplies and tried to sleep. At night, men went out into no man's land to repair wire, listen for enemy activity or raid enemy trenches. Much of the time was boredom, broken by sudden terror during shelling or attacks. Snipers shot anyone who raised their head above the parapet.

Soldiers from many countries and backgrounds shared these conditions. Alongside British, French and German soldiers were men from India, Canada, Australia, New Zealand and South Africa, and colonial troops from French West and North Africa. Labour units, including the South African Native Labour Contingent and the Chinese Labour Corps, worked behind the lines, unloading ships, building roads and railways and carrying supplies, often under shellfire. Their experiences were part of the war too, although they were less often recorded in famous poems and memoirs.

Units were rotated, spending several days in the front line, then time in support and reserve trenches, and then rest behind the lines, though the pattern varied.

The constant danger, noise of shelling and sight of death caused severe psychological trauma. At the time it was called shell shock. Some sufferers shook uncontrollably, could not speak or relived terrifying moments. At first, many officers saw it as weakness or cowardice; some soldiers were even executed for desertion or cowardice when they may have been suffering from trauma. Today it would be recognised as post-traumatic stress.

## Subtopic: Songs and Poetry as Sources

Music and poetry were an important part of soldiers' lives, and they are valuable sources for historians.

Songs. Soldiers marched, worked and relaxed to songs. Some were popular music-hall songs from home, such as It's a Long Way to Tipperary, which became a marching song in 1914, and Pack Up Your Troubles in Your Old Kit-Bag, published in 1915, with its cheerful chorus urging soldiers to smile. Keep the Home Fires Burning, written by Ivor Novello in 1914, expressed longing for home. Soldiers also made up their own words to familiar tunes, often funny, rude or bitter, mocking officers, army food and the war itself. These parody songs show the humour that helped men cope, and they reveal attitudes that official propaganda hid. Music also helped morale at home, in concerts and on records.

Poetry. Many soldiers wrote poetry, and some of it is among the most famous writing about war.

Early poems often expressed patriotism and idealism. Rupert Brooke's poem The Soldier, written in 1914, begins, If I should die, think only this of me: that there's some corner of a foreign field that is for ever England. It presents death in war as noble and meaningful. Brooke died in 1915 of an infection on his way to Gallipoli, before he experienced the trenches.

In Flanders Fields, written in 1915 by the Canadian doctor John McCrae after the death of a friend near Ypres, describes poppies growing between the crosses of the dead. It made the poppy a symbol of remembrance, worn today on Remembrance Day, 11 November.

As the war went on, many soldier-poets wrote with anger and grief about the reality of war. Siegfried Sassoon, a decorated officer, wrote bitter poems criticising generals and the people at home who did not understand the suffering, and in 1917 he publicly protested against the continuation of the war. Wilfred Owen, who met Sassoon in a hospital for shell-shocked officers, wrote Dulce et Decorum Est, which describes a gas attack in horrifying detail: men blind, lame and coughing, and a soldier drowning in gas. The title comes from a Latin phrase meaning it is sweet and fitting to die for one's country, which Owen calls the old lie. Owen was killed on 4 November 1918, one week before the armistice.

Women also wrote poetry about the war, from the home front and from hospitals. Some, like Jessie Pope, wrote cheerful recruiting verses urging young men to join; Owen originally addressed Dulce et Decorum Est to her. Others, such as Vera Brittain, who served as a nurse and lost her fiancé, her brother and close friends, wrote with deep grief. Their writing shows that the war affected families at home as well as soldiers at the front.

The contrast between Brooke's The Soldier and Owen's Dulce et Decorum Est shows how attitudes changed as the reality of trench warfare became known.

The questions for this section are with you now: soldiers' songs, patriotic and protest poetry, and how attitudes changed.

## Subtopic: Analysing a Source, Calculation and the Error Museum

When you use a poem or song as a historical source, ask the same questions as for any source. Who wrote it, and what was their experience? When was it written, early or late in the war, and before or after the writer saw combat? What was its purpose and audience? What attitude does it show, and what language and images does it use? How reliable and representative is it?

For example, The Soldier was written by a man who had not yet fought in the trenches, early in the war, when enthusiasm was high. It is useful evidence of idealistic attitudes in 1914, but not of trench conditions. Dulce et Decorum Est was written by a soldier who had fought and suffered shell shock, late in the war. It is powerful evidence of experience and anger, though as a poem it uses strong images to persuade. Neither is the whole truth; together they show a change. Not all soldiers shared Owen's view: some remained committed to the war to the end, and letters and diaries show a range of attitudes.

Other creative sources include paintings, photographs, cartoons, trench newspapers and memoirs. Official war artists painted scenes from the front, and some, such as Paul Nash, produced haunting images of shattered landscapes. Soldiers produced their own humorous newspapers, such as The Wipers Times, named after the soldiers' pronunciation of Ypres, which mocked the army and the war. Like songs and poems, these sources show how soldiers saw their own experience, which official reports often did not.

A calculation. On the first day of the Battle of the Somme, 1 July 1916, the British army suffered about 57 470 casualties, which includes killed, wounded, missing and captured. About 19 240 of them were killed. The share killed was 19 240 divided by 57 470, which is about 0.33, or about one third. It remains the worst single day in the history of the British army.

The error museum. Exhibit one: soldiers spent all their time in the front line fighting. False: units were rotated, and much time was spent on routine, work and boredom. Exhibit two: all war poetry was against the war. False: early poems such as The Soldier were patriotic. Exhibit three: tanks were used from the start of the war. False: they were first used in September 1916. Exhibit four: casualties means only deaths. False: casualties include the wounded, missing and captured as well as the dead.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: stuck in the trenches; mud, rats and fear; and songs and poems.

## Subtopic: Stuck in the Trenches

Plain words now. In 1914 the armies dug long ditches called trenches, from Belgium to Switzerland. Machine guns, barbed wire and big guns made it very hard to attack. So neither side could win: that is stalemate.

Armies tried new weapons, like poison gas in 1915 and tanks in 1916. Planes flew over the lines to take photographs and later to fight.

Big battles killed huge numbers of men but moved the line only a little.

## Subtopic: Mud, Rats and Fear

Trenches were wet and muddy. Rats and lice were everywhere. Wet feet caused trench foot. Food was cold and tinned. Between the enemy trenches was no man's land.

Soldiers from India, Africa, Canada and Australia were there too.

Soldiers took turns in the front line. Much of the time was boring, then suddenly terrifying. Many suffered shell shock, which we now call trauma.

## Subtopic: Songs and Poems

Soldiers sang songs like It's a Long Way to Tipperary and made up funny words to old tunes. Early poems, like Rupert Brooke's The Soldier, were proud and patriotic. Later poems, like Wilfred Owen's Dulce et Decorum Est, showed the horror of war.

Pocket summary of the lesson. New weapons made defence strong, so armies dug trenches and stalemate followed on the Western Front. Life in the trenches meant mud, rats, lice, trench foot, boredom, danger and shell shock. Songs helped soldiers cope, and poems show attitudes changing from patriotism, like Brooke, to anger, like Owen and Sassoon. Poems are sources: ask who, when, why and what they show.
