Two stories from 1916 and 1917 sit at the heart of South Africa's memory of the First World War. In July 1916, the South African Infantry Brigade was ordered to capture and hold a small wood in northern France called Delville Wood, at all costs. Six days later, only a fraction of the men walked out unhurt. In February 1917, on a foggy night in the English Channel, the troopship SS Mendi, carrying more than eight hundred men of the South African Native Labour Contingent, was struck by another ship and sank in about twenty minutes. More than six hundred South Africans died. Today we study these two events, what happened, and how they have been remembered, and forgotten, in South Africa.
