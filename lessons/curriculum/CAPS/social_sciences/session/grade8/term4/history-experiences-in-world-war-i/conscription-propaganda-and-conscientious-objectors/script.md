# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines how Britain raised its armies and shaped public opinion during the First World War, through volunteering, conscription and propaganda, and the experience of conscientious objectors who refused to fight. The anchor is Britain between 1914 and 1918, with comparisons to South Africa and other countries. The governing idea is that modern war required the whole society to be mobilised, that governments used both persuasion and compulsion to do so, and that this raised hard questions about the rights of individuals in wartime. Using sources such as posters to explain propaganda, and describing the treatment of objectors, is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Volunteers: Answering the Call in 1914

When Britain declared war in August 1914, it had a small professional army of about a quarter of a million regular soldiers, many of them stationed across the empire. The British Expeditionary Force sent to France was small compared with the conscript armies of Germany, France and Russia. Lord Kitchener, the new Secretary of State for War, was one of the few leaders who believed that the war would be long, and he called for a large new army of volunteers.

The response was enormous. About 750 000 men volunteered by the end of September 1914, and by early 1916 about two and a half million men had joined up. Men volunteered for many reasons: patriotism and a sense of duty; anger at reports of German actions in Belgium; a desire for adventure and to see the world; the belief that the war would be short; pressure from friends, family and employers; and, for some, the promise of regular pay, food and clothing when work was scarce.

Pals battalions were units made up of men from the same town, workplace or sports club, who were promised that they could serve together. They encouraged recruitment, because friends joined together. But when a Pals battalion suffered heavy losses in a single battle, such as on the first day of the Somme in July 1916, a whole town could lose many of its young men in one day.

Many boys under the minimum age lied about their age to join, and recruiting officers, who were paid for each recruit, sometimes accepted them without question. Historians estimate that around a quarter of a million under-age boys served in the British army during the war, some as young as fourteen.

Recruitment also reached across the empire. In South Africa, recruiting meetings, newspapers and posters called for volunteers for service in Africa and Europe. Some black South African leaders, including members of the South African Native National Congress, hoped that loyal service would be rewarded with more rights after the war, and they supported recruitment for the Native Labour Contingent. Those hopes were disappointed, because the government did not extend political rights to black South Africans after the war.

Social pressure was strong. In 1914 a retired admiral, Charles Fitzgerald, started the Order of the White Feather, encouraging women to hand white feathers, a symbol of cowardice, to men not in uniform. Some feathers were given to men who were on leave, who had been wounded or who did essential work, causing great distress.

South Africa did not introduce conscription. All its forces were volunteers, including those who fought in German South West Africa and East Africa and the South African Infantry Brigade in France. Australia also relied entirely on volunteers after voters rejected conscription in referendums in 1916 and 1917.

The questions for this section are with you now: volunteering in 1914, Pals battalions, under-age soldiers and social pressure.

## Subtopic: Conscription in Britain

Conscription is compulsory military service: a law that requires people, usually men of certain ages, to serve in the armed forces. Most European powers had conscription before the war, but Britain had never used it for a major war.

By 1915 the number of volunteers was falling, while casualties on the Western Front and at Gallipoli were very high. The government first tried a scheme in which men were asked to register and declare whether they were willing to serve if called. When this did not produce enough recruits, Parliament passed the Military Service Act in January 1916. It introduced conscription for unmarried men and widowers without children aged 18 to 41, starting in March 1916. In May 1916 a second act extended conscription to married men. Later in the war, the upper age limit was raised to 51.

Some men were exempt. Men in essential war work, such as coal mining, munitions and some farming, could be excused, because their work was vital to the war effort at home. Men who were medically unfit, those who were the only support of dependants, and ministers of religion could also claim exemption. Local tribunals, made up of local officials and a military representative, heard applications for exemption.

Conscription was controversial. Supporters argued that it was fair because it shared the burden of war, and necessary because Britain needed more soldiers to win. Opponents, including many Liberals, trade unionists and pacifists, argued that it went against British traditions of individual freedom, and that no government should force a person to kill against their conscience. In Ireland, then part of the United Kingdom, conscription was not applied in 1916, and an attempt to extend it there in 1918 caused mass protests and helped strengthen support for Irish independence.

In Canada, conscription was introduced in 1917 and caused deep divisions, especially among French-speaking Canadians. These examples show that conscription was a political issue across the British Empire, not only a military one.

Conscription also changed the relationship between the state and the citizen. For the first time in modern British history, the government could order a man to leave his home and job and risk his life. Many people accepted this as a necessary sacrifice in a national emergency; others saw it as a dangerous increase in state power. Similar debates arise whenever countries consider compulsory service, and they are a useful way to think about rights and duties in a democracy.

Conscription helped Britain to raise a very large army. Altogether, more than five million men served in the British army during the war, about half as volunteers and about half as conscripts.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Military Service Acts of 1916, exemptions, tribunals and arguments for and against conscription.

## Subtopic: Propaganda and Censorship

Propaganda is information, often one-sided or exaggerated, that is spread deliberately to influence what people think and do. During the First World War, governments on all sides used propaganda to encourage recruitment, keep up morale, raise money, save food and build hatred of the enemy.

Recruitment posters used strong images and slogans. The Kitchener poster pointed at the viewer. Another famous poster showed a father being asked by his children, Daddy, what did YOU do in the Great War?, making men feel guilty for not joining. Posters aimed at women urged them to send their men to fight. Others encouraged people to buy war bonds, which were loans to the government, or to save food.

Atrocity propaganda described the enemy as cruel and barbaric. After German troops invaded Belgium, there were real killings of civilians and destruction of towns such as Leuven, then called Louvain. But British propaganda also spread exaggerated or false stories, which were sometimes collected in official reports. Germans were portrayed as Huns, savages threatening civilisation. German propaganda in turn portrayed Britain as greedy and hypocritical, and the British blockade as an attempt to starve German women and children.

Censorship is the control of information by the government, preventing the publication of material it considers harmful. In Britain, the Defence of the Realm Act, called DORA, passed in August 1914, gave the government wide powers, including control of newspapers. Reports from the front were censored to hide the scale of losses and bad news, and soldiers' letters home were read and censored. Journalists were at first not allowed near the front at all.

Propaganda was also aimed at neutral countries, especially the United States, because both sides hoped to win American support. Britain cut Germany's undersea telegraph cables early in the war, which gave British news a great advantage in reaching American newspapers. Inside Britain, the government created official bodies to produce propaganda, and well-known writers were recruited to write pamphlets and books supporting the war.

Film was a new form of propaganda. The official film The Battle of the Somme, released in 1916, was seen by millions of people in Britain. It showed real scenes of soldiers, although some scenes were staged.

When you analyse a propaganda source, ask: Who made it? When and why? Who was the audience? What message does it give, and what techniques does it use, such as emotion, guilt, fear, pride or hatred? What does it leave out? Propaganda is valuable evidence of what governments wanted people to believe, even when it is unreliable about the facts.

The questions for this section are with you now: propaganda posters, atrocity stories, censorship under DORA and analysing sources.

## Subtopic: Conscientious Objectors, Calculation and the Error Museum

A conscientious objector, often called a CO or conchie, is a person who refuses to serve in the armed forces because of their conscience, usually for religious or moral reasons. Many British objectors were Quakers, a Christian group opposed to all war, or members of other churches. Others objected for political reasons, for example socialists who believed the war was fought for the benefit of the rich.

The Military Service Act allowed men to claim exemption on grounds of conscience, which was unusual at the time. About sixteen thousand men in Britain registered as conscientious objectors. Their cases were heard by tribunals, which often treated them with suspicion and hostility.

Objectors fell into different groups. Some accepted non-combatant service, such as working in the Non-Combatant Corps, building roads and carrying supplies, or serving as stretcher-bearers and ambulance workers at the front, where many showed great courage. Some accepted alternative civilian work of national importance, such as farm work. Absolutists refused any service that helped the war effort. Many absolutists were arrested, court-martialled and sent to prison, where conditions were harsh. Some were sent to France, where under military law they could be sentenced to death for refusing orders; in 1916 a group of objectors were sentenced to death, but the sentences were changed to ten years' hard labour. Dozens of objectors died as a result of their treatment, and many suffered lasting harm to their health.

Women also opposed the war. Some suffragists and socialist women campaigned for peace, and in 1915 women from several countries, including Britain, met at an international congress in The Hague in the Netherlands to call for an end to the fighting. Organisations such as the No-Conscription Fellowship, founded in 1914, supported objectors, kept records of their treatment and campaigned against conscription. Their work shows that opposition to the war was organised, not only individual.

Objectors were widely despised as cowards or traitors, and some lost their jobs. After the war, they were denied the right to vote for five years. Today many people see them as people of principle, and their stand helped to establish the right to conscientious objection.

A calculation. Conscription in 1916 applied to men aged 18 to 41. Counting both ends, from 18 to 41 there are 41 minus 18 plus 1, which is 24 different ages. When the upper limit was raised to 51, the range from 18 to 51 covered 51 minus 18 plus 1, which is 34 ages, so 10 more ages were included.

The error museum. Exhibit one: everyone in Britain supported the war. False: pacifists, some socialists and conscientious objectors opposed it or refused to fight. Exhibit two: conscientious objectors were all cowards. False: many showed great courage as stretcher-bearers or by enduring prison. Exhibit three: propaganda posters are useless as evidence. False: they show what governments wanted people to think and feel. Exhibit four: Britain had conscription from the start of the war. False: it began in 1916.

The questions for this section are with you now: conscientious objectors, their treatment, the age-range calculation and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Joining Up

Plain words now. In 1914 Britain had only a small army. Lord Kitchener asked for volunteers, and huge numbers joined. Friends from the same town joined together in Pals battalions. Some boys lied about their age.

Posters showed a pointing finger saying Your Country Needs You.

Some women handed white feathers to men not in uniform, to call them cowards. South Africa never forced men to join; all its soldiers were volunteers.

## Subtopic: Forced to Fight, and Persuaded

By 1916 Britain needed more men, so it made a law forcing men aged 18 to 41 to join. That is conscription. Miners and other essential workers could stay home.

The government also used propaganda: posters, films and stories to make people support the war and hate the enemy. It censored newspapers and letters so bad news was hidden.

## Subtopic: Saying No

Some men said their conscience would not let them fight. They were called conscientious objectors. Some carried stretchers or did farm work. Others refused everything and went to prison, where some died.

Pocket summary of the lesson. In 1914 Britain relied on volunteers, answering Kitchener's call; Pals battalions and white feathers added pressure; South Africa used only volunteers. In 1916 conscription began for men aged 18 to 41, with exemptions and tribunals. Propaganda posters, films and atrocity stories built support, and DORA allowed censorship. About sixteen thousand conscientious objectors refused to fight; many did other work, and absolutists were imprisoned.
