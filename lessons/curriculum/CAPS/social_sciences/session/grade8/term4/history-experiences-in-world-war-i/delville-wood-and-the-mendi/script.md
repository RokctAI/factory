# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines two key South African experiences of the First World War: the Battle of Delville Wood in July 1916 and the sinking of the SS Mendi in February 1917. The anchor is the contrast between how these two events were remembered: Delville Wood was honoured for decades as a symbol of white South African sacrifice, while the Mendi, in which mostly black South Africans died, was largely ignored by the government until after 1994. The governing idea is that both events involved great courage and loss, and that how a society chooses to remember its past reflects its values and power relations. Describing both events and explaining how they have been commemorated is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: South Africans on the Western Front

South Africa entered the war in 1914 as a dominion of the British Empire. After the campaign in German South West Africa ended in 1915, the government raised volunteer forces for service overseas. The South African Overseas Expeditionary Force included the 1st South African Infantry Brigade, made up of four battalions of white volunteers from different parts of the country, together with artillery, medical and other units. Its commander was Brigadier-General Henry Lukin.

The brigade first fought in Egypt and Libya in early 1916, against the Senussi, a group allied to the Ottoman Empire. It then moved to France and joined the 9th Scottish Division in time for the great British offensive on the Somme, which began on 1 July 1916.

South Africa's armed forces were segregated. Black South Africans were not permitted to serve as armed combat soldiers in Europe, because the government feared that arming and training black men would threaten white minority rule after the war. Instead, black South Africans were recruited into the South African Native Labour Contingent, the SANLC, formed in 1916. About twenty-one thousand men served in France with the SANLC, unloading ships at ports, cutting timber, building roads and railways, and moving supplies. They lived in closed compounds, similar to mine compounds in South Africa, separated from other soldiers and from French civilians, so that they would not be exposed to ideas of equality.

Coloured South Africans served in the Cape Corps, which fought as armed soldiers in East Africa and later in Palestine against the Ottoman forces. Indian South Africans served mainly as stretcher-bearers and in support units.

Many black South Africans joined the SANLC for reasons similar to those of other volunteers: pay, adventure, loyalty to the British Crown, and the hope, encouraged by some leaders of the South African Native National Congress, that loyal service would be rewarded with political rights after the war. Others were reluctant, and some chiefs and communities opposed recruitment, remembering the 1913 Natives Land Act and other injustices.

The SANLC's work was vital to the war effort. Huge quantities of food, ammunition, timber and equipment had to be unloaded at French ports and carried to the front, and the British army was desperately short of labour. Labourers from China, Egypt, India and other countries did similar work. The men of the SANLC worked long hours, often in cold weather they were not used to, and some were killed by German air raids and shelling behind the lines. Hundreds died from illness, especially pneumonia and other diseases made worse by the cold European winters.

These facts are essential background to both Delville Wood and the Mendi, because they explain who was where, and why their stories were remembered so differently.

## Subtopic: The Battle of Delville Wood, July 1916

Delville Wood was a small forest of about sixty-three hectares near the village of Longueval, on the Somme. In July 1916 it formed a key part of the German defences, and the British wanted to capture it to protect their advance.

On 14 July 1916, the 9th Scottish Division captured Longueval. On 15 July, the South African Brigade was ordered to take Delville Wood and to hold it at all costs. About 3 150 men entered the wood that morning. They quickly took most of it and began digging in among the trees and roots. But the wood stuck out into the German lines, so the Germans could fire into it from three sides.

For the next six days, the South Africans faced counter-attack after counter-attack by German infantry, and some of the heaviest artillery bombardments of the war. At times, hundreds of shells fell on the wood every minute. Trees were reduced to splintered stumps, and the ground was churned into craters. Food and water could hardly be brought in, and the wounded could not easily be taken out. It rained, and men fought in mud among the dead. The fighting was often hand to hand. German troops also suffered very heavy losses.

On the evening of 20 July, the survivors were relieved by other British troops. Only about 780 men answered the roll call, and many of those were wounded. Lieutenant-Colonel William Tanner, who commanded the remnants of the brigade during the final stage, was among the wounded. The wood was not fully captured by the British until the end of August 1916.

Private William Faulds of the 1st South African Infantry was awarded the Victoria Cross, Britain's highest award for bravery, for rescuing wounded comrades under heavy fire at Delville Wood.

Historians use many sources to reconstruct the battle: official war diaries kept by each battalion, casualty lists, letters and diaries of soldiers, photographs and maps, and memoirs written after the war. German records describe the battle from the other side and show how costly it was for them too. Comparing these sources helps historians to check numbers and understand what the soldiers experienced, and to avoid relying only on the heroic story that later grew around the battle.

Delville Wood became a symbol of courage and sacrifice for white South Africans, and especially for the idea of English-speaking and Afrikaans-speaking South Africans fighting together. In 1926, the South African National Memorial was unveiled at Delville Wood, and the anniversary was commemorated every year. A museum was added in 1986. For most of the twentieth century, however, the official memorial honoured the white soldiers and largely left out black South Africans who served in the war.

## Subtopic: The Sinking of the SS Mendi, February 1917

The SS Mendi was a passenger steamship used as a troopship. In January 1917 she sailed from Cape Town carrying 802 men of the 5th Battalion of the South African Native Labour Contingent, together with their white officers and non-commissioned officers and the ship's crew. After stopping in Lagos, Nigeria, and Plymouth, England, she set off across the English Channel to Le Havre in France on the night of 20 February 1917, escorted by a destroyer.

In the early hours of 21 February, in thick fog off the Isle of Wight, the Mendi was struck on the side by the SS Darro, a much larger cargo ship that was travelling at full speed without sounding proper fog signals. The Darro's bow cut deep into the Mendi, which began to sink quickly. In about twenty minutes she was gone.

Many men had been asleep below decks and were trapped. Others reached the deck in the dark and cold. Most could not swim, the water was icy, and there were not enough working lifeboats for everyone. The Darro, badly damaged but afloat, stood by but did not lower its boats to help, and her captain, Henry Stump, did not attempt a rescue. The escort destroyer rescued many survivors.

About 646 people died: 607 black members of the SANLC, 9 of their white officers and non-commissioned officers, and 30 members of the ship's crew. So 616 South Africans died in total.

According to the accounts of survivors, recorded and passed on later, the Reverend Isaac Wauchope Dyobha, a minister serving as an interpreter, called on the men on the sinking deck to be calm and brave, saying that they were dying like brothers, the sons of Africa, and the men stamped a death drill, or dance, on the deck. Historians note that these words reached us through oral tradition and later writing, and the exact words cannot be confirmed; but the story became a powerful symbol of dignity and courage in the face of death.

Survivors were taken to England, and many were later sent on to France to complete their service. The bodies of most of the men were never recovered; they are commemorated by name on the Hollybrook Memorial in Southampton, and a few who washed ashore are buried in cemeteries in England and France. The wreck lies on the seabed off the Isle of Wight and is now protected as a war grave.

A British Board of Trade inquiry found the Darro's captain responsible for travelling too fast in fog and failing to help, and his licence was suspended for one year. Many South Africans felt this was far too light a punishment.

## Subtopic: Memory and Forgetting, Calculation and the Error Museum

Delville Wood and the Mendi were remembered very differently in South Africa for most of the twentieth century.

After the war, the government did not award the British War Medal or the Victory Medal to members of the SANLC, although white South African soldiers and labourers from other parts of the empire received them. The Mendi dead were not given a national memorial in South Africa, and their story was hardly taught in white schools. In black communities, however, the Mendi was remembered in churches, songs, poems and newspapers, and Mendi Day was commemorated on 21 February by organisations including the African National Congress. The poet S. E. K. Mqhayi wrote a famous poem in isiXhosa in memory of the men.

After the first democratic election in 1994, the Mendi became part of national memory. In 1995 Queen Elizabeth II and President Nelson Mandela together unveiled a memorial to the Mendi in Soweto. South Africa created the Order of Mendi Decoration for Bravery as one of its highest national honours, and the South African Navy named a frigate SAS Mendi. In 2016, at the centenary of Delville Wood, the memorial there was rededicated to honour all South Africans who served in the war, including black South Africans and the men of the Mendi.

A calculation. About 3 150 men of the South African Brigade entered Delville Wood, and about 780 answered the roll call afterwards. The share who answered was 780 divided by 3 150, which is about 0.25, or one quarter. So about three quarters were killed, wounded, missing or captured. On the Mendi, 607 SANLC men and 9 officers and non-commissioned officers died: 607 plus 9 is 616 South Africans, and with 30 crew the total was 646.

The error museum. Exhibit one: the Mendi was sunk by enemy action. False: it sank after a collision with a British ship, the Darro, in fog. Exhibit two: black South Africans fought as armed soldiers at Delville Wood. False: the brigade there was white; black South Africans served as unarmed labourers in the SANLC. Exhibit three: the Mendi has always been part of South Africa's official memory. False: it was largely ignored by the government until after 1994, though black communities kept its memory alive. Exhibit four: Delville Wood was won in one day. False: the South Africans held on for six days of heavy fighting, and the wood was only fully taken at the end of August.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: Delville Wood, the Mendi, and remembering.

## Subtopic: Delville Wood

Plain words now. In July 1916, white South African soldiers were told to take a wood in France called Delville Wood and hold it at all costs. The trees were blown to pieces, and the men fought in mud.

They held it for six days under terrible shelling and attacks. About 3 150 went in; only about 780 answered the roll call afterwards.

Black South Africans were not allowed to carry guns in Europe. They worked in the Native Labour Contingent instead.

## Subtopic: The Mendi

On 21 February 1917, the troopship Mendi was carrying black South African labourers to France. In thick fog near the Isle of Wight, a bigger ship, the Darro, crashed into it. Most men could not swim, and the sea was icy cold.

The Mendi sank in about twenty minutes. 616 South Africans died.

The story says Reverend Isaac Wauchope Dyobha told the men to be brave, and they danced a death drill on the deck.

## Subtopic: Remembering

For many years, the government honoured Delville Wood but forgot the Mendi. Black communities kept the Mendi's memory alive. After 1994, South Africa began to honour the Mendi too, with memorials and a bravery award.

Pocket summary of the lesson. South Africa fought in a segregated way: white brigades, the Cape Corps and an unarmed black labour contingent. At Delville Wood, July 1916, the brigade held on for six days, and only about one quarter answered the roll call. The SS Mendi sank after a collision on 21 February 1917, and 616 South Africans died. Delville Wood was honoured for decades; the Mendi was largely ignored by the state until after 1994. How we remember shows what a society values.
