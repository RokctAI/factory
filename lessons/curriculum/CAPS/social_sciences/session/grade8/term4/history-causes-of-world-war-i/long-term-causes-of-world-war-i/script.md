# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the long-term causes of the First World War, which began in 1914. The anchor is Europe between 1871 and 1914, when the great powers armed themselves, formed rival alliances, competed for colonies and were driven by strong nationalism. The governing idea is that these long-term causes created tension and made a general war more likely, so that a single crisis in 1914 could set off a chain reaction; historians still debate how much weight each cause deserves and how far any one country was to blame. Explaining these causes with evidence is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Militarism and the Arms Race

Militarism is the belief that a country should build up strong armed forces and be ready to use them to defend or advance its interests. In militaristic societies, the army and navy enjoy great prestige, military leaders have strong influence over government, and war is seen as a normal, even glorious, way of settling disputes.

Before 1914, all the great powers of Europe built large armies. Most of them, including Germany, France, Austria-Hungary and Russia, used conscription, which means that young men were required by law to serve in the army for a period and then remained in the reserve, ready to be called up. As a result, these countries could mobilise millions of men within weeks. Britain was different: it relied on a smaller professional army of volunteers and on its powerful navy.

Military planners prepared detailed war plans. Germany's Schlieffen Plan, drawn up in the early 1900s, aimed to avoid fighting France and Russia at the same time on two fronts. It planned to defeat France quickly by sweeping through neutral Belgium, and then turn east to face Russia, which was expected to mobilise slowly. Such plans depended on speed and railway timetables, which meant that once mobilisation began, it was very hard to stop.

The naval race between Britain and Germany increased tension. Britain, an island with a worldwide empire, depended on its navy to protect trade routes and colonies. From 1898, Germany passed naval laws to build a large modern fleet. In 1906 Britain launched HMS Dreadnought, a fast battleship with big guns that made older battleships out of date. Germany responded by building its own dreadnoughts, and the two countries competed to build more. Each side saw the other's building as a threat.

Military spending rose sharply in the years before the war. Governments raised taxes and borrowed money to pay for soldiers, guns, warships and railways that could carry troops to the borders. In 1913 France increased military service from two to three years, and Germany enlarged its army, each in response to the other. Generals in several countries believed that a war was bound to come and that it was better to fight sooner, before rivals grew stronger. Such thinking made leaders less willing to compromise in a crisis.

An arms race is a competition between countries to build more and better weapons than their rivals. It creates fear and suspicion, because each side believes the other is preparing for war, and it makes war seem more likely and more winnable. It also gives military leaders and arms manufacturers a strong voice in government decisions.

The questions for this section are with you now: militarism, conscription, war plans and the naval arms race.

## Subtopic: The Alliance System

An alliance is an agreement between countries to support each other, often promising to help in war. Between 1879 and 1914, the great powers of Europe formed two rival alliance groups.

The Triple Alliance. In 1879 Germany and Austria-Hungary formed the Dual Alliance, promising to support each other if attacked by Russia. In 1882 Italy joined, making it the Triple Alliance. The German chancellor, Otto von Bismarck, built these alliances partly to keep France isolated, because France wanted revenge for its defeat by Prussia and the German states in the Franco-Prussian War of 1870 to 1871, when it lost the provinces of Alsace and Lorraine.

The Triple Entente. France and Russia formed an alliance in 1894, because both felt threatened by Germany. Britain had followed a policy of avoiding European alliances, sometimes called splendid isolation, but the German naval build-up worried it. In 1904 Britain and France signed the Entente Cordiale, a friendly understanding settling their colonial disputes. In 1907 Britain and Russia reached a similar agreement. Together, Britain, France and Russia formed the Triple Entente. An entente is an understanding rather than a firm military alliance, so Britain was not strictly bound to fight, but it had close links and military talks with France.

How did the alliances contribute to war? They divided Europe into two armed camps, so that a dispute between two countries could quickly involve all their allies. Each side felt surrounded: Germany feared encirclement by France and Russia, while Britain and France feared German power. Alliances could also make leaders take risks, because they expected allies to support them. In 1914, Germany gave Austria-Hungary strong support against Serbia, and Russia supported Serbia, which brought in France and then Britain.

Many agreements were secret, or partly secret, so leaders could not be sure exactly what other countries had promised. Uncertainty bred suspicion. In a crisis, each government had to guess how its rivals and their allies would react, and a wrong guess could be disastrous. Diplomats tried to manage crises through conferences, and several crises before 1914 were settled without war, which led some leaders to believe that the next one would be settled too.

However, historians point out that alliances did not make war automatic. Italy, a member of the Triple Alliance, did not join Germany in 1914 and instead entered the war on the side of the Entente in 1915. Britain's decision to fight was influenced strongly by the German invasion of Belgium, whose neutrality Britain had guaranteed in the Treaty of London of 1839.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Triple Alliance, the Triple Entente and how alliances contributed to war.

## Subtopic: Imperialism and Nationalism

Imperialism is the policy of a powerful country extending its control over other territories, especially by acquiring colonies. As you learned in Term 3, European powers raced to colonise Africa and parts of Asia in the late 1800s.

Imperial rivalry created tension between the great powers. Britain and France had the largest empires. Germany, united only in 1871, came late to the scramble for colonies and wanted what its emperor, Kaiser Wilhelm II, called a place in the sun. German colonies included German South West Africa, now Namibia, and German East Africa, now mainly Tanzania. Clashes over colonies included the Moroccan crises of 1905 and 1911, when Germany challenged French control of Morocco. On both occasions Britain supported France, which strengthened the Entente and increased German resentment. Competition for colonies, markets and raw materials made each power suspicious of the others.

Nationalism is a strong feeling of loyalty and pride in one's nation, sometimes combined with a belief that one's nation is superior to others. In its extreme form, nationalism encouraged people to see war as a way to prove national greatness. Newspapers, schools and popular songs often glorified the nation and the army.

Imperialism also connected Europe's quarrels to the rest of the world. When war came, colonies were drawn in. Soldiers and labourers from India, Africa, Australia, Canada, New Zealand and South Africa served in the war, and fighting took place in Africa as well as in Europe. South African forces invaded German South West Africa in 1915 and fought in German East Africa, showing how imperial rivalry turned a European war into a world war.

Nationalism took different forms. In France, many wanted revenge for 1871 and the return of Alsace and Lorraine. In Germany, pride in the new empire and its industry fed ambition. In Britain, people were proud of their empire and navy. In the Balkans in south-eastern Europe, nationalism threatened the multinational empires. Slav peoples such as the Serbs wanted independence or union with Serbia, and Serbia hoped to unite the South Slavs. Austria-Hungary, which ruled many Slavs, saw Serbian nationalism as a danger to its survival. In 1908 Austria-Hungary annexed Bosnia and Herzegovina, which had many Serbs, angering Serbia and Russia. Russia saw itself as the protector of Slav peoples. The Balkan Wars of 1912 and 1913 left Serbia larger and more confident, and Austria-Hungary more worried.

The Ottoman Empire, which had once ruled most of the Balkans, was growing weaker, and the great powers competed for influence as it lost territory. Austria-Hungary wanted to stop Serbia from growing, Russia wanted influence and access to the Mediterranean through the straits near Constantinople, and Germany built links with the Ottomans, including plans for a railway to Baghdad. Rival ambitions in this region added to the danger, because a crisis there involved the interests of several great powers at once. Small states such as Serbia, Bulgaria and Greece also had their own goals, and they fought each other as well as the Ottomans in the Balkan Wars.

That is why the Balkans were called the powder keg of Europe: a region where a single spark could set off an explosion that involved the great powers.

The questions for this section are with you now: imperial rivalry, the Moroccan crises, nationalism and the Balkans.

## Subtopic: Weighing the Causes, Calculation and the Error Museum

Historians often remember the long-term causes with the letters M, A, I and N: militarism, alliances, imperialism and nationalism. These causes were connected. Nationalism encouraged support for militarism and empire; imperial rivalry led to the ententes; the naval race pushed Britain closer to France; alliances made military plans more dangerous; and Balkan nationalism threatened Austria-Hungary, which was allied to Germany.

Historians do not all agree on which cause mattered most, or on who was most responsible. After the war, the Treaty of Versailles placed the blame on Germany and its allies. Some historians, such as Fritz Fischer in the 1960s, argued that German leaders deliberately risked a major war to expand German power. Others argue that all the powers shared responsibility, or that leaders stumbled into a war that few of them fully wanted, a view expressed in the image of sleepwalkers. A balanced answer explains the evidence for different views rather than blaming one country without reasons.

When you write an essay on causes, avoid simply listing the four letters. Instead, explain how each cause increased tension, give evidence with dates, and then show the links between causes. A strong conclusion might argue that the long-term causes made war likely but not certain, and that decisions taken by leaders in the summer of 1914 turned tension into war. This shows that you understand both causes and choices.

A calculation. Counting the years helps to show how long tension had been building. The Franco-Prussian War ended in 1871, and the First World War began in 1914, so French resentment over Alsace and Lorraine had lasted 1914 minus 1871, which is 43 years. The Triple Alliance was formed in 1882, and the Triple Entente was completed in 1907, which is 1907 minus 1882, or 25 years later. HMS Dreadnought was launched in 1906, 8 years before the war began.

The error museum. Exhibit one: the war had only one cause. False: historians identify several long-term causes, as well as an immediate cause, that worked together. Exhibit two: the alliances forced every member to fight in 1914. False: Italy stayed out at first and joined the Entente in 1915. Exhibit three: Britain had a large conscript army like Germany. False: Britain relied on a smaller volunteer army and a powerful navy before the war. Exhibit four: all historians agree that only Germany was to blame. False: there is debate, and many historians stress shared responsibility.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the causes link together, historical debate, the year calculations and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: guns and ships, two teams, and colonies and pride.

## Subtopic: Guns and Ships

Plain words now. Before 1914, the big countries of Europe built huge armies. Most made young men join the army. This is militarism. Germany had a plan to beat France fast by going through Belgium, then fight Russia.

Countries spent more and more money on soldiers and guns. Some generals thought war was coming anyway.

Britain and Germany raced to build the biggest battleships, like HMS Dreadnought. Each side was scared of the other, so they built even more.

## Subtopic: Two Teams

Europe split into two teams. The Triple Alliance was Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy. The Triple Entente was Britain, France and Russia.

Some promises were secret, so nobody was sure what others would do.

If one country got into a fight, its friends might join. That made a small fight dangerous. But it was not automatic: Italy did not join Germany, and later fought on the other side.

## Subtopic: Colonies and Pride

Countries competed for colonies in Africa and Asia. Germany wanted more, and quarrelled with France over Morocco. That is imperialism.

Because of colonies, soldiers from Africa, India and other places, including South Africa, also fought.

People were very proud of their own country, and some thought it was better than others. That is nationalism. In the Balkans, Serbs wanted their own nation, and Austria-Hungary was afraid.

These four causes built up over many years, from 1871 to 1914, so tension grew slowly but steadily.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Four long-term causes: militarism, big armies and an arms race; alliances, two rival teams; imperialism, competition for colonies; and nationalism, strong national pride, especially in the Balkans, the powder keg of Europe. They worked together, and historians still debate who was most to blame.
