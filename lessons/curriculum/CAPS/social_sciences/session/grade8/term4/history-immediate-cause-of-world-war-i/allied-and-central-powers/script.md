# Part 1 — Expert

This session identifies the two sides in the First World War, the Allied Powers and the Central Powers, and locates them on a map. The anchor is a map of Europe in 1914 to 1918, showing the Central Powers in the middle of the continent, surrounded by the Allies. The governing idea is that the war began between the European alliance blocs but became a world war as more countries joined, as empires brought in their colonies, and as fighting spread across several fronts and the oceans; South Africa entered as part of the British Empire. Naming the two sides, the countries that joined later and the main fronts is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: The Allied Powers

Sometimes the word Entente is used for the Allies in older books.

The Allied Powers, also called the Allies or the Entente Powers, grew out of the Triple Entente. In August 1914 the main Allies were France, Russia and Britain. Serbia and Belgium, both invaded, fought on the Allied side from the start, and Montenegro joined Serbia.

Britain's declaration of war brought in the whole British Empire, including the dominions of Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Newfoundland and the Union of South Africa, as well as India and the colonies in Africa, Asia and the Caribbean. Soldiers and labourers from across the empire served in Europe, Africa and the Middle East. France also drew on its empire, bringing soldiers and workers from West Africa, North Africa and Indochina.

Japan joined the Allies in August 1914, under an alliance it had made with Britain in 1902. It captured German territories in China and the Pacific.

Italy had been a member of the Triple Alliance, but it did not join Germany and Austria-Hungary in 1914, arguing that the alliance was defensive and that Austria-Hungary had started the war. In 1915, after secret negotiations in the Treaty of London, Italy joined the Allies in return for promises of territory from Austria-Hungary. Romania joined the Allies in 1916, Portugal entered the war in 1916, and Greece joined in 1917.

The United States of America stayed neutral at first, although it traded with and lent money to the Allies. In April 1917 it declared war on Germany. Two main reasons were Germany's unrestricted submarine warfare, in which German submarines, called U-boats, attacked merchant ships, including American ships, without warning; and the Zimmermann Telegram, a secret German message proposing that Mexico join Germany against the United States in return for territory. The sinking of the passenger liner Lusitania in 1915, with American passengers on board, had earlier turned many Americans against Germany. The United States called itself an associated power rather than a full ally, but it fought alongside the Allies, and its fresh troops, money and supplies helped them win in 1918.

China also declared war on Germany in 1917, and Brazil and several other Latin American countries joined the Allied side in the later years of the war, although most did not send large forces.

Russia left the war after the revolutions of 1917. The new Bolshevik government signed the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk with Germany in March 1918, giving up large territories.

The Allies had important advantages: larger populations, the resources of overseas empires, the British navy's control of the seas, which allowed them to blockade Germany, and, from 1917, the support of the United States.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Allied Powers and the countries that joined them during the war.

## Subtopic: The Central Powers

The Central Powers were so called because they were located in the centre of Europe, between Russia in the east and France in the west. They grew out of the Dual Alliance of Germany and Austria-Hungary.

Germany was the strongest of the Central Powers, with a large, well-trained army, powerful industry and the second-largest navy in the world. Its leaders included Kaiser Wilhelm II and, later in the war, the generals Paul von Hindenburg and Erich Ludendorff, who came to control much of German policy.

Austria-Hungary was a large, multinational empire ruled by Emperor Franz Joseph until his death in 1916, and then by Emperor Karl. Its army fought against Serbia, Russia and, from 1915, Italy, but it suffered heavy losses and depended increasingly on German help. The many nationalities within the empire were not all loyal, and by 1918 the empire was breaking apart.

The Ottoman Empire, ruled from Constantinople, now Istanbul, joined the Central Powers at the end of October and beginning of November 1914. Its entry opened new fronts in the Middle East and the Caucasus and closed the straits between the Mediterranean and the Black Sea to Allied ships, cutting a key supply route to Russia. To reopen the straits, the Allies attacked the Gallipoli peninsula in 1915, but the campaign failed with heavy losses on both sides, including Australian and New Zealand troops, known as the Anzacs.

Bulgaria joined the Central Powers in October 1915, hoping to gain territory from Serbia, which it had lost in the Second Balkan War. With Bulgarian help, the Central Powers overran Serbia by the end of 1915.

The Central Powers also tried to stir up opposition to the Allies inside their empires. The Ottoman sultan, as caliph, called for a holy war against Britain, France and Russia, hoping that Muslims in their colonies would rise up, but the call had limited effect. Germany supported nationalist and revolutionary movements, for example by helping the Russian revolutionary Lenin return to Russia in 1917.

So there were four Central Powers: Germany, Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire and Bulgaria.

The Central Powers had some advantages. They could move troops quickly between fronts using interior railway lines, and the German army was very efficient. But they faced serious problems. They were surrounded and had to fight on several fronts at once. The British naval blockade cut off imports of food and raw materials, causing severe shortages and hunger in Germany and Austria-Hungary. Their combined population and resources were smaller than those of the Allies, especially after the United States joined.

In autumn 1918, the Central Powers collapsed one after another: Bulgaria signed an armistice in September, the Ottoman Empire in October, Austria-Hungary in early November and Germany on 11 November 1918.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Central Powers, their strengths and weaknesses, and their collapse in 1918.

## Subtopic: A World War: Fronts and Empires

The First World War was fought on several fronts. A front is the line where opposing armies face each other.

The Western Front ran for about seven hundred kilometres from the coast of Belgium through northern France to the Swiss border. After the German advance was stopped at the Battle of the Marne in September 1914, both sides dug trenches, and the front changed little until 1918. Huge battles such as Verdun and the Somme in 1916 and Passchendaele in 1917 cost hundreds of thousands of lives for small gains.

The Eastern Front, from the Baltic Sea to the Black Sea, was longer and more mobile. Germany and Austria-Hungary fought Russia and later Romania. Germany won major victories, such as Tannenberg in 1914, and after the Russian revolutions the front collapsed.

Other fronts included the Italian Front in the Alps, the Balkan Front in Serbia and Greece, the Gallipoli campaign, and the Middle East, where British and imperial forces fought the Ottoman Empire in Mesopotamia, now Iraq, and in Palestine. The war was also fought at sea: the British blockade, German U-boat attacks and the Battle of Jutland in 1916, the largest naval battle of the war.

The war in Africa. German colonies in Africa were attacked by Allied forces. Togoland and Kamerun, now Togo and Cameroon, were taken by British and French forces. South Africa, a dominion of the British Empire, entered the war in 1914. Its government, led by Prime Minister Louis Botha and Jan Smuts, agreed to invade German South West Africa, now Namibia. Some Afrikaners who opposed fighting for Britain, so soon after the South African War, rose in a rebellion in 1914, led by men including Manie Maritz, Christiaan de Wet and Christian Beyers, but government forces put it down. South African forces then conquered German South West Africa by July 1915.

South Africans also fought in German East Africa, now mainly Tanzania, where the German commander Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck led a long guerrilla campaign and did not surrender until after the armistice in November 1918. Many African carriers and labourers died there from disease and hardship.

The war in Africa had serious effects on African people. Hundreds of thousands of Africans were recruited, often by force, as carriers to transport supplies through areas without roads or railways, because horses and oxen died from disease carried by the tsetse fly. Tens of thousands of carriers died from disease, hunger and exhaustion. Villages lost crops and cattle to passing armies. These experiences are an important part of the history of the war, even though they were often left out of older accounts that focused on Europe.

South African troops, white, black, coloured and Indian, served in Europe and the Middle East. Black South Africans served in the South African Native Labour Contingent, but not as armed soldiers. You will study Delville Wood and the sinking of the SS Mendi in a later lesson.

The questions for this section are with you now: the fronts of the war, the war in Africa and South Africa's role.

## Subtopic: Mapping the Sides, Calculation and the Error Museum

When you draw or read a map of Europe in the First World War, use two colours, one for the Allies and one for the Central Powers, and a third for neutral countries. You should see the Central Powers forming a block across the middle of Europe, from Germany through Austria-Hungary and Bulgaria to the Ottoman Empire. Around them are the Allies: France and Belgium to the west, Britain across the sea, Italy to the south, Russia to the east, and Serbia, Romania and Greece in the Balkans. Neutral countries included Switzerland, Spain, the Netherlands, Denmark, Norway and Sweden.

Changing sides and joining late can be shown on a series of maps, one for each year. A map for 1914 shows the original two blocs; a map for 1915 adds Italy to the Allies and Bulgaria to the Central Powers; a map for 1917 adds the United States, far across the Atlantic; and a map for 1918 shows Russia leaving the war and large territories in the east under German control after Brest-Litovsk. Comparing such maps is a good way to understand how the balance of the war changed over time.

Add arrows to show the Schlieffen Plan's attack through Belgium, and lines to show the Western and Eastern Fronts. Mark the British naval blockade in the North Sea. This makes it clear why Germany feared a two-front war and why the blockade was so damaging.

A calculation. The war lasted from August 1914 to November 1918. From August 1914 to August 1918 is 4 years, or 48 months, and from August to November 1918 is another 3 months, so the war lasted about 48 plus 3, which is 51 months. The United States joined in April 1917. From April 1917 to November 1918 is 19 months, so the United States fought for 19 of the 51 months, or 19 divided by 51, which is about 37 percent of the war.

The error museum. Exhibit one: Italy fought with Germany because it was in the Triple Alliance. False: Italy stayed neutral in 1914 and joined the Allies in 1915. Exhibit two: the United States fought from the beginning. False: it joined in April 1917. Exhibit three: the war was fought only in Europe. False: it was also fought in Africa, the Middle East, Asia and at sea. Exhibit four: all South Africans supported joining the war. False: some Afrikaners rebelled in 1914, and black South Africans were not allowed to serve as armed soldiers.

The questions for this section are with you now: mapping the two sides, the length of the war, the United States' share and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: two sides, a war around the world, and colours on the map.

## Subtopic: Two Sides

Plain words now. The Allies were Britain and its empire, France and Russia, with Serbia and Belgium. Later Japan, Italy, Romania, Portugal, Greece and the United States joined them. Russia left in 1918.

The Central Powers were Germany, Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire and Bulgaria. They were in the middle of Europe, surrounded by the Allies.

## Subtopic: War Around the World

The war was not only in Europe. There were fronts in France and Belgium, in Russia, in Italy, in the Middle East, at sea and in Africa.

South Africa was part of the British Empire. Louis Botha and Jan Smuts led South African forces into German South West Africa, now Namibia, and won by July 1915. Some Afrikaners rebelled against joining. South Africans also fought in East Africa and in Europe.

## Subtopic: Colours on the Map

On a map, colour the Central Powers in the middle, the Allies around them and neutral countries like Switzerland and Spain in a third colour. Germany had to fight on two sides, east and west.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Allies: Britain and its empire including South Africa, France, Russia, Serbia, Belgium, then Japan, Italy in 1915, Romania, Portugal, Greece and the United States in 1917; Russia left in 1918. Central Powers: Germany, Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire and Bulgaria, in the centre of Europe. Fronts: Western, Eastern, Italian, Balkan, Gallipoli, Middle East, Africa and the sea. The war lasted about 51 months.
