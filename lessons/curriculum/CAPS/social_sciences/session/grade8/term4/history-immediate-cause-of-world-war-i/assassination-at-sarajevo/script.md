# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the immediate cause of the First World War: the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand at Sarajevo on 28 June 1914 and the July Crisis that turned it into a general war. The anchor is the chain of events from the shooting to Britain's declaration of war on 4 August 1914. The governing idea is that the assassination was the spark that lit the powder keg created by the long-term causes, and that a series of decisions by governments, each reacting to the others, turned a regional crisis into a world war. Distinguishing immediate from long-term causes and sequencing the events of 1914 are examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Background: Bosnia, Serbia and Austria-Hungary

To understand the assassination, we must understand Bosnia. Bosnia and Herzegovina, in the Balkans, had been part of the Ottoman Empire for centuries. In 1878 Austria-Hungary took over its administration, and in 1908 Austria-Hungary formally annexed it, making it part of the empire. This angered Serbia, the neighbouring independent Slav kingdom, because many Bosnians were Serbs, and Serbian nationalists hoped to unite all South Slavs in one state. Russia, which saw itself as the protector of the Slavs, was also angry but was not ready for war in 1908 and had to accept the annexation.

Austria-Hungary was a multinational empire. It included Germans, Hungarians, Czechs, Slovaks, Poles, Croats, Serbs, Slovenes, Romanians, Italians and others. Its rulers feared that if Serbian nationalism succeeded, other nationalities would demand independence too, and the empire would break apart. Some leaders, especially the army chief of staff, Conrad von Hötzendorf, wanted a war to crush Serbia.

After the Balkan Wars of 1912 and 1913, Serbia doubled in size and grew more confident, which increased Austria-Hungary's fears.

Secret nationalist groups worked for South Slav unity. The Black Hand, officially called Unification or Death, was a secret society founded in Serbia in 1911. Its leaders included Serbian army officers, among them Colonel Dragutin Dimitrijević, known as Apis, the head of Serbian military intelligence. In Bosnia, a youth movement called Young Bosnia brought together students who opposed Austro-Hungarian rule. Some of its members were ready to use violence, including assassination, to achieve their goals.

Franz Ferdinand was the nephew of the elderly Emperor Franz Joseph and heir to the throne. He was in Bosnia to watch army exercises. The visit was on 28 June, a day with special meaning for Serbs, because it was the anniversary of the Battle of Kosovo in 1389, a symbol of Serbian national identity. Many Serbian nationalists saw the visit as a provocation.

Security for the visit was weak. The archduke travelled in an open car so that the crowds could see him, the route had been published in advance in local newspapers, and there were relatively few police along the streets. Warnings about possible danger had been given but were not taken seriously enough. These decisions made the conspirators' task much easier.

Ironically, Franz Ferdinand was thought to favour giving the Slavs of the empire more rights, which some nationalists feared would weaken support for union with Serbia.

## Subtopic: The Assassination: 28 June 1914

Members of Young Bosnia, including Gavrilo Princip, received weapons, training and help crossing the border from members of the Black Hand in Serbia. Six young assassins, organised by Danilo Ilić, waited along the route that the archduke's motorcade would take beside the Miljacka River in Sarajevo.

The first attempt failed. As the cars passed, Nedeljko Čabrinović threw a bomb at the archduke's car. It bounced off and exploded under the car behind, injuring officers and bystanders. Čabrinović swallowed poison and jumped into the river, but the poison failed and the river was shallow, so he was arrested. The other conspirators either lost their nerve or could not get a clear view, and the motorcade sped on to the town hall.

After a reception at the town hall, the archduke decided to visit the wounded in hospital. The route was changed, but the driver of his car was not properly informed. He turned into Franz Joseph Street, following the original route. When told of his mistake, he stopped to reverse. Princip happened to be standing nearby. He stepped forward and fired two shots at close range. One struck Franz Ferdinand in the neck, and the other struck Sophie in the abdomen. Both died soon afterwards.

Princip tried to shoot himself but was seized by the crowd and arrested. Because he was under twenty years old, he could not be sentenced to death under Austro-Hungarian law. He was sentenced to twenty years in prison and died of tuberculosis in prison in 1918, before the war ended.

Sources about the assassination include court records from the trial of the conspirators in October 1914, statements by witnesses, newspaper reports and later memoirs. Historians compare these sources carefully, because some accounts were written long afterwards and some were shaped by the political interests of the writers. For example, details such as exactly where Princip was standing, or what he was doing just before the car stopped, vary between accounts. This is a reminder that even a well-known event must be reconstructed from evidence.

Investigations quickly showed that the weapons had come from Serbia and that the conspirators had been helped by Serbian officers. Whether the Serbian government itself knew of the plot has been debated by historians; it seems that the prime minister, Nikola Pašić, had some warning of a plan but did not act effectively to stop it.

Princip later said at his trial that he was a Yugoslav nationalist who wanted all South Slavs to be free from Austria-Hungary, and that he had not intended to kill Sophie; the shot that killed her had been meant for the governor of Bosnia, Oskar Potiorek, who was travelling in the same car. Views of Princip remain divided today. To some Serbs he is a hero who fought foreign rule; to many others, including many Bosnians, he is a terrorist whose act led to a war that killed millions. Historians try to explain his motives and context without simply praising or condemning him.

The assassination shocked Europe. At first many people expected it to be a regional matter between Austria-Hungary and Serbia. Few imagined that it would lead to a world war.

The questions for this section are with you now: the events of 28 June 1914 in Sarajevo and what happened to the assassin.

## Subtopic: The July Crisis and the Road to War

The weeks after the assassination are called the July Crisis. Each decision led to the next, like a row of falling dominoes, although historians stress that at each step leaders made choices.

Austria-Hungary decided to use the assassination to deal with Serbia once and for all. It first asked Germany for support. On 5 and 6 July, Germany promised to support Austria-Hungary whatever action it took against Serbia. This promise is called the blank cheque, because, like a signed cheque with no amount filled in, it gave Austria-Hungary freedom to act knowing it had Germany's backing.

On 23 July, Austria-Hungary sent Serbia an ultimatum, a final list of demands with a short deadline of 48 hours. The demands included suppressing anti-Austrian propaganda and organisations and allowing Austro-Hungarian officials to take part in the investigation inside Serbia. Many people at the time saw the ultimatum as designed to be rejected. Serbia accepted most of the demands, but refused to allow Austro-Hungarian officials to take part in investigations on Serbian soil, because that would undermine its independence.

On 28 July, exactly one month after the assassination, Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia and soon began shelling Belgrade. Russia, as protector of the Slavs and Serbia's supporter, began to mobilise, and on 30 July ordered general mobilisation. Germany demanded that Russia stop, and when it did not, Germany declared war on Russia on 1 August.

Because of the Schlieffen Plan, Germany expected to fight France as well. It declared war on France on 3 August and demanded passage through Belgium. Belgium refused. When German troops invaded Belgium, Britain, which had guaranteed Belgian neutrality in 1839, sent an ultimatum, and when it expired, declared war on Germany on 4 August 1914.

Not every leader wanted a general war. Some diplomats, including the British foreign secretary, Sir Edward Grey, proposed conferences to settle the dispute, and the German Kaiser and the Russian Tsar exchanged telegrams, signed Willy and Nicky, urging each other to keep the peace. These efforts failed, partly because military timetables put pressure on leaders to mobilise quickly before their rivals did, and partly because each side believed the other would back down. Once mobilisation began, generals argued that stopping it would leave their country exposed.

Britain's declaration of war brought in the whole British Empire, including the Union of South Africa, Canada, Australia, New Zealand and India. Within days, most of Europe was at war, and the conflict soon spread to the colonies, the oceans and the Middle East.

The questions for this section are with you now: the blank cheque, the ultimatum to Serbia and the declarations of war.

## Subtopic: Immediate and Long-Term Causes, Calculation and the Error Museum

Historians distinguish between immediate causes and long-term causes. A long-term cause is a condition that builds up over years and makes an event more likely, such as militarism, alliances, imperialism and nationalism. An immediate cause, or trigger, is the event that sets it off, such as the assassination at Sarajevo. Without the long-term causes, the assassination would probably have remained a local crisis, as earlier Balkan crises had been. Without some trigger, the tension might have continued without war, or a different crisis might have set it off.

The July Crisis shows how the long-term causes worked. Nationalism explains the assassination and Austria-Hungary's fear of Serbia. The alliance system explains why Germany backed Austria-Hungary and Russia backed Serbia, and why France was drawn in. Militarism explains the speed of mobilisation and the Schlieffen Plan's attack through Belgium. Imperial rivalry had deepened distrust between Britain and Germany.

Historians also ask whether the war could have been avoided. Some argue that if Germany had restrained Austria-Hungary instead of giving the blank cheque, or if Russia had delayed mobilisation, the crisis might have been settled by negotiation. Others argue that the tensions were so deep that, sooner or later, a crisis would have led to war. Thinking about such questions helps you to understand that history is shaped by both long-term conditions and individual decisions, and that the outcome in 1914 was not inevitable.

A calculation. The assassination took place on 28 June 1914. Austria-Hungary declared war on 28 July, which is 30 days later. Britain declared war on 4 August, which is 7 days after that. So it took only 30 plus 7, which is 37 days, for an assassination in Sarajevo to become a war involving all the great powers of Europe.

The error museum. Exhibit one: the assassination was the only cause of the war. False: it was the immediate cause, but the long-term causes explain why it led to a general war. Exhibit two: Serbia rejected all of Austria-Hungary's demands. False: Serbia accepted most of them but refused to let Austro-Hungarian officials investigate on Serbian soil. Exhibit three: Princip was executed. False: he was too young to be executed under Austro-Hungarian law and died in prison in 1918. Exhibit four: Britain went to war to support Serbia. False: the invasion of Belgium was the main reason given, together with concern about German power and links with France.

The questions for this section are with you now: immediate and long-term causes, the 37-day calculation and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: the shot in Sarajevo, the dominoes falling, and the spark and the powder.

## Subtopic: The Shot in Sarajevo

Plain words now. Bosnia was ruled by Austria-Hungary, but many Bosnian Serbs wanted to join Serbia. On 28 June 1914, the heir to Austria-Hungary's throne, Archduke Franz Ferdinand, visited Sarajevo with his wife Sophie.

The route had been printed in the newspapers, and the archduke rode in an open car.

A bomb missed his car. Later his driver took a wrong turn and stopped. Gavrilo Princip, a young Bosnian Serb, shot both of them. Princip was too young for the death penalty and died in prison.

## Subtopic: Dominoes Fall

Germany promised to back Austria-Hungary: the blank cheque. Austria-Hungary sent Serbia a list of tough demands. Serbia agreed to most, but not all. On 28 July Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia.

Some leaders tried to stop the war with talks and letters, but they failed.

Russia got ready to help Serbia. Germany declared war on Russia, then on France, and marched through Belgium. Britain had promised to protect Belgium, so on 4 August Britain declared war on Germany.

## Subtopic: Spark and Powder

The shot was the spark. The long-term causes were the powder. Historians still ask whether the war could have been stopped if leaders had chosen differently.

Without the powder, the spark might have gone out. It took only 37 days from the shot to Britain joining the war.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Austria-Hungary ruled Bosnia, and Serbian nationalists wanted South Slav unity. On 28 June 1914 Princip shot Franz Ferdinand and Sophie in Sarajevo. Germany gave the blank cheque; Austria-Hungary sent an ultimatum and declared war on Serbia on 28 July; Russia mobilised; Germany declared war on Russia and France and invaded Belgium; Britain declared war on 4 August. The assassination was the immediate cause; the long-term causes explain why it became a world war.
