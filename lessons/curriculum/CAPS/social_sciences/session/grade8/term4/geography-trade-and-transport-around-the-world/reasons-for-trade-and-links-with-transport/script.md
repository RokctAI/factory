# Part 1 — Expert

This session reviews and extends what you learned about trade in Grade 6. The anchor is South Africa's trade with the world: what it exports, what it imports and who its main partners are. The governing idea is that countries trade because resources, climate, skills and technology are unevenly spread across the world, so that each country can gain by selling what it produces well and buying what it lacks, and that trade depends completely on transport, which moves goods between producers and markets. Explaining the reasons for trade, using the terms of trade correctly and explaining the links between trade and transport are examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Why Countries Trade

Trade is the buying and selling, or exchange, of goods and services. Goods are physical products, such as maize, coal, cars or clothing. Services are things people do for others, such as banking, tourism, transport and education. Trade takes place within a country, called internal or domestic trade, and between countries, called international trade.

Countries trade for several reasons.

Resources are unevenly distributed. Minerals, fertile soil, forests, fish and energy sources are found in some places and not others. South Africa has some of the largest reserves of platinum, manganese and chromium in the world, as well as gold, coal and iron ore, but it has very little oil, so it must import most of the crude oil and much of the fuel it uses. Countries with oil, such as Saudi Arabia, Nigeria and Angola, export it.

Climate differs. Some crops grow well only in certain climates. Tea, coffee, cocoa and bananas grow in tropical and subtropical regions; wheat, apples and grapes in temperate and Mediterranean climates. South Africa exports citrus fruit, grapes, apples and wine, and imports rice, much of its coffee and tropical products.

Skills, technology and capital differ. Some countries have the factories, machines, skilled workers and research to make complex products, such as aircraft, computer chips, medicines and machinery. Others specialise in raw materials or simpler manufactured goods.

Specialisation and comparative advantage. Even if a country could produce everything it needs, it is usually more efficient to specialise in what it does best and trade for the rest. This idea is called comparative advantage. For example, South Africa can mine platinum more efficiently than it can manufacture smartphones, so it exports platinum and imports phones.

Demand and choice. People want variety: fruit out of season, foreign foods, different brands, and goods that their own country does not make. Trade gives consumers more choice, often at lower prices.

Trade benefits countries by earning income from exports, creating jobs in export industries and transport, giving access to goods and technology that the country cannot produce, and building relationships between countries. But trade can also create problems: dependence on a few exports whose prices rise and fall, competition from cheap imports that can close local factories, and unequal relationships between richer and poorer countries.

Trade also happens within South Africa every day. Maize from the Free State is milled and sold in Gauteng; fish from the West Coast is sold in Cape Town and inland; fruit from Limpopo reaches supermarkets in every province; manufactured goods from Gauteng factories are sold in rural towns. Internal trade depends on the same transport networks of roads, railways and pipelines as international trade.

The questions for this section are with you now: what trade is, the reasons countries trade and the benefits and problems of trade.

## Subtopic: Imports, Exports and Trading Partners

Exports are goods and services sold to other countries. Imports are goods and services bought from other countries. The balance of trade is the value of exports minus the value of imports. If exports are worth more than imports, a country has a trade surplus; if imports are worth more, it has a trade deficit.

South Africa's main exports are minerals and metals, such as platinum group metals, gold, coal, iron ore, manganese and chromium; vehicles and vehicle parts, made by international car companies in factories in the Eastern Cape, Gauteng and KwaZulu-Natal; machinery; and agricultural products, especially citrus fruit, grapes, apples, wine, nuts and wool. Tourism, in which visitors from other countries spend money in South Africa, is also an important export of services.

South Africa's main imports include crude oil and refined fuels, machinery and electrical equipment, such as computers and phones, vehicles and parts, chemicals and medicines, and some foods, such as rice and wheat.

South Africa's trading partners are spread across the world. China has been its largest single trading partner in recent years. Other major partners include the United States, Germany, the United Kingdom, Japan, India and countries in the Middle East. Trade with other African countries, especially neighbours such as Botswana, Namibia, Mozambique, Zimbabwe and Zambia, is very important for South Africa's manufactured goods.

Countries make trade agreements to make trade easier, by reducing or removing taxes on imports, called tariffs, and other barriers. South Africa is a member of the Southern African Customs Union, SACU, which includes Botswana, Eswatini, Lesotho and Namibia and dates back to 1910, making it one of the oldest customs unions in the world; goods move between members without tariffs. South Africa is also a member of the Southern African Development Community, SADC, which promotes trade and cooperation in the region, and of the African Continental Free Trade Area, under which trading began in 2021, aiming to create a single market across Africa.

World trade is organised partly through the World Trade Organization, which sets rules for international trade and settles disputes between countries.

Some countries depend heavily on a single export. For example, Botswana depends largely on diamonds, and Zambia on copper. When world prices for these products fall, their economies suffer. Countries try to diversify, which means developing a wider range of exports.

The value of trade changes with prices and exchange rates. When the rand is weak against the United States dollar, South African exports become cheaper for foreign buyers, which can increase exports, while imports, such as fuel, become more expensive. When world prices for platinum, gold or coal rise, South Africa earns more from the same amount of exports. That is why the news often reports the exchange rate and commodity prices.

The questions for this section are with you now: imports, exports, the balance of trade, South Africa's trade and trading partners, and trade agreements.

## Subtopic: Trade Depends on Transport

Trade cannot happen without transport. Goods must be moved from where they are produced to where they are sold, sometimes across a town, often across a continent or an ocean. The cost, speed and reliability of transport affect what can be traded, where, and at what price.

Transport and trade grew together through history. Ancient trade routes, such as the trans-Saharan caravan routes and the Indian Ocean trade between East Africa, Arabia, India and China, depended on camels, sailing ships and the monsoon winds. The industrial revolution brought steamships and railways, which carried raw materials to factories and manufactured goods to markets around the world. In the twentieth century, trucks, aircraft and pipelines added new options.

One of the most important changes was containerisation, which began in the 1950s. Goods are packed into standard steel containers of the same size, usually twenty or forty feet long. A container can be lifted by crane from a truck onto a train, from a train onto a ship, and from the ship onto another truck at the other end, without being unpacked. This made loading much faster and cheaper and reduced theft and damage. Ports now measure their traffic in twenty-foot equivalent units, or TEUs. Containerisation is one of the main reasons why world trade grew so quickly after the 1960s.

Most of the world's trade by volume, around four-fifths, travels by sea, because ships can carry enormous loads cheaply over long distances. Air transport carries a small share by volume but a much larger share by value, because it is used for valuable, light or urgent goods.

Transport hubs and networks are essential. Ports, airports, railway junctions and road interchanges are hubs where goods change from one mode of transport to another. In South Africa, the port of Durban is the busiest container port in the country and one of the busiest in Africa, linked by road, rail and pipeline to Gauteng. O. R. Tambo International Airport is the country's main air cargo hub. Landlocked neighbours, such as Botswana, Zimbabwe, Zambia and Lesotho, depend on South African and Mozambican ports and transport routes for much of their trade.

Transport costs affect trade. If transport is slow, expensive or unreliable, because of congested ports, poor roads or broken railways, goods become more expensive and exports less competitive. Improving transport infrastructure is therefore an important way to increase trade.

Trade routes also depend on a few narrow passages, called chokepoints, such as the Suez Canal, the Strait of Malacca and the Panama Canal. When one of them is blocked or unsafe, ships must take longer routes. In recent years, when ships avoided the Red Sea and Suez Canal, many sailed around the Cape of Good Hope instead, which increased shipping traffic along the South African coast.

The questions for this section are with you now: why trade depends on transport, containerisation, sea and air freight, and transport hubs.

## Subtopic: Calculations, Evidence and the Error Museum

Calculations help to describe trade. Suppose, as an example for the method, a country exports goods worth one hundred and ten billion rand in a year and imports goods worth ninety-five billion rand. The balance of trade is one hundred and ten minus ninety-five, which is fifteen billion rand, a surplus. If the next year exports fall to ninety billion rand while imports stay at ninety-five billion, the balance is ninety minus ninety-five, which is minus five billion rand, a deficit. A fall in the price of a major export, such as platinum, could cause this kind of change.

Containers can also be counted. Suppose a container ship carries eight thousand TEUs and unloads one quarter of them at Durban. Eight thousand divided by four is two thousand TEUs. If a container train carries sixty TEUs, two thousand divided by sixty is about thirty-three point three, so it would take thirty-four trains to move all of them inland by rail, because a part-load still needs a train. These example figures show why ports need strong rail and road links.

When answering questions on trade, use the correct terms and give South African or African examples. For example: South Africa has a comparative advantage in mining platinum, so it exports platinum group metals and imports smartphones, which it does not make; these goods travel mainly by sea in containers through ports such as Durban.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: confusing imports and exports. Exports go out; imports come in. Two: saying a trade deficit means a country has stopped trading. It means imports are worth more than exports. Three: saying all trade travels by air because it is fastest. Most trade by volume travels by sea, because it is cheapest for heavy goods. Four: forgetting services, such as tourism, as part of trade. Five: describing trade without explaining the role of transport.

Layout for marks in a trade question: define the key terms, give reasons for trade with examples, describe South Africa's main exports and imports, and explain how transport makes trade possible.

The questions for this section are with you now: the balance of trade calculation, counting containers and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

## Subtopic: Why We Trade

Trade is buying and selling. Countries trade because no country has everything. South Africa has platinum, gold and coal, but very little oil, so it buys oil from other countries. Some fruits grow only in hot places, others only in cool places.

Countries are good at different things. It makes sense to make what you are good at and buy the rest.

Think of it like a school tuck shop swap: you have extra apples, your friend has extra bread, so you swap.

Keep that swap in mind and try the questions for this part: why countries trade.

## Subtopic: In and Out

Exports are things a country sells to other countries. Imports are things it buys from other countries.

South Africa exports platinum, gold, coal, iron ore, cars and fruit like oranges and grapes. It imports oil, machines, phones, medicines and rice. China is its biggest trading partner.

South Africa and its neighbours, Botswana, Eswatini, Lesotho and Namibia, trade without taxes on goods. That is the customs union.

Keep in and out in mind and try the questions for this part: imports and exports.

## Subtopic: Moving the Goods

Trade needs transport. Goods must get from where they are made to where they are sold.

Most goods by weight go by ship, packed into big steel boxes called containers that cranes lift from trucks to trains to ships. Expensive, light or urgent things go by plane. Durban is South Africa's busiest container port.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Countries trade because resources, climate, skills and technology are unevenly spread, and because specialisation is efficient. Exports are sold abroad, imports are bought from abroad, and the balance of trade is exports minus imports. South Africa exports minerals, vehicles and fruit, and imports oil, machinery and technology, and belongs to SACU, SADC and the African free trade area. Trade depends on transport: most goods by volume go by sea in containers, through ports such as Durban.
