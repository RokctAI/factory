# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a case study of a South African harbour: the Port of Durban. The anchor is the port itself, on the Bay of Natal in KwaZulu-Natal. The governing idea is that Durban became the country's busiest port because of its sheltered natural bay, its position on the east coast closest to the economic heartland of Gauteng among South Africa's own ports, and its strong road, rail and pipeline links, and that its efficiency matters for the whole South African and southern African economy. Describing the location, cargoes, ships and transport links of a South African harbour is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Location and Site

The Port of Durban lies on the east coast of South Africa, in eThekwini, KwaZulu-Natal, on the shores of the Indian Ocean. Its site is a large natural bay, the Bay of Natal, sheltered from the open sea by a long, high ridge of vegetated sand dunes called the Bluff to the south, and by a sandspit called the Point to the north. This sheltered bay provided calm water where ships could anchor safely, which is rare on South Africa's straight, exposed coastline.

The bay had one major problem: a sandbar across the entrance, built up by waves and currents moving sand along the coast. In the nineteenth century, ships often had to wait outside for high tide, and some were wrecked trying to cross the bar. Engineers built breakwaters, long walls that extend into the sea, and dredged the entrance, removing sand from the sea floor, so that by the early twentieth century large ships could enter. Dredging still continues today, because the currents keep bringing sand. In the late 2000s, the entrance channel was widened and deepened so that larger, modern ships could enter.

The port's situation, its position relative to other places, is just as important. Durban is the South African port closest by road and rail to Gauteng, the country's economic heartland, about six hundred kilometres away. Its hinterland, meaning the inland area that it serves, includes KwaZulu-Natal, Gauteng, Mpumalanga, the Free State and North West, and extends to neighbouring landlocked countries such as Lesotho, Eswatini, Botswana, Zimbabwe and Zambia. It is also on major shipping routes between Asia, the Middle East and the Atlantic.

The port grew with the economy. It began as a small trading settlement in 1824, grew with the sugar industry of Natal from the 1850s, and expanded rapidly after railways linked it to the Witwatersrand gold fields in the 1890s. In the twentieth century, industry grew around the harbour, and Durban became the main gateway for imports and exports to and from Gauteng.

Today the port is managed by Transnet National Ports Authority, and terminals are operated by Transnet Port Terminals and private companies. The port covers a large area of land and water inside the bay, with dozens of berths, places where ships tie up to load and unload.

A useful way to remember the difference between site and situation is to picture two separate questions. Site asks: what is the ground like exactly where the port stands? At Durban the answer is a large, sheltered bay behind a sandy spit called the Bluff, with flat land around the water that could be dredged and built on. Situation asks: where is the port compared with everything around it? Here the answer is that Durban faces the Indian Ocean shipping routes to Asia and the Middle East, and it is the closest large harbour to the industrial heartland of Gauteng by road and rail. Both questions matter. A perfect bay in the wrong place would stay a fishing village, and a perfect location with no shelter would never become a major port.

## Subtopic: Exports, Imports and Terminals

The Port of Durban handles a greater variety and value of cargo than any other port in South Africa. It is divided into terminals, each specialised for particular cargoes.

Containers. The Durban Container Terminal is the largest container terminal in South Africa and one of the largest in Africa. In a typical recent year it has handled close to three million twenty-foot equivalent units, more than half of South Africa's container traffic. Containers bring in consumer goods, such as clothing, electronics, household goods and furniture, machinery, parts and chemicals, mainly from Asia, especially China, and Europe. Containers carry out manufactured goods, processed food, chemicals and refrigerated fruit.

Vehicles. Durban has one of the largest car terminals in Africa. Cars made in South Africa, including at a large car factory in Prospecton, close to the port, are exported, mainly to Europe, and imported vehicles arrive from Asia and elsewhere.

Liquid bulk. At Island View and other liquid bulk terminals, tankers unload crude oil, petrol, diesel and chemicals, which are stored in large tanks and sent inland, mainly by pipeline to Gauteng.

Dry bulk and break-bulk. A bulk sugar terminal at Maydon Wharf exports raw sugar from the sugar mills of KwaZulu-Natal. Other dry bulk cargoes include grain, which can be imported or exported depending on the harvest, as well as minerals and timber products. Break-bulk cargo, goods that are not in containers, such as steel products, large machinery and timber, is also handled.

Other activities. The port has a fishing harbour, ship repair facilities and dry docks, a cruise ship terminal and a small craft harbour for yachts. The South African Navy and the national sea rescue service also use the bay.

Exports from Durban therefore include vehicles, sugar, citrus and other fruit, chemicals, manufactured goods, steel and some minerals. Imports include consumer goods, machinery, vehicles, fuel and crude oil, chemicals and, in some years, grain. The port earns income from fees charged to ships and cargo, and supports tens of thousands of jobs in shipping, logistics, warehousing, trucking and industry around the harbour.

Notice that a port does not handle one kind of cargo in one place. Each terminal is built for a type of cargo, because the equipment is different. Containers need tall gantry cranes and large stacking yards. Cars need ramps and parking decks. Liquid bulk such as fuel needs pipelines and storage tanks, while dry bulk such as grain or coal needs conveyor belts and silos. When you read a map of the harbour, try to match each terminal to the cargo it serves. This habit helps in an examination, because a question may show a labelled map and ask why a certain terminal is placed beside deep water or next to a railway line.

## Subtopic: Specialised Ships and Transport Links

Different cargoes need different, specialised ships, and the port must be able to handle them all.

Container ships carry thousands of containers, stacked in cells in the hold and on deck. They are loaded and unloaded by ship-to-shore gantry cranes, huge cranes that move along rails on the quay. The size of ships has increased greatly; some modern container ships are too large for many ports, which is why ports like Durban have deepened their channels and berths.

Car carriers, or roll-on roll-off ships, look like giant floating boxes, with many internal decks. Vehicles are driven on and off through ramps at the stern or side.

Tankers carry crude oil, refined fuels or chemicals in sealed tanks, and are connected to shore pipelines at special berths.

Bulk carriers carry loose cargo such as sugar or grain in large holds, loaded by conveyor belts or grabs.

Reefers, or refrigerated ships, and refrigerated containers carry fruit, meat and fish at controlled temperatures.

Tugboats are small, powerful boats that help large ships manoeuvre through the narrow entrance and into their berths, and pilots, experienced sailors who know the port, board ships to guide them in.

A port is only as useful as its links with land transport. Durban is linked to its hinterland by road, mainly the N3 to Pietermaritzburg and Gauteng, and the N2 north and south along the coast; thousands of trucks enter and leave the port every day. It is linked by rail, along the Durban to Gauteng corridor, one of the busiest freight railway lines in the country, with container trains to an inland terminal at City Deep in Johannesburg. It is linked by pipeline: the multi-product pipeline carries petrol, diesel and jet fuel from Durban to Gauteng. And it is linked by air through King Shaka International Airport, north of the city, which handles air cargo and passengers.

Intermodal transport is central to the port's work. A container may arrive by ship from China, be lifted onto a train to City Deep, and then be carried by truck to a shop or factory in Gauteng, all without being opened.

Think of the port as a meeting point of transport modes rather than an end point. Goods arrive by sea and leave by road, rail or pipeline, and the reverse happens for exports. If any one of these links is slow, the whole chain slows down. A container ship can be unloaded quickly, but if trucks queue for hours at the gates or trains are delayed, containers pile up in the yards and the next ship must wait at anchorage. That is why planners speak about the port and its corridors together, especially the busy route between Durban and Gauteng.

## Subtopic: Challenges, Calculations and the Error Museum

Ports face many challenges, and Durban's challenges affect the whole economy.

Congestion and efficiency. When ships must wait outside the port because berths, cranes or trucks are not available, or when containers take a long time to move through the port, costs rise for importers and exporters. In recent years, Durban has had serious delays because of equipment breakdowns, ageing cranes, shortages of skilled operators and problems on the rail line, and ships sometimes waited for days at anchor. Investment in new equipment and partnerships with private operators aim to improve efficiency.

Road congestion and safety. Thousands of trucks use the roads around the port and the N3 every day, causing traffic jams, accidents and pollution in nearby communities.

Space. The port is surrounded by the city, so there is little room to expand. Plans have been made for a new dug-out port on the site of the old Durban airport, south of the city.

Natural hazards. The floods of April 2022 in KwaZulu-Natal damaged port access roads, the rail line and terminals, and stopped operations for days. Storms and rising sea levels are long-term risks.

A calculation shows the scale of the port. Suppose, as an approximate example, that Durban handles two point nine million TEUs in a year. Two million nine hundred thousand divided by three hundred and sixty-five is about seven thousand nine hundred and fifty TEUs every day. If each truck carries one forty-foot container, which is two TEUs, that is about three thousand nine hundred and seventy truckloads a day, if every container went by road. This shows why rail links are so important for reducing road traffic.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying Durban is South Africa's only port. There are eight commercial ports. Two: confusing site and situation. Site is the bay and the Bluff; situation is its position relative to Gauteng and shipping routes. Three: saying a harbour handles only one type of cargo. Durban has specialised terminals for containers, vehicles, liquids and bulk. Four: forgetting the port's links with road, rail, pipeline and air. Five: ignoring the port's challenges.

Layout for marks in a harbour case study: describe the location, site and situation; list the main exports and imports; describe two specialised ships; explain the links with other transport; and discuss one challenge with a possible solution.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: the safe bay, what goes in and out, and the ships, trucks, trains and pipes.

## Subtopic: A Safe Bay

The Port of Durban is on the east coast, in KwaZulu-Natal. It has a natural bay, protected from big waves by the Bluff, a long hill of sand dunes.

Sand keeps building up at the entrance, so the port has to dig it out, which is called dredging.

Durban is the closest South African port to Gauteng, so it handles lots of Gauteng's goods.

Picture a calm bay behind a green hill, with the rough ocean outside.

Keep that bay in mind and try the questions for this part: the location of the port.

## Subtopic: What Goes In and Out

Durban handles containers full of goods like clothes, phones and machines from China and Europe. It sends out cars made in South Africa, sugar from KwaZulu-Natal, fruit and chemicals. Tankers bring in fuel.

Each kind of cargo has its own part of the port: a container terminal, a car terminal, a fuel terminal and a sugar terminal.

Keep those terminals in mind and try the questions for this part: exports and imports.

## Subtopic: Ships, Trucks, Trains and Pipes

Different ships carry different things: container ships, car carriers, tankers, bulk carriers and fridge ships. Tugboats help big ships into the port.

From the port, goods go inland by trucks on the N3, by train to City Deep in Johannesburg, and fuel by pipeline. King Shaka airport is nearby.

The port has problems too: delays, traffic jams and damage from the 2022 floods.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Port of Durban is on a sheltered natural bay protected by the Bluff, kept open by breakwaters and dredging, and is the South African port closest to Gauteng. It is the busiest port in the country, with specialised terminals for containers, vehicles, liquid bulk, sugar and other cargo. It handles container ships, car carriers, tankers, bulk carriers and reefers, and is linked inland by the N3, the railway to City Deep, the fuel pipeline and King Shaka airport. It faces challenges of congestion, efficiency, space and natural hazards.

Keep the busy port in mind, and take the final questions of the lesson: the Port of Durban.
