# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines South Africa's major transport network: national roads, railways, airports and harbours. The anchor is a map of the network, centred on Gauteng and linked to eight commercial ports on the coast and to the neighbouring countries. The governing idea is that the shape of the network reflects the history and geography of South Africa's economy, built to connect mines, farms and industry in the interior to the ports, and that the network serves not only South Africa but the whole southern African region. Identifying the main routes, hubs and ports on a map and explaining the pattern are examinable this term.

## Subtopic: National Roads and Corridors

South Africa has one of the largest road networks in Africa. The most important roads are the national roads, numbered with the letter N, managed by the South African National Roads Agency, SANRAL. Some sections are toll roads, where drivers pay a fee to use the road.

The N1 runs from Cape Town north-east through the Karoo to Bloemfontein, Johannesburg and Pretoria, and on through Polokwane to Beitbridge on the border with Zimbabwe. It is the main route between the Western Cape and Gauteng and to Zimbabwe and countries further north.

The N2 runs along the southern and eastern coast, from Cape Town through Mossel Bay, George, Gqeberha, East London, Mthatha and Durban, and then inland to Ermelo in Mpumalanga. It links the coastal cities and the Garden Route.

The N3 runs from Durban through Pietermaritzburg, up the Drakensberg escarpment at Van Reenen's Pass, through Harrismith, to Johannesburg. It is the busiest freight route in the country, carrying goods between the port of Durban and Gauteng.

The N4 runs from the Botswana border through Rustenburg and Pretoria to Mbombela and Komatipoort, and on to Maputo in Mozambique. Part of it forms the Maputo Development Corridor, which links Gauteng to the port of Maputo.

Other national roads include the N7 from Cape Town to Namibia, the N12 and N14 across the interior, and the N11 to Botswana and Limpopo.

A transport corridor is a main route, or a group of routes, along which goods and people move between important places, often with roads, railways, pipelines and power lines running side by side. South Africa's most important corridor is the Durban to Gauteng corridor, with the N3, a main railway line and the fuel pipeline. Other corridors link Gauteng to Cape Town, to Maputo, to Richards Bay and to the neighbouring countries.

The road network is densest in Gauteng, where many roads meet and urban highways carry millions of vehicles a day, and thinnest in the dry Northern Cape and Karoo, where towns are far apart. Rural roads, many of them gravel, link villages and farms to the main roads. Some rural areas still have poor roads, which makes it hard for people to reach markets, schools and clinics.

Border posts are important points on the road network. Beitbridge on the N1, the busiest border post in southern Africa, links South Africa and Zimbabwe across the Limpopo River; Lebombo near Komatipoort links South Africa and Mozambique; and Skilpadshek and Kopfontein link South Africa and Botswana. Long queues of trucks at border posts can delay goods for days, so governments work on one-stop border posts and faster customs procedures to speed up regional trade.

The questions for this section are with you now: the main national roads, transport corridors and the pattern of the road network.

## Subtopic: Railways and Airports

South Africa's railway network is the largest in Africa, with roughly twenty thousand kilometres of track used by the freight railway. Most of it is operated by Transnet Freight Rail, a state-owned company. The network grew from the 1860s onwards, and it was built mainly to link the ports to the diamond fields at Kimberley and the gold fields of the Witwatersrand, and later to coal, iron ore and other mines, farming regions and industrial centres.

The main lines radiate from Gauteng to the ports: to Durban, to Cape Town via Kimberley, to Gqeberha and East London, and to Maputo. Specialised heavy-haul lines carry bulk exports: the iron ore line from Sishen to Saldanha Bay and the coal line from Mpumalanga to Richards Bay. Lines also connect to Botswana, Zimbabwe, Mozambique, Namibia, Eswatini and beyond, so goods from as far as the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Zambia can reach South African ports by rail.

Passenger rail includes Metrorail commuter services in Gauteng, Cape Town, Durban and Gqeberha and East London, run by the Passenger Rail Agency of South Africa, PRASA; long-distance passenger trains; the Gautrain in Gauteng; and luxury tourist trains such as the Blue Train.

Airports. South Africa has many airports, and the Airports Company South Africa, ACSA, manages the main ones. O. R. Tambo International Airport, east of Johannesburg in Ekurhuleni, is the busiest airport in South Africa and one of the busiest in Africa, a hub for flights to the rest of Africa, Europe, Asia, the Middle East and the Americas, and the country's main air cargo hub. Cape Town International Airport is the second busiest, serving tourism and exports of fruit and flowers. King Shaka International Airport, north of Durban, opened in 2010. Other ACSA airports include those at Bloemfontein, Gqeberha, East London, George, Kimberley and Upington. Lanseria, north-west of Johannesburg, is a privately owned airport. Many smaller airports and airfields serve towns, mines and game lodges.

Airports are located on large areas of flat land, usually on the edge of cities, with good road and sometimes rail links, and away from tall buildings and mountains. Their noise affects nearby residential areas, which is one reason why new airports are often built far from city centres.

Railway gauge, the distance between the two rails, matters for regional trade. South Africa and most of southern Africa use the same narrow gauge, called Cape gauge, of one thousand and sixty-seven millimetres, so trains can run across borders between South Africa, Botswana, Zimbabwe, Zambia, Mozambique and Namibia. The Gautrain, by contrast, uses the wider standard gauge used in Europe and much of the world.

The questions for this section are with you now: the railway network, its history and main lines, passenger rail, and the main airports.

## Subtopic: Harbours and the Shape of the Network

South Africa has eight commercial harbours, managed by the Transnet National Ports Authority. From west to east they are Saldanha Bay, Cape Town, Mossel Bay, Port Elizabeth, Ngqura, East London, Durban and Richards Bay.

Saldanha Bay has the deepest natural harbour on the coast and exports iron ore from the Northern Cape. Cape Town handles containers, especially fruit exports, fishing and ship repair. Mossel Bay serves the offshore gas fields and fishing. Port Elizabeth and the deep-water Port of Ngqura, both in Nelson Mandela Bay, handle containers, vehicles, manganese and other cargo; Ngqura was built from 2002 and opened in 2009 as a deep-water container port. East London exports vehicles from the nearby car factory and handles grain. Durban is the busiest port in South Africa and handles the most containers, as well as vehicles, fuel and many other cargoes. Richards Bay is one of the largest coal export terminals in the world and also handles other bulk cargoes such as minerals and timber.

South Africa's coastline is long, but it has few good natural harbours. Much of it is straight, exposed to strong winds and waves, with river mouths blocked by sandbars. Most ports had to be built with breakwaters and dredging, which is expensive.

Now look at the whole network on a map. Most routes form a pattern like the spokes of a wheel, with Gauteng at the hub, connected to the ports around the coast. This pattern has clear causes. Gauteng grew around the gold mines from 1886 and became the largest industrial and commercial area in southern Africa. Because Gauteng is inland, more than five hundred kilometres from the nearest ports at Maputo and Durban, everything it imported and exported had to travel overland, so roads, railways and pipelines were built to link it to the ports. Mines in other areas, such as iron ore in the Northern Cape and coal in Mpumalanga, also needed links to ports.

The network also extends across borders. Landlocked countries, such as Botswana, Zimbabwe, Zambia, Lesotho and Eswatini, rely on South African roads, railways and ports for much of their trade, and South Africa's network links with ports in Mozambique and Namibia. South Africa is therefore a transport hub for the whole southern African region.

The network is also shaped by relief. The Great Escarpment, the steep edge between the high interior plateau and the coastal lowlands, is a barrier that roads and railways must climb. Passes such as Van Reenen's Pass on the N3, and the long, winding railway lines up the escarpment, show the effort and cost of crossing it. Mountain ranges in the Western Cape, such as the Hex River Mountains, required tunnels and passes too.

The questions for this section are with you now: the eight commercial harbours, the coastline, and the hub-and-spoke pattern of the network.

## Subtopic: Map Skills and the Error Museum

To read a transport map, use the key to identify national roads, railways, airports and ports, and use the scale to measure distances.

A calculation helps with journey times. The road distance from Durban to Johannesburg along the N3 is roughly five hundred and seventy kilometres. Suppose, as an example for the method, a truck averages seventy-five kilometres per hour, including slower sections on the escarpment. Five hundred and seventy divided by seventy-five is seven point six hours, so the journey takes about seven and a half hours of driving, plus rest stops. Drivers must take rest breaks by law, so the real journey takes longer.

On a map with a scale of one to ten million, one centimetre represents ten million centimetres, which is one hundred kilometres. If the straight-line distance between Cape Town and Johannesburg measures about twelve point eight centimetres, the real straight-line distance is about one thousand two hundred and eighty kilometres. The road distance along the N1 is longer, about one thousand four hundred kilometres, because roads follow valleys, passes and towns.

When describing the network, use evidence from the map: name the roads, lines, airports and ports, and describe their direction and the places they connect. Then explain the pattern using history and economics.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying Johannesburg has a harbour. Gauteng is inland, which is why it needs road, rail and pipeline links to the coast. Two: confusing the N1, N2 and N3. Three: saying that all freight goes by rail. Most freight in South Africa goes by road. Four: forgetting that neighbouring countries depend on South Africa's network. Five: describing the pattern without explaining it.

Layout for marks in a transport network question: name at least two major roads, two railway lines, two airports and two ports from the map, describe the pattern, and explain it using the location of Gauteng, mining and the ports.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading transport maps, the journey time and distance calculations and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: roads, trains and planes, and ports.

## Subtopic: Roads Across the Country

South Africa's main roads are called N roads. The N1 goes from Cape Town to Johannesburg and on to Zimbabwe. The N2 runs along the coast from Cape Town to Durban. The N3 joins Durban and Johannesburg, and it is the busiest road for trucks.

Gauteng has the most roads, because it is the busiest area. Places in the dry Karoo and Northern Cape have fewer roads.

Picture the N roads as big red lines across the map.

Trucks to Zimbabwe cross the border at Beitbridge, the busiest border post in southern Africa.

Keep those red lines in mind and try the questions for this part: national roads.

## Subtopic: Trains and Planes

Railways were first built to carry diamonds, gold and other minerals between the mines and the ports. Today they still carry coal to Richards Bay and iron ore to Saldanha Bay. People ride commuter trains and the Gautrain.

O. R. Tambo in Johannesburg is the busiest airport. Cape Town International is second. King Shaka serves Durban.

Trains also cross the borders to Botswana, Zimbabwe, Mozambique and Namibia, because most countries in southern Africa use the same width of track.

Keep the railway lines and the airport in mind and try the questions for this part: railways and airports.

## Subtopic: Ports and the Wheel

South Africa has eight big ports: Saldanha Bay, Cape Town, Mossel Bay, Port Elizabeth, Ngqura, East London, Durban and Richards Bay. Durban is the busiest.

Look at the map: roads, railways and pipelines go from Gauteng to the ports like the spokes of a wheel. That is because Gauteng is inland, and its goods must reach the sea. Neighbouring countries use South Africa's roads and ports too.

Pocket summary of the lesson. South Africa's network has national roads such as the N1, N2, N3 and N4, a large railway network built to link mines to ports, major airports led by O. R. Tambo, and eight commercial harbours led by Durban. The network forms a hub-and-spoke pattern centred on inland Gauteng, linked to the ports, because of the history of mining and industry. It also serves the neighbouring countries.

Remember that roads and railways have to climb the steep Great Escarpment to get from the coast to the high inland plateau, which is why passes like Van Reenen's Pass are so important.

Keep the wheel in mind, and take the final questions of the lesson: South Africa's transport network.
