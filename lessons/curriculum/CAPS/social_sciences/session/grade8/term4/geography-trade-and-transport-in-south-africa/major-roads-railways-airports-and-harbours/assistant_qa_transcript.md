### subtopic_1 — National Roads and Corridors
**Assistant:** Why was Johannesburg built so far from the sea?

**Tutor:** Because it was built where the gold was, not where it would be convenient for transport. Most of the world's great cities grew on coasts, rivers or trade routes, because transport by water was cheap and easy. Johannesburg is different. In 1886, gold was discovered on the Witwatersrand, a ridge of rock on the highveld, about six hundred kilometres from Durban and more than five hundred kilometres from Maputo, and the gold-bearing reefs stretched underground for many kilometres. Thousands of people rushed to the area, and Johannesburg grew from a mining camp into a city within a few years. Because the gold fields were so rich, mining companies and governments were willing to pay the high cost of building transport links to the coast. Railways from Cape Town, Port Elizabeth, East London, Durban and Lourenço Marques, now Maputo, raced to reach the Witwatersrand in the early 1890s, and later roads and pipelines followed. Industry grew around the mines, using the transport links and the large population, and Gauteng became the largest economic centre in southern Africa. This is why South Africa's transport network has such a strong hub-and-spoke pattern centred on an inland city. It is also why South Africa depends so heavily on its long road, rail and pipeline corridors: if the link between Durban and Gauteng is disrupted, by floods, strikes or accidents, goods for the whole economic heartland are affected. It is a good example of how a natural resource can shape a country's geography for more than a century.

### subtopic_2 — Railways and Airports
**Assistant:** Why do neighbouring countries use South African ports instead of their own?

**Tutor:** Several of South Africa's neighbours, including Botswana, Zimbabwe, Zambia, Lesotho and Eswatini, are landlocked, which means they have no coastline and no ports of their own. To import and export goods by sea, they must send them overland through a neighbouring country. Their choice of port depends on distance, the quality of the roads and railways, the efficiency of the port, border delays and cost. South African ports, especially Durban, have long been important for these countries because they are well connected by road and rail, handle a wide range of cargo, and have regular shipping services to the rest of the world. Lesotho, which is completely surrounded by South Africa, has no choice but to trade through South Africa. However, South Africa's neighbours also use other ports. Zimbabwe, Zambia and Malawi use the Mozambican ports of Beira and Nacala, and the Maputo port is close to Gauteng and Mpumalanga, and is used by South Africa itself. Namibia's port of Walvis Bay serves Botswana and Zambia through corridors such as the Trans-Kalahari corridor. Tanzania's port of Dar es Salaam serves Zambia and the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Competition between ports encourages them to be efficient. Membership of SACU and SADC makes cross-border trade easier. For South Africa, serving neighbours brings income and jobs in transport and port services, but it also means that delays or problems at South African ports affect the whole region.

### subtopic_3 — Harbours and the Shape of the Network
**Assistant:** Why are toll roads used and are they fair?

**Tutor:** Toll roads are used to pay for building, improving and maintaining major roads. Instead of paying for the road only from general taxes, the government, or a private company working with the government, charges a fee to the vehicles that use the road. The idea is that the people who benefit most from a high-quality road pay for it, and that the money can be used to build roads faster than the national budget would allow. Many sections of South Africa's national roads, such as parts of the N1, N3 and N4, are toll roads, often built and run by private companies under long-term agreements with SANRAL. Supporters argue that tolls have produced safe, well-maintained highways and that heavy trucks, which cause the most damage, pay more. Critics raise several concerns. Tolls increase transport costs, which may raise the price of goods. People who live near toll roads and must use them every day, including commuters and small businesses, may pay a lot. Poorer travellers may use alternative roads, which can be less safe. In Gauteng, electronic tolling on urban freeways, introduced in 2013, was strongly opposed by many residents and organisations because commuters had no practical alternative, and many did not pay, until the government ended the system in 2024. So the fairness of tolls depends on the situation: tolls on long-distance highways used mainly by freight may be accepted, while tolls on commuter routes without good alternatives are often seen as unfair. That is a good example of a transport issue where there are different points of view.

### subtopic_4 — Map Skills and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do I describe the pattern of a transport network in an exam?

**Tutor:** Use a three-step approach: describe, give evidence, explain. First, describe the overall pattern in one sentence, for example: the network has a hub-and-spoke pattern, with most major routes radiating from Gauteng to the coastal ports and to neighbouring countries. Second, give evidence from the map, naming specific roads, railways, airports and ports and the places they connect: the N3 and the main railway link Gauteng to Durban; the N1 links Gauteng to Cape Town and to Beitbridge; the N4 links Gauteng to Maputo; railways carry coal to Richards Bay and iron ore to Saldanha Bay; O. R. Tambo airport is near Johannesburg. You can also mention where the network is dense, such as Gauteng and around the major cities, and where it is sparse, such as the Northern Cape and Karoo. Third, explain the pattern using geography and history: Gauteng is the country's economic heart because of the gold mines and the industry that followed, but it is inland, so routes were built to connect it to the ports; mining areas such as Sishen and the Mpumalanga coalfields needed dedicated lines to export ports; dry, thinly populated areas have fewer routes because there are fewer people and economic activities; neighbouring landlocked countries depend on the network. Finally, if the question asks, comment on a consequence or a problem, such as congestion on the N3 or the need to move more freight to rail. This structure shows the examiner that you can read the map and understand why the network looks the way it does.
