# Part 1 — Expert

This session compares road and rail transport in South Africa and considers the requirements for future transport networks. The anchor is the Durban to Gauteng corridor, where the N3 highway and a main railway line run side by side. The governing idea is that each mode has strengths and weaknesses, that the choice depends on what is carried, how far, how fast and at what cost, and that a sound future network combines modes so that each does what it does best. Listing advantages and disadvantages, applying them to examples and suggesting improvements are examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Road Transport: Advantages and Disadvantages

Road transport moves goods and people by truck, bus, minibus taxi, car and bicycle along roads. In South Africa, road carries most of the country's freight by tonnage, a share that has grown since the 1990s as rail declined.

Advantages of road transport. First, flexibility: a truck can go almost anywhere there is a road, so it can deliver from door to door, from a factory gate straight to a shop, with no need to load and unload at stations. Second, speed over short and medium distances: for a trip of a few hundred kilometres, a truck is often faster than a train, especially when the train must wait for loading, shunting and unloading. Third, timing: trucks can leave whenever the load is ready, rather than waiting for a scheduled train. Fourth, roads reach rural areas, small towns and farms where there is no railway. Fifth, building a road is generally cheaper per kilometre than building a railway, and many people and companies can use it.

Disadvantages of road transport. First, a truck carries far less than a train, so moving bulk goods over long distances needs many trucks, many drivers and much more fuel per tonne. Second, heavy trucks damage road surfaces, causing potholes and costly repairs, and the cost of repair is paid mostly from public money. Third, road transport causes congestion, especially on busy corridors and near ports. Fourth, it produces more air pollution and greenhouse gases per tonne carried than rail. Fifth, road accidents kill thousands of people in South Africa every year, and crashes involving heavy trucks can be especially serious, closing highways for hours.

Passenger road transport follows the same pattern. Minibus taxis carry most commuters who use public transport in South Africa, because they run on many routes, stop almost anywhere and reach townships and rural areas that trains and buses do not serve. Private cars give the most freedom of all, but they are expensive to own and run, and many cars carrying one or two people each fill the roads quickly. Buses carry more people per vehicle than cars or taxis and are cheaper per passenger, but they follow fixed routes and timetables.

Think also about who pays. When a truck uses a national road, the company pays for fuel, the driver and tolls on some sections, but the damage it does to the road surface is repaired mainly with public money. Economists say that part of the true cost of road freight is carried by the public rather than by the company that chose the truck. This is one argument that planners use for moving heavy, long-distance freight to rail.

Road is therefore the best choice for small loads, for short and medium distances, for door-to-door delivery and for places without railways. It is a poor choice for very heavy bulk goods over long distances.

## Subtopic: Rail Transport: Advantages and Disadvantages

Rail transport moves goods and people by train along fixed tracks. South Africa's freight railway, run mostly by Transnet Freight Rail, is the largest network in Africa.

Advantages of rail transport. First, capacity: one long train can carry thousands of tonnes, so it is ideal for bulk goods such as coal, iron ore, grain, cement and containers. The heavy-haul line from Sishen to Saldanha Bay runs some of the longest trains in the world, more than three kilometres long. Second, rail uses less fuel per tonne carried than road, so it is cheaper for bulk goods over long distances and produces less pollution per tonne. Third, every train takes many trucks off the roads, which reduces congestion, road damage and accidents. Fourth, many trains run on electricity, which avoids burning diesel along the route. Fifth, commuter trains can carry large numbers of passengers into cities cheaply.

Disadvantages of rail transport. First, trains can only go where there are tracks, so goods usually need a truck at each end of the journey, which adds time and handling costs. Second, building a railway is very expensive, and tracks must follow gentle slopes, so they need long curves, tunnels and bridges in hilly areas. Third, trains run to timetables and need time to load and assemble, so rail is slower for small, urgent loads. Fourth, in South Africa, rail has suffered from years of under-investment, a shortage of working locomotives, poor maintenance, cable theft and vandalism, which have made services unreliable and pushed customers onto the roads.

Passenger rail has similar strengths and weaknesses. Metrorail commuter trains in Cape Town, Gauteng and Durban were designed to move very large numbers of workers cheaply, and a full commuter train carries far more people than a line of minibus taxis. The Gautrain, which links Johannesburg, Pretoria and O. R. Tambo International Airport, shows that fast, safe and reliable trains attract passengers who would otherwise drive. However, when commuter services are cancelled because of theft, vandalism or breakdowns, workers must find another way to get to their jobs, usually at a higher cost.

Railways also shape where activity happens. Mines, factories and grain silos were built beside railway sidings, and towns grew at railway junctions such as De Aar. When a branch line closes, the businesses that depended on it must turn to trucks, and some small towns lose an important link.

Rail is therefore the best choice for heavy, bulky goods over long distances and for large numbers of commuters on fixed routes. It is a poor choice for small loads, short distances and places far from the tracks.

## Subtopic: Comparing Modes on the Durban to Gauteng Corridor

The Durban to Gauteng corridor is the best place to compare road and rail in South Africa. It links the busiest port to the economic heart of the country, more than five hundred kilometres inland and over a thousand metres higher.

On this corridor, the N3 carries a very large share of the freight in heavy trucks. They climb the escarpment at Van Reenen's Pass, and the road carries many thousands of trucks a day. The result is congestion near Durban and on the pass, damage to the road, accidents that can close the highway, and long queues of trucks at the port gates.

The railway line beside the N3 could carry much more of this freight. Container trains can run from the port to inland terminals such as City Deep in Johannesburg, where containers are transferred to trucks for the last short trip. This combination is called intermodal transport: each mode does the part of the journey it does best, with rail for the long haul and road for the short delivery.

Why does so much freight still go by road? Because many customers need reliable, flexible delivery, and the railway has not always provided it. When trains are delayed or locomotives break down, companies switch to trucks, which can leave at any time. Shifting freight from road to rail, often called road-to-rail, is a goal of national transport planning, but it depends on making rail reliable again.

Other modes also play a part on this corridor. A pipeline carries fuel from Durban to Gauteng, which takes many tanker trucks off the N3. Pipelines are the cheapest and safest way to move large amounts of liquids and gas over long distances, but they can only carry those products and cost a great deal to build. Air transport links King Shaka International Airport and O. R. Tambo International Airport in about an hour, but it is used only for people and for light, valuable or urgent goods, because it is the most expensive mode per tonne. So the corridor is really a bundle of modes, each with its own job.

When you compare modes in an examination, use clear criteria: cost per tonne, speed, flexibility, capacity, safety, effect on the environment and suitability for the goods. Then reach a reasoned conclusion. For example, iron ore from the Northern Cape to Saldanha Bay should go by rail; fresh vegetables from a farm to a nearby market should go by road.

## Subtopic: Future Networks, Calculation and the Error Museum

What does South Africa need from its future transport networks? Several requirements stand out.

First, maintenance and reliability: existing roads and railways must be repaired and kept in good condition, and rail services must run on time, or customers will not use them. Second, a better balance between road and rail, with more bulk and long-distance freight on rail. Third, integration: roads, railways, ports, airports and pipelines must connect smoothly, with intermodal terminals where goods change mode easily. Fourth, safety and security, including protection against cable theft and vandalism. Fifth, sustainability: less pollution, more electric and cleaner vehicles, and public transport that reduces the number of cars. Sixth, access for all: good roads and affordable transport for rural communities and for people in townships far from jobs. Seventh, regional links that support trade with neighbouring countries, with faster border crossings.

Planners also talk about smart transport. Electronic tolling, tracking systems for trucks and containers, and online booking at port gates can reduce queues and waiting time. Better information helps companies choose the right mode and time for each load. Investment can come from the government, from state-owned companies such as Transnet, and from private companies working in partnership with the state. Whatever the source of money, the aim is the same: a network that moves goods and people safely, cheaply and reliably, and that serves the whole country rather than only its richest areas.

A calculation. A freight train has 100 wagons, and each wagon carries 30 tonnes. The train therefore carries 100 times 30, which is 3 000 tonnes. A heavy truck carries about 34 tonnes. How many trucks would carry the same load? Divide: 3 000 divided by 34 is about 88.2, so at least 89 trucks are needed, since 88 trucks would carry only 2 992 tonnes. One train can take about ninety trucks off the road.

The error museum. Exhibit one: road transport is always better than rail. False: road suits small loads and short distances, but rail suits heavy bulk goods over long distances. Exhibit two: rail can deliver to every door. False: trains run only on tracks, so most journeys need a truck at one or both ends. Exhibit three: building more roads always ends congestion. False: new roads often attract more traffic, and better public transport and rail are also needed. Exhibit four: one train equals one truck. False: a train can carry as much as about ninety trucks.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: trucks, trains and using both together.

## Subtopic: Trucks: Go Anywhere, But Costly

Plain words now. A truck can go almost anywhere there is a road. It picks up at the factory and drops off at the shop. That is door-to-door delivery. It is quick for short trips and it can leave any time.

But a truck carries only a small load. Many trucks are needed for big loads. They use a lot of fuel, make more pollution per tonne, damage roads, cause traffic jams and are involved in serious accidents.

Taxis are also road transport. They take many people to work every day and stop almost anywhere.

So: trucks are good for small loads and short trips. They are not good for huge loads going far.

## Subtopic: Trains: Big Loads, Fixed Tracks

A train is like a very long truck on rails. One train can carry as much as about ninety trucks. It uses less fuel per tonne and makes less pollution. It takes trucks off the road.

But trains can only go where there are tracks. You usually still need a truck at the start or the end. Railways cost a lot to build. In South Africa, trains have often been late because of broken locomotives and cable theft.

Trains carry people too. Commuter trains and the Gautrain move many workers in the big cities.

So: trains are good for big, heavy loads going far, like coal and iron ore.

## Subtopic: Using Both Together

The best plan uses both. A train carries containers from Durban to Johannesburg. Then trucks take them the last short distance to shops and factories. That is called intermodal transport.

For the future, South Africa needs to fix roads and railways, make trains reliable, link the modes together, keep transport safe, cut pollution and give everyone, also in rural areas, a way to travel.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Road: flexible, door to door, good for small loads and short trips, but costly in fuel, pollution, road damage and accidents. Rail: huge loads, cheaper and cleaner per tonne for long trips, but only on tracks and expensive to build. Best plan: combine them. Future networks: maintained, reliable, linked, safe, clean and fair.
