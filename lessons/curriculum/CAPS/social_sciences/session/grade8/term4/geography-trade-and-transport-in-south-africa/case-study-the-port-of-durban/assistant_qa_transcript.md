### subtopic_1 — Location and Site
**Assistant:** Why can't the port just dig the sandbar away once and for all?

**Tutor:** Because the sandbar is created by natural processes that never stop. Along the KwaZulu-Natal coast, waves usually approach the shore at an angle, driven by winds and swells from the south. As each wave washes up the beach at an angle and the water runs straight back down, it moves sand a little way along the coast. This process, called longshore drift, carries huge amounts of sand northwards along the coast every year, supplied by rivers that bring sediment from inland. Where the coastline is interrupted by a bay entrance, the current slows and the sand is deposited, building up a sandbar across the mouth. That is how the bar at the entrance of the Bay of Natal formed in the first place, and it is why sand keeps returning even after dredging. Engineers built breakwaters, long walls of rock and concrete, to guide currents and keep the entrance channel deep, but they also trap sand on one side, and the beaches on the other side can lose sand, which is one reason why Durban's beaches sometimes need sand pumped onto them. So the port has to dredge regularly, using special ships that suck up sand from the channel floor and dump it elsewhere, often onto beaches. This is an excellent example of how physical geography, in this case coastal processes, affects human activities like shipping and creates ongoing costs that must be managed.

### subtopic_2 — Exports, Imports and Terminals
**Assistant:** Why does it matter to ordinary people if the port is slow?

**Tutor:** Because almost everything people buy, and many of the jobs they depend on, are connected to the port. When ships wait for days to unload, or containers take a long time to leave the port, importers pay extra fees for ships and containers, and goods arrive late. Those extra costs are passed on to shops and then to consumers in higher prices for clothing, electronics, medicines, parts and many other goods. Fuel, which also arrives through Durban, affects the price of transport and food. Delays can cause shortages of goods in shops and of parts in factories, which may have to slow down or stop production. Exporters are affected too. A farmer exporting citrus needs the fruit to reach Europe or Asia quickly and in good condition; if containers are delayed at the port, fruit can spoil or miss its market, losing money for farmers and threatening farm jobs. Car factories and other manufacturers need reliable ports to export on time; if the port is unreliable, international companies may choose to invest in other countries instead of South Africa, which means fewer jobs. Shipping lines may also add extra charges for using slow ports, or skip them altogether. Because the port serves neighbouring countries too, its problems affect the whole region. So port efficiency is not only a technical issue; it affects prices, jobs and economic growth for millions of people.

### subtopic_3 — Specialised Ships and Transport Links
**Assistant:** Why is a new port planned at the old airport site?

**Tutor:** Because the existing Port of Durban has almost no room to grow, while demand for container handling is expected to increase over the coming decades. The current harbour is surrounded by the city: the CBD and the Point to the north, the Bluff to the south and industrial and residential areas around the rest of the bay. There is little spare land for new terminals, stacking areas or rail yards. When King Shaka International Airport opened in 2010, the old Durban International Airport, south of the city, closed to passenger flights, leaving a large area of flat land near the coast and the existing port. Planners proposed digging out a new harbour there, a dug-out port, which would create new deep berths and container terminals connected to the sea by a channel, with space for modern equipment and good road and rail connections. Supporters argue that this would greatly increase capacity, allow larger ships, create jobs and keep Durban competitive with other ports in Africa. Critics and community organisations have raised concerns about the very high cost, the impact on nearby communities such as those in south Durban, which already live with industrial pollution, the loss of wetlands and other environmental damage, and the possibility that improving efficiency at the existing port might be a cheaper solution. The project has been delayed and redesigned several times. It is a good example of the debates involved in planning future transport networks: economic benefits must be weighed against costs, environmental impacts and the needs of local communities.

### subtopic_4 — Challenges, Calculations and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do I write a good harbour case study answer?

**Tutor:** Structure your answer around the key headings in the syllabus and support each one with specific details. Start with location: give the name of the port, the province and the coast, and describe its site, for example the sheltered Bay of Natal protected by the Bluff, and its situation, the closest South African port to Gauteng with a large hinterland that includes neighbouring countries. Next, describe exports and imports, giving at least three of each with examples of where they come from or go to, such as vehicles from the factory at Prospecton exported to Europe, sugar from KwaZulu-Natal, and consumer goods in containers from China. Then describe specialised ships, naming at least two and explaining how they are designed for their cargo, such as container ships loaded by gantry cranes and car carriers with ramps and internal decks. Then explain links with other transport: the N3 road, the rail corridor to City Deep, the fuel pipeline and King Shaka airport, and how containers move intermodally. Finally, if the question asks, discuss a challenge and a solution, such as congestion and investment in equipment, or the lack of space and the dug-out port proposal. Use geographical terms correctly: site, situation, hinterland, terminal, berth, dredging, intermodal and TEU. Where possible, include a figure, such as the number of TEUs, to show scale. A well-organised answer with specific evidence under each heading will earn far more marks than a general description of ports.
