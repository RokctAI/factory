### subtopic_1 — From the Coast to Kumasi, 1850 to 1874
**Assistant:** Why did the Asante see the Dutch handover of Elmina as a threat?

**Tutor:** Because Elmina had a special place in Asante trade and politics, and its transfer to Britain meant that Britain would control the entire coast, cutting the Asante off from independent access to the sea and to European traders other than the British. For many years, the Dutch at Elmina had maintained good relations with the Asante, who claimed that the Dutch paid them a kind of rent, called a note, for Elmina, and saw the town as linked to Asante authority. The Dutch fort gave the Asante an outlet on the coast that was not controlled by their Fante rivals or by the British, and it allowed them to trade, including for firearms, with a European power that was not hostile to them. When the Dutch decided to leave the Gold Coast in 1872 and handed their forts to Britain, without asking the Asante, the Asantehene's government saw it as a direct threat to Asante interests and claims. It meant that all European trade on the coast would now pass through British-controlled ports, under British rules, and that the British, already allied with the Fante, would dominate the coast completely. That is why Asante forces invaded the coastal region in 1873. The British, in turn, used the invasion to justify Wolseley's expedition. So the handover of Elmina was a turning point: it removed the last balance between European powers on the coast and led directly to the war that ended with the burning of Kumasi.

### subtopic_2 — The Scramble and the Exile of Prempeh I
**Assistant:** Was Prempeh I right not to fight in 1896?

**Tutor:** Historians and Asante people have debated this, and a strong answer can explore both sides. Prempeh had several reasons to avoid war. The Asante had been badly defeated in 1874, when Kumasi was burned, and had since been weakened by civil wars and the loss of tributary states. By 1896, the British had much stronger weapons, including machine guns, and a well-prepared expedition. Prempeh had seen how European forces with machine guns were defeating African armies elsewhere. He believed that fighting would lead to the destruction of Kumasi and the deaths of many of his people, and he hoped that by cooperating he could negotiate, keep the kingdom together and perhaps keep his throne. He had also tried diplomacy, sending envoys to London. On the other hand, his decision did not save the kingdom's independence: he was humiliated, arrested and exiled, and Asante became a protectorate anyway. Some people argue that resistance might have made the British pay a higher price or negotiate. Others point out that the War of the Golden Stool in 1900, when the Asante did fight, was also defeated, with great loss of life, which suggests that Prempeh's judgement about British military power was realistic. Many Asante remember Prempeh with great respect for putting his people's lives first, and he returned from exile in 1924 and was recognised as Kumasihene, and his successor was restored as Asantehene in 1935. A balanced answer explains his reasons and the consequences, and recognises that he faced an impossible choice.

### subtopic_3 — The War of the Golden Stool
**Assistant:** Why is Yaa Asantewaa remembered as a hero?

**Tutor:** Because she led the last great war of the Asante against British colonisation, at a moment when many male leaders hesitated, and because she became a symbol of courage, resistance and the strength of African women. Yaa Asantewaa was the queen mother of Ejisu, one of the states of the Asante union. Queen mothers were powerful figures in Akan society, advising chiefs and helping choose rulers, but leading an army in war was unusual. Her grandson, the chief of Ejisu, had been exiled with Prempeh in 1896, so she was acting for her state. According to tradition, when the chiefs met after Hodgson's insult and some were reluctant to fight, she spoke out, saying that if the men would not defend the Golden Stool, the women would. She was chosen to lead, and the war she led besieged the British in their fort for months and forced the British to send a large relief force. Although the war was lost and she died in exile in the Seychelles in 1921, the Golden Stool was never captured, which Asante people see as a victory for the spirit of the nation. In Ghana today, Yaa Asantewaa is honoured with a museum at Ejisu, a girls' school named after her, statues, songs and festivals, and her story is told as an example of patriotism and courage. Historians also study her as an example of the political power of women in Akan society, which colonial rule often undermined.

### subtopic_4 — Timelines, Sources and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why did the British need African soldiers to conquer Asante?

**Tutor:** Because the British army could not, and did not want to, send large numbers of British soldiers to West Africa, and African soldiers were cheaper, more numerous and better able to survive the climate and diseases of the region. British soldiers were expensive to transport, pay and supply, and before quinine became widely used, many died of malaria and other diseases in West Africa; the region was even called the white man's grave by Europeans. So Britain relied heavily on soldiers recruited in Africa and the Caribbean. In the Asante wars, the British forces included West India Regiment soldiers from the Caribbean, many of them of African descent; African troops from other British colonies, such as Hausa soldiers from northern Nigeria, who formed the core of the Gold Coast constabulary; soldiers from Sierra Leone; and men from coastal Gold Coast states who were allies or enemies of the Asante. Thousands of African carriers, men and women, carried ammunition, food and equipment along the forest paths, because there were no roads or draught animals suited to the forest. In 1900, the relief forces that broke the siege of Kumasi came largely from other British West African territories. This pattern was repeated across Africa: colonial armies were mostly African. Historians point to this to explain the speed of conquest, and to show that colonialism exploited divisions among Africans and drew on African labour even in conquering other Africans.
