# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the results of British colonisation for the Asante and for Britain. The anchor is the period from the annexation of Asante in 1901 to the independence of Ghana in 1957. The governing idea is that colonisation cost the Asante their independence and placed them under British control, while drawing the region into a colonial economy based on cocoa, gold and timber that brought wealth to British companies and the colonial state and opportunities and burdens to African farmers and workers, and that colonial education, economic change and resistance helped create the nationalist movement that won independence. Explaining these results for both sides is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Political Results for the Asante

The most important result of colonisation for the Asante was the loss of independence. After 1901, Asante was a British colony, ruled by a Chief Commissioner in Kumasi, under the governor of the Gold Coast. The Asantehene was in exile, and the Asante union was broken up: each state dealt separately with British officials. Britain made the laws, collected taxes, controlled the courts, police and army, and decided who could be recognised as a chief.

Britain ruled through indirect rule. Chiefs were kept in place, or appointed, to collect taxes, keep order and settle local disputes, under the supervision of British district commissioners. Chiefs who cooperated were rewarded; those who resisted could be removed, or destooled, by the colonial government. This changed the nature of chieftaincy. Traditionally, chiefs were chosen by their communities and could be removed by them, and they ruled with councils of elders. Under colonial rule, chiefs became answerable to British officials, and some used their new position for personal gain, which led to tensions with their people.

Over time, the British changed their approach. Prempeh I was allowed to return from exile in 1924, and was recognised as Kumasihene, the ruler of Kumasi, in 1926. In 1935, Britain restored the Asante Confederacy, and Prempeh's successor, Prempeh II, was recognised as Asantehene, with a Confederacy Council of Asante chiefs. This restoration was partly a recognition of the strength of Asante identity, and partly a way to use the authority of the Asantehene to support indirect rule.

The Golden Stool remained in Asante hands. After the incident of 1921, when it was found and damaged by road workers, the British promised not to interfere with it, and it was used again in ceremonies. Its survival meant that the spiritual and political heart of the Asante nation had not been destroyed, and it remains central to Asante identity today.

Asante was also drawn into a larger political unit. The Gold Coast Colony, Asante, the Northern Territories, and from 1919 the western part of German Togoland, administered by Britain under a League of Nations mandate, were governed together. When independence came, these regions became one country, Ghana, and the Asante became citizens of a new nation alongside the Fante, Ga, Ewe, the peoples of the north and many others. In the 1950s, some Asante leaders formed the National Liberation Movement, which wanted a federal system that would give Asante more self-government. This shows that the relationship between Asante identity and Ghanaian nationhood was not always easy.

The questions for this section are with you now: the loss of independence, indirect rule, the restoration of the Asante Confederacy and the place of the Asante in Ghana.

## Subtopic: Economic Results: Cocoa, Gold and Railways

Colonisation transformed the economy of Asante and the Gold Coast.

Cocoa. Cocoa was introduced to the Gold Coast in the nineteenth century, most famously by Tetteh Quarshie, who planted seeds from Fernando Po in 1879, and by Basel Mission missionaries. African farmers saw the opportunity and planted cocoa on a large scale, first in the south-east and then in the forests of Asante and the west. Cocoa farming was mainly carried out by African farmers on their own land, using family labour and hired workers, often migrants from the north and neighbouring colonies. Some farmers bought land in new areas with money from cocoa, and some became wealthy. By about 1911, the Gold Coast was the world's largest producer of cocoa. Cocoa became the colony's most important export, and the colonial government's revenue depended on it.

But cocoa farmers did not control the price. Cocoa was bought by European trading companies, which in the 1930s formed agreements among themselves to keep the price paid to farmers low. In 1937 and 1938, Gold Coast farmers organised a cocoa hold-up, refusing to sell their crop, which forced the British government to investigate. The colony's dependence on one crop also made it vulnerable to falls in world prices and to diseases such as swollen shoot, which struck cocoa trees in the 1940s.

Gold and minerals. Large-scale gold mining began under British companies, especially the Ashanti Goldfields Corporation, founded in 1897, which mined at Obuasi. Mining employed thousands of African workers, many of them migrants, often in hard and dangerous conditions. Profits went mainly to shareholders in Britain. Manganese, diamonds and bauxite were also mined in the colony.

Railways and roads. The British built a railway from the port of Sekondi to the gold mines at Tarkwa, reaching Kumasi in 1903, and later a line from Accra to Kumasi, completed in 1923. A deep-water harbour was built at Takoradi, opened in 1928. Roads were built, and motor lorries, many owned by African entrepreneurs, carried cocoa from farms to railway stations and ports. These transport links were designed mainly to carry exports to the coast.

Taxes and labour. The colonial government raised revenue from customs duties on imports and exports, and also used forced labour, especially in the early years, to carry loads and build roads. Workers from the Northern Territories, which the British treated mainly as a labour reserve, migrated south to work on cocoa farms and in mines.

Cocoa also changed land and society in Asante. Land that had been held by lineages and allocated by chiefs became more valuable, and disputes over land sales, rents and inheritance increased. Chiefs who controlled land could become rich by granting it to cocoa farmers from other areas, which sometimes caused conflict with their people. Cocoa wealth paid for new houses, schools, churches and lorries, changing towns and villages across the forest belt.

The questions for this section are with you now: cocoa farming, gold mining, railways, taxes and labour in colonial Asante and the Gold Coast.

## Subtopic: Social Results and the Road to Independence

Colonisation also brought social changes to Asante and the Gold Coast.

Education and religion. Christian missions, including the Basel Mission, the Wesleyan Methodists and the Catholic Church, built schools and churches across the colony, and many Asante became Christians, while many kept their own beliefs or combined them. Education expanded slowly, mainly at primary level. Under Governor Gordon Guggisberg, from 1919 to 1927, the colonial government invested more in education and opened Achimota College near Accra in 1927, which trained many future leaders. But secondary and higher education remained limited, and most children, especially in the north, did not attend school.

Health and towns. Colonial medical services opened hospitals in towns such as Kumasi and Accra, but rural areas had few services. Kumasi grew into a major commercial city, with a large central market, the railway station and trading companies, and attracted migrants from across the colony and beyond.

Social change. Cocoa wealth created a new class of prosperous African farmers, traders, lorry owners and professionals. At the same time, migrant workers from the north and from neighbouring colonies did much of the hard labour, and inequalities between regions grew, with the north left poor.

Nationalism. The educated class, the cocoa farmers, workers, ex-servicemen and chiefs all had grievances against colonial rule: lack of political power, low cocoa prices, high prices of imported goods, unemployment and racism. After the Second World War, in which many Gold Coast soldiers served, these grievances came together. In 1947, the United Gold Coast Convention was founded, and Kwame Nkrumah became its secretary. In February 1948, police fired on ex-servicemen marching to petition the governor in Accra, killing several, and riots followed. In 1949, Nkrumah formed the Convention People's Party, which demanded self-government now and organised strikes and boycotts. Nkrumah was imprisoned, but his party won the election of 1951, and he was released to lead the government under British supervision. On 6 March 1957, the Gold Coast, including Asante, became independent as Ghana, named after an ancient West African empire. It was the first colony in sub-Saharan Africa to gain independence from European colonial rule.

For Britain, the results included wealth from cocoa, gold, timber and other products, profits for companies, markets for British goods, and prestige; the Gold Coast was often called a model colony. Britain also bore costs: wars, administration and, later, the loss of a profitable colony. Independence was negotiated peacefully, which allowed Britain to keep economic and diplomatic ties through the Commonwealth.

The questions for this section are with you now: education, health, social change, the rise of nationalism and Ghana's independence, and the results for Britain.

## Subtopic: Weighing Results and the Error Museum

Weighing the results of colonisation for the Asante and for Britain requires evidence and balance.

A calculation shows the growth of cocoa. In the early 1890s, the Gold Coast exported almost no cocoa. By 1911, it exported about forty thousand tonnes a year. If the industry grew from close to zero to forty thousand tonnes in about twenty years, the average increase was forty thousand divided by twenty, which is two thousand tonnes more every year. This rapid growth was achieved mainly by African farmers, investing their own labour, land and money, which shows that Africans were active economic participants, not only victims.

Another calculation places the colonial period in time. Asante was annexed in 1901 and became part of independent Ghana in 1957. Nineteen fifty-seven minus nineteen hundred and one is fifty-six. So British colonial rule over Asante lasted fifty-six years, less than one long human lifetime. A person born in independent Asante in the 1890s could have lived to see Ghana's independence.

Results can be weighed using a table. For the Asante, losses included independence, the exile of rulers, deaths in war, changes to chieftaincy, dependence on cocoa prices and control by foreign companies. Gains included new crops and income for some farmers, some schools and hospitals, railways and roads, and in the end a role in a new independent nation. For Britain, gains included raw materials, profits, markets and prestige; costs included wars and administration. Who gained most? Most historians argue that the greatest profits went to British companies and the colonial state, while African farmers and workers carried most of the risk and labour.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the Asante simply accepted colonial rule after 1901. They protested, organised cocoa hold-ups and joined nationalist movements. Two: saying British settlers grew the cocoa. African farmers did. Three: saying the Golden Stool was captured. It never was. Four: saying railways were built to serve Asante needs. They were built mainly for exports. Five: forgetting that Ghana's independence in 1957 included Asante, and that some Asante leaders wanted federalism.

Layout for marks in an essay on the results of colonisation for the Asante and Britain: introduce the annexation, discuss political, economic and social results for the Asante, discuss results for Britain, weigh who gained and who lost, and conclude with the road to independence.

The questions for this section are with you now: the cocoa calculation, weighing results and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Ruled From Outside

After 1901, the Asante were ruled by British officials. Chiefs had to obey the British, who could remove them.

Prempeh I came home in 1924. In 1935, the British let the Asante have an Asantehene and a council again. The Golden Stool stayed safe with the Asante.

When Ghana became independent, the Asante became Ghanaians, together with many other peoples.

Picture a chief who once answered to his people, now answering to a British officer.

The Asante kept their language, Twi, their festivals and their sense of being one nation, even under British rule.

## Subtopic: Cocoa and Gold

In 1879, Tetteh Quarshie planted cocoa. African farmers grew it everywhere, and by about 1911 the Gold Coast was the biggest cocoa producer in the world. But European companies set the price, and in 1937 farmers refused to sell to protest low prices.

A British company dug gold at Obuasi. Railways carried cocoa and gold from Kumasi to the sea.

## Subtopic: Freedom in 1957

Missions built schools and churches. Achimota College trained future leaders. People who had been to school, soldiers who had fought in the war, farmers and workers all wanted change.

Kwame Nkrumah formed a party in 1949 and called for self-government now. On 6 March 1957, the Gold Coast, including Asante, became free as Ghana.

Pocket summary of the lesson. After 1901, the Asante lost their independence and were ruled through chiefs under British officials, though the Asante Confederacy was restored in 1935 and the Golden Stool stayed safe. The economy was built on cocoa, grown by African farmers, and gold mined by British companies, with railways to the coast. Missions spread education and Christianity. Britain gained profits, markets and prestige. Grievances led to nationalism, and Ghana became independent on 6 March 1957.
