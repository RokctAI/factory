### subtopic_1 — The Rise of the Asante Kingdom
**Assistant:** Why was the Golden Stool so important that no one could sit on it?

**Tutor:** Because it was not a piece of furniture but a sacred symbol of the whole Asante nation, its unity, its ancestors and its survival. In Akan culture, stools were already important symbols of authority and of the soul of their owners and lineages. When a chief was installed, he was said to be enstooled, and when he was removed, destooled. After a chief died, his stool might be blackened and kept as a shrine to his spirit. The Golden Stool took this idea and made it national: according to tradition, Okomfo Anokye declared that it contained the sunsum, the spirit, of all the Asante people, and that their strength and well-being depended on its safety. That is why the chiefs buried their own stools, showing that their separate states had become one nation under the Golden Stool. The Asantehene was its guardian, not its owner, and even he did not sit on it; on great occasions, it was placed on its own stool, beside the king. This belief made the Golden Stool a powerful force for unity in a large and diverse empire. It also explains why, as you will learn in the next lesson, a British governor's demand to sit on it in 1900 was felt as an extreme insult and sparked a war. A historian would say that the Golden Stool helped turn a confederation of chiefdoms into a state with a shared identity, which is one reason the Asante were so strong.

### subtopic_2 — Gold, Trade and the Coast
**Assistant:** How could the Asante take part in the slave trade?

**Tutor:** This is a difficult and important question, and historians answer it by looking at the context carefully, without either excusing or exaggerating. Slavery and the capture of people in war existed in many societies around the world, including in West Africa, before the transatlantic trade. In the Asante kingdom, war captives and people condemned for crimes could be made slaves or servants, and some were incorporated into Asante families over generations. When European traders arrived on the coast and offered firearms, gunpowder, cloth and other goods in exchange for captives, the demand grew enormously. States like the Asante, and many others along the West African coast, became involved, selling captives taken in wars, often against neighbouring peoples, and sometimes fighting wars partly to obtain captives. Firearms obtained in the trade helped the Asante expand their empire. So the Asante were active participants and profited from the trade. At the same time, the transatlantic trade was created and driven by European demand for labour on plantations in the Americas, financed by European merchants and protected by European governments, and it was Europeans who transported millions of Africans across the Atlantic and held them in chattel slavery, a form of slavery that treated people as property for life and passed the status to their children. Today, Ghana recognises this painful history: the slave dungeons at Cape Coast and Elmina are memorials, visited by people of African descent from around the world.

### subtopic_3 — Explorers, Envoys and Rising Tension
**Assistant:** Why should historians be careful with Bowdich's account of Kumasi?

**Tutor:** Because, although Bowdich was an eyewitness and his account is very valuable, he saw the Asante through the eyes of a young British official with particular purposes and assumptions. Bowdich was sent by the British trading company on the coast to negotiate with the Asantehene, so he wanted to show his employers that the Asante were a powerful state worth dealing with, and he may have emphasised certain things for that reason. He did not speak the Asante language, Twi, and relied on interpreters, so some of what he reported may have been misunderstood. He judged Asante customs by European standards, admiring some things, such as the order of the city, the wealth of the court and the discipline of the people, and condemning others, such as human sacrifice at certain ceremonies, without fully understanding their religious meaning. He stayed only a few months and saw mainly the capital and the court, not ordinary life across the kingdom. His book was written for European readers and to make his name. Despite these limits, his descriptions and drawings of Kumasi's streets, buildings, festivals and court ceremonies are among the best evidence we have for the period, and they help counter the false idea that Africa had no organised states. So a historian would say: Bowdich's account is useful for detailed eyewitness information about Kumasi and the court in 1817, but it must be compared with Asante oral traditions and other sources, because it reflects his limited view and his purposes.

### subtopic_4 — Sources, Time and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why did the Asante win in 1824 but lose in 1826?

**Tutor:** The two battles show that warfare between the Asante and the British was evenly balanced in this period, and that outcomes depended on leadership, numbers, allies, terrain and weapons. In 1824, Governor Charles MacCarthy marched inland with a relatively small force of British soldiers, local militia and Fante allies, underestimating the Asante army. He divided his forces, and his ammunition ran short; reports say that boxes thought to contain ammunition contained other supplies. At Nsamankow, in the forest, the large, well-organised Asante army surrounded and defeated his force, and MacCarthy was killed. The forest terrain suited Asante tactics, with their divisions moving through the bush to encircle the enemy. In 1826, the situation was different. The Asante army advanced towards the coast, close to Accra, onto more open country at Katamanso. The British and their allies, including coastal states such as the Ga, the Fante and others who feared Asante domination, had gathered a much larger combined force, and the British used Congreve rockets, which had a terrifying effect, even if they were not very accurate. The Asante were defeated with heavy losses. So the lesson for historians is that, before the late nineteenth century, the British had no overwhelming technological advantage over the Asante, and success depended on alliances and circumstances. That changed with the breech-loading rifles, machine guns and quinine of the scramble era, which you will see in the next lesson.
