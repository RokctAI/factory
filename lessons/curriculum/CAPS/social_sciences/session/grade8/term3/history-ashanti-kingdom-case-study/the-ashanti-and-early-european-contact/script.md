# Part 1 — Expert

This session begins the case study of the Asante kingdom, in what is now Ghana. The anchor is the rise of the Asante kingdom around 1700 under Osei Tutu and the Golden Stool, and its relations with European traders on the coast of the Gold Coast. The governing idea is that the Asante built a powerful, wealthy and well-organised state, based on gold, trade and military strength, which dealt with Europeans as equals and rivals for nearly two centuries, before the scramble for Africa changed the balance of power. Describing the Asante state and its early contact with European traders and explorers is examinable this term. In this lesson both spellings, Asante and Ashanti, refer to the same people and kingdom.

## Subtopic: The Rise of the Asante Kingdom

The Asante are an Akan people who live in the forest region of what is now central Ghana. In the seventeenth century, there were several small Akan states in the region, and the Asante chiefdoms around Kumasi were ruled by a larger, powerful state called Denkyira, to which they paid tribute.

Around the end of the seventeenth century, Osei Tutu, a leader of Kumasi, united several Asante chiefdoms. With his friend and adviser, the priest Okomfo Anokye, he created institutions that bound the chiefdoms together. The most important was the Golden Stool. According to tradition, Okomfo Anokye brought the stool down from the sky, and it was understood to contain the spirit, or sunsum, of the whole Asante nation. The chiefs buried their own stools, the symbols of their separate authority, and swore loyalty to the Golden Stool and to Osei Tutu as Asantehene, king of all the Asante. No one, not even the king, was allowed to sit on the Golden Stool.

The new union defeated Denkyira in about 1701, at the battle of Feyiase, and the Asante became independent. Over the eighteenth century, under rulers such as Opoku Ware, the Asante expanded by conquest, until their empire covered most of what is now Ghana and parts of neighbouring countries. Some conquered states were incorporated into the kingdom; others paid tribute.

The Asante state was well organised. The Asantehene ruled with a council of senior chiefs, and important decisions, such as going to war, were discussed in a national assembly. The queen mother, the Asantehemaa, was a powerful figure who advised the king and played a key role in choosing a new Asantehene from the royal lineage. Kumasi had a large bureaucracy of officials who managed taxes, trade, roads and diplomacy, kept records and used Muslim scribes to write letters in Arabic. A network of great roads radiated from Kumasi to all parts of the empire, with checkpoints and messengers, so that news and orders could travel quickly.

The army was large and disciplined. It was organised into divisions, with scouts, an advance guard, a main body, wings and a rear guard, and from the eighteenth century many soldiers carried firearms bought from European traders.

Asante culture was rich. Kumasi was a planned city with broad streets and decorated buildings. Asante goldsmiths made jewellery and regalia, weavers produced kente cloth, and the people used gold weights, small brass figures, to weigh gold dust, which served as money. Festivals such as the Odwira festival brought the nation together each year.

## Subtopic: Gold, Trade and the Coast

The wealth of the Asante came from gold and trade. The forest region contained rich gold deposits, mined and panned by Akan people for centuries. Gold dust was the currency of the kingdom, and the Asantehene controlled the largest nuggets, which belonged to the state.

The Asante traded in two directions. To the north, traders carried kola nuts, a valued stimulant grown in the forest, to the markets of the savanna and the Sahel, where they were exchanged for cloth, salt, livestock, leather goods and other products, and linked to trans-Saharan trade routes. To the south, the Asante traded with Europeans on the coast, about two hundred kilometres away.

European traders had arrived on this coast long before the Asante kingdom existed. The Portuguese built the castle of São Jorge da Mina, now called Elmina, in 1482, to trade for gold, which is why Europeans called the region the Gold Coast. In the seventeenth century, the Dutch captured Elmina in 1637, and the English, Danes, Swedes and Germans built forts and trading lodges along the coast. Cape Coast Castle became the main English, later British, base. By the eighteenth century there were dozens of European forts on a stretch of coast only a few hundred kilometres long, more than anywhere else in Africa.

The Europeans did not rule the land around their forts. They paid rent to local African rulers, usually Fante and other coastal peoples, and depended on African traders and middlemen to bring goods from the interior. The coastal Fante states acted as middlemen between the Asante and the Europeans, which often led to conflict between the Asante and the Fante.

In the eighteenth century, the transatlantic slave trade became the main trade on the Gold Coast. The Asante, like other African states, sold captives, many of them taken in war, to European traders in exchange for firearms, gunpowder, cloth, metal goods, alcohol and other goods. The forts became prisons where enslaved people were held in dungeons before being shipped across the Atlantic. Cape Coast and Elmina castles are today world heritage sites and memorials to the slave trade, which you studied in Grade 7.

When Britain abolished the slave trade in 1807, the Asante economy had to adjust. Asante leaders were surprised and at first unwilling, because the trade had been profitable, but they increasingly turned to exporting gold, kola, ivory and, later, other goods. The British, meanwhile, began to use their naval power to suppress the slave trade along the coast.

## Subtopic: Explorers, Envoys and Rising Tension

In the early nineteenth century, contact between the Asante and the British increased, and so did tension.

The British wanted to expand trade inland, to protect the coastal Fante, who had become their allies, and, after 1807, to end the slave trade. The Asante wanted to control trade to the coast, to keep the Fante and other coastal states under their authority, and to buy firearms and other goods.

In 1817, the British sent a mission to Kumasi led by Thomas Bowdich. Bowdich was impressed by the size, order and wealth of Kumasi, by the Asantehene's court, and by the great annual festival he witnessed. His book, published in 1819, described the Asante as a powerful and sophisticated state, and included drawings of Kumasi. A treaty was made, but disputes continued over the British relationship with the coastal states. Another envoy, Joseph Dupuis, visited in 1820. These accounts are valuable sources, but historians read them carefully, because the authors saw Asante through European eyes and had their own purposes.

The Asante treated European powers as rivals and trading partners, not as superiors. The Asantehene received European envoys, negotiated treaties, sent his own ambassadors to the coast, and expected the British to recognise Asante authority over the coastal states. Asante rulers sent some young men of the royal family to be educated in Europe; two princes, Owusu Ansa and Owusu Nkwantabisa, were educated in England in the 1830s and 1840s and later served as diplomats and advisers.

Tension became war in the 1820s. The British governor, Sir Charles MacCarthy, supported the Fante against the Asante. In January 1824, at the battle of Nsamankow, Asante forces defeated MacCarthy's army, and MacCarthy was killed. Two years later, in 1826, the British and their allies defeated the Asante at the battle of Katamanso, near Accra, using new weapons including rockets. A peace treaty in 1831 recognised the independence of the coastal states from Asante. For the next forty years, there was an uneasy balance: the Asante remained powerful and independent in the interior, while British influence grew on the coast.

This early period shows that the relationship between the Asante and Britain was a long one, with periods of trade, diplomacy and war, and that the Asante were a strong state that could defeat British forces. The balance only shifted decisively in the age of the scramble, which you will study in the next lesson.

The questions for this section are with you now: the British missions to Kumasi, Asante diplomacy and the wars of the 1820s.

## Subtopic: Sources, Time and the Error Museum

Historians of the Asante use many kinds of sources. Asante oral traditions, preserved by royal historians and drummers, tell the story of Osei Tutu, Okomfo Anokye and the Golden Stool, and of the kings and wars that followed. European accounts, such as those of Bowdich and Dupuis, give detailed eyewitness descriptions of Kumasi and the court in the early nineteenth century. Arabic documents written by Muslim scribes at the Asante court, company records of European trading companies, archaeological evidence from Kumasi and the forts, and objects such as gold weights, regalia and kente cloth all add to the picture.

Each kind of source has strengths and limits. Oral traditions preserve the Asante view of their own history and the meaning of institutions such as the Golden Stool, but they may include religious or symbolic elements and may be told to support the royal family. European accounts give eyewitness detail and dates, but the authors often misunderstood Asante customs, judged them by European standards, and wrote for European readers with commercial or political aims. Using both together gives a fuller picture.

A calculation helps to place the Asante in time. The kingdom became independent around 1701 and was finally annexed by Britain in 1901, which you will study next lesson. Nineteen hundred and one minus seventeen hundred and one is two hundred. So the Asante kingdom was an independent power for about two hundred years. Elmina castle was built in 1482, two hundred and nineteen years before the Asante won independence in 1701, because seventeen hundred and one minus fourteen eighty-two is two hundred and nineteen. Europeans had been trading on this coast for more than two centuries before the Asante kingdom was founded, but for all that time they did not control the interior.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying Europeans ruled the Gold Coast from 1482. For centuries they only held coastal forts and paid rent to African rulers. Two: saying the Asante were a small, simple society. They had a large empire, a bureaucracy, a road network and a disciplined army. Three: saying the Asantehene sat on the Golden Stool. No one sat on it; it represented the nation's spirit. Four: treating Bowdich's account as a neutral, complete description. Five: forgetting that Africans, including the Asante, took part in the slave trade, and that the trade was driven by European demand.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: the Golden Stool, gold and forts, and talking and fighting with the British.

## Subtopic: The Golden Stool

Around 1700, Osei Tutu united the Asante chiefdoms. His priest, Okomfo Anokye, presented the Golden Stool, which the Asante believed held the spirit of the whole nation. Osei Tutu became the first Asantehene, the king, and Kumasi became the capital. Nobody ever sat on the Golden Stool.

The Asante defeated Denkyira around 1701 and grew into a big, rich empire with roads, officials and a strong army.

Picture the stool covered in gold, too precious to sit on, holding a whole nation together.

Keep that stool in mind and try the questions for this part: the rise of the Asante.

## Subtopic: Gold and Forts

The Asante were rich because of gold. Gold dust was their money. They traded kola nuts to the north and traded with Europeans on the coast to the south.

Europeans built forts on the coast, starting with the Portuguese at Elmina in 1482. They did not rule the land; they paid rent to African rulers. During the slave trade, captives were sold at the forts and shipped across the Atlantic. The Asante took part in this trade.

Keep the forts on the coast in mind and try the questions for this part: gold and trade.

## Subtopic: Talking and Fighting

In 1817, the British sent Thomas Bowdich to Kumasi. He was amazed by the city. The Asante treated the British as equals, sending their own envoys and even sending princes to study in England.

But they disagreed about the coast. In 1824, the Asante defeated a British army and killed its governor, MacCarthy. In 1826, the British won a battle near Accra.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Asante kingdom was founded around 1700 by Osei Tutu with the Golden Stool, and became independent around 1701. It was a large, well-organised empire based on gold and trade, with Kumasi as its capital. Europeans traded from coastal forts from 1482, including in captives during the slave trade, but did not rule the interior. In the early nineteenth century, the Asante and the British traded, negotiated and fought, and the Asante remained powerful and independent.
