### subtopic_1 — Political Results for the Asante
**Assistant:** Why did the British restore the Asante Confederacy in 1935?

**Tutor:** For a mixture of practical and political reasons, and historians see it as both a recognition of Asante strength and a strategy of colonial rule. After 1901, the British had tried to govern Asante by dealing with each state and chief separately, keeping the Asantehene in exile. But Asante identity, centred on the Golden Stool and the memory of the kingdom, remained very strong, and many Asante chiefs and people continued to look to the royal family for leadership. Ruling without the Asantehene was difficult, because disputes between chiefs, land conflicts and questions of custom needed an authority that the Asante would accept. At the same time, British colonial policy across Africa in the 1920s and 1930s placed great emphasis on indirect rule, the idea that colonies should be governed through traditional authorities, which were seen as cheaper and more stable. Restoring the Asantehene, under British supervision, offered a way to use his authority to support colonial administration, to collect taxes and keep order. Prempeh I's return in 1924 and his good relations with the British prepared the way, and after his death in 1931, his successor, Osei Agyeman Prempeh II, was recognised as Asantehene in 1935, with a Confederacy Council. The Asante welcomed the restoration as a return of their kingdom's dignity, while the British saw it as strengthening indirect rule. Both views are part of the story, which is why a balanced answer mentions both.

### subtopic_2 — Economic Results: Cocoa, Gold and Railways
**Assistant:** If African farmers grew the cocoa, why didn't they become rich?

**Tutor:** Some did become prosperous, but most did not capture most of the value of the crop, because they did not control the price, the trade or the processing. Cocoa farmers sold their beans through African brokers to European trading companies, such as the United Africa Company, which shipped them to Europe, where they were processed into chocolate and other products. The big profits were made in shipping, trading and manufacturing, which were controlled by European firms. In the 1930s, the large trading companies agreed among themselves on the prices they would pay, keeping prices low for farmers. Farmers also paid high prices for imported goods sold by the same companies. Many farmers borrowed money against future crops and fell into debt. Prices on the world market rose and fell sharply, for example during the Great Depression, so farmers' incomes were unpredictable. Cocoa trees also take years to grow and can be destroyed by disease, like swollen shoot in the 1940s. After the Second World War, the colonial government created a marketing board that bought all cocoa at a fixed price, which reduced uncertainty but also allowed the government to keep part of the value as revenue. Despite all this, cocoa did create a class of wealthy farmers and helped finance schools, houses and businesses in Asante and the south. A balanced answer shows that African farmers were successful entrepreneurs who built the industry, but that the colonial trading system limited how much of the wealth they could keep.

### subtopic_3 — Social Results and the Road to Independence
**Assistant:** Why did Asante leaders want a federal system before independence?

**Tutor:** Because they feared that a strong central government in Accra, led by Nkrumah's Convention People's Party, would reduce the power of the Asantehene and the chiefs, and would take Asante's cocoa wealth without giving Asante a fair say. By the 1950s, Asante was the heart of cocoa production, and the colonial government, and then Nkrumah's government, fixed the price paid to cocoa farmers through the marketing board, which kept the price low and used the surplus for national development. Many Asante farmers were angry when, in 1954, the government froze the price paid to farmers even though world prices were high. Asante chiefs and leaders also had a long history as an independent kingdom and a strong sense of identity, and some believed that the CPP, which drew much support from young people and towns, wanted to weaken chieftaincy. In 1954, they formed the National Liberation Movement, which demanded a federal constitution that would give regions like Asante their own governments. There was serious political violence in Kumasi in the mid-1950s. The British insisted on another election in 1956 before granting independence, and the CPP won again, so Ghana became independent in 1957 with a unitary constitution, though with some regional assemblies. This history shows that independence did not erase older identities, and that building a nation out of colonial borders involved real debates about power, wealth and tradition.

### subtopic_4 — Weighing Results and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Was the Gold Coast really a model colony?

**Tutor:** It depends on who is judging, and by which standard. British officials called the Gold Coast a model colony for several reasons. It was profitable, with large exports of cocoa, gold and timber, and its government paid for itself from customs duties. It had relatively few European settlers, so there were no large land seizures like those in Kenya or Southern Rhodesia, and most land remained in African hands. It had some of the best schools in colonial Africa, such as Achimota, a growing educated class and, in the 1950s, a peaceful transition to self-government and independence. From a British point of view, it showed colonial rule working smoothly. But from an African point of view, the picture is different. The colony was conquered through war, including the burning of Kumasi in 1874 and the war of 1900. Its rulers were exiled, its chiefs made answerable to foreign officials, and its people had no vote in the government until the 1950s. Its economy depended on one main crop, whose price was controlled by European companies, and its gold profits went mainly to British shareholders. Forced labour was used, especially in early years, and the Northern Territories were neglected and used as a labour reserve. In 1948, police shot ex-servicemen in Accra. So model colony is a phrase that tells us more about British views than about African experiences. A historian would use the phrase carefully, explaining who used it and why, and balancing it with evidence of costs.
