# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the British colonisation of the Gold Coast and the conquest of the Asante kingdom. The anchor is the sequence of events from the war of 1873 to 1874 to the annexation of 1901. The governing idea is that Britain moved from controlling a strip of coast to conquering the whole region in less than thirty years, driven by trade, rivalry with France and Germany during the scramble, and the wish to remove the Asante as an independent power, and that the Asante resisted by war and diplomacy, culminating in the War of the Golden Stool, which was defeated but ensured that the Golden Stool itself was never captured. Explaining the stages of British colonisation and Asante resistance is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: From the Coast to Kumasi, 1850 to 1874

By the middle of the nineteenth century, Britain had become the main European power on the Gold Coast. The Danes sold their forts to Britain in 1850, and in 1872 the Dutch handed over their forts, including Elmina, to Britain. Britain now controlled the whole coastline, and British influence over the coastal Fante and other states had grown into something close to rule.

The Asante saw the transfer of Elmina as a threat. Elmina had long been connected to the Asante, who claimed rights there and had used it as an outlet to the sea. In 1873, an Asante army invaded the coastal region to reassert Asante authority.

Britain responded with a major military expedition under General Sir Garnet Wolseley. The expedition was carefully planned. Engineers built a road through the forest towards Kumasi, with bridges and supply depots. Wolseley's force included British regiments, West Indian soldiers and African troops and carriers recruited on the coast. They had breech-loading rifles, artillery and rockets, and used quinine to protect against malaria. After hard fighting in the forest, including the battle of Amoafo, the British entered Kumasi in February 1874. The Asantehene, Kofi Karikari, had left the city. The British burned Kumasi, including the royal palace, and withdrew quickly before the rainy season.

The Treaty of Fomena, signed in 1874, required the Asante to pay a large indemnity in gold, to give up claims to Elmina and the coastal states, to keep the trade routes open and to end human sacrifice. In the same year, Britain formally declared the coastal region the Gold Coast Colony, with its capital later moved to Accra.

The defeat weakened the Asante. Several states that had been part of the empire broke away, and civil wars and disputes over the succession followed in the 1880s. However, the Asante kingdom was not destroyed. In 1888, Agyeman Prempeh was installed as Asantehene, and he worked to restore the unity and authority of the kingdom.

The war of 1873 to 1874 showed how the balance of power had changed since the 1820s. In 1824, the Asante had defeated a British force in the forest. Fifty years later, the British arrived with better rifles, better medicine, a planned supply line and thousands of African carriers and soldiers, and were able to reach and burn the capital. Wolseley's campaign was celebrated in Britain as a model expedition, and newspapers reported it in great detail, presenting the Asante as a savage enemy. Asante accounts remember the destruction of Kumasi as a national disaster, and the palace treasures taken by British soldiers are still held in British museums, though some objects have recently been returned on loan to Kumasi.

The questions for this section are with you now: British control of the coast, the war of 1873 to 1874, the burning of Kumasi and the Treaty of Fomena.

## Subtopic: The Scramble and the Exile of Prempeh I

In the 1880s and 1890s, the scramble for Africa changed British policy. France was expanding from the west and north, through Côte d'Ivoire and the interior, and Germany had taken Togoland to the east in 1884. British officials and merchants feared that if Britain did not take Asante and the lands to its north, France or Germany would. British traders also wanted direct access to the gold, rubber, timber and other products of the interior, and to the markets of the north.

Britain offered Prempeh I a treaty of protection in 1891. Prempeh refused, replying that the Asante kingdom wished to remain on friendly terms with all white men, but would not commit itself to any policy that made it the subject of one power. He sent an embassy of Asante envoys to London in 1894 and 1895 to negotiate directly with the British government, but the British refused to receive them officially.

In late 1895, Britain sent a military expedition to Kumasi, claiming that the Asante had not paid the indemnity of 1874, had not kept the trade routes open and continued human sacrifice. Prempeh, wishing to avoid the destruction of his people in a war he believed he could not win, decided not to fight. In January 1896, the British entered Kumasi without resistance. In a humiliating ceremony, Prempeh was forced to submit and to promise a huge payment in gold, which he could not pay at once. He, the queen mother and other members of the royal family and leading chiefs were arrested.

Prempeh was exiled, first to Elmina, then to Sierra Leone, and in 1900 to the Seychelles, islands in the Indian Ocean, thousands of kilometres from home. Britain declared Asante a protectorate and placed a British Resident in Kumasi, with a fort and a garrison. The British wanted to break the power of the Asantehene completely, and they began to search for the Golden Stool, which the Asante had hidden.

The British also had economic plans for Asante. Gold mining companies were interested in the rich deposits around Obuasi, and in 1897, soon after the occupation, a British company began large-scale mining there. Traders wanted the forest's rubber and timber. Plans for a railway from the coast to Kumasi were made, to carry gold and other goods to the port. These economic interests help explain why Britain wanted direct control rather than a treaty of friendship.

The questions for this section are with you now: the scramble in West Africa, Prempeh's diplomacy, the British occupation of Kumasi in 1896 and Prempeh's exile.

## Subtopic: The War of the Golden Stool

In March 1900, Governor Frederic Hodgson visited Kumasi. Speaking to the assembled chiefs, he demanded the payment of the indemnity, and then asked where the Golden Stool was, saying that as the representative of the paramount power he should be sitting on it. To the Asante, this was a profound insult. The Golden Stool held the spirit of the nation; no one, not even the Asantehene, sat on it. Hodgson's demand showed how little the British understood, or respected, Asante beliefs.

The chiefs met in secret. Some hesitated. According to Asante tradition, Yaa Asantewaa, the queen mother of Ejisu, a state in the Asante union, challenged them, saying that if the men would not go forward, the women would, and that she would call upon her fellow women to fight. She was chosen as war leader, a rare position for a woman.

The war began at the end of March 1900. Asante forces besieged the British fort in Kumasi, where the governor, his officials, soldiers and others had taken refuge. They cut telegraph lines and built stockades to block the roads. The siege lasted for weeks; food ran short, and many in the fort died of disease and hunger. In June, Hodgson and part of the garrison broke out and escaped. In July, a British relief force arrived and lifted the siege.

Over the following months, British forces, including many African soldiers from other British colonies, defeated the Asante in a series of battles and burned villages. Yaa Asantewaa was captured in 1901 and exiled to the Seychelles, where she died in 1921. On 1 January 1902, by an order made in September 1901, Britain formally annexed Asante as a British colony, and the Northern Territories, the lands to the north, became a British protectorate. The Gold Coast Colony, Asante and the Northern Territories were ruled together by the governor of the Gold Coast.

But the British never captured the Golden Stool. The Asante had hidden it, and it remained hidden for twenty years. When it was accidentally found and stripped of some of its gold ornaments by road workers in 1921, the Asante tried the men, and the British, by then aware of its significance, assured the Asante that they would not interfere with it. The stool remains a symbol of the Asante nation today.

The war of 1900 was costly for both sides. Thousands of Asante fighters and civilians died, and many villages were destroyed. The British and their African soldiers also suffered heavy losses from fighting and disease. After the war, the British imprisoned or exiled many leading chiefs and confiscated land and property. But they also learned that the Golden Stool could not be treated as a mere object of power, and later governors avoided touching it.

The questions for this section are with you now: Hodgson's demand, Yaa Asantewaa, the siege of Kumasi and the annexation of Asante.

## Subtopic: Timelines, Sources and the Error Museum

A timeline helps to see how quickly British control expanded. In 1850 the Danes sold their forts; in 1872 the Dutch handed over theirs; in 1874 Kumasi was burned and the Gold Coast Colony declared; in 1896 Prempeh was arrested and Asante became a protectorate; in 1900 the War of the Golden Stool was fought; and from 1 January 1902 Asante was a colony. From 1874 to 1902 is twenty-eight years, because nineteen hundred and two minus eighteen seventy-four is twenty-eight. In less than thirty years, an independent kingdom that had defeated a British army in 1824 became a British colony.

Sources on these events come from both sides. British sources include official reports, despatches, newspapers and the memoirs of officers and of Lady Hodgson, the governor's wife, who wrote an account of the siege. They give dates and details but present British actions as necessary and Asante resistance as rebellion. Asante sources include oral traditions about Yaa Asantewaa, the Golden Stool and Prempeh, songs and the testimony of chiefs, as well as letters written by Prempeh and the Asante envoys to London. They show Asante reasons for resistance and the meaning of the Golden Stool. Comparing them shows how the same event, for example the war of 1900, was a rebellion to the British and a war of independence to the Asante.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the British conquered Asante in one war. It took several wars and stages from 1874 to 1901. Two: saying Prempeh I was defeated in battle in 1896. He chose not to fight and was arrested. Three: saying the British captured the Golden Stool. They never did. Four: forgetting that many soldiers on the British side were Africans from other colonies. Five: describing the 1900 war only from the British point of view, as a rebellion.

Layout for marks in an essay on the British colonisation of the Gold Coast: introduce the situation by 1872, explain each stage with dates, describe Asante resistance and diplomacy, explain why the British succeeded, and conclude on the significance of the Golden Stool remaining free.

The questions for this section are with you now: the timeline of British expansion, sources from both sides and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Kumasi Burns

By 1872, Britain held all the forts on the coast, including Elmina. The Asante saw this as a threat and attacked.

In 1874, a British army under Wolseley marched through the forest with rifles and cannons, and burned Kumasi. Britain made the coast a colony, the Gold Coast Colony. The Asante had to pay gold.

But the Asante kingdom survived, and in 1888 Prempeh I became Asantehene.

## Subtopic: A King Taken Away

In the 1890s, the scramble was on. Britain feared France or Germany would take Asante first. Britain offered Prempeh protection. He said no: the Asante wanted to stay independent.

In 1896, British soldiers came to Kumasi. Prempeh did not fight, to save his people. The British arrested him and his family and sent them far away, in the end to the Seychelles islands.

Then the British started searching for the Golden Stool.

The British also wanted Asante gold, and a mining company began work at Obuasi in 1897.

## Subtopic: Yaa Asantewaa's War

In 1900, the British governor demanded to sit on the Golden Stool. The Asante were deeply insulted. Yaa Asantewaa, a queen mother, said that if the men would not fight, the women would. She led the war. The Asante besieged the British fort in Kumasi for months.

The British sent more soldiers and won. Yaa Asantewaa was sent to the Seychelles. From 1902 Asante was a British colony. But the British never found the Golden Stool.
