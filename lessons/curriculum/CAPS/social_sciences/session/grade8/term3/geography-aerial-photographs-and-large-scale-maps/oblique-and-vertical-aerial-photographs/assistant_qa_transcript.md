### subtopic_1 — What an Aerial Photograph Is
**Assistant:** Why does the scale change across an oblique photograph?

**Tutor:** Because different parts of an oblique photograph are at very different distances from the camera. Think about standing at the end of a long, straight road and taking a photograph along it. The lamp posts close to you look tall and far apart, while the lamp posts in the distance look tiny and close together, even though they are all the same height and evenly spaced. This is called perspective. An oblique aerial photograph works the same way: the camera is tilted, so the ground in the foreground is much closer to the camera than the ground in the background. A soccer field in the foreground might appear several centimetres long, while an identical field near the horizon might appear only a few millimetres long. That means one centimetre on the photograph represents a small distance in the foreground and a much larger distance in the background, so there is no single scale for the whole photograph. On a vertical photograph, by contrast, the camera looks straight down, so all points on flat ground are roughly the same distance from the camera, and the scale is roughly the same everywhere. Even a vertical photograph has some distortion, especially near the edges and where the land is hilly, because hilltops are closer to the camera than valleys. That is why mapmakers correct vertical photographs to make orthophotographs, in which the scale is the same everywhere.

### subtopic_2 — Oblique and Vertical Photographs Compared
**Assistant:** How can I tell a low oblique from a high oblique photograph?

**Tutor:** The simplest test is the horizon. Look at the top part of the photograph. If you can see the line where the land or sea meets the sky, the photograph is a high oblique, because the camera was tilted enough to include the distance and the sky. If you cannot see the horizon, and the whole photograph shows the ground, but you can still see the sides of buildings, the faces of hills or the trunks of trees, the photograph is a low oblique, because the camera was tilted only slightly. If you see only the tops of features, with no sides and no horizon, it is a vertical photograph. There are other differences too. A high oblique photograph usually covers a much larger area, because it includes ground far into the distance, but it has a very large change of scale from front to back and less detail in the background. A low oblique covers a smaller area, with more detail and a smaller change of scale. In exam questions, you are often shown a photograph and asked to identify its type and give a reason. A strong answer names the type and gives clear evidence from the photograph, for example: this is a high oblique photograph, because the horizon is visible and the sides of the buildings can be seen, and features in the background appear smaller than those in the foreground.

### subtopic_3 — Natural and Constructed Features
**Assistant:** If a round shape on a vertical photograph could be a tree or a tank, how do I decide?

**Tutor:** Use more than one clue, and especially look at tone, shadow, texture and association. A tree seen from above usually has an irregular, slightly fuzzy edge, a rough texture made by its leaves and branches, and a dark or medium tone. Its shadow, falling to one side, has an uneven shape, with the outline of the crown and sometimes a thin trunk. Trees often appear in groups, along streets, in gardens or in parks, and their sizes vary. A water tank, on the other hand, usually has a perfectly circular, sharp edge, a smooth texture and often a very light tone, because it is made of metal or concrete that reflects light. Its shadow is a regular shape, showing straight vertical sides. Tanks are often associated with other constructed features: they may be on a hill above a town, beside a farmstead or windmill, or in groups in an industrial area or at a refinery, often connected by pipes or roads. A traffic circle, which is also round, will have roads leading into it from several directions, and it may have a grassy centre. So the answer is to combine clues: shape alone is not enough, but shape plus edge, tone, texture, shadow and surroundings usually makes the answer clear. In an exam, show this reasoning: name the feature and give two or three clues that support your answer.

### subtopic_4 — Calculating Scale and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why does flying higher make the scale smaller?

**Tutor:** Because the scale of a vertical photograph is the ratio of the camera's focal length to the flying height, and if you increase the flying height, you divide by a bigger number. Think of it with your own eyes. When you stand close to a tree, it fills your view; when you walk far away, it looks small. The same is true for a camera in an aircraft: the further it is from the ground, the smaller each feature appears on the photograph, so a given length on the photograph represents a longer distance on the ground. With a lens of one hundred and fifty-two millimetres, a flying height of one thousand five hundred and twenty metres gives a scale of one to ten thousand, so one centimetre on the photograph is one hundred metres on the ground. If the height is doubled to three thousand and forty metres, the scale becomes one to twenty thousand, so one centimetre is two hundred metres, and each feature appears half as long. The advantage is that a larger area is covered in each photograph; the disadvantage is that there is less detail. Remember that a small scale means a small fraction, such as one twenty-thousandth, which is smaller than one ten-thousandth, even though the number twenty thousand is bigger. Mapmakers choose a flying height that gives the right balance between detail and area for the job they need to do.
