# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces aerial photographs as a source of geographical information. The anchor is the comparison between an oblique aerial photograph, taken at an angle, and a vertical aerial photograph, taken looking straight down. The governing idea is that the angle of the camera controls what the photograph shows and how it can be used: oblique photographs are easy to recognise but have changing scale, while vertical photographs look like a map, have a nearly constant scale and can be used to measure, but take practice to read. Identifying the two kinds, reading them with interpretation clues and recognising natural and constructed features are examinable this term.

## Subtopic: What an Aerial Photograph Is

An aerial photograph is a photograph of the earth's surface taken from above, from an aircraft, a helicopter, a drone or a balloon. Aerial photographs are different from satellite images, which you studied in Term 1. Satellite images are taken from space, hundreds of kilometres up, and are made of digital data collected by sensors, often including light we cannot see. Aerial photographs are taken from much lower down, usually a few hundred metres to a few kilometres above the ground, so they show more detail of smaller areas.

Aerial photographs are used by many people. Town planners use them to see how cities are growing and where new roads or houses are needed. Farmers and agricultural officers use them to monitor crops and soil erosion. Engineers use them to plan roads, railways and dams. Environmental scientists use them to monitor wetlands, forests and coastlines. Mapmakers use vertical photographs to make and update maps. In South Africa, the national mapping organisation, the Chief Directorate: National Geo-spatial Information, takes aerial photographs of the whole country and uses them to produce maps, including orthophoto maps at a scale of one to ten thousand.

There are two main kinds of aerial photograph, depending on the angle of the camera. A vertical aerial photograph is taken with the camera pointing straight down, at right angles to the ground. An oblique aerial photograph is taken with the camera tilted, at an angle to the ground. Oblique photographs are further divided into low oblique photographs, in which the camera is tilted only a little and the horizon is not visible, and high oblique photographs, in which the camera is tilted more and the horizon is visible.

Because aerial photographs record what is actually on the ground at one moment, they are an excellent source of evidence. Comparing photographs of the same place taken in different years shows change over time: the growth of a township, the spread of an informal settlement, the building of a new highway or the loss of farmland. Every aerial photograph should therefore have a date, so that you know when the information was recorded.

Today, drones have made aerial photography much cheaper and more common. They can fly low and take very detailed photographs of small areas, for example to inspect a building site, count animals in a game reserve or map a small village.

## Subtopic: Oblique and Vertical Photographs Compared

Oblique and vertical photographs look very different, and each has advantages and disadvantages.

An oblique aerial photograph shows features from the side and the top at the same time, much as you would see them from a tall building or a hill. You can see the sides of buildings, the height of mountains, the shape of trees and the slope of the land. This makes it easy to recognise features, so oblique photographs are often used in books, newspapers, advertising and tourism. However, the scale changes across the photograph. Features in the foreground, close to the camera, appear large, and features of the same size in the background appear much smaller. Tall features can hide what is behind them. Because the scale is not constant, you cannot use an oblique photograph to measure distances or areas accurately. In a high oblique photograph, the horizon is visible and a large area is shown; in a low oblique photograph, the horizon is not visible and a smaller area is shown in more detail.

A vertical aerial photograph shows the earth from directly above, like a map. You see only the tops of features: roofs, treetops, road surfaces, fields and water. There is no horizon. Because all parts of the flat ground are roughly the same distance from the camera, the scale is almost the same across the photograph, so a vertical photograph can be used to measure distances and areas, and it can be compared directly with a map of the same area. Vertical photographs cover a definite area with no hidden ground behind tall features, which is why they are used for mapmaking. However, features can be hard to recognise because we are not used to seeing things from straight above. A round shape might be a water tank, a tree or a traffic circle.

When vertical photographs are corrected so that the scale is exactly the same everywhere, removing distortion caused by the camera lens and the height of the land, they are called orthophotographs. Orthophoto maps combine an orthophotograph with map information such as contour lines, place names, grid references and a scale. South Africa's orthophoto maps at one to ten thousand are widely used in Geography examinations together with topographic maps at one to fifty thousand.

To summarise: if you want to recognise and describe features, an oblique photograph is easier; if you want to measure, map or compare with a map, a vertical photograph is better.

Photographs for mapping are taken in a careful pattern. The aircraft flies in straight parallel lines across the area, and the camera takes a photograph at regular intervals, so that each photograph overlaps the next by about sixty per cent along the flight line and neighbouring flight lines overlap by about twenty to thirty per cent. Because each piece of ground appears in two overlapping photographs taken from slightly different positions, viewing the pair through a stereoscope gives a three-dimensional view of the land, which mapmakers use to draw contour lines.

## Subtopic: Natural and Constructed Features

Features on aerial photographs are of two kinds. Natural features are formed by nature: mountains, hills, valleys, cliffs, rivers, lakes, wetlands, beaches, sand dunes, natural forests and grassland. Constructed features, also called human-made or built features, are made by people: buildings, roads, railways, bridges, dams, canals, harbours, airports, fields, plantations, sports fields and power lines.

To identify features on a photograph, especially a vertical one, geographers use a set of interpretation clues.

Tone or colour means how light or dark a feature appears. On black-and-white photographs, deep, clear water usually appears very dark, while sand, concrete and dry grass appear light. Healthy dense vegetation is often dark grey or dark green.

Texture means how smooth or rough a surface looks. A smooth texture may be water, a tarred road or a sports field; a rough, uneven texture may be a forest or bush.

Shape is very useful, because constructed features usually have regular shapes, such as straight lines, right angles, rectangles and circles, while natural features usually have irregular shapes. A river meanders in curves; a canal is straight. A natural forest has an irregular edge; a plantation is in neat blocks with straight edges and rows.

Size helps to tell features apart: a house roof and a factory roof may have the same shape but very different sizes. You can judge size by comparing with features you know, such as cars or a soccer field.

Shadow shows the height and shape of features. On a vertical photograph, a tall building, a tree or a tower casts a long shadow that shows its side profile, and all shadows point in the same direction, away from the sun.

Pattern means how features are arranged. Rows of identical small houses make a regular pattern; an informal settlement makes an irregular, dense pattern; an orchard shows trees in neat rows.

Association, or site, means the surroundings of a feature. A long straight line with stations next to it is a railway; a large flat open area with long straight strips next to buildings is probably an airport; a wall across a valley with water behind it is a dam.

Use several clues together. For example, a large, regular, light-toned rectangle with smaller white lines inside it, next to a school building, is probably a sports field.

Season and time of day also affect what you see. In winter on the highveld, grassland is dry and light-toned, while in summer it is green and darker. Crops look different before and after harvest. Photographs taken in the early morning or late afternoon have long shadows, which make heights easier to see, while photographs taken near midday have short shadows. Always check the date and, if possible, the time.

## Subtopic: Calculating Scale and the Error Museum

The scale of a vertical aerial photograph depends on two things: the focal length of the camera lens, and the height of the aircraft above the ground. The scale is the focal length divided by the flying height, using the same units.

Suppose a camera with a focal length of one hundred and fifty-two millimetres takes a photograph from a height of one thousand five hundred and twenty metres above the ground. First, change the units so they are the same: one hundred and fifty-two millimetres is zero point one five two metres. Then divide: zero point one five two divided by one thousand five hundred and twenty is one ten-thousandth. So the scale is one to ten thousand, the same scale as South African orthophoto maps. One centimetre on the photograph represents ten thousand centimetres on the ground, which is one hundred metres.

The higher the aircraft flies, the smaller the scale. If the same camera were flown at three thousand and forty metres, twice as high, the scale would be one to twenty thousand. Each feature would look half as long on the photograph, but a larger area would be covered.

Once you know the scale, you can measure. If a road on a one to ten thousand photograph measures four point five centimetres, its real length is four point five times one hundred metres, which is four hundred and fifty metres.

Aerial photographs of the same place from different years can be compared to show change. To compare, find features that have not changed, such as a main road or a river, to match the photographs, then look for new buildings, roads, fields or open land that has disappeared.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: confusing aerial photographs with satellite images. Aerial photographs are taken from aircraft or drones, much lower. Two: trying to measure distance on an oblique photograph. Its scale changes. Three: saying vertical photographs show the sides of buildings. They show only roofs, though shadows give clues to height. Four: forgetting to convert units in the scale calculation. Five: identifying a feature from one clue alone. Use several clues together.

Layout for marks in a photograph question: state the type of photograph and the evidence, such as whether the horizon is visible, identify features using at least two clues each, and classify each as natural or constructed.

## Subtopic: Pictures From the Sky

An aerial photograph is a photo of the ground taken from above, from a plane, a helicopter or a drone. It is closer to the ground than a satellite, so it shows more detail.

Planners, farmers, engineers and mapmakers use aerial photographs to see what is on the ground and how it has changed.

There are two kinds. If the camera points straight down, it is a vertical photograph. If the camera is tilted, it is an oblique photograph.

Try it with your phone: hold it flat above your desk and take a photo, then tilt it and take another.

## Subtopic: Tilted or Straight Down

An oblique photo is tilted. You see the sides of buildings and maybe the horizon. It is easy to recognise things, but things far away look smaller, so you cannot measure with it.

A vertical photo looks straight down, like a map. You see only roofs, road tops and treetops. It is harder to recognise things, but you can measure with it.

Easy to recognise: oblique. Easy to measure: vertical.

A high oblique shows the horizon. A low oblique does not, but you can still see the sides of things.

## Subtopic: Nature or People?

Natural features are made by nature: rivers, mountains, beaches, forests. Constructed features are made by people: roads, buildings, dams, fields.

Clues help you: people make straight lines and neat shapes; nature makes curves and irregular shapes. Shadows show how tall things are. Dark smooth areas are often water.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Aerial photographs are taken from aircraft or drones. Vertical photographs look straight down, like a map, with a nearly constant scale, so you can measure on them. Oblique photographs are tilted, show the sides of features and sometimes the horizon, and are easy to recognise but cannot be used to measure. Scale equals focal length divided by flying height. Features can be natural or constructed, and you identify them using tone, texture, shape, size, shadow, pattern and association.
