### subtopic_1 — Large-Scale Maps and Their Tools
**Assistant:** Why do examiners give both a topographic map and an orthophoto map?

**Tutor:** Because the two sources do different jobs, and using them together shows a deeper understanding. The topographic map covers a larger area and shows the whole setting of a town: the relief, the rivers, the main roads and railways, the surrounding farms and other settlements, and the symbols for many features that cannot easily be recognised on a photograph, such as a police station, a post office, a type of road or a power line. It tells you what things are, because the mapmakers have already classified them with symbols. The orthophoto map covers a smaller area in much more detail, and shows what the land actually looks like: the size and shape of buildings, the density of houses, trees and gardens, swimming pools, the condition of roads, informal settlements, sports fields and much more. Some things, like the difference between high-income and low-income residential areas, are much easier to see on the orthophoto than on the topographic map, while some things, like the name and type of a public building, are easier on the topographic map. Examiners want you to move between the two: to find the area of the orthophoto on the topographic map, to use the map's symbols to confirm what you see on the photograph, and to use the photograph to add detail to the map. They also test your skills of scale, direction and measurement on both. So the pair tests both your knowledge of land use and your map skills at the same time.

### subtopic_2 — Evidence for Each Land Use Zone
**Assistant:** How can I tell a shopping centre from a factory on a vertical photograph?

**Tutor:** Both can have large, flat roofs, so you need to look at the surroundings and other details, which is the clue called association. A suburban shopping centre is usually surrounded by a very large car park, often with many cars parked in neat rows close to the entrances. It is located at or near the junction of main roads, and is surrounded by residential areas, because its customers live nearby. There may be smaller buildings around it, such as a fuel station, fast-food outlets and banks, and landscaping such as trees and lawns. There are usually no railway sidings, storage tanks, stockpiles or chimneys. A factory, on the other hand, is usually in an industrial area with other similar large buildings around it, rather than houses. Its open space is used as a yard for storing materials, containers or vehicles, with trucks and loading bays rather than rows of cars. It may have a railway siding, storage tanks, chimneys or stockpiles, and it is often on the edge of town, near a railway, highway or harbour. On a topographic map, an industrial area may be labelled, and a shopping centre may also be named. So look for parking pattern, neighbours, transport links and the presence of industrial features. Using two or three of these clues together will usually give you a clear and well-supported answer.

### subtopic_3 — Reading a Town: a Worked Example
**Assistant:** Why is it wrong to say an area is rich because the people drive expensive cars?

**Tutor:** Because that evidence cannot be seen on the source, and a geographical answer must be based on evidence from the photograph or map in front of you. On a vertical aerial photograph at one to ten thousand, cars appear as tiny rectangles, and you cannot tell their make, model or price. Even on a very detailed photograph, you cannot know what people earn or who drives which car. The same applies to statements such as the people are well educated or the houses are expensive inside: you cannot see these things. Examiners give marks for evidence that is actually visible, such as plot size, house size, density, trees and gardens, swimming pools, tennis courts, street pattern and distance from industry. These visible features are strongly linked to income, because high-income households can afford large plots, pools and gardens in attractive locations, so they are valid evidence. A good habit is to ask yourself, before writing any piece of evidence, can I point to this on the source? If you can, use it. If you cannot, leave it out or turn it into something visible. For example, instead of the people are rich, write the plots are large and many houses have swimming pools, which suggests a high-income area. This is the difference between an assumption and evidence, and it is an important skill not only in Geography but in History and every other subject that uses sources.

### subtopic_4 — Measuring, Evidence and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do I describe where something is on a map?

**Tutor:** Use direction, distance and reference points together. First, use the north arrow to give compass directions: north, north-east, east, south-east, south, south-west, west and north-west. Always describe the position of one feature in relation to another, for example the heavy industry is south-east of the CBD. Make sure you describe direction from the right starting point: the industry is south-east of the CBD, which means the CBD is north-west of the industry. Second, give distance using the scale: measure the straight-line distance or the distance along a road, and convert it using the scale, for example about one point five kilometres south-east of the CBD. Third, use reference points such as rivers, railways, roads or named places: the industry lies along the railway, south of the river. Fourth, where a question asks for an exact location, use the grid. On South African maps, positions are given in degrees, minutes and seconds of latitude and longitude, for example twenty-six degrees twelve minutes south, twenty-eight degrees four minutes east, and you work them out by counting the minutes and seconds from the nearest grid lines. Finally, describe the site of the feature: on the flood plain, on the hilltop, along the coast. A complete description might be: the golf course is about two kilometres north-west of the CBD, on high ground on the northern bank of the river. That gives the examiner clear, checkable information.
