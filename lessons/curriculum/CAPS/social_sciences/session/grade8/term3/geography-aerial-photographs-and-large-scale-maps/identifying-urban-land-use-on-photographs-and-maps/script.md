# Part 1 — Expert

This session brings together urban land use and map work. The anchor is a matched pair of sources: a vertical aerial photograph or orthophoto map at one to ten thousand, and a topographic map at one to fifty thousand of the same urban area. The governing idea is that every urban land use zone leaves visible evidence, in the size, shape, pattern and surroundings of features on a photograph, and in the symbols and street pattern on a map, and that a good answer always names the land use and supports it with evidence. Identifying land uses on aerial photographs and large-scale maps, and measuring on them, is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Large-Scale Maps and Their Tools

A large-scale map shows a small area in great detail. Remember that a large scale is a large fraction, such as one to ten thousand, and a small scale is a small fraction, such as one to one million. In South Africa, the two map types used most in schools are topographic maps at one to fifty thousand and orthophoto maps at one to ten thousand.

A topographic map at one to fifty thousand shows the physical and human features of an area using symbols, colours and lines. It shows relief with contour lines, rivers, dams and wetlands, vegetation, roads, railways, buildings, built-up areas, power lines, and many other features, each with a symbol explained in the key, also called the legend. On a one to fifty thousand map, one centimetre represents fifty thousand centimetres, which is five hundred metres. South African topographic maps are named and numbered using a system based on latitude and longitude, so a map numbered 2628 covers the area between twenty-six and twenty-seven degrees south and twenty-eight and twenty-nine degrees east, and smaller sheets within it are given letters.

An orthophoto map at one to ten thousand is a corrected vertical aerial photograph with map information printed on it: contour lines, names of roads and places, spot heights, a grid and a scale. It shows the real appearance of the land in much more detail than a topographic map, because it is five times larger in scale: on a one to ten thousand map, one centimetre represents one hundred metres. An orthophoto map usually covers only a small part of the area shown on the topographic map, and the area it covers is often outlined on the topographic map.

Every map has tools that you must use correctly. The title tells you the area. The scale lets you measure distance and area. The key explains the symbols. The north arrow shows direction, so you can describe where one feature is in relation to another using compass directions. The grid, of lines of latitude and longitude in degrees, minutes and seconds, lets you give the exact position of a feature. The date tells you how up to date the information is.

When you compare a topographic map and an orthophoto map, remember that they have different scales. A feature that is one centimetre long on the topographic map would be five centimetres long on the orthophoto map, because fifty thousand divided by ten thousand is five. Find a large feature that appears on both, such as a main road, a railway, a river or a dam, to match the two maps and orient yourself.

## Subtopic: Evidence for Each Land Use Zone

Each urban land use zone has typical evidence on a vertical photograph and on a map.

The CBD. On a photograph: the largest and tallest buildings, close together, casting long shadows; little or no green space; a dense grid of streets; many vehicles; large parking areas or parking garages; the meeting point of main roads and often a railway station or taxi rank. On a map: a dense built-up area with large buildings close together, many public buildings such as the post office, police station, town hall and churches shown with their symbols, and the convergence of main roads and railway lines.

Industrial zones. On a photograph: large buildings with big flat or saw-tooth roofs, few houses, large yards with stored materials or vehicles, wide roads for trucks, and railway sidings leading into sites. Heavy industry shows storage tanks, chimneys, cooling towers, stockpiles and conveyor belts, often beside a harbour, railway or river. On a map: features may be labelled as an industrial area or works, with railway sidings, power lines, reservoirs and large building symbols, usually on the edge of the built-up area.

High-income residential areas. On a photograph: large plots with big houses, lots of trees and gardens, swimming pools that appear as small bright blue or light shapes, tennis courts, curved streets, crescents and cul-de-sacs, and possibly a golf course. On a map: widely spaced building symbols, curved streets, and recreation features such as golf courses.

Middle-income residential areas. On a photograph: medium-sized houses on medium plots, regular streets, some gardens, townhouse complexes and blocks of flats. On a map: building symbols closer together in a regular pattern.

Low-income residential areas. On a photograph: many small houses of similar size and shape in long, straight, closely spaced rows on small plots, few trees, and often dirt roads or paths; informal settlements appear as very dense, irregular clusters of small roofs with narrow, winding paths. On a map: dense clusters of building symbols in a regular grid, often on the edge of the town, sometimes separated from other areas by a railway, highway or open land.

Recreation and services. Sports fields appear as large rectangles of smooth texture, often with markings and a running track; golf courses as irregular green areas with smooth fairways and sand bunkers; schools as buildings next to sports fields; cemeteries as rows of small regular marks with paths. On maps, these have their own symbols.

Transport. Highways appear as wide roads with interchanges, railways as narrow straight or gently curving lines with stations and yards, and airports as long straight runways with large hangars and terminal buildings.

## Subtopic: Reading a Town: a Worked Example

Let us read an imaginary town step by step, the way you would in an examination. The town lies on a river, with a railway running from east to west through its centre.

Step one: orient yourself. Find north, find the scale, and find large features: the river, the railway, the main roads.

Step two: find the CBD. Near the centre of the town, where the main road crosses the railway, there is a station. Around it, the buildings are the largest and closest together, the street grid is densest, and the map shows a post office, a police station, a town hall and several churches. On the orthophoto, this area has the tallest buildings, with long shadows and almost no trees. This is the CBD. Evidence: convergence of roads and railway, large dense buildings, public buildings, tall buildings with shadows.

Step three: find industry. East of the CBD, alongside the railway, there are large buildings with big roofs, a railway siding branching into the site, and large round tanks. A smaller area of medium-sized, flat-roofed buildings lies beside the main road to the north. The first is heavy industry, because of the size, the tanks and the siding. The second is light industry, because of the smaller buildings and road access.

Step four: find the residential areas. North-west of the CBD, on higher ground with a view over the river, there are large plots, many trees, swimming pools, curved streets and a golf course: a high-income area. South of the railway, beyond the heavy industry, there are long rows of small houses close together, with few trees: a low-income area. Next to it, an area of dense, irregular small structures without a street pattern is an informal settlement. Notice that the low-income area is separated from the CBD and from the high-income area by the railway and the industry, a pattern that reflects apartheid planning.

Step five: find recreation, services and transport. Along the river, which floods sometimes, there is a long strip of open land with sports fields and a park. Schools appear as large buildings beside sports fields. A cemetery lies on the edge of the town.

Step six: explain. For each zone, explain its location: the CBD at the most accessible point; heavy industry beside the railway, on the edge, downwind of the high-income area; the high-income area on high ground away from industry; the low-income area far from the centre, separated by buffer zones; recreation on the flood plain where building is unsafe.

Not every town fits this pattern exactly. In a coastal town, the CBD may be beside the harbour rather than in the middle. In a mining town, the mine dumps and shafts may lie right next to the centre. In a small town, there may be no heavy industry at all, and light industry may be a few workshops beside the main road. Always read the actual evidence on the source rather than forcing the town into a model.

## Subtopic: Measuring, Evidence and the Error Museum

Measuring on maps supports your answers. On a one to ten thousand orthophoto map, one centimetre represents one hundred metres. Suppose a sports field measures one centimetre by zero point seven centimetres on the map. On the ground, that is one hundred metres by seventy metres. Its area is one hundred times seventy, which is seven thousand square metres, or zero point seven hectares, because one hectare is ten thousand square metres. Suppose an industrial site measures two centimetres by three centimetres on the same map. That is two hundred metres by three hundred metres, which is sixty thousand square metres, or six hectares.

On a one to fifty thousand topographic map, one centimetre represents five hundred metres. If the distance from the CBD to a township measures twelve centimetres along the road, the real distance is twelve times five hundred metres, which is six thousand metres, or six kilometres. This kind of measurement is evidence for statements about the distance that people must travel to work.

Every answer about land use should follow the pattern: claim, evidence, explanation. For example: Area B is a high-income residential area. Evidence: large plots, swimming pools and curved streets. Explanation: it is on high ground away from the industrial area, where land is attractive and expensive.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: naming a land use without giving evidence. Two: using evidence that cannot be seen on the source, such as saying the people are rich because they drive expensive cars, when cars cannot be identified. Three: forgetting that the two maps have different scales, and measuring on the orthophoto as if it were one to fifty thousand. Four: mixing up direction, for example describing the industry as east of the CBD when the north arrow shows it is south. Five: confusing a large flat roof of a shopping centre in a suburb with industry. Use association: a shopping centre has large parking areas and is surrounded by houses.

Layout for marks: identify the land use, give two pieces of evidence from the source, explain the location, and where asked, measure and show your working with units.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: the two kinds of map, spotting zones, and giving evidence.

## Subtopic: Two Kinds of Map

A topographic map at one to fifty thousand uses symbols to show roads, rivers, buildings and the shape of the land. One centimetre on it is five hundred metres.

An orthophoto map at one to ten thousand is a real photo from straight above, with names and lines added. It shows much more detail. One centimetre on it is one hundred metres.

Always check the title, scale, key and north arrow first.

Think of it like this: the topographic map is a drawing; the orthophoto map is a photo with labels.

Keep those two maps in mind and try the questions for this part: large-scale maps.

## Subtopic: Spot the Zone

Each zone has its clues.

The CBD: tall buildings close together, long shadows, where roads and railways meet.

Industry: big roofs, tanks, chimneys, railway lines going into the sites.

Rich homes: big gardens, pools, curved streets.

Poorer homes: small houses in long tight rows. Informal settlements: crowded small roofs with winding paths.

Parks and sports fields: big smooth green areas, often next to rivers.

Use more than one clue each time. One clue can trick you; two or three clues together are much safer.

Keep the clues in mind and try the questions for this part: spotting land use zones.

## Subtopic: Show Your Evidence

In an exam, always say what the land use is, then give your clues, then explain why it is there. For example: this is industry, because I can see big roofs and a railway line going in; it is next to the railway because it needs to move heavy goods.

Use the scale to measure: on a one to fifty thousand map, twelve centimetres is six kilometres.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Topographic maps at one to fifty thousand and orthophoto maps at one to ten thousand are the main large-scale maps. Each land use zone has visible evidence: the CBD has tall, dense buildings where roads meet; industry has large roofs, tanks and sidings; high-income areas have large plots and pools; low-income areas have dense rows of small houses; recreation is often on flood plains. Good answers give a claim, evidence and an explanation, and measurements with units.

Keep claim, evidence and explanation in mind, and take the final questions of the lesson: urban land use on photographs and maps.
