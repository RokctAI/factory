# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the map of Africa after the scramble and the causes of colonisation. The anchor is the political map of Africa in 1914, showing seven colonising powers: Britain, France, Germany, Belgium, Portugal, Italy and Spain. The governing idea is that colonisation had several connected causes: economic, the demand of industrialised Europe for raw materials, markets and profits; political and strategic, rivalry between European powers and national prestige; and social and ideological, including missionary activity and racist ideas of European superiority, made possible by new technology. Reading the colonial map and explaining the causes of colonisation are examinable this term.

## Subtopic: The Map of Africa in 1914

By 1914, seven European countries controlled almost the whole of Africa.

Britain had the largest African empire by population. It stretched, with some gaps, from Egypt and Anglo-Egyptian Sudan in the north, through Uganda and British East Africa, now Kenya, and Nyasaland, now Malawi, to Northern and Southern Rhodesia, now Zambia and Zimbabwe, the Bechuanaland Protectorate, Basutoland and Swaziland, and the Union of South Africa, which was a self-governing dominion of the British Empire. In West Africa, Britain held Nigeria, the Gold Coast, now Ghana, Sierra Leone and the Gambia. British Somaliland, on the Horn of Africa, was also British. Cecil John Rhodes had dreamed of a line of British territory from the Cape to Cairo, but German East Africa blocked it until 1918.

France had the largest African empire by area. French West Africa included what are now Senegal, Mali, Guinea, Côte d'Ivoire, Burkina Faso, Niger, Benin and Mauritania. French Equatorial Africa included what are now Gabon, the Republic of the Congo, the Central African Republic and Chad. France also ruled Algeria, Tunisia, most of Morocco, which became a protectorate in 1912, Madagascar and French Somaliland, now Djibouti. Much of France's territory was in the Sahara and the Sahel.

Germany, a late arrival, held four colonies: Togoland, Kamerun, German South West Africa, now Namibia, and German East Africa, now mainly Tanzania, with Rwanda and Burundi. Germany lost all of them after the First World War.

Belgium held the Belgian Congo, now the Democratic Republic of the Congo, taken over from King Leopold II in 1908.

Portugal held Angola, Mozambique, Portuguese Guinea, now Guinea-Bissau, and islands such as Cape Verde and São Tomé and Príncipe.

Italy held Eritrea, Italian Somaliland and Libya, which it seized from the Ottoman Empire in 1911 and 1912. Italy had tried to conquer Ethiopia but was defeated.

Spain held small territories: Spanish Morocco in the north, Río de Oro in what is now Western Sahara, and Spanish Guinea, now Equatorial Guinea.

Only two African states were independent in 1914. Ethiopia, under Emperor Menelik II, defeated an Italian invasion at the Battle of Adwa in 1896. Liberia, founded in the nineteenth century by freed African Americans with American support, remained independent, though it lost territory to its British and French neighbours.

Colonial borders often ignored African realities. Some cut across the lands of single peoples: the Maasai were divided between British East Africa and German East Africa, the Ewe between British and German Togoland, and the Somali among British, French and Italian territories and Ethiopia. Other borders grouped together peoples with very different histories, languages and political systems inside one colony. Many of these borders became the borders of independent African states in the 1950s and 1960s.

The questions for this section are with you now: the colonising countries of Africa and the territories they held in 1914.

## Subtopic: Economic Causes

The most important causes of colonisation were economic, and they were closely linked to the industrial revolution, which you studied in Term 1.

Raw materials. Europe's factories needed huge quantities of raw materials that could not be grown or mined in Europe, or not cheaply enough. Africa had many of them: palm oil, used for soap, candles and to lubricate machines; rubber, in great demand for bicycle and car tyres, hoses and electrical insulation; cotton for textile mills; cocoa and coffee; groundnuts; timber; and minerals such as gold, diamonds and copper. The discovery of diamonds and gold in South Africa, which you studied in Term 2, showed how valuable African resources could be.

Markets. Industrial countries produced more goods than their own people could buy. Factory owners and governments looked for new markets abroad for cloth, metal goods, guns, alcohol and other manufactured goods. Colonies could be forced to buy goods from the colonising country, often by placing taxes on goods from other countries.

Investment and profit. Banks and investors in Europe had large amounts of capital to invest, and colonies offered opportunities for profitable investment in mines, plantations, railways and trading companies. Chartered companies, private companies given government permission to govern and trade in a territory, played a major role. Examples include the Royal Niger Company in Nigeria, the Imperial British East Africa Company in Kenya and Uganda, and the British South Africa Company of Cecil Rhodes in what became the Rhodesias.

Competition and economic depression. From 1873, Europe experienced a long period of slower economic growth, sometimes called the Long Depression. Countries turned to protecting their own industries and markets, and colonies were seen as secure sources of raw materials and secure markets that rivals could not take away.

Some historians, then and later, argued that economic causes were the most important of all. The British writer John Hobson, in 1902, and the Russian revolutionary Vladimir Lenin, in 1917, both argued that imperialism was driven by the need of capitalists to find profitable investments abroad. Other historians argue that many colonies were not very profitable at first, and that political and strategic causes were just as important. Most historians today combine several explanations.

Some areas were especially valuable. The Gold Coast had gold and, from the 1890s, a booming cocoa industry run largely by African farmers. The Congo had rubber and later copper, especially in Katanga. Southern Africa had diamonds and gold. Egypt grew cotton for British mills. Other areas, such as large parts of the Sahara, had little immediate economic value, which shows that economics alone cannot explain every claim.

The questions for this section are with you now: raw materials, markets, investment, chartered companies and economic competition as causes of colonisation.

## Subtopic: Political, Strategic and Social Causes

Political and strategic causes were also important.

Nationalism and prestige. In the late nineteenth century, nationalism was strong in Europe. Many people believed that a great nation should have a great empire. Colonies were a symbol of national power and pride. For France, colonies helped restore national prestige after its defeat by Germany in 1870 and 1871. For newly united Germany and Italy, colonies showed that they had joined the ranks of great powers.

Rivalry and the balance of power. European powers competed with each other. If one power took a territory, others feared being left out and rushed to claim land before rivals did. This competition, more than careful planning, explains the speed of the scramble.

Strategic interests. Some territories were taken mainly for their strategic value. The Suez Canal, opened in 1869, became the main route between Britain and India, so Britain occupied Egypt in 1882 and later Sudan to protect it. The Cape of Good Hope also protected the sea route. Ports, coaling stations for steamships and naval bases were valuable.

Social and ideological causes. Missionaries wanted to spread Christianity and set up mission stations, schools and hospitals. Many missionaries genuinely believed they were helping, and some criticised abuses by colonial officials and settlers, but missionaries also often called for colonial rule to protect their work. Many Europeans believed in a so-called civilising mission, the idea that Europeans had a duty to bring their religion, laws and way of life to Africans. This idea rested on racist beliefs that Europeans were superior and Africans inferior, beliefs that were supported by pseudo-scientific theories, including social Darwinism, the false application of ideas about evolution to human societies. The British poet Rudyard Kipling's phrase the white man's burden, from 1899, expressed this attitude. These ideas are rejected today as racist and false, but at the time they were used to justify conquest. Some Europeans also saw colonies as places for settlement, where poor or unemployed people could start new lives.

Technology made colonisation possible. Steamships, railways and the telegraph made travel and communication faster. Quinine, used to prevent and treat malaria, reduced deaths among Europeans in tropical Africa. New weapons, especially breech-loading rifles and the Maxim machine gun, invented in 1884, gave European armies a huge advantage in battle.

The causes worked together. Economic interests, national rivalry, strategic concerns and ideas of superiority reinforced each other, and new technology made it possible to act on them.

The questions for this section are with you now: nationalism, rivalry, strategic interests, the civilising mission, racism and technology as causes of colonisation.

## Subtopic: Explaining Causes and the Error Museum

Historians often classify causes into types, because this helps to organise an answer. A useful method is to use the headings economic, political, strategic and social or ideological, and to give evidence for each.

A calculation helps to show the scale of the scramble. Africa covers about thirty point four million square kilometres. If about ninety per cent was under European control by 1914, that is about zero point nine times thirty point four million, which is about twenty-seven point four million square kilometres. Europe, by comparison, covers about ten million square kilometres. Twenty-seven point four divided by ten is about two point seven, so European powers controlled an area of Africa almost three times the size of Europe itself.

Sources from the period show the causes clearly. A speech by a French politician in the 1880s, Jules Ferry, argued that colonies were needed as markets for French industry and that superior races had a duty towards inferior races. This one source shows both economic and ideological causes, and its racism is obvious to a modern reader. A cartoon from 1892 showed Cecil Rhodes standing astride Africa from Cape Town to Cairo, holding a telegraph wire. It shows the ambition of British imperialists and the role of technology. When you use such sources, identify the author, date and purpose, and explain which cause each shows.

African perspectives are also essential. African rulers and people did not see colonisation as a civilising mission, but as invasion, loss of land and loss of independence. Many resisted, which you will study next lesson.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying colonisation had a single cause. Several causes worked together. Two: confusing the colonising powers, for example saying Angola was British. It was Portuguese. Three: presenting the civilising mission as a fact rather than a racist justification. Four: forgetting the role of technology, which made colonisation possible but did not cause it on its own. Five: saying all of Africa was colonised by 1914. Ethiopia and Liberia remained independent.

Layout for marks in an essay on the causes of colonisation: introduction, a paragraph each on economic, political and strategic, and social and ideological causes with evidence, a paragraph on technology as the enabler, and a conclusion weighing which causes were most important.

The questions for this section are with you now: classifying causes, the area calculation, sources on the causes and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Seven Colours on the Map

By 1914, seven European countries had divided Africa.

Britain had a long line of colonies from Egypt down to South Africa, and Nigeria and Ghana in the west. France had a huge area in the north and west, much of it desert. Germany had Namibia, Tanzania, Cameroon and Togo. Belgium had the Congo. Portugal had Angola and Mozambique. Italy had Libya, Eritrea and Somalia. Spain had small pieces.

Only Ethiopia and Liberia were still free. Ethiopia beat Italy at the Battle of Adwa in 1896.

Picture a quilt in seven colours, with two patches left in their own colours.

South Africa was British too, but it governed itself as the Union from 1910.

## Subtopic: Money and Power

Why did Europe want Africa? First, money. Factories needed rubber, palm oil, cotton, cocoa, gold and copper. They also needed people to sell their goods to. Companies wanted to make profits from mines and farms.

Second, power. Each country wanted to look strong and not be left behind by its rivals. Britain wanted to protect the Suez Canal, its road to India.

Think of it like a race: once one country started grabbing land, the others ran faster.

Rich farming areas and mines were the biggest prizes: cocoa in Ghana, rubber and copper in the Congo, gold and diamonds in South Africa.

Keep the race in mind and try the questions for this part: economic and political causes.

## Subtopic: Beliefs and Machines

Some Europeans believed they had a duty to civilise Africans. This idea was racist and wrong: African societies had their own laws, beliefs, art and governments. Missionaries wanted to spread Christianity, and some also helped with schools and clinics.

Machines made it possible: steamships, railways, the telegraph, quinine against malaria, and the Maxim machine gun.

Pocket summary of the lesson. By 1914, Britain, France, Germany, Belgium, Portugal, Italy and Spain controlled almost all of Africa; only Ethiopia and Liberia were independent. The causes of colonisation were economic, such as raw materials, markets and profits; political and strategic, such as prestige, rivalry and the Suez route; and social and ideological, such as missionary work and racist ideas of a civilising mission. New technology made colonisation possible.
