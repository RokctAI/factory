### subtopic_1 — Africa Before the Scramble
**Assistant:** If the Berlin Conference didn't draw the borders, why is it so famous?

**Tutor:** Because it is a powerful symbol of how the colonisation of Africa happened, and because it set the rules that made the scramble faster and more orderly for Europeans. The image of European diplomats sitting around a map of Africa in Berlin, with no Africans present, captures the central injustice of colonialism: decisions about Africa and its people were made in Europe, by Europeans, for European interests. Even though the borders were drawn later, the conference was the first time the major powers met to agree collectively on how African territory and trade should be claimed. It recognised Leopold II's claim to a vast area of Central Africa, the Congo Free State, which became the scene of some of the worst abuses of the colonial era. The principle of effective occupation pushed each power to turn paper claims into real control quickly, which led to a rush of treaty-making, military expeditions and new colonial administrations in the years that followed. So historians see the conference as a turning point in the speed and character of the scramble, even if it did not start it. Its fame also comes from its place in African memory and politics: after independence, African leaders and writers often pointed to Berlin as the moment when Africa's fate was decided without its people, and the conference became a reference point in discussions about colonial borders, which the Organisation of African Unity decided in 1964 to keep, to avoid wars between new states.

### subtopic_2 — Who Met in Berlin and Why
**Assistant:** Why did African rulers sign treaties with Europeans if it meant losing their land?

**Tutor:** In most cases, they did not believe they were giving away their land or sovereignty, and many treaties were obtained through misunderstanding, deception or pressure. African rulers had long experience of making agreements with Europeans and with other African states about trade, friendship, alliances and protection. When a European agent arrived with a treaty, a ruler might understand it as an agreement to trade with that power, to allow missionaries or traders in, or to receive protection against a rival, perhaps another African state or another European power. The documents, however, were written in European languages that most rulers could not read, and translations were often inaccurate or deliberately misleading. A treaty that a ruler thought was about friendship might, in the European text, say that he placed his territory under the sovereignty or protection of a European power. Some agents gave gifts, alcohol or promises in return for marks on documents, and some treaties were signed by people who had no authority to give away land. In other cases, rulers signed under the threat of force, or because they hoped to use one European power against another, or against African rivals. Later, when Europeans used these treaties to claim full control, many rulers protested and resisted. So treaties were less a fair agreement than a tool that Europeans used to make their claims look legal to each other, which is exactly what the Berlin Conference's rules required.

### subtopic_3 — What the Conference Decided
**Assistant:** What happened in the Congo Free State?

**Tutor:** The Congo Free State, recognised during the Berlin Conference, was a huge area of Central Africa, about seventy-six times the size of Belgium, that King Leopold II ruled as his personal possession from 1885 to 1908. Leopold promised free trade and the improvement of the lives of African people, but in practice he and the companies he licensed ran the territory to make as much profit as possible. In the 1890s, world demand for rubber, used for bicycle and later car tyres and in industry, rose sharply, and the Congo had vast amounts of wild rubber in its forests. Leopold's administration and its private army, the Force Publique, forced villages to deliver quotas of rubber. People who failed to meet the quotas were beaten, imprisoned or killed; women were taken as hostages until the men brought rubber; villages were burned; and soldiers were known to cut off hands to prove they had used their bullets. Many people died from violence, starvation and disease, and the birth rate fell. Historians estimate that the population fell by millions during this period, although exact figures are debated because no reliable census exists. Missionaries, journalists and campaigners, including the British consul Roger Casement, the journalist Edmund Dene Morel and African American writer George Washington Williams, exposed these abuses. International outrage forced Leopold to hand the territory to the Belgian state in 1908. The Congo Free State is a stark example of the gap between the humanitarian language of the Berlin Conference and the reality of colonial rule.

### subtopic_4 — Reading the General Act and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How can I write a balanced paragraph on the Berlin Conference?

**Tutor:** Start with a clear topic sentence, support it with accurate evidence, include more than one perspective, and finish by linking back to the question. For example, if the question asks about the significance of the Berlin Conference, a topic sentence could be: the Berlin Conference of 1884 to 1885 was significant because it set the rules by which European powers claimed Africa, and it did so without any African participation. Then give evidence: it was called by Bismarck and attended by fourteen countries; it agreed free trade in the Congo basin, recognised Leopold II's Congo Free State and established the principle of effective occupation; no African ruler was invited. Next, show balance by mentioning a different interpretation: some historians argue that the scramble had already begun before 1884, with the British occupation of Egypt in 1882 and competing claims on the Congo, and that the conference only managed it; and the conference did not draw Africa's borders, which were set in later agreements. Then explain your judgement: nevertheless, by recognising European claims and encouraging effective occupation, it sped up the scramble, so that European control rose from about ten per cent of Africa in 1870 to about ninety per cent by 1914. End with a link: its exclusion of Africans made it a lasting symbol of colonial injustice. That structure, claim, evidence, other view, judgement and link, will earn high marks.
