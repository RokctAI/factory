# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the Berlin Conference of 1884 to 1885, a key event in the scramble for Africa, the rapid colonisation of almost the whole continent by European powers between about 1880 and 1914. The anchor is the conference itself, held in Berlin from November 1884 to February 1885. The governing idea is that the conference did not divide Africa on the map at a single stroke, as is often claimed, but set rules that European powers agreed to follow in claiming African territory, especially the principle of effective occupation, and that by recognising European claims without African participation it encouraged and sped up the scramble. Explaining the causes, decisions and significance of the conference is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Africa Before the Scramble

In 1870, most of Africa was ruled by Africans. European powers held only small areas, mostly on the coasts. Britain had the Cape Colony and Natal in the south, and trading posts and small colonies in West Africa, such as Sierra Leone, the Gambia and the Gold Coast forts. France had conquered Algeria from 1830 and held Senegal. Portugal had long-established footholds in Angola and Mozambique, mostly on the coast. Historians estimate that in 1870, only about one tenth of Africa was under European control.

The rest of the continent was made up of African states and societies of many kinds: powerful kingdoms and empires such as the Asante in West Africa, the Sokoto Caliphate in what is now northern Nigeria, Buganda in East Africa, Ethiopia in the Horn of Africa, and the Zulu and Basotho in the south; smaller chiefdoms and city-states; and societies without central rulers. African states traded with Europeans and with each other, made alliances, fought wars and adopted new technology, including firearms.

Europeans knew little about the interior of Africa in the early nineteenth century. Explorers such as Mungo Park, Heinrich Barth, David Livingstone and Henry Morton Stanley travelled along the great rivers, usually guided and supplied by Africans, and published accounts that made Europeans more interested in the continent's resources. Missionaries set up stations in many areas. Traders bought palm oil, ivory, gum, rubber and other products, especially after the transatlantic slave trade was abolished by Britain in 1807 and gradually suppressed.

From the 1870s, European interest grew rapidly. The industrial revolution had created a demand for raw materials and new markets, and new technology made it much easier for Europeans to travel, fight and survive in Africa. Rivalry among European powers grew, especially after the unification of Germany in 1871 and Italy in 1861 to 1870, which created new powers that wanted colonies. In 1882, Britain occupied Egypt to protect the Suez Canal, opened in 1869, which was the short route to India. In the same years, King Leopold II of Belgium, acting privately through organisations he controlled and employing Stanley, made treaties with rulers along the Congo River, and France, through the explorer Pierre Savorgnan de Brazza, made treaties on the northern bank of the Congo. Portugal, supported by Britain, claimed the mouth of the Congo. These competing claims threatened to lead to conflict between European powers.

## Subtopic: Who Met in Berlin and Why

The Berlin Conference was called by the German Chancellor, Otto von Bismarck, together with France, to settle disputes over the Congo and West Africa and to agree rules for future claims. Bismarck had not been very interested in colonies, but by 1884 Germany had begun to claim territory, in South West Africa, Togoland and Kamerun, and Bismarck wanted to show that Germany was a major power that could host and guide international decisions. He also hoped to limit British power and to keep the European powers from going to war with each other over Africa.

The conference opened on 15 November 1884 and closed on 26 February 1885. Fourteen countries were represented: Germany, Austria-Hungary, Belgium, Denmark, France, Great Britain, Italy, the Netherlands, Portugal, Russia, Spain, Sweden-Norway, the Ottoman Empire and the United States. Some had large interests in Africa, such as Britain, France, Portugal and Germany; others had few or none, but were invited as major powers or trading nations.

No African state or ruler was invited or consulted. The Sultan of Zanzibar, whose territory was affected, asked to be represented and was refused. This absence is the most important thing to understand about the conference. European diplomats discussed rules for claiming African land and trade as if Africa were empty, or as if African rulers had no rights that needed to be considered. The treaties that Europeans had made with African rulers were treated as transfers of sovereignty, even though many rulers understood them as agreements of friendship, trade or protection, and many were signed by people who could not read the language in which they were written.

The delegates spoke about high ideals. They declared that they wanted to bring civilisation, end the slave trade, which still existed in parts of East and Central Africa, and protect African peoples and missionaries. Historians point out that these statements served to justify European expansion, and that the main interests at stake were trade, territory and the balance of power among European states. Some delegates may have sincerely believed in the ideals, and the anti-slavery cause had real supporters, but the conference took no step to ask Africans what they wanted.

The conference also reflected the balance of power in Europe. Britain, with the largest navy and empire, wanted to protect its trade and its route to India. France wanted to rebuild its prestige after its defeat by Germany in 1870 and 1871. Portugal wanted recognition of its old claims. Bismarck, by acting as host and broker, placed Germany at the centre of European diplomacy. Africa was, in a sense, a board on which European rivalries were played out.

The questions for this section are with you now: who called the Berlin Conference, who attended, who was excluded and what interests were at stake.

## Subtopic: What the Conference Decided

The decisions of the conference were set out in a document called the General Act of the Berlin Conference, signed on 26 February 1885. Its main points were these.

Free trade in the Congo basin. All nations would be free to trade in the large area of the Congo River basin, and ships of all nations could navigate the Congo River freely. The Niger River was also declared open to free navigation, though Britain, which controlled much of the lower Niger, was given responsibility for it.

Recognition of the Congo Free State. In separate agreements made during the conference, the powers recognised the claims of King Leopold II's International Association of the Congo, which became the Congo Free State, a huge territory in Central Africa controlled personally by Leopold, not by the Belgian government. Leopold promised free trade and humane treatment. In practice, his agents used forced labour, hostage-taking, mutilation and killing to force people to collect wild rubber, and the population suffered terribly. After an international campaign exposing these abuses, Belgium took over the territory as the Belgian Congo in 1908.

The principle of effective occupation. A power that took possession of a new piece of the African coast, or established a protectorate there, had to notify the other powers, so that they could object, and had to establish enough authority to protect existing rights and freedom of trade. This was meant to stop powers from making claims on paper only. In practice, it encouraged powers to rush to establish posts, sign treaties and send soldiers and officials, so that their claims would be recognised.

Anti-slavery commitments. The powers declared that they would work to suppress the slave trade and to protect missionaries, scientists and explorers.

It is a common error to say that the conference drew the borders of Africa. It did not. Most borders were drawn in many separate agreements between European powers over the following thirty years, often with straight lines along lines of latitude and longitude or rivers, drawn by people who had never seen the land. But the conference created the rules and the atmosphere in which this happened, and it signalled that the European powers accepted each other's right to divide Africa among themselves.

Over the following years, a series of bilateral agreements fixed many borders. For example, Britain and Germany agreed in 1890 on boundaries in East Africa; in the same treaty Germany received the island of Heligoland in the North Sea, and Zanzibar became a British protectorate. Britain and France agreed on boundaries in West Africa in the 1890s and settled their rivalry on the Nile after a tense confrontation at Fashoda in Sudan in 1898. Each of these agreements divided African peoples without asking them.

## Subtopic: Reading the General Act and the Error Museum

The General Act is a primary source, written by European diplomats in 1885. Here is a summary of one article: the powers bind themselves to watch over the preservation of the native tribes, and to care for the improvement of the conditions of their moral and material well-being, and to help in suppressing slavery and especially the slave trade. When you read this, ask: who wrote it, when, for whom and why? It was written by representatives of European governments, with no African input, to set rules for European expansion and to present that expansion as humane. It is useful for showing how Europeans justified colonisation and what they claimed their aims were. Its limitation is that it shows European intentions as they wished them to be seen, not what actually happened, and not African views. Evidence from the Congo Free State shows that the promise to care for the well-being of African peoples was broken in the most brutal way.

A calculation shows the speed of the scramble. In 1870, about ten per cent of Africa was under European control. By 1914, about ninety per cent was, with only Ethiopia and Liberia independent. Ninety minus ten is eighty percentage points in forty-four years, because nineteen fourteen minus eighteen seventy is forty-four. Most of that change happened in the thirty years after the Berlin Conference. On average, that is nearly two percentage points of the whole continent every year, an area larger than many European countries.

Historians interpret the conference differently. Some see it as the moment when Europe formally began to carve up Africa. Others argue that the scramble had already started before 1884, driven by events in Egypt, the Congo and West Africa, and that the conference only managed the rivalry. Most agree that the conference sped up the scramble and that its exclusion of Africans symbolises the injustice of colonial rule.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the Berlin Conference drew all the borders of Africa. It set rules; borders were drawn later in many agreements. Two: saying African rulers attended. None did. Three: saying the Congo Free State belonged to the Belgian government in 1885. It was Leopold II's personal possession until 1908. Four: taking the General Act's humanitarian language at face value. Five: forgetting that the conference happened in 1884 to 1885, not in 1914 or 1910.

Layout for marks in a paragraph on the Berlin Conference: state when and why it was called, name its main decisions, explain the significance of the exclusion of Africans, and link it to the speed of the scramble.

## Subtopic: Before Berlin

In 1870, Africans ruled most of Africa. Europeans had only small pieces, mostly on the coasts: the British in the Cape and West Africa, the French in Algeria and Senegal, the Portuguese in Angola and Mozambique.

Then things changed. European factories wanted raw materials. New countries like Germany and Italy wanted colonies. Britain took over Egypt in 1882 to guard the Suez Canal. Leopold II of Belgium and France both wanted the Congo.

The Europeans were starting to argue. So they decided to meet.

## Subtopic: A Meeting Without Africans

From November 1884 to February 1885, fourteen countries met in Berlin. Bismarck, the German leader, hosted it. Their aim was to agree rules so they would not go to war over Africa.

Not one African was invited. They talked about Africa as if no one lived there.

They said they wanted to end slavery and bring civilisation. But their real interests were trade, land and power.

Picture the room: a big map of Africa on the wall, and no Africans at the table.

## Subtopic: The Rules of the Scramble

The conference agreed rules. Everyone could trade on the Congo River. Leopold II got the Congo Free State as his own, and his agents later forced people to collect rubber with terrible cruelty. And the most important rule: if you want to claim land, you must tell the others and really take control of it. That rule made the powers rush.

The conference did not draw all the borders. They were drawn later, often as straight lines on maps.

Pocket summary of the lesson. In 1870, most of Africa was ruled by Africans. European rivalry grew, and in 1884 to 1885 fourteen countries met in Berlin, hosted by Bismarck, with no Africans present. They agreed free trade on the Congo, recognised Leopold's Congo Free State and set the rule of effective occupation. This sped up the scramble, and by 1914 about ninety per cent of Africa was under European control, with only Ethiopia and Liberia independent.
