In November 1884, diplomats from fourteen countries gathered around a table in the official residence of the German Chancellor, Otto von Bismarck, in Berlin. On the wall hung a large map of Africa. For more than three months they discussed trade on the Congo and Niger rivers, and the rules by which European powers could claim African coastlines. Not a single African ruler or representative was invited. Today we study the Berlin Conference of 1884 to 1885: why it was called, who attended, what it decided, and why it became a symbol of the scramble for Africa. We also practise reading the conference's decisions as a historical source.
