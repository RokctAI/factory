# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the results of the colonisation of Africa. The anchor is the colonial map and economy of about 1914 to 1960, and the legacy that independent African states inherited. The governing idea is that colonisation transformed Africa politically, by imposing borders and foreign rule; economically, by organising African economies to export raw materials to Europe; and socially, through land loss, forced labour, new religions, education and languages, and that while some new infrastructure, schools and medical services were introduced, they were built mainly to serve colonial interests and came at a great human cost. Explaining and evaluating the results of colonisation is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Political Results

Colonisation took away the independence of almost every African state and society. African kings, chiefs and councils lost the power to make laws, raise taxes, conduct foreign relations and command armies. Where they survived, under indirect rule, they did so as agents of colonial government, answerable to European officials. Many rulers who resisted were killed, exiled or replaced. Colonial governments were run by European officials, who were not elected by Africans and did not answer to them.

Colonisation created new borders. The borders of colonies were drawn mainly by European agreements, often along lines of latitude and longitude or rivers, with little regard for the peoples who lived there. Some borders divided peoples who shared a language, culture and history, such as the Somali, divided among British, French and Italian territories and Ethiopia; the Ewe, divided between British and German Togoland and later between Ghana and Togo; and the Maasai, divided between Kenya and Tanganyika. Other borders grouped together peoples who had never been part of one state, sometimes with histories of rivalry. Nigeria, for example, was formed in 1914 by joining the British protectorates of Northern and Southern Nigeria, bringing together the Hausa-Fulani emirates of the north, the Yoruba of the south-west, the Igbo of the south-east and hundreds of other groups.

When African countries became independent, mostly between 1956 and 1975, they kept the colonial borders. In 1964, the Organisation of African Unity agreed that existing borders should be respected, because redrawing them could lead to endless wars. Some conflicts after independence, such as civil wars and border disputes, have been linked by historians to the way colonial borders were drawn, although they also had other causes.

Colonial rule also affected politics in other ways. Colonial governments often favoured certain groups, for example by recruiting soldiers or officials mainly from one group, or by giving one group more education, which created tensions that continued after independence. In Rwanda and Burundi, Belgian rulers introduced identity cards classifying people as Hutu or Tutsi, hardening divisions that were later exploited with terrible consequences.

At the same time, colonial rule produced resistance and nationalism. Africans educated in mission schools and abroad, soldiers who served in the world wars, workers in mines and cities, and farmers angered by taxes and land loss formed political organisations that demanded rights and, later, independence. Ghana, under Kwame Nkrumah, became the first sub-Saharan African colony to win independence from colonial rule, on 6 March 1957, and in 1960 alone seventeen African countries became independent.

The questions for this section are with you now: loss of independence, colonial borders, divisions and the rise of nationalism.

## Subtopic: Economic Results

Colonial economies were organised mainly to serve the colonising countries. Colonies produced raw materials for European industry and bought manufactured goods from Europe. This is sometimes called an export economy or extractive economy.

Many colonies came to depend on one or two export products, a situation called monoculture or dependence on primary products. The Gold Coast became the world's largest producer of cocoa by about 1911; Senegal and the Gambia depended on groundnuts; Uganda and Sudan on cotton; Northern Rhodesia and the Belgian Congo on copper; and South Africa on gold. When world prices for these products fell, as in the Great Depression of the 1930s, colonial economies suffered badly. Many independent African countries inherited this dependence and still rely heavily on exporting a few raw materials.

Land was taken, especially in settler colonies. In Kenya, the fertile central highlands became known as the White Highlands, reserved for European farmers. In Southern Rhodesia, the Land Apportionment Act of 1930 divided land by race. In Algeria and South Africa, settlers took most of the best farmland. Africans were pushed into reserves, became labour tenants or squatters on European farms, or migrated to work.

Labour was forced or pushed. Colonial governments imposed hut and poll taxes payable in cash, which forced Africans to work for wages or grow cash crops. Many colonies used forced labour to build roads, railways and public works. In the Congo Free State, forced rubber collection led to atrocities. Migrant labour systems, like those you studied in South Africa, spread across southern and central Africa.

Infrastructure was built, but mainly for extraction. Colonial governments and companies built railways, roads, ports and telegraph lines. These were usually designed to carry raw materials from mines and plantations to the coast for export, not to connect African regions with each other. That is why many African railway systems still run inland from ports, with few links between neighbouring countries.

Industry was limited. Colonial powers generally discouraged manufacturing in the colonies, which would compete with European industry, so most colonies had very little industry at independence, apart from South Africa and a few others.

Europe benefited from cheap raw materials, markets, profits for companies and investors, and jobs in colonial administration. Historians debate how much colonies contributed to European wealth overall, but some companies, investors and industries clearly gained a great deal.

Not all Africans were simply victims of the colonial economy. In the Gold Coast, cocoa was grown mainly by African farmers on their own land, and some became wealthy, investing in houses, schools and transport. In Nigeria, African traders and farmers expanded the palm oil and groundnut trades. African entrepreneurs ran transport businesses, shops and markets. But they worked within a system in which prices, trade rules and the most profitable businesses were controlled by European companies and governments, and in which Africans had no political power to change the rules.

The questions for this section are with you now: export economies, monoculture, land loss, forced labour, infrastructure and benefits to Europe.

## Subtopic: Social and Cultural Results

Colonisation also changed African societies and cultures in deep ways.

Religion. Christianity spread rapidly in many regions through missions, and by the end of the colonial period, large parts of Africa south of the Sahara had become mainly Christian. Islam also spread, especially in West and East Africa, sometimes helped by colonial roads and trade. Many Africans adapted Christianity to their own cultures, founding independent African churches. At the same time, missionaries and colonial officials often condemned African religious beliefs and customs, and some practices were banned.

Education. Most schools in colonial Africa were run by missions. They taught reading, writing, arithmetic, European languages and Christianity, and trained teachers, clerks, nurses and ministers. Education gave some Africans new skills and opportunities, and many educated Africans became leaders of nationalist movements. But schooling was limited: most children did not go to school, secondary and university education was very scarce, and the curriculum often taught European history and values rather than African ones. At independence, many countries had very few university graduates.

Languages. European languages became the languages of government, law, education and business. Today, English, French and Portuguese are official languages in many African countries, alongside African languages. This allows communication across ethnic groups and with the world, but it has also meant that African languages were often given lower status.

Health. Colonial medical services introduced vaccination and some hospitals, and campaigns against diseases such as sleeping sickness. But colonial rule also spread disease, through the movement of soldiers and labourers, crowded mines and compounds, and disrupted farming, and health services were concentrated in towns and among Europeans.

Racism and inequality. Colonial societies were built on racial hierarchy. Europeans held the highest positions and enjoyed better housing, wages and services, while Africans faced discrimination, segregation and restrictions on movement, land and political rights. The racist ideas used to justify colonisation shaped attitudes for generations.

Urbanisation and social change. New colonial towns, ports, mining centres and administrative capitals grew, and people moved to them for work, changing family life and traditional authority. Migrant labour separated families.

Historians continue to debate whether colonisation brought any benefits. Some point to railways, schools, hospitals and new crops. Others answer that these were built mainly to serve colonial interests, that Africans paid for much of them through taxes and labour, that Africans were already developing and adopting new technology before colonisation, and that the costs in lives, land, independence and dignity were enormous. A balanced answer explains both arguments and reaches a reasoned judgement.

Colonisation also changed the position of women. In many societies, women had held important roles in farming, trade and politics, such as queen mothers in Asante. Colonial officials often dealt only with men, recognised men as landowners and household heads, and offered education and paid work mainly to men, which reduced women's status and rights in some areas. At the same time, some women found new opportunities in trade, towns, churches and schools, and women played a leading part in many protests against colonial rule.

The questions for this section are with you now: religion, education, language, health, racism and the debate about the benefits of colonisation.

## Subtopic: Weighing the Results and the Error Museum

A calculation shows the scale of decolonisation. In 1950, only four African countries were generally counted as independent: Egypt, Ethiopia, Liberia and South Africa, which was ruled by its white minority. Ghana became independent in 1957, and seventeen countries became independent in 1960 alone. Today, Africa has fifty-four countries that are members of the United Nations. Fifty-four minus four is fifty. So about fifty African countries gained independence after 1950, most of them between 1956 and 1975, a period of only about twenty years.

Historians use a method called weighing to judge results. List the results under headings, political, economic and social, and for each, ask: who gained, who lost, how much, and for how long? For example, railways in the Gold Coast carried cocoa and gold to the coast, which earned money for British companies and the colonial government, and for some African cocoa farmers, but they did not connect Ghanaian regions well with each other, and they were built partly with taxes and forced labour.

Sources reflect different views. A colonial governor's report may stress roads built and schools opened. A nationalist speech, such as Kwame Nkrumah's at independence, may stress exploitation and the loss of freedom. A peasant's oral account may describe forced labour and taxes. Each is useful, and each must be read for its purpose.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying colonisation only brought benefits, or only brought harm, without evidence. Weigh both. Two: saying Africa had no development before colonisation. African states had governments, trade, technology, art and learning. Three: saying the Berlin Conference created all the problems of modern Africa. Many results came from decades of colonial rule and from choices after independence. Four: saying railways were built to help Africans travel. They were built mainly for exports. Five: forgetting the results for Europe, such as raw materials, markets and profits.

Layout for marks in an essay on the results of colonisation: introduction with a clear argument, paragraphs on political, economic and social results with evidence and examples, a paragraph weighing benefits against costs, and a conclusion that answers the question.

The questions for this section are with you now: the decolonisation calculation, weighing results, sources and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

## Subtopic: Rulers Without Power

After colonisation, African kings and chiefs lost their power. European officials ruled, and Africans could not vote for them.

Europeans drew borders, often as straight lines on maps. Some borders split peoples apart, like the Somali. Others pushed many different peoples into one colony, like Nigeria.

After independence, African countries kept these borders to avoid wars over them. Ghana was the first sub-Saharan colony to become free, in 1957.

Picture a ruler drawing straight lines across a map of people.

Some African rulers stayed in place, but they now had to obey European officials.

## Subtopic: Raw Materials Out, Goods In

Colonies were made to send raw materials to Europe: cocoa from Ghana, copper from Zambia and the Congo, cotton from Uganda, gold from South Africa. They bought factory goods from Europe.

Many colonies depended on one or two crops or minerals. When prices fell, people suffered.

Settlers took the best land, like the White Highlands in Kenya. Taxes forced people to work for Europeans.

Railways ran from mines and farms to the ports, to carry raw materials out.

Keep that picture of goods going out in mind and try the questions for this part: economic results.

## Subtopic: Schools, Churches and Languages

Missionaries built schools and churches, and many Africans became Christian. Some Africans who went to school later led the fight for independence. But most children never went to school.

English, French and Portuguese became the languages of government. Colonial societies were racist: Europeans got the best jobs and services.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Colonisation took away African independence, imposed foreign rule and drew borders that divided some peoples and combined others. Colonial economies exported raw materials and depended on a few products; land was taken, taxes and forced labour pushed people into work, and railways were built for exports. Socially, Christianity, mission education and European languages spread, alongside racism and inequality. Some infrastructure, schools and health care were introduced, but mainly for colonial interests and at great cost. Colonial rule also produced the nationalist movements that won independence.

Keep the borders, the railways and the schools in mind, and take the final questions of the lesson: results of colonisation.
