### subtopic_1 — From the Coast to the Interior
**Assistant:** If Africans had guns too, why did the Maxim gun make such a difference?

**Tutor:** Because the Maxim gun was a completely different kind of weapon from the guns most African armies had, and because of how battles were fought. Many African armies by the 1880s did have firearms, often muzzle-loading muskets that had to be loaded through the barrel after each shot, which was slow and had a short range. Some, like Samori Touré's army and Ethiopia's, had acquired modern breech-loading rifles, which were much better. But the Maxim gun, invented by Hiram Maxim in 1884, was the first fully automatic machine gun. It used the energy of each shot to load and fire the next, so it could fire about five hundred to six hundred rounds a minute, as long as the trigger was held and ammunition lasted, and it was cooled with water so it could keep firing. A few machine guns, operated by small crews, could produce the firepower of hundreds of riflemen. When large armies attacked across open ground, as at Omdurman, they were cut down before they could reach the enemy. African commanders learned quickly and adapted, for example by avoiding open battles and using guerrilla tactics, ambushes, forests and mountains, which made some resistance last for years. Europeans also tried to block the sale of modern weapons and machine guns to Africans. So the Maxim gun did not win every war, but it gave European-led forces a decisive advantage in open battle, which is why it became a symbol of the scramble.

### subtopic_2 — Why Colonisation Was So Fast
**Assistant:** Why did some African rulers help the Europeans?

**Tutor:** Because African rulers made decisions based on their own interests and circumstances, just as European leaders did, and in the moment, allying with a European power often seemed the best way to protect their people or defeat a rival. Africa was not one country; it was made up of many states with their own histories of rivalry and conflict. A ruler threatened by a powerful neighbour might see a European power as a useful ally, and might expect to benefit from the neighbour's defeat. Some rulers hoped that cooperation would allow them to keep their position and some independence, and under indirect rule, some did keep power, wealth and influence. Others were pressured or tricked into treaties. Some groups that had been conquered or dominated by a powerful African state welcomed its defeat. For example, the Swazi fought alongside the British against the Pedi in 1879, and some groups in Sudan and West Africa allied with Europeans against powerful states. Historians caution against judging these choices only with hindsight: rulers did not know that European powers would take over the whole continent, and many alliances that seemed sensible at the time later led to the loss of independence for everyone. Understanding these alliances helps to explain the speed of colonisation without suggesting that Africans were simply passive victims or that they welcomed colonial rule; it shows that they were active participants making difficult choices.

### subtopic_3 — African Resistance
**Assistant:** Why is Adwa so important to people across Africa?

**Tutor:** Because Adwa showed, at the height of the scramble, that a European army could be defeated by an African state, and that colonisation was not inevitable. On 1 March 1896, the army of Emperor Menelik II and Empress Taytu defeated an Italian invasion force so decisively that Italy had to sign a peace treaty recognising Ethiopia's full independence. At a time when European writers and politicians claimed that Africans were inferior and destined to be ruled, Adwa proved the opposite. It became a source of pride for Ethiopians and for Africans and people of African descent everywhere. In the twentieth century, Ethiopia's independence, its ancient Christian kingdom and its victory at Adwa inspired Pan-African movements. Many independent churches in South Africa and elsewhere took the name Ethiopian, as a symbol of African self-reliance, and the colours of the Ethiopian flag, green, yellow and red, were adopted by many African countries after independence. Ethiopia was later invaded and occupied by fascist Italy from 1936 to 1941, which shows that its independence was always under threat, but Adwa remained a powerful symbol. When Ethiopian Emperor Haile Selassie helped found the Organisation of African Unity in Addis Ababa in 1963, the choice of the city reflected Ethiopia's special place in African history. So Adwa is remembered not only as a battle, but as a symbol of African resistance and dignity.

### subtopic_4 — Weighing Reasons and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do historians know about African resistance if most sources were written by Europeans?

**Tutor:** Historians use a wide range of sources and read European sources critically, and over the last sixty years African historians in particular have greatly expanded our knowledge. Colonial records, military reports, officials' diaries and newspapers describe resistance, though usually from the colonisers' point of view, often calling resisters rebels, bandits or fanatics. Historians read these sources against the grain, looking for evidence of African actions, motives and organisation that the authors did not intend to reveal. African sources are also rich. Some African states had written records: Ethiopia had royal chronicles in Ge'ez and Amharic, and West African states had Arabic-language documents, letters and chronicles; Samori Touré and other rulers wrote letters to European officials. Oral traditions, histories passed down by word of mouth, often by specialist historians such as the griots of West Africa, preserve memories of wars, leaders and treaties. Praise poems, songs and proverbs contain historical information. Archaeology and material culture, such as weapons, forts and battle sites, add more evidence. In the twentieth century, historians recorded interviews with elderly people who remembered events such as the Maji Maji rebellion. By combining all of these, historians can reconstruct resistance from African perspectives. When you use sources in an exam, it is a strong point to note whose voice is present and whose is missing, and to explain how other sources might fill the gap.
