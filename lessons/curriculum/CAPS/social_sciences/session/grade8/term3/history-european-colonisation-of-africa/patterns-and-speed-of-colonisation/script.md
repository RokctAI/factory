# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the patterns of colonisation in Africa and the reasons for its speed. The anchor is the contrast between Omdurman in 1898, where machine guns destroyed a large African army, and Adwa in 1896, where an African state defeated a European invasion. The governing idea is that European powers colonised Africa quickly because of a combination of technological advantages, medical advances, the use of African allies and divisions, treaties and chartered companies, and the timing of conquest, but that Africans resisted everywhere, and where an African state could match European weapons and organisation, as Ethiopia did, conquest could fail. Explaining the patterns and speed of colonisation, and African resistance, is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: From the Coast to the Interior

Colonisation followed recognisable patterns. In most regions, it began on the coast, where Europeans already had trading posts, forts and harbours, and then moved inland along rivers, trade routes and later railways.

In West Africa, the British and French pushed inland from coastal bases in Senegal, the Gambia, Sierra Leone, the Gold Coast and Lagos, following the Niger, Senegal and other rivers. In Central Africa, Leopold II's agents and the French followed the Congo River and its tributaries. In East Africa, the British and Germans moved inland from the coast of the Indian Ocean towards the great lakes, along caravan routes used for centuries by African and Arab traders, and later along railways such as the Uganda Railway from Mombasa, completed to Lake Victoria in 1901. In southern Africa, as you have studied, expansion moved north from the Cape and Natal, and in North Africa, France expanded from Algeria into Tunisia and Morocco, and Britain from Egypt into Sudan.

There were different types of colonies. Settler colonies, such as Algeria, South Africa, Southern Rhodesia and Kenya, attracted large numbers of European settlers, who took the best land for farming and mining and dominated the colonial government. In non-settler colonies, such as Nigeria and the Gold Coast, few Europeans settled permanently; colonial officials, soldiers, missionaries and traders ruled and traded, while Africans continued to farm their own land and grow crops for export. Some territories were protectorates, in which the colonial power claimed to protect an existing ruler, who kept some authority, while in fact the colonial power controlled foreign policy, taxes and much more.

Colonial powers also used different methods of rule. The British often used indirect rule, governing through existing African rulers and chiefs, who collected taxes and kept order under the supervision of British officials. This was cheaper and used existing structures, as in northern Nigeria under the emirs of the Sokoto Caliphate. The French more often used direct rule, appointing French officials and African officials loyal to France, and pursuing a policy of assimilation, which aimed in theory to make Africans culturally French, although in practice very few Africans were given French citizenship.

Patterns of colonisation also depended on African political geography. Strong, centralised kingdoms sometimes resisted for years, while smaller, divided societies could be taken more quickly, but were also harder to control, because there was no single ruler to defeat or negotiate with.

Colonial rule was also built on taxation. Colonial governments introduced hut taxes and poll taxes, payable in cash, which forced Africans to grow cash crops or work for wages on European farms, mines and railways in order to pay. Taxes paid for the costs of colonial administration, so that colonies would not be a burden on taxpayers in Europe. Forced labour was also widely used to build roads and railways, and in some colonies, such as the Congo Free State and parts of French Equatorial Africa, it reached brutal levels.

## Subtopic: Why Colonisation Was So Fast

Between about 1880 and 1914, European powers took control of almost the whole continent. Several factors explain this speed.

Weapons. The most important factor was the gap in military technology. European armies had breech-loading rifles, which fired faster and further than older muskets, and from the 1880s the Maxim gun, the first fully automatic machine gun, which could fire hundreds of rounds a minute. They also had artillery and, on rivers and lakes, armed steamboats. Many African armies had older guns, or spears and shields, and European powers tried to stop modern weapons from being sold to Africans. A small European-led force with machine guns could defeat a much larger African army, as at Omdurman in 1898.

Medicine and transport. Quinine reduced deaths from malaria among Europeans. Steamships, railways and the telegraph allowed troops and supplies to be moved quickly and allowed officials to communicate with their governments.

African soldiers. Most of the soldiers in colonial armies were Africans, recruited, sometimes by force, from other regions, such as the Senegalese riflemen in the French army, the West African Frontier Force in British West Africa, and the askari in German East Africa. European powers also formed alliances with African rulers against their rivals.

African divisions. Africa was not one nation. Rivalries between African states, or within them, allowed Europeans to divide and rule, making alliances with one group against another. Some rulers allied with Europeans to defeat their own rivals, not realising that the Europeans would later turn on them.

Treaties and chartered companies. Treaties, often misunderstood or obtained by pressure, gave Europeans a claim to land that other European powers recognised. Chartered companies, such as the Royal Niger Company and the British South Africa Company, raised private money and armies to conquer and govern territories, reducing the cost to governments.

Timing and disease. In some regions, African societies were weakened at the time of conquest by drought, famine and disease. In the 1890s, rinderpest, a cattle disease, swept through eastern and southern Africa, killing a huge proportion of the cattle in many regions, which destroyed the wealth and food supply of cattle-keeping peoples and made resistance much harder.

European unity at Berlin. The European powers agreed rules among themselves at Berlin, so they rarely fought each other in Africa during the scramble, while African states did not have a similar agreement.

The questions for this section are with you now: weapons, medicine, African soldiers, divisions, treaties, companies and disease as reasons for the speed of colonisation.

## Subtopic: African Resistance

Africans resisted colonisation everywhere. Resistance took many forms: war, diplomacy, rebellion, refusal to pay taxes or work, migration away from colonial control, and religious movements.

Military resistance. Some African states fought long wars. Samori Touré built a powerful state in West Africa, with an army equipped with modern rifles, some made in his own workshops, and resisted the French from the early 1880s until he was captured in 1898. The Asante of the Gold Coast fought the British several times, which you will study in the case study. In Sudan, the Mahdist state defeated an Egyptian army and the British general Charles Gordon at Khartoum in 1885, and held power until Omdurman in 1898. In southern Africa, the Zulu defeated the British at Isandlwana in 1879, the Pedi resisted until 1879, and the Ndebele and Shona rose against the British South Africa Company in 1896 and 1897.

Ethiopia's victory. The most successful resistance was in Ethiopia. Emperor Menelik II unified the country, built up a large army and bought modern rifles and artillery, partly from European suppliers, including Italy and France. When Italy invaded, claiming that a treaty made Ethiopia an Italian protectorate, Ethiopian forces defeated the Italian army at Adwa on 1 March 1896. Italy was forced to recognise Ethiopian independence. Adwa became a symbol of African pride and resistance across the continent and the world.

Rebellions after conquest. Many rebellions broke out after colonial rule was established, often against forced labour, taxes and land seizures. In German South West Africa, the Herero rose in 1904 and the Nama soon after. The German commander issued an extermination order, and German forces drove Herero people into the Omaheke desert, where many died of thirst and starvation, and held survivors in concentration camps. A large majority of the Herero people and around half of the Nama people died. Historians and, since 2021, the German government recognise these events as genocide. In German East Africa, the Maji Maji rebellion of 1905 to 1907 united many peoples against forced cotton growing. Fighters believed that sacred water, maji, would protect them from bullets. The rebellion was crushed, and the Germans used famine as a weapon; many tens of thousands, and possibly far more, died. In Natal, as you studied in Term 2, the Bambatha Rebellion of 1906 was crushed.

Other forms of resistance included refusing to pay taxes, working slowly, deserting, moving away from areas of colonial control, and forming independent churches and new religious movements that challenged colonial authority.

Women also played important roles in resistance. In the Gold Coast, the Asante queen mother Yaa Asantewaa led a war against the British in 1900, which you will study in the case study. In Zimbabwe, the spirit medium Nehanda Charwe Nyakasikana was a leader of the rising of 1896 to 1897 and was executed by the British; she became a symbol of later struggles for independence.

The questions for this section are with you now: military resistance, Ethiopia's victory at Adwa and rebellions after conquest.

## Subtopic: Weighing Reasons and the Error Museum

Historians weigh the reasons for the speed of colonisation differently. Some stress European technology, especially the Maxim gun. The British writer Hilaire Belloc wrote a famous verse in 1898 that expresses the arrogance of the time: whatever happens, we have got the Maxim gun, and they have not. Other historians stress African factors: divisions, alliances, the role of African soldiers, and the effects of drought and disease. Others stress European diplomacy, especially the agreements that kept European powers from fighting each other. Most historians agree that no single factor explains the speed; it was the combination.

A calculation shows the power of technology at Omdurman. Reported figures vary, but suppose, using commonly cited figures, about ten thousand Sudanese soldiers were killed and about fifty soldiers on the British-led side died. Ten thousand divided by fifty is two hundred. So for every soldier lost on the British-led side, about two hundred Sudanese soldiers were killed. The casualty figures show the enormous effect of machine guns and artillery against soldiers charging across open ground.

Compare Adwa. Ethiopian forces, much larger than the Italian army and armed with modern rifles and artillery, inflicted a decisive defeat on the Italians, killing thousands of Italian and allied Eritrean soldiers. The difference was that Ethiopia had closed much of the technology gap, had a unified command and knew the ground.

Sources show different viewpoints. A British officer's account of Omdurman might describe it as a glorious victory; a Sudanese oral account might remember it as a massacre. Belloc's verse shows an attitude, not a fact. Ethiopian chronicles celebrate Adwa as a national triumph. Each must be read for its author, audience and purpose.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying Africans did not resist. They resisted everywhere. Two: explaining the speed of colonisation only by European technology. Divisions, African soldiers, disease and diplomacy mattered too. Three: saying all of Africa was conquered. Ethiopia defeated Italy and remained independent. Four: confusing indirect rule, through African rulers, with direct rule. Five: forgetting that most colonial soldiers were Africans.

Layout for marks in an essay: introduce the speed of the scramble, explain the main reasons in order of importance, discuss African resistance with examples, explain Ethiopia's success as an exception, and conclude.

The questions for this section are with you now: weighing the reasons for the speed of colonisation, the Omdurman calculation and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Coast First, Then Inland

Europeans usually started on the coast, where they already had forts and harbours, and then moved inland along rivers, old trade routes and new railways.

Some colonies had many European settlers who took the best land, like Kenya and Algeria. Others had few settlers, and Africans kept farming their own land, like Nigeria and Ghana.

The British often ruled through African chiefs. That is indirect rule. The French often used their own officials. That is direct rule.

## Subtopic: Why So Fast?

Why could Europe take Africa so quickly? Machine guns and modern rifles. Quinine against malaria. Steamships, railways and the telegraph. Armies made up mostly of African soldiers. Rivalries between African groups that Europeans used. And a cattle disease, rinderpest, that killed huge numbers of cattle and made people poor and hungry.

At Omdurman in 1898, machine guns killed about ten thousand Sudanese soldiers in a few hours.

## Subtopic: Africans Fought Back

Africans resisted everywhere. Samori Touré fought the French for years. The Zulu won at Isandlwana. The Herero and Nama rose against Germany and were killed in a genocide. In German East Africa, the Maji Maji rebellion fought forced cotton growing.

And Ethiopia won. At Adwa in 1896, Emperor Menelik II's army defeated Italy. Ethiopia stayed free.

Colonisation was fast because of machine guns and rifles, quinine, transport, African soldiers, divide and rule, treaties, chartered companies and rinderpest. Africans resisted everywhere, and Ethiopia defeated Italy at Adwa in 1896 and stayed independent.
