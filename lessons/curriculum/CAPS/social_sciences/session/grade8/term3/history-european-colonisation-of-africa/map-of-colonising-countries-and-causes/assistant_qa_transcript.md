### subtopic_1 — The Map of Africa in 1914
**Assistant:** Why did France end up with so much land but Britain with more people?

**Tutor:** Because the two powers focused on different kinds of territory, for different reasons. France's empire in Africa was enormous in area largely because it included most of the Sahara Desert and the dry Sahel region south of it. French military officers in West Africa pushed inland from Senegal along the Niger River and across the desert, partly to link their territories in North Africa, West Africa and Equatorial Africa into one connected block, and partly for prestige. Much of this land was very thinly populated and had limited economic value at the time. Britain, on the other hand, concentrated on territories that were economically valuable, densely populated or strategically important. Nigeria, for example, was one of the most populous regions of Africa, with productive farming and trade in palm oil and later cocoa and groundnuts. Egypt, with its huge population along the Nile, was taken for strategic reasons connected to the Suez Canal. South Africa had gold, diamonds and a growing settler population. The Gold Coast had cocoa and gold. Britain also had the strongest navy and long-established trading posts on the coasts, which gave it access to the richest coastal regions. So when you compare empires, it is important to ask what measure you are using: area, population, economic value or strategic importance. A map coloured by area can give a misleading impression of which empire was richest or most important.

### subtopic_2 — Economic Causes
**Assistant:** Did missionaries help or harm Africans?

**Tutor:** Historians give a mixed answer, and a good essay explains both sides with evidence. On the one hand, missionaries built many of the first schools, hospitals and clinics in Africa, translated the Bible into African languages and developed written forms of many languages, and trained teachers, nurses and clergy. Many African leaders of the twentieth century, including many founders of the South African Native National Congress, such as John Dube, were educated at mission schools. Some missionaries also criticised colonial abuses and defended African land rights; for example, missionaries helped expose the atrocities in the Congo Free State. Many Africans chose to become Christians and built African churches, some of which became independent of European control. On the other hand, many missionaries believed that African cultures were inferior and tried to replace African customs, beliefs and practices, sometimes with great disrespect. Missionaries often called for colonial rule to protect their work, and their reports helped create the image of Africa as a place that needed to be civilised, which was used to justify conquest. Some mission stations became centres of colonial influence, and some missionaries acted as translators and negotiators in treaties that took away African land. So missionaries were part of the colonial process, even when their intentions were good. The most balanced answer recognises the benefits of education and health care that many Africans valued, and the harm caused by cultural disrespect and support for colonial rule.

### subtopic_3 — Political, Strategic and Social Causes
**Assistant:** Why were racist ideas so widely believed in Europe at the time?

**Tutor:** Several factors combined to make racist ideas seem acceptable to many Europeans in the nineteenth century, even though we know today that they were false. First, Europe's industrial and military power had grown enormously, and many Europeans wrongly concluded that their technological advantage proved that they were naturally superior, rather than recognising that it was the result of specific historical circumstances. Second, so-called scientists of the time developed theories that divided humanity into races and ranked them, using measurements of skulls and other pseudo-scientific methods. Some applied Charles Darwin's ideas about evolution and natural selection to human societies, in a distorted way called social Darwinism, claiming that stronger races had a natural right to dominate weaker ones. These theories have been completely rejected by modern science, which shows that human beings are one species with no biological basis for ranking races. Third, the history of the transatlantic slave trade had already created and spread racist stereotypes of Africans to justify enslavement. Fourth, most Europeans had little or no direct knowledge of Africa; they learned about it from explorers' books, newspapers, missionary reports, exhibitions and advertisements, which often presented Africans as primitive. These images ignored African kingdoms, trade networks, art, literature and complex systems of government. Racist ideas were useful to those who wanted to colonise, because they made conquest seem like a duty rather than a crime. Understanding how such ideas spread helps us recognise and challenge prejudice today.

### subtopic_4 — Explaining Causes and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do I decide which cause was most important in an essay?

**Tutor:** First, show that you understand all the main causes, then make a clear argument about their relative importance, supported by evidence. You might argue that economic causes were most important, because industrialisation created the demand for raw materials and markets, chartered companies led the way in many areas, and the richest territories, such as South Africa's mines, were fiercely fought over. Or you might argue that political rivalry was most important, because many territories were taken not because they were profitable, but because powers feared that rivals would take them, as shown by the speed of the scramble after 1884 and by the huge areas of desert claimed by France. Or you might argue that strategic causes drove key events, such as the British occupation of Egypt in 1882 to protect the Suez Canal, which helped trigger the scramble. Whatever you choose, explain why that cause was more important than the others, and show how the causes were connected: for example, economic competition fed political rivalry, and racist ideology made conquest seem justified. You can also distinguish between causes and enabling factors: technology did not cause colonisation, but it made it possible. A strong conclusion might say that the scramble resulted from economic pressures of industrialisation and political rivalry between European powers, justified by racist ideology and made possible by new technology, with economic and political causes the most important. Examiners reward a clear, consistent argument more than a list.
