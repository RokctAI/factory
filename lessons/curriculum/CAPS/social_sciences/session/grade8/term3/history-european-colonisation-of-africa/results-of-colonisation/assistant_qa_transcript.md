### subtopic_1 — Political Results
**Assistant:** Why didn't African countries change the colonial borders after independence?

**Tutor:** Because African leaders feared that trying to redraw borders would lead to endless conflict. By the time most countries became independent in the late 1950s and 1960s, the colonial borders had existed for more than half a century. Governments, armies, economies, railways, schools and political parties had all developed within them. Almost every border cut across some ethnic or language group, so if one border were changed to unite a divided people, many others could be challenged too, and almost every country would face claims from its neighbours or demands for separation from within. Leaders such as Julius Nyerere of Tanzania argued that the borders, though unjust in origin, should be accepted to preserve peace and allow the new states to focus on development. In 1964, at a meeting in Cairo, the Organisation of African Unity resolved that member states would respect the borders existing at independence. There were also Pan-Africanists, such as Kwame Nkrumah, who hoped that African unity, perhaps a united states of Africa, would eventually make the borders less important. Some conflicts did arise over borders and over groups divided by them, for example the Somali claims on Ethiopian and Kenyan territory, and civil wars such as the Nigerian Civil War from 1967 to 1970, which had several causes, including tensions within borders set by colonial rule. But the decision to keep the borders means that the map of Africa today is still, in large part, the map drawn by colonial powers.

### subtopic_2 — Economic Results
**Assistant:** If colonial governments built railways and schools, wasn't colonisation partly good for Africa?

**Tutor:** This is one of the most debated questions in African history, and the best answers examine the evidence carefully rather than giving a simple yes or no. It is true that colonial governments and missions built railways, roads, ports, schools and hospitals, introduced new crops and new medical treatments, and that some Africans gained education and skills that they used, among other things, to lead their countries to independence. But historians ask several further questions. First, who were these things built for? Railways were designed mainly to carry raw materials to the coast for export, not to serve African needs, and schools were few and often aimed at producing clerks and workers for the colonial system. Second, who paid? Much infrastructure was paid for by taxes on Africans and built with forced or poorly paid African labour, often in dangerous conditions. Third, what was lost? Colonisation cost millions of lives in wars of conquest, rebellions, forced labour and famines, took land, destroyed independent states and imposed racist rule. Fourth, what might have happened otherwise? Before colonisation, African states such as Ethiopia, Asante and Egypt were already adopting new technology, building armies and trading with the world, so it is not true that railways and schools could only have come through colonisation. Many historians conclude that some developments occurred during colonisation, but that they served colonial interests and came at a very high cost. Whatever you conclude, support it with evidence and acknowledge the other side.

### subtopic_3 — Social and Cultural Results
**Assistant:** Why do many African economies still depend on exporting raw materials?

**Tutor:** Because the economic structures created during the colonial period have been very difficult to change, although many countries have made progress. Under colonial rule, African economies were organised to produce raw materials, such as cocoa, coffee, cotton, groundnuts, copper and gold, for European industry. Transport networks ran from mines and plantations to ports. Manufacturing was discouraged, so few factories, skilled industrial workers or local businesses developed. Profits often flowed to companies based in Europe. At independence, new governments inherited economies that depended on a few exports, with little industry, few trained managers and engineers, and transport links that faced the sea rather than neighbouring countries. Changing this required huge investment, skills and time. Several other factors made it harder after independence: world prices for raw materials often fell or changed wildly, making planning difficult; many countries borrowed money and fell into debt; trade rules sometimes made it hard to sell processed goods to richer countries; and conflicts and poor governance in some countries damaged economies. Today, many African countries are working to add value to their raw materials, for example by processing cocoa into chocolate or refining minerals locally, to build regional trade through the African Continental Free Trade Area, and to grow services and technology sectors. So present-day dependence on raw materials is partly a legacy of colonialism and partly the result of later choices and global conditions, which is the kind of balanced explanation examiners look for.

### subtopic_4 — Weighing the Results and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How did colonisation lead to its own end?

**Tutor:** In several ways, colonial rule created the people, ideas and organisations that eventually brought it to an end. Mission schools and colonial education produced a class of educated Africans, teachers, clerks, lawyers, journalists and clergy, who could read European political ideas about rights, democracy and self-determination and see the contradiction between those ideas and colonial rule. Some studied in Europe and America and met other Africans and people of African descent in Pan-African congresses, such as the one held in Manchester in 1945, attended by Kwame Nkrumah and Jomo Kenyatta. Colonial economies drew people into towns, mines, ports and railways, where workers formed trade unions and organised strikes, as in the Gold Coast and Nigeria. Hundreds of thousands of Africans served in the First and Second World Wars, fighting for freedom against Germany and Italy, and returned home asking why they did not have freedom themselves. The Second World War also weakened Britain and France economically, and the United States and the Soviet Union, the new superpowers, were generally opposed to old-style colonial empires. The Atlantic Charter of 1941 spoke of the right of all peoples to choose their government, and nationalists used these words. Nationalist parties, such as Nkrumah's Convention People's Party in the Gold Coast, organised mass support. In some colonies, independence came through negotiation; in others, such as Algeria, Kenya, Angola and Mozambique, through armed struggle. So colonial rule contained the seeds of its own end.
