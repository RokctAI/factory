### subtopic_1 — Urbanisation and Migration
**Assistant:** If cities have so much unemployment, why do people keep moving there?

**Tutor:** Because people compare their chances in the city with their chances at home, not with a perfect situation. Even if unemployment in a city is high, the chance of finding some work, formal or informal, is usually much higher than in a rural village where there may be almost no jobs at all. Wages in the city are also usually higher. A young person might reason like this: at home I have almost no chance of earning money; in the city I might find a job in construction, a shop or a factory, or I can earn something by trading or doing piece work, and over time I may find something better. Cities also offer chances that rural areas cannot: further education, training, a wider range of jobs, and contact with people and networks that can lead to opportunities. Family networks reduce the risk: a relative may offer a place to sleep and help with finding work. There is also the role of hope and perception: people hear about the success stories of those who did well in the city, and less about those who struggled. Finally, migration is often a household strategy: even if the migrant earns only a little, sending some of it home as remittances can make a big difference to the family's income. So migration continues even when cities have high unemployment, because for many people it is still the best option available.

### subtopic_2 — Push Factors
**Assistant:** Why is Africa less urbanised than other continents but urbanising faster?

**Tutor:** Africa is less urbanised mainly because of its history and its economy. During the colonial period, most African economies were organised to export raw materials, such as minerals and crops, and colonial governments often restricted Africans from living permanently in towns, as South Africa did with pass laws. Most people continued to live in rural areas and depend on farming. Industrialisation, which drew people into cities in Europe and later in Asia and Latin America, came later and on a smaller scale in most African countries. So in the 1950s, only a small share of Africans lived in towns. Since independence, however, African cities have grown very fast for several reasons. First, Africa has the fastest-growing population in the world, because birth rates are still relatively high, so both rural and urban populations are increasing rapidly. Second, rural push factors, such as small plots, land degradation, drought and conflict, combined with the pull of jobs, education and services in cities, drive large numbers of people to migrate. Third, natural increase within cities, where many young adults live and have children, adds to growth. Because Africa starts from a lower level of urbanisation, there is much more room for the urban share to rise, so the rate of change is high. United Nations projections suggest that a large share of the world's urban growth in the coming decades will take place in Africa, which makes planning for African cities one of the great challenges of this century.

### subtopic_3 — Pull Factors and the Decision to Move
**Assistant:** Is migration from neighbouring countries to South Africa the same as rural-urban migration?

**Tutor:** It is related, but it is not the same, and it is useful to see the similarities and the differences. International migration from countries such as Zimbabwe, Mozambique, Lesotho and Malawi to South Africa is driven by many of the same push and pull factors as internal rural-urban migration: unemployment and low wages at home, economic or political crises, drought, and the pull of jobs, higher wages and services in South African cities. Many international migrants also end up in South African cities, so international migration adds to urban growth. But international migration has extra obstacles and issues. Migrants must cross borders, which requires passports, visas and permits, and many people who cannot get documents move without them, which makes them vulnerable to exploitation, arrest and deportation. They may face language barriers and discrimination. In South Africa there have been outbreaks of xenophobic violence, most seriously in 2008, when attacks on foreign nationals left about sixty people dead and many thousands displaced, and again in later years. At the same time, migration between South Africa and its neighbours has a very long history: from the late nineteenth century, the mines recruited workers from Mozambique, Lesotho, Malawi and other countries, as you learned in History. So international migration is part of a long regional pattern, and a balanced answer recognises both its economic contributions and the challenges it brings.

### subtopic_4 — Measuring Urbanisation and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do remittances affect rural areas?

**Tutor:** Remittances, the money that migrants send home, can have both positive and negative effects on rural areas, and geographers study both. On the positive side, remittances are often the main source of cash for rural households in South Africa and neighbouring countries. They pay for food, school fees and uniforms, health care, building or improving houses, and sometimes for starting small businesses, buying livestock or farm equipment. They help families survive droughts and other shocks. Some migrants also bring home new skills and ideas. In some villages, houses built with remittance money show clearly which families have members working in the cities. On the negative side, migration takes away many young, energetic and often better-educated people, especially young men in the past and increasingly young women today. This can leave rural areas with mainly older people and children, a situation sometimes called a skewed population structure. Farming may suffer because there are fewer people to work the land, so families depend even more on remittances. Children may grow up without one or both parents at home, often cared for by grandparents. And if the migrant loses their job or becomes ill, the whole household is affected. In History this term, you studied the impact of migrant labour on families in the mining era, and many of the same patterns continue today, though now in different forms. So remittances keep many rural households going, but they do not on their own solve the deeper problems that push people to leave.
