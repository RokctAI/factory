# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the social issues of rapid city growth. The anchor is the gap between the speed at which people arrive in cities and the speed at which governments can provide housing, services, clinics and schools. The governing idea is that when urban populations grow faster than planning and investment, the result is informal settlements, backlogs in water, sanitation and electricity, and overcrowded health and education services, and that these problems fall most heavily on the poor, but can be reduced through upgrading, investment and community action. Describing these issues, explaining their causes and evaluating responses are examinable this term.

## Subtopic: The Housing Challenge

Rapid urbanisation creates an enormous demand for housing. When people arrive in a city faster than formal houses can be built, they find shelter in other ways: renting rooms or backyard shacks from township residents, crowding into existing houses and flats, occupying empty buildings, or building their own shelters in informal settlements.

Informal settlements are found in almost every South African city. They often grow on land that is not suitable or safe for housing, such as flood plains along rivers, steep slopes, land under power lines, beside railway lines and highways, or on old dumps and wetlands, because that is the land that is empty. Shelters are made of corrugated iron, wood, plastic and other available materials. Plots are small and irregular, there are few roads, and emergency vehicles often cannot get in.

Living conditions in informal settlements create serious risks. Shack fires spread quickly because shelters are close together and made of flammable materials, and people use paraffin stoves, candles and open fires for cooking, heating and light. Large fires have destroyed thousands of homes at once, for example in Imizamo Yethu in Hout Bay in 2017. Flooding is a major danger for settlements on flood plains: in April 2022, severe floods in KwaZulu-Natal killed more than four hundred people and destroyed or damaged thousands of homes, many of them in informal settlements and on steep slopes. People living without secure tenure, meaning without a legal right to their land, may face eviction.

South Africa's government has built millions of subsidised houses since 1994, but the backlog, the number of households still waiting for adequate housing, remains large, because the urban population keeps growing and new households keep forming. Today, most South African households live in formal dwellings, but fewer than one in ten live in informal dwellings, and in the large metropolitan areas the share is higher.

Responses to the housing challenge include building subsidised houses; providing serviced sites, where the government provides a plot with water, sanitation and roads, and people build their own homes; upgrading informal settlements in place, rather than removing people, by adding roads, services and secure tenure step by step; building affordable rental housing in well-located areas; and the People's Housing Process, in which communities help to plan and build their own homes. Each approach has strengths and weaknesses, and many cities combine them.

Rapid growth is not only a South African issue. Across Africa, large shares of city residents live in informal settlements, such as Kibera in Nairobi and Makoko, built on stilts over the lagoon in Lagos. In many African cities, the majority of new urban residents find housing informally, because formal housing is too expensive or simply not available. Governments across the continent face the same challenge of providing secure, safe and affordable homes for fast-growing populations.

The questions for this section are with you now: why informal settlements grow, the risks people face there and the responses to the housing challenge.

## Subtopic: Water, Sanitation and Electricity

Basic services are the services every household needs for health and dignity: clean water, safe sanitation, electricity and refuse removal. Providing them is mainly the responsibility of municipalities. When cities grow rapidly, especially through informal settlements, these services fall behind.

Water. In formal areas, most households have a tap inside the house or in the yard. In many informal settlements, people collect water from communal standpipes, carrying it in containers, and one tap may be shared by many households. Long queues and long walks, mainly by women and children, take time away from work and school. When water is stored in open containers, it can become contaminated. South Africa is a water-scarce country, and rapid city growth increases demand. During the drought of 2015 to 2018, Cape Town came close to Day Zero, when municipal taps would have been turned off, and residents had to cut water use drastically.

Sanitation. Safe sanitation means toilets that safely separate human waste from people, such as flush toilets connected to sewers or properly managed pit latrines. In many informal settlements, people use shared chemical or portable toilets, pit latrines, or no toilets at all, which leads to the spread of diseases such as diarrhoea and cholera, and creates safety risks, especially for women and children who must walk to toilets at night. Sewage systems in growing cities can also become overloaded, polluting rivers and beaches.

Electricity. Most South African households now have electricity, but many informal settlements still lack legal connections. People use paraffin, candles, wood and gas, which cause fires and indoor air pollution, or make illegal connections, which can cause electrocution and fires. Rapid growth also places pressure on the national electricity supply, which has faced shortages and load shedding.

Refuse removal. Where refuse is not collected, rubbish piles up, attracting rats and flies, blocking drains and causing flooding and disease.

Why do services fall behind? Municipalities need money, skilled staff, planning and time to build pipes, sewers, power lines and roads. In informal settlements, the layout is dense and irregular, so it is difficult to fit infrastructure in. Some settlements are on land the municipality does not own or that is unsafe. And municipalities face financial and management problems. When services are not delivered, residents often protest, and service delivery protests have been common in South Africa.

Some solutions are low-cost and community-based. Communal ablution blocks with toilets, showers and basins can serve many households where individual connections are not yet possible. Prepaid electricity meters and formal connections reduce illegal connections and the dangers they bring. Rainwater tanks, solar lights and solar water heaters reduce the pressure on municipal services. Regular refuse collection, sometimes run by local cooperatives that employ residents, keeps drains clear and creates jobs.

The questions for this section are with you now: water, sanitation, electricity and refuse removal in growing cities.

## Subtopic: Health Care and Education

Rapid urban growth also puts pressure on social services, especially health care and education.

Health. Crowded living conditions, poor sanitation, unsafe water and indoor smoke from paraffin and wood increase the spread of disease. Tuberculosis spreads easily in crowded, poorly ventilated homes, and South Africa has one of the highest rates of tuberculosis in the world. HIV, diarrhoeal diseases among young children, and respiratory illnesses are also major health problems in poor urban areas. Injuries from fires, violence and road accidents add to the burden.

Public clinics and hospitals in growing areas are often overcrowded. Patients may queue for hours, and there may be shortages of staff and medicines. Many clinics are far from new settlements, so people must spend time and money on transport. Private health care is available in cities, but most people cannot afford it. Responses include building new clinics in growing areas, mobile clinics that visit settlements on set days, community health workers who visit homes to support patients with tuberculosis, HIV and other conditions, and campaigns to improve hygiene and vaccinate children.

Education. Rapid growth means more children needing places in schools. In growing areas, schools become overcrowded, with large classes, too few classrooms, desks and textbooks, and teachers under pressure. Some new settlements have no school nearby, so children must travel long distances, often on foot, or in overloaded vehicles. Learners who live in informal settlements may struggle to study at home because of crowding, noise, lack of electricity for light and the time spent collecting water. In contrast, schools in wealthier suburbs often have smaller classes and more resources, which reflects the inequality of South African cities.

Responses include building new schools and classrooms in growing areas, including mobile classrooms as a temporary measure; providing scholar transport; school nutrition programmes, which provide meals to millions of learners; and no-fee schools, which allow learners from poorer communities to attend without paying school fees.

These issues are connected. A child in a crowded informal settlement may become ill because of poor sanitation, miss school, fall behind and later struggle to find work. Improving housing and services therefore also improves health and education.

Safety is another social issue connected to rapid growth. Dense settlements with few streetlights, narrow paths and long walks to taps, toilets and taxi ranks can be dangerous, especially at night and especially for women and children. Better lighting, safer public spaces and community policing forums are part of the response.

The questions for this section are with you now: health care and education in rapidly growing cities.

## Subtopic: Calculating Pressure and the Error Museum

Ratios help to measure pressure on services. Suppose, as an example for the method, a school has one thousand two hundred learners and thirty classrooms. One thousand two hundred divided by thirty is forty learners per classroom. If the settlement grows and the school admits another three hundred learners without building new classrooms, there are one thousand five hundred learners and thirty classrooms. One thousand five hundred divided by thirty is fifty learners per classroom, an increase of ten per classroom, or twenty-five per cent, because ten divided by forty is zero point two five.

Similarly, suppose a clinic serves a population of twenty thousand people with five nurses. Twenty thousand divided by five is four thousand people per nurse. If the population grows to thirty thousand with no extra nurses, thirty thousand divided by five is six thousand people per nurse, half as many again. These example figures show how growth without investment increases pressure.

Geographers also map access, for example by drawing circles of a certain distance around clinics and schools to show which settlements are within walking distance and which are not. This helps planners decide where new facilities are needed.

Rapid urban growth has positive effects too. Cities offer more jobs, education, health care and services than most rural areas, and many people's lives improve after they move. Urban growth can support economic development. The challenge is to manage growth so that its benefits are shared.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: blaming the residents of informal settlements for the problems. The causes include rapid growth, history, land prices and lack of investment. Two: saying that building houses alone solves the problem. Services, transport, jobs, health and education must come too. Three: saying that services are equally available across cities. They differ greatly between rich and poor areas. Four: saying urbanisation is only negative. Cities also bring opportunities. Five: forgetting the links between issues, for example between sanitation and health, and between health and education.

Layout for marks in an essay on the social issues of urban growth: introduce rapid growth, discuss housing, services, health and education with South African examples, explain the causes, evaluate at least two responses, and conclude.

The questions for this section are with you now: ratios of pressure on services, mapping access and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Too Many, Too Fast

When lots of people come to a city very fast, there are not enough houses. So people rent backyard rooms or build shacks in informal settlements.

Informal settlements are often on dangerous land, like river banks that flood or steep slopes. Shacks are close together, so fires spread fast. In 2022, floods in KwaZulu-Natal killed more than four hundred people.

The government has built millions of houses, but more people keep coming. Now it often upgrades settlements where they are, adding roads, water and toilets.

## Subtopic: Water, Toilets and Power

Every home needs clean water, a safe toilet, electricity and rubbish collection. In many informal settlements, people share one tap with many families, use shared toilets or none, and use candles and paraffin for light.

Dirty water and no toilets spread diseases like diarrhoea. Candles and paraffin cause fires. Rubbish blocks drains and brings rats.

Municipalities must provide these services, but it takes money, planning and time, and cities are growing very fast.

Some places use shared toilet and shower blocks, solar lights and water tanks while they wait for full services.

Keep the shared tap in mind and try the questions for this part: services.

## Subtopic: Clinics and Classrooms

When cities grow fast, clinics have long queues and classrooms get crowded. Crowded homes spread tuberculosis. Children may have to walk far to school, and may struggle to study at home with no light and no space.

Solutions include new clinics, mobile clinics, community health workers, new classrooms, school meals and no-fee schools.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Rapid city growth creates a housing backlog and informal settlements, often on unsafe land, with risks of fire and flooding. Water, sanitation, electricity and refuse removal fall behind, causing disease and danger. Clinics and schools become overcrowded. These problems are connected and fall most on the poor. Responses include subsidised houses, serviced sites, upgrading in place, mobile clinics, new schools, school meals and no-fee schools. Cities also bring opportunities, so the challenge is to manage growth fairly.
