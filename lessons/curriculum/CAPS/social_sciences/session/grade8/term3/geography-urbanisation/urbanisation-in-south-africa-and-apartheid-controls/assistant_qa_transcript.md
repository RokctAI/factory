### subtopic_1 — From Mining Towns to Industrial Cities
**Assistant:** If the economy needed African workers in the cities, why did the government try to keep them out?

**Tutor:** Because the government wanted African labour without African residence, rights or political power in the cities, and it believed it could have one without the other. White farmers, mine owners and factory owners all needed African workers. But many white voters feared that if African families settled permanently in the cities, they would compete for jobs, especially with white workers, demand houses, schools and services, and, above all, demand political rights, since they would be permanent members of urban society. The idea behind the 1923 Act and later apartheid laws was that African people should be in the cities only as temporary workers, whose permanent homes were in the reserves or homelands. Migrant labour suited this idea: workers came on contracts, lived in hostels and returned home. The pass laws and influx control were tools to allow in exactly the number of workers employers needed, and to remove those who were not needed. The homelands were meant to give African people political rights far away, so that they could be denied rights in South Africa itself. The contradiction was that the economy kept growing and needed more settled, skilled workers, while the laws tried to keep people temporary. Employers increasingly complained, people kept coming despite arrests, and by the 1980s the system had become unworkable. That contradiction between economic need and political control is the key to understanding South African urbanisation in the twentieth century.

### subtopic_2 — Pass Laws, Influx Control and Group Areas
**Assistant:** What was life like under Section 10?

**Tutor:** For many African people, Section 10 meant living in constant uncertainty. If you did not qualify for Section 10 rights, by birth and continuous residence, fifteen years of lawful residence, or ten years with one employer, you could only stay in the city with a permit tied to a specific job. If you lost that job, you could lose the right to stay and be sent to a homeland you might never have lived in. Police carried out raids, often at night, checking passes in homes, on the streets and at workplaces, and people without the right stamps were arrested, fined or imprisoned, and endorsed out, meaning removed from the area. Families were split: a man with a permit to work in the city might not be allowed to bring his wife and children, who had to stay in the homeland, or live with him illegally and risk arrest. Women faced particular difficulties, which is one reason the women's march of 1956 was so powerful. Some people paid bribes, borrowed documents or worked illegally. Others built shacks in informal settlements, hoping not to be noticed. The fear of the knock on the door, the long queues at pass offices and the humiliation of being stopped and searched are strong themes in memoirs, songs, plays and photographs from the period. These personal sources help us understand that influx control was not only a planning policy but a daily experience that shaped people's lives, families and sense of belonging.

### subtopic_3 — Homelands, Displaced Urbanisation and the End of Influx Control
**Assistant:** Why did influx control collapse in the 1980s?

**Tutor:** Several pressures came together. First, economic pressure: South Africa's economy needed a larger, more settled and more skilled urban workforce, and many employers argued that influx control made it hard to find and keep workers. Second, demographic pressure: the African population was growing fast, the homelands could not support it, and people kept moving to the cities whether the law allowed it or not. Third, resistance: communities in informal settlements, such as Crossroads in Cape Town, refused to be removed, organised, gained support from churches and other organisations, and attracted international attention. Mass political resistance in the townships in the mid-1980s made the country increasingly difficult to govern. Fourth, cost and practicality: arresting, prosecuting and removing hundreds of thousands of people every year was enormously expensive and was not working. Fifth, international pressure: sanctions and criticism made apartheid's costs higher. In 1985 a government commission recommended ending influx control, and in 1986 the pass laws and influx control were abolished, replaced by what the government called orderly urbanisation, which still tried to control where people lived through housing and land policies. The result was rapid growth of informal settlements around the cities in the late 1980s and 1990s. The end of influx control was one of the signs that apartheid was breaking down, several years before the release of Nelson Mandela in 1990 and the first democratic election in 1994.

### subtopic_4 — Commuting, Sources and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why does it still take so long for many people to get to work today?

**Tutor:** Because the spatial pattern created by apartheid has been very hard to change, and in some ways it was repeated after 1994. Under apartheid, African townships and homeland settlements were deliberately placed far from city centres and white suburbs, where most jobs were, with buffer zones of open land, railways, highways or industry in between. Displaced urbanisation put millions of people even further away, in places like KwaNdebele, Botshabelo and Mdantsane. After 1994, people could live anywhere legally, but land close to jobs was expensive, and most new government housing was built on cheap land on the edges of cities, often next to existing townships, so the distance between homes and jobs remained. At the same time, jobs have moved too, from old CBDs to suburban business districts such as Sandton, which are often even further from townships. Public transport systems were designed for the apartheid pattern and are often slow, crowded or unreliable, so many commuters rely on minibus taxis and spend a large share of their income on transport. Today, cities are trying to change this through transit-oriented development, building housing near transport routes, rapid bus systems, upgrading townships so that more jobs and services are local, and densifying well-located areas. But changing the shape of a city takes decades. This is why the history of apartheid urbanisation is still part of Geography, not only of History.
