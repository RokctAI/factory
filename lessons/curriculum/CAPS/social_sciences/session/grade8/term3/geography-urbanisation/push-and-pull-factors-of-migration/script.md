# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces urbanisation and the migration that drives it. The anchor is rural-to-urban migration in Africa, with South Africa as the main example. The governing idea is that people move when the disadvantages of staying in one place, the push factors, and the attractions of another, the pull factors, outweigh the costs and risks of moving, and that in Africa today millions of people make this decision every year, making African cities among the fastest-growing in the world. Defining urbanisation and migration, and identifying and explaining push and pull factors, are examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Urbanisation and Migration

Urbanisation is the increase in the proportion of a country's population that lives in urban areas, towns and cities. It is not the same as city growth. A city can grow because its population increases, but urbanisation only happens if the urban share of the whole population rises. For example, if both the rural and the urban population grow at the same rate, the cities grow, but the level of urbanisation stays the same.

Cities grow in two main ways. The first is natural increase: more babies are born in the city than people die. The second is migration: people move into the city from elsewhere. Migration is the movement of people from one place to another to live, either for a long time or permanently. Rural-urban migration, the movement of people from the countryside to towns and cities, is the main cause of urbanisation in Africa, although natural increase in cities is now also very important.

Migration can be internal, within one country, such as from the Eastern Cape to Gauteng, or international, across borders, such as from Zimbabwe to South Africa. It can be voluntary, when people choose to move, usually for work or education, or forced, when people are made to move by war, persecution, disaster or government policy. People who move into a place are called immigrants or in-migrants; people who leave are emigrants or out-migrants. Refugees are people forced to flee their country because of war or persecution.

Around the world, more than half of all people now live in urban areas. Africa is less urbanised than other continents, with roughly two out of every five people living in towns and cities, but it is urbanising faster than any other continent. Cities such as Lagos in Nigeria, Kinshasa in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Cairo in Egypt, Luanda in Angola, Nairobi in Kenya and Dar es Salaam in Tanzania have grown enormously since the 1960s, and several of them are now among the largest cities in the world.

South Africa is one of the most urbanised countries in Africa. About two-thirds of its people live in urban areas. Gauteng, the smallest province by area, about one and a half per cent of the country's land, had about fifteen point one million people in the 2022 census, about one in four South Africans. Cape Town, eThekwini and the other metropolitan areas have also grown quickly.

Migration does not only go in one direction. Some people move from small towns to big cities, a process called step migration, because they first move to a nearby town and later to a larger city. Others move between cities, such as from Gqeberha to Cape Town. Some people return from cities to rural areas when they retire or lose their jobs, which is called return migration. And many people move back and forth regularly, living in the city for work and returning home for holidays, which is called circular migration, a pattern with deep roots in South Africa's history of migrant labour.

## Subtopic: Push Factors

Push factors are the conditions that make people want to leave a place. In rural areas of Africa, and in South Africa, the most important push factors are economic.

Lack of jobs and low incomes are the strongest push factors. In many rural areas there are few jobs outside farming, and farm work is often seasonal and poorly paid. Many young people finish school and find no work at all. In South Africa, unemployment in the former homeland areas of the Eastern Cape, Limpopo and KwaZulu-Natal is especially high.

Problems with farming push people away too. Small plots, overcrowded and overused land, soil erosion, and a lack of money for seeds, fertiliser and equipment make it hard to make a living from the land. Droughts and floods can destroy harvests and kill livestock. Climate change is making some areas drier and rainfall less reliable. In South Africa, the history of land dispossession, including the Land Act of 1913 and the homelands, left many rural families with too little land.

Lack of services is another push factor. Many rural areas have poorly resourced schools, few clinics and hospitals, long distances to the nearest services, poor roads, and in some places no piped water or electricity. Young people who want further education must leave, because there are few colleges and universities in rural areas.

Social and political factors can push people too. Some people leave to escape conflict, violence, crime or discrimination. Some leave because of family problems, or because they want more personal freedom than they have in a small community. Across Africa, wars and political instability have forced millions of people to flee their homes, including people from the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Somalia, South Sudan and Sudan.

Natural disasters, such as droughts, floods and cyclones, can force people to move suddenly. Cyclone Idai, which struck Mozambique, Zimbabwe and Malawi in March 2019, destroyed homes and farms and displaced hundreds of thousands of people.

Push factors usually act together. A young person in a rural village may have no job, a family plot too small to support everyone, a school that cannot prepare them for university, and a long walk to the nearest clinic. Each factor on its own might not be enough to make them leave, but together they make staying very difficult.

The questions for this section are with you now: the economic, environmental, social and political push factors that drive people from rural areas.

## Subtopic: Pull Factors and the Decision to Move

Pull factors are the attractions that draw people to a place. Cities pull people mainly with opportunities.

The most important pull factor is the hope of employment and higher incomes. Cities have many more jobs, in factories, shops, offices, construction, transport, domestic work and the informal sector, and wages are usually higher than in rural areas. Even when formal jobs are hard to find, people may earn money through informal work, such as street trading, hairdressing, car washing or running a spaza shop.

Better services pull people too. Cities have more and better schools, colleges and universities, clinics and hospitals, piped water, electricity, sanitation, shops, banks and government offices. Young people come to the cities to study at universities and colleges, such as those in Johannesburg, Pretoria, Cape Town and Durban.

Social pull factors matter a great deal. Most migrants move to places where they already have family or friends, who can help them with a place to stay, information about jobs and support in a new environment. This is called chain migration. Cities also offer entertainment, sport, culture, and the freedom and excitement of urban life, which many young people find attractive.

Some pull factors are based on perception rather than reality. People may believe that it is easy to find work and housing in the city, but when they arrive they may find high unemployment, expensive transport, crowded housing and crime. Even so, many stay, because life in the city, with its chances of work and education, may still be better than life at home, and because returning home can be seen as failure.

The decision to migrate is often made by a whole household, not only by one person. A family may decide that one member, often a young adult, should go to the city to earn money and send some home. Money sent home by migrants is called remittances, and in many rural areas of South Africa and neighbouring countries, remittances are a major source of household income. Many migrants keep strong links with their rural homes, returning for holidays, family events and retirement, so that families live across both rural and urban places.

Geographers summarise migration with a simple model: people weigh up the push factors at home, the pull factors in the city, and the obstacles in between, such as the cost of the journey, distance, border controls and lack of information.

The pull of a particular city depends on its economy. Gauteng pulls people mainly because of its large and varied economy, including finance, manufacturing, trade, government in Pretoria and the remaining mines. Cape Town pulls people with jobs in services, tourism and the port, and many migrants from the Eastern Cape move there. eThekwini pulls people from rural KwaZulu-Natal with jobs at the port and in manufacturing. Smaller cities and towns also attract migrants, especially those with universities, mines or government offices.

The questions for this section are with you now: the pull factors of cities, chain migration and how households decide to migrate.

## Subtopic: Measuring Urbanisation and the Error Museum

A calculation shows the difference between city growth and urbanisation. The level of urbanisation is the urban population divided by the total population, times one hundred. Suppose, as an example for the method, a country has ten million people, and four million live in urban areas. Four million divided by ten million is zero point four, times one hundred is forty per cent. Ten years later, the population is twelve million, with six million in urban areas. Six divided by twelve is zero point five, so the level of urbanisation has risen to fifty per cent. The urban population grew by two million, which is fifty per cent, because two divided by four is zero point five. The rural population stayed at six million. Both city growth and urbanisation happened.

Now compare a real example. In the 2022 census, Gauteng had about fifteen point one million people out of South Africa's sixty-two point zero million. Fifteen point one divided by sixty-two point zero is about zero point two four, or twenty-four per cent, nearly one in four people, living on about one point five per cent of the land.

Migration is studied using census data, surveys and interviews with migrants. Interviews are especially useful because they show the reasons behind people's decisions. A migrant might say: I came to Johannesburg because there was no work at home, and my cousin said there were jobs in construction. That one sentence contains a push factor, a pull factor and chain migration.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying urbanisation means a city is growing. Urbanisation is a rise in the urban share of the population. Two: mixing up push and pull factors. Push factors are at the place people leave; pull factors are at the place they go to. Three: saying migrants move only because of pull factors. Usually push and pull work together. Four: saying everyone who moves to the city finds a job. Many face unemployment and poor housing. Five: forgetting that many migrants keep links with home and send remittances.

Layout for marks in a migration question: define the key term, give two push factors and two pull factors with South African or African examples, explain how they combine, and mention one obstacle or consequence.

## Subtopic: Why People Move

Urbanisation means that more and more of a country's people live in towns and cities.

People move from the countryside to the city. This is called rural-urban migration. Some move inside the country, like from the Eastern Cape to Gauteng. Some cross borders, like from Zimbabwe to South Africa.

About two out of every three South Africans live in towns and cities. Gauteng is tiny, but about one in four South Africans live there.

## Subtopic: Pushed Away

Push factors are the things that make people want to leave home.

No jobs. Farms that are too small, or land worn out. Drought that kills crops and cattle. Schools and clinics far away. No running water or electricity. Sometimes war, crime or disaster.

In South Africa, many rural families were left with too little land because of laws in the past, like the Land Act of 1913.

Usually it is not one thing. It is many things together.

A young person may leave because of no job, a tiny farm and no college nearby, all at once.

Keep those push factors in mind and try the questions for this part: push factors.

## Subtopic: Pulled to the City

Pull factors are the things that attract people to the city: jobs and better pay, schools, colleges and universities, hospitals, water and electricity, shops, and the excitement of city life. Many people go where family or friends already live, who help them find a place and a job.

Many migrants send money home to their families. This is called remittances.

But the city is not always what people hoped. Jobs can be hard to find, and housing can be crowded.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Urbanisation is the rise in the share of people living in urban areas, caused mainly by rural-urban migration and natural increase. Push factors, such as unemployment, poor farming conditions, drought, lack of services, conflict and disasters, drive people from rural areas. Pull factors, such as jobs, higher incomes, education, health care, services and family networks, attract them to cities. Africa is urbanising faster than any other continent, and South Africa is one of its most urbanised countries.
