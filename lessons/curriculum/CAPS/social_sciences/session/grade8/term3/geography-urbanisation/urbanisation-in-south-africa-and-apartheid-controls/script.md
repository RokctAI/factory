# Part 1 — Expert

This session gives an overview of urbanisation in South Africa, including the population controls of segregation and apartheid. The anchor is the contrast between the growth of the economy, which needed workers in the cities, and laws that tried to prevent African people from settling permanently in urban areas. The governing idea is that South African urbanisation was shaped by both economic forces and political control: mining and industry pulled people to the cities, while pass laws, influx control, group areas and homelands tried to keep the African population rural and temporary, until these controls collapsed in the 1980s and urbanisation accelerated. Explaining this history and its effects on today's cities is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: From Mining Towns to Industrial Cities

Before the mineral revolution, South Africa's towns were small. Cape Town, Durban, Port Elizabeth and the inland towns were trading, administrative and farming centres, and most people, black and white, lived in rural areas.

The discovery of diamonds at Kimberley in 1867 and 1870, and of gold on the Witwatersrand in 1886, changed this. Kimberley grew into a town of tens of thousands within a few years. Johannesburg, founded in 1886 as a mining camp, became the largest city in southern Africa within a generation. The mines needed huge numbers of workers, and, as you learned in History, they relied on African migrant workers who came on contracts, lived in compounds and hostels, and returned to rural homes. Railways were built to link the ports with the mines, and port cities such as Durban and Cape Town grew with the trade.

In the twentieth century, manufacturing grew, especially during and after the Second World War, from 1939 to 1945, when South African factories produced goods that could no longer be imported. Factories in the Witwatersrand, Pretoria, Durban, Cape Town and Port Elizabeth needed workers, and many African families moved to the cities, not only as migrant workers but to settle permanently. In the 1940s, African urban populations grew very quickly. There were not enough houses, and people built informal settlements on the edges of the cities. In 1944, a community leader named James Mpanza led thousands of families to occupy open land near Orlando, in what became Soweto, demanding housing.

White people also urbanised rapidly in the first half of the twentieth century. Many poor white farmers, especially Afrikaners, moved to the cities after droughts, the destruction of farms in the South African War and changes in farming, and found work in the mines, the railways and factories.

By the middle of the twentieth century, South Africa had become an urban, industrial society, but African urbanisation was seen by many white voters and politicians as a threat. They feared competition for jobs, the growth of African political organisation in the cities, and the end of white political control. This fear shaped the laws that followed.

Coloured and Indian South Africans were also part of urbanisation. Many coloured families lived in Cape Town and other Western Cape towns, working as artisans, fishers, factory workers and domestic workers. Many Indian families moved from the sugar estates into Durban and Pietermaritzburg, working as market gardeners, traders, factory workers and in services. Their neighbourhoods, such as District Six in Cape Town and the Grey Street area and Cato Manor in Durban, were lively mixed communities before apartheid planning broke many of them up.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the mineral revolution and industrialisation drove urbanisation in South Africa.

## Subtopic: Pass Laws, Influx Control and Group Areas

From early in the twentieth century, laws controlled African urbanisation. The Natives (Urban Areas) Act of 1923 allowed towns to set up separate locations for African residents and to control who could live there. It was based on the idea, stated by a government commission in 1922, that Africans should be allowed in the towns only to serve the needs of white people, and should leave when they ceased to do so.

After 1948, the apartheid government made these controls much stricter. Influx control was the system of laws that limited the number of African people allowed into urban areas. In 1952, a law required every African man, and later every African woman, to carry a reference book, a pass book, known as the dompas, containing their identity, employment and permission to be in an area. Under Section 10 of a 1952 law, an African person could stay in an urban area for more than seventy-two hours only if they had been born there and lived there continuously, had lived there lawfully for fifteen years, or had worked for one employer there for ten years, or was the wife or dependent child of such a person, or had a special permit. Anyone else could be arrested and sent away. Millions of people were arrested under the pass laws over the following decades.

Opposition to passes was strong. Women organised a great march to the Union Buildings in Pretoria on 9 August 1956, when about twenty thousand women protested against passes for women. On 21 March 1960, at Sharpeville, police opened fire on people protesting against the pass laws, killing sixty-nine.

The Group Areas Act of 1950 divided cities into separate areas for each racial group and led to forced removals from mixed neighbourhoods such as District Six, Sophiatown and Cato Manor. African people were moved to townships on the edges of the cities, such as Soweto, and coloured and Indian people to separate areas such as Mitchells Plain and Chatsworth.

The government also built hostels for single migrant workers, and limited family housing for Africans in the cities, especially in the Western Cape, where a policy gave coloured workers preference for jobs and made it very difficult for African families to live there legally. The aim was that the African population of the cities should be as small and as temporary as possible.

These controls also affected how cities were built. Townships were laid out with wide roads that made them easy to police, few entrances and exits, and buffer zones that separated them from white areas. Shops, businesses and services in townships were restricted, so residents had to travel to white areas to shop and work. Every part of this design aimed to keep African residents separate, controlled and temporary.

## Subtopic: Homelands, Displaced Urbanisation and the End of Influx Control

The apartheid government's plan for the countryside was the homelands, also called Bantustans. Under laws passed from 1959, the government divided the African population into ethnic groups and assigned each to a homeland, based on the former reserves, which made up only about thirteen per cent of the land. African people were to be citizens of the homelands, not of South Africa, and were to live and exercise political rights there. Four homelands were declared independent by South Africa: Transkei in 1976, Bophuthatswana in 1977, Venda in 1979 and Ciskei in 1981. No other country recognised them.

The homelands could not support their growing populations. Land was overcrowded and eroded, and there were few jobs. Hundreds of thousands of people were forcibly moved into the homelands from white farms, from so-called black spots, which were African-owned land in areas declared white, and from the cities.

Because people needed to be near jobs but were not allowed to live in the cities, a pattern called displaced urbanisation developed. Large, dense settlements grew inside homelands but close to their borders with industrial areas: for example, Mdantsane near East London, Botshabelo near Bloemfontein, Ga-Rankuwa and Mabopane near Pretoria, and the settlements of KwaNdebele north-east of Pretoria. These were urban in size and density, but officially rural and part of homelands. Workers commuted long distances every day by bus and train, often subsidised by the government, or became weekly migrants. The government also encouraged industries to move to border areas near homelands, with incentives, so that African workers could work there without living in white areas.

By the 1980s, influx control was collapsing. The economy needed more workers in the cities; people kept coming despite the laws; informal settlements grew, such as Crossroads in Cape Town, whose residents resisted removal for years; and the cost of enforcing the pass laws was enormous. In 1986, the government abolished influx control and the pass laws. Urbanisation accelerated, and informal settlements grew rapidly around the cities.

In 1994, with the first democratic election, the homelands were reincorporated into South Africa, and the Group Areas Act, already repealed in 1991, was gone. People could legally live anywhere. Since then, urbanisation has continued, and today about two-thirds of South Africans live in urban areas. But the spatial pattern of apartheid, with townships and former homeland settlements far from jobs, remains visible in every city.

The movement of people after 1986 was not only from rural areas to cities. Many people who had been living illegally in backyards and hostels could now bring their families to the cities, and people moved from the overcrowded homeland settlements to informal settlements closer to jobs. This is one reason why informal settlements grew so quickly around Johannesburg, Cape Town and Durban in the late 1980s and early 1990s.

## Subtopic: Commuting, Sources and the Error Museum

A calculation shows the human cost of displaced urbanisation. Suppose, as an example for the method, a worker in KwaNdebele travelled two and a half hours each way to work in Pretoria. That is five hours a day. Over a five-day working week, five times five is twenty-five hours a week. Over a working year of fifty weeks, twenty-five times fifty is one thousand two hundred and fifty hours spent travelling, more than fifty full days. Researchers in the 1980s recorded journeys of this length, with workers leaving home before three in the morning. These are example figures for the calculation, but they reflect what commuters described.

Historians and geographers studying apartheid urbanisation use many sources: laws and government reports, census data, maps of group areas and homelands, newspaper reports, photographs, and the testimony of people who lived through forced removals, many collected by community projects such as the District Six Museum in Cape Town. Government sources from the apartheid era often described removals as slum clearance or voluntary resettlement. Testimony from residents describes them as the destruction of communities. When using these sources, ask who produced them and why.

The legacy of these laws is still visible. Many people still live far from jobs and spend a large share of their income and time on transport. Former homeland settlements remain poorer than the cities. The pattern of townships, buffer zones and wealthy suburbs remains. Understanding urbanisation in South Africa is impossible without this history.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying apartheid stopped African urbanisation. It slowed and redirected it, but did not stop it; displaced urbanisation and informal settlements grew. Two: confusing the homelands with townships. Homelands were rural territories for each ethnic group; townships were urban residential areas. Three: saying influx control ended in 1994. The pass laws were abolished in 1986. Four: saying all homelands became independent. Only four were declared independent, and no other country recognised them. Five: treating apartheid-era government reports on removals as neutral.

Layout for marks in an essay on urbanisation in South Africa: introduce economic causes, explain the controls and their purpose, describe displaced urbanisation, explain the collapse of influx control, and conclude with the legacy in today's cities.

## Subtopic: How the Cities Grew

When diamonds and gold were found, towns like Kimberley and Johannesburg grew fast. Mines needed workers, and many came as migrant workers.

Later, factories grew, especially during the Second World War, and many more families moved to the cities to stay. There were not enough houses, so people built shacks.

Many white people also moved from farms to cities.

Picture Johannesburg in 1886: just a mining camp. Fifty years later: a big city.

## Subtopic: Passes and Removals

The government made laws to keep African people out of the cities, or there only for work. Every African adult had to carry a pass book, the dompas. Without the right stamp, you could be arrested and sent away.

The Group Areas Act moved people out of mixed areas like District Six and Sophiatown into separate townships.

People fought back: twenty thousand women marched to Pretoria in 1956, and at Sharpeville in 1960 police killed sixty-nine protesters.

Keep the dompas in mind and try the questions for this part: pass laws and removals.

## Subtopic: Homelands and Long Journeys

The government made homelands, areas for African people far from the cities. They were crowded and had few jobs. So big settlements grew just inside the homelands, near the cities, and workers travelled for hours by bus every day.

In 1986 the pass laws ended, and in 1994 the homelands were joined back to South Africa. Now anyone can live anywhere, but many people still live far from jobs.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The mineral revolution and industrialisation pulled people into South Africa's cities. Segregation and apartheid tried to control African urbanisation with pass laws, influx control, Section 10, the Group Areas Act and the homelands. This led to forced removals and displaced urbanisation, with long commutes. Influx control collapsed and was abolished in 1986, and urbanisation sped up. Today about two-thirds of South Africans are urban, but the apartheid pattern is still visible.
