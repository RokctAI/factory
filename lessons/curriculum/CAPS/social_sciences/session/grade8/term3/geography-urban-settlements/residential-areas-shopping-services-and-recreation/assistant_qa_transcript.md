### subtopic_1 — High-, Middle- and Low-Income Residential Areas
**Assistant:** Why were new RDP houses often built far from the city centre if apartheid was over?

**Tutor:** Mainly because of the cost of land and the pressure to build large numbers of houses quickly. After 1994, the government promised to build a million houses in five years, and the housing subsidy programme went on to provide millions of houses. Each subsidised house had a fixed budget. Land near the city centre and in established suburbs was very expensive, and much of it was already owned and developed. Cheap, large, empty pieces of land were usually found on the edges of the cities, often next to existing townships, and building there allowed the government to provide more houses with the same money. Bulk services such as water pipes and sewers sometimes already existed near the townships. The result, however, was that many new housing projects repeated the apartheid pattern: poor households were again placed far from jobs, shops and services, and had to spend a large part of their income and many hours a day on transport. Critics pointed this out from the late 1990s onwards. Since then, policy has changed to emphasise building sustainable human settlements: mixed-income developments, rental housing in inner cities, upgrading informal settlements where they are, and building near transport corridors. Progress has been slow, but the aim is to undo the spatial pattern of apartheid, not only to provide houses. This is a good example of a policy with good intentions that had unintended effects, which is something geographers look for.

### subtopic_2 — Apartheid Planning and the Unequal City
**Assistant:** Why do many high-income suburbs in South Africa have such high walls?

**Tutor:** High walls, electric fences, security gates and security estates are mainly a response to fear of crime, and they are much more common in South African cities than in many other countries. South Africa has high levels of crime, including burglary and violent crime, and households that can afford it invest in physical security and private security companies. Over time, high walls have become a normal feature of many middle- and high-income suburbs, and gated communities and security estates, with their own perimeter walls, guards and access control, have grown rapidly since the 1990s. Geographers study this because it changes the city. Walls and gated estates make streets less lively and less safe for pedestrians, because there are fewer people walking and fewer windows overlooking the street. They can increase separation between rich and poor, even when they live near each other, and can block public access to roads and open spaces. Some researchers argue that walls do not necessarily reduce crime, and that busy streets with people around, good lighting and community involvement can be more effective. So high walls are an example of how social conditions, in this case inequality and crime, shape the physical form of residential areas. In an exam, you could use this as evidence that residential areas reflect not only income and history, but also present-day social issues.

### subtopic_3 — Shopping Centres, Services and Recreation
**Assistant:** Are informal settlements the same as townships?

**Tutor:** No, and it is important not to confuse them. A township, in South African usage, is a residential area that was built, usually by the government, for black African, coloured or Indian people during segregation and apartheid, often on the edge of a city. Examples include Soweto, Gugulethu, Umlazi, Mamelodi, Mitchells Plain and Chatsworth. Most townships are formal settlements, with surveyed plots, formal houses, roads, schools and services, although services were often poor under apartheid and many houses are small. Today, townships are home to people of all income levels, from poor households to middle-class families, and many have busy shopping centres, businesses and cultural life. An informal settlement is an area where people have built their own shelters, often from corrugated iron, wood and plastic, on land that they do not formally own or rent, usually without a proper layout, roads, water connections, toilets or electricity. Informal settlements grow when people move to cities faster than formal housing can be provided, and they can be found on the edges of townships, on open land within cities, along railway lines or on unsafe land such as flood plains. Some townships contain informal settlements, and some informal settlements have been upgraded into formal areas. So the two can overlap, but they are different types of residential area, and an exam answer should use the terms carefully.

### subtopic_4 — Density, Evidence and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why do wealthier areas have more parks?

**Tutor:** There are several reasons, some economic and some historical. Economically, high-income areas have low housing density, with large plots, so there is more private green space in gardens, and the city could set aside land for parks and golf courses because land was not being used intensively for housing. Wealthier residents also pay higher property rates and can pressure the municipality to maintain parks well, and private sports clubs and golf courses are often located nearby. Historically, apartheid planning gave white suburbs the best land and generous public facilities, including parks, swimming pools and sports grounds, while townships were planned with minimal recreation space, because the government did not see African residents as permanent members of the city. Townships were built as dense rows of small houses with few open spaces, and much open land that did exist was used as buffer zones rather than parks. Since 1994, municipalities have tried to build more parks, sports fields and community facilities in townships and informal settlements, but the gap remains large. This matters because recreation space affects health, safety, children's development and community life. Geographers measure this by calculating green space per person in different areas. When you write about recreation land, it is a strong point to note that its distribution reflects both density and history.
