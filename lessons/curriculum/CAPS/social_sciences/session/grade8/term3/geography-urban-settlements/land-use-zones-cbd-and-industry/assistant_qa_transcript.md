### subtopic_1 — Urban Settlements and Land Use
**Assistant:** Why is the CBD of Cape Town not in the middle of the city?

**Tutor:** Because the CBD grows at the most accessible point when a city develops, not at the geographical centre of the city's present area. Cape Town began in 1652 as a refreshment station for ships of the Dutch East India Company at Table Bay. The harbour was the reason for the settlement, so the earliest buildings, the fort, the Castle, the Company's Garden and the streets of the town, were built between the bay and the slopes of Table Mountain. Ships, goods and people arrived by sea, so the area beside the harbour was the most accessible place. When railways were built in the nineteenth century, the main station was placed close to the harbour and the town centre. The city then grew outwards in the only directions possible, since the sea and the mountain blocked it on two sides: along the coast and over the Cape Flats to the east and south. As a result, the CBD is at one edge of the modern metropolitan area, close to the harbour, not in the middle. Durban is similar: its CBD grew next to the bay and harbour. Johannesburg is different, because it grew around the gold mines on the Witwatersrand ridge, so its old CBD is just north of the mining belt. In an exam, if you are asked to explain the location of a CBD, link it to the original reason for the settlement and to the transport routes that made it accessible.

### subtopic_2 — The Central Business District
**Assistant:** Why don't more people live in the CBD if it is so convenient?

**Tutor:** Historically, several things worked against people living in the CBD. The first is land value. Land in the CBD is so expensive that offices and shops, which can earn high incomes from each square metre, were able to pay more for it than housing, so most CBD buildings were built as offices and shops. The second is the environment. CBDs can be noisy and congested, with little green space, few schools and few places for children to play, so many families preferred the suburbs. The third, in South Africa, is history. Under apartheid, the Group Areas Act reserved central city areas for white people, and many black people were forcibly removed from inner-city neighbourhoods such as District Six in Cape Town and Sophiatown in Johannesburg, so the inner city was kept mainly for business and for white residents, many of whom later moved to the suburbs. However, this is changing. From the 1990s, many people moved into inner-city flats in areas like Hillbrow and Berea in Johannesburg, because they were close to jobs and transport. Today, cities encourage more people to live in the CBD by converting old office buildings into flats, improving safety and public spaces, and supporting affordable rental housing. Living in the centre can reduce long commutes and the cost of transport, which is a big issue in South African cities. So the answer is that the CBD has had few residents for economic and historical reasons, but planners now see value in bringing people back.

### subtopic_3 — Light and Heavy Industry
**Assistant:** Why would anyone build homes next to an oil refinery?

**Tutor:** Usually, people did not freely choose to live there; the location was decided by planning and history. In south Durban, the area around the refineries and chemical plants was developed from the 1930s onwards as a major industrial zone, close to the harbour and the railway. Under apartheid, the Group Areas Act decided where people of each racial group could live. Communities classified as Indian and coloured, such as Merebank and Wentworth, and African townships such as Umlazi, were placed in south Durban, near the industry and its pollution, while white suburbs were mainly placed in less polluted areas. Workers also needed to live near their jobs, and housing near industry was cheap. Once communities had formed, with schools, churches, families and social networks, moving was very difficult. Today, residents' organisations, such as the South Durban Community Environmental Alliance, have campaigned for better air quality monitoring, stricter controls on emissions and better health care, pointing to high rates of asthma and other illnesses. This is an example of what is called environmental justice: the idea that poorer communities and communities affected by past discrimination should not carry an unfair share of pollution. When you study urban land use in South Africa, it is important to see that the pattern of zones was shaped not only by economic forces such as land value and accessibility, but also by political decisions about who should live where.

### subtopic_4 — Zones on Maps and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How can I tell light industry from heavy industry on a photograph?

**Tutor:** Look for clues about size, materials, transport and pollution. Light industry usually appears as rows of fairly small, low, rectangular buildings with flat or slightly sloping roofs, often similar in shape, arranged along tarred roads in an industrial park, with parking areas and loading bays for trucks and delivery vans. There are usually no tall chimneys, large storage tanks or heaps of raw materials, and the site is often close to residential areas or main roads. Heavy industry looks very different. The buildings are much larger and irregular in shape, and you may see tall chimneys, cooling towers, large round storage tanks for oil, gas or chemicals, conveyor belts, cranes, stockpiles of coal, ore or other raw materials, and smoke or steam. Railway lines and sidings often run right into the site, and the site may be next to a harbour or a river. The whole area is usually large, with open land around it, and further from houses. On a topographic map, similar clues appear as symbols and labels: power stations, reservoirs, pipelines, works or industrial area labels, and railway sidings. In an exam, give at least two clues from the photograph or map and then explain what they tell you; for example, the storage tanks and pipeline suggest an oil refinery, which is heavy industry because it processes bulky raw materials.
