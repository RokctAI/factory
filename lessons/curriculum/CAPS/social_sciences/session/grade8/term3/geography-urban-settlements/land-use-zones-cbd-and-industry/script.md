# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces urban settlements and the land use zones inside them. The anchor is the central business district, the CBD, at the heart of a city, and the industrial zones, light and heavy, that lie around it. The governing idea is that land in a city is used in different ways in different places, and that the pattern is explained by accessibility, land value and the needs of each activity: the most accessible and expensive land at the centre is used for offices and shops, while industry needs space, transport links and, for heavy industry, distance from homes. Identifying and explaining land use zones, especially the CBD and industrial zones, is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Urban Settlements and Land Use

Urban settlements are towns and cities. Compared with rural settlements, they are much larger, much denser, and most people work in secondary activities, such as manufacturing and construction, and tertiary activities, such as trade, transport, finance, government, education and health. Urban settlements provide high-order services for the people of the surrounding region: hospitals, universities, large shopping centres, banks, courts and government offices.

South Africa's largest urban areas include Johannesburg, Cape Town, eThekwini, which includes Durban, Ekurhuleni, Tshwane, which includes Pretoria, Nelson Mandela Bay, which includes Gqeberha, Buffalo City, which includes East London, and Mangaung, which includes Bloemfontein. Johannesburg, Ekurhuleni and Tshwane have grown together to form a huge urban region in Gauteng, which geographers call a conurbation.

Land use means the way land is used. In a city, land is used for many purposes: commerce, meaning shops and offices; industry; residence, meaning housing; transport, such as roads, railways, stations and airports; recreation, such as parks and sports fields; services, such as schools, hospitals and police stations; and open space. Areas where one main land use dominates are called land use zones. The main urban land use zones are the central business district, the industrial zones, the residential zones, and zones for recreation and services. Between zones there are often transition zones, where land use is mixed and changing.

Why do zones form? Different activities compete for land, and they need different things. Some activities need to be very accessible, where many people can easily reach them, such as offices and shops. Some need a lot of space, such as factories and warehouses. Some need to be near transport routes, such as railways, highways and harbours. Some need to be away from noise and pollution, such as homes. Land at the centre of a city, where many roads and railways meet, is the most accessible, and because many activities want it, it is also the most expensive. Activities that can pay high prices, such as banks and big shops, win the central land. Activities that need cheaper land and more space are pushed outwards.

Geographers have made models to show these patterns, for example a model of rings around the centre, or a model of sectors along transport routes. Real cities do not match any model exactly, because of physical features like mountains, rivers and the sea, and because of history. In South African cities, apartheid planning separated residential areas by race, which created a very particular pattern that you will study later this term.

## Subtopic: The Central Business District

The central business district, or CBD, is the commercial heart of a city. It is usually the oldest part of the city, often grown from the original settlement, and it lies where the main roads and railways meet. Because it is the most accessible place in the city, land there is the most expensive.

The CBD has several typical features. It has the tallest buildings, because land is so expensive that developers build upwards to use every square metre. It has offices of banks, insurance companies, law firms and big businesses, and government buildings, such as city halls, provincial legislatures and courts. It has shops, especially department stores and specialised shops selling high-order goods such as jewellery, clothing and electronics. It has the main transport terminals, such as railway stations, bus terminals and minibus taxi ranks. It has heavy traffic and many pedestrians during the day, but few people live there, so it can be quiet at night. Parking is scarce and expensive. There are few open spaces, though there may be a historic square or park.

Examples include the CBDs of Johannesburg, around Commissioner Street and the Carlton Centre; Cape Town, between the harbour and the Company's Garden; Durban, around West Street, now Dr Pixley kaSeme Street; and Pretoria, around Church Square.

CBDs change over time. In many South African cities, from the 1980s and 1990s, many businesses moved out of the old CBD to new office and shopping areas in the suburbs, such as Sandton in Johannesburg, Umhlanga in Durban, and Century City in Cape Town. Reasons included traffic congestion, lack of parking, crime and the cost and age of buildings in the old CBD, and the attraction of new, spacious, car-friendly developments near wealthier suburbs. These new centres are sometimes called decentralised business districts. The old CBDs did not die: they became centres for informal trade, public transport, government offices and housing, and in recent years many cities have invested in urban renewal, converting old office buildings into flats and improving public spaces, as in Maboneng and Braamfontein in Johannesburg.

Around the CBD there is often a zone in transition, an older area of mixed land use with small businesses, workshops, older housing and some decay, where land use is changing.

Informal trade is an important part of South African CBDs. Street traders sell food, clothing, phone accessories and many other goods on pavements and in markets near taxi ranks and stations, where thousands of commuters pass every day. Warwick Junction in Durban, next to the main taxi and train terminals, is one of the largest informal markets in the country. Informal trade provides income for many people, but cities must balance the needs of traders with pedestrian space, safety and hygiene.

## Subtopic: Light and Heavy Industry

Industry means the processing of raw materials or parts into finished products, and it is a secondary activity. Cities have two main kinds of industrial zone: light industry and heavy industry.

Light industry makes smaller, lighter products, uses relatively small amounts of raw materials and energy, and causes little pollution or noise. Examples include clothing, food processing, printing, furniture, electronics assembly, and workshops such as panel beaters and small engineering works. Light industry often uses skilled workers and needs good road access to deliver to shops and customers. Light industrial zones are often located near main roads and highways, near residential areas where workers live, and in the transition zone around the CBD or in planned industrial parks on the edge of suburbs. Their buildings are usually low, flat-roofed factories and warehouses, often with loading bays. Examples include Montague Gardens in Cape Town, Pinetown in eThekwini and many industrial parks in Midrand.

Heavy industry processes large amounts of bulky raw materials, uses a lot of energy and water, and produces heavy products. Examples include steelworks, oil refineries, chemical plants, cement factories, power stations and large engineering works. Heavy industry needs a lot of cheap, flat land; good railway, harbour or pipeline links to bring in raw materials and send out products; a large supply of water and power; and distance from residential areas, because it causes air, water and noise pollution. Heavy industrial zones are often on the edge of the city, along railways, beside harbours or downwind of the residential areas. Examples include the steel and engineering industries of Vanderbijlpark and Vereeniging, the oil refineries and chemical plants of south Durban, the industries around the port of Saldanha Bay, and the Sasol synthetic fuel plant at Secunda.

Industrial location also follows the rule of accessibility. Factories try to reduce the cost of moving raw materials in and products out. That is why heavy industry is often near ports, railways and raw materials, and why light industry is often near customers and workers.

Industry affects the people who live nearby. In south Durban, communities such as Merebank and Wentworth live very close to refineries and chemical plants. Residents have campaigned for years about air pollution and health problems such as asthma. This is partly a result of apartheid planning, which placed black, coloured and Indian residential areas next to heavy industry, while wealthier white suburbs were placed further away. Today, cities try to use buffer zones, such as parks, open land or highways, to separate heavy industry from homes.

The questions for this section are with you now: light and heavy industry, their location and their effects.

## Subtopic: Zones on Maps and the Error Museum

On a topographic map or a street map, you can identify land use zones from clues. The CBD has a dense grid of streets, many large buildings shown close together, a concentration of public buildings such as the post office, police station and town hall, and the meeting point of main roads and railways, often with a station. Industrial zones may be labelled as industrial areas, have large buildings with few streets between them, and be next to railway sidings, which are short branch lines leading into factory sites. Heavy industry may show symbols for power stations, chimneys, reservoirs, tanks or pipelines. Residential areas show many small buildings in regular rows or curving streets, with schools, churches and parks among them.

A calculation shows why the CBD builds upwards. Suppose, as an example for the method, a plot in the CBD covers one thousand two hundred square metres, and a ten-storey building is built covering the whole plot. Floor space is one thousand two hundred times ten, which is twelve thousand square metres. If a single-storey building were built on the same plot, it would have only one thousand two hundred square metres. Because the land is expensive, the developer can spread its cost over ten times as much floor space by building upwards. If the land cost thirty-six million rand, the land cost per square metre of floor space would be thirty-six million divided by twelve thousand, which is three thousand rand, compared with thirty thousand rand for a single storey. These are example figures, but the pattern is real.

When answering questions about land use, always link the land use to the reason for its location. For example, heavy industry is located on the edge of the city next to the railway and harbour because it needs space, transport for bulky raw materials and distance from homes.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying many people live in the CBD. Few people live there compared with the suburbs, though urban renewal is adding flats. Two: confusing light and heavy industry. Heavy industry processes bulky materials and pollutes; light industry makes smaller goods with little pollution. Three: saying the CBD is always in the geographical centre of the city. It is at the most accessible point, which may be near the coast or a harbour, as in Cape Town and Durban. Four: forgetting that apartheid planning influenced where industry and homes were placed. Five: describing a zone without giving evidence from the map.

Layout for marks in a land use question: identify the zone, give two clues from the map or photograph, and explain the location using accessibility, land value and needs.

The questions for this section are with you now: identifying zones on maps, the floor space calculation and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Different Jobs, Different Places

A town or city is a big settlement where most people work in shops, offices, factories, schools and hospitals, not on farms.

Different parts of the city are used for different things. Some parts are for offices and shops. Some for factories. Some for houses. Some for parks. Each part is called a land use zone.

The middle of the city is easy for everyone to reach, so lots of businesses want to be there, and the land costs the most.

Think of the city as a dartboard: the bullseye costs the most.

## Subtopic: The CBD

The CBD, the central business district, is the busy centre of the city. It has tall office buildings, banks, big shops, government buildings and the main taxi ranks, bus stops and train station.

Buildings are tall because the land is so expensive. Lots of people work there in the day, but not many live there, so it can be quiet at night.

Some businesses moved out to newer places like Sandton because of traffic and crime, but many cities are fixing up their old centres.

Picture the skyline: the tallest buildings bunched in the middle.

Keep that skyline in mind and try the questions for this part: the CBD.

## Subtopic: Factories Big and Small

Light industry makes small things like clothes, food and furniture. It is quite clean and quiet, so it can be near houses and main roads.

Heavy industry makes big things like steel and fuel. It needs lots of land, railways or a harbour, and lots of water and power. It is noisy and dirty, so it should be far from homes.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Urban settlements are large and most people work in secondary and tertiary activities. Land in cities is divided into land use zones. The CBD is the most accessible and expensive area, with tall buildings, offices, shops and transport terminals. Light industry makes small goods with little pollution and is located near roads and workers. Heavy industry processes bulky materials, needs space, transport, water and power, and is located on the edge of cities, away from homes. Location is explained by accessibility, land value and the needs of each activity.

Remember the three words that explain every zone: accessibility, land value and needs.
