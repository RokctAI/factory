# Part 1 — Expert

This session continues the study of urban land use zones, focusing on residential areas and the shopping, service and recreation areas that serve them. The anchor is the contrast between high-, middle- and low-income residential areas in South African cities. The governing idea is that residential areas differ in housing density, plot size, building quality, services and location, and that in South Africa these differences were shaped both by income and by apartheid planning, which separated people by race and placed many poorer communities far from jobs and services. Describing and explaining residential areas, shopping centres, services and recreation is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: High-, Middle- and Low-Income Residential Areas

Residential land, used for housing, covers the largest area of most cities. Geographers classify residential areas by the income of the people who live there, because income affects the kind of housing people can afford.

High-income residential areas have large houses on big plots, often a thousand square metres or more, with gardens, swimming pools, garages and, in many South African suburbs, high walls, electric fences and security gates. Housing density, meaning the number of houses on each hectare of land, is low. Streets are often curved, quiet and lined with trees, and there are private schools, parks and golf courses nearby. Many high-income areas are on the edges of cities or on high ground with good views, away from industry and pollution, and residents usually travel by private car. Security estates, gated communities with their own walls and guards, have become common. Examples include parts of Sandton and Waterkloof, and Constantia in Cape Town.

Middle-income residential areas have medium-sized houses on smaller plots, townhouse complexes and blocks of flats. Density is higher than in high-income areas. Streets are often in a regular grid, with schools, clinics, corner shops, small shopping centres and sports fields within reach. Many people own a car, but many also use public transport.

Low-income residential areas have the highest density. They include townships built by the government in the apartheid era, such as Soweto, Umlazi, Mdantsane and Khayelitsha; hostels originally built for migrant workers; government-subsidised houses built since 1994, often called RDP houses, with a minimum floor area of forty square metres; backyard rooms and shacks; and informal settlements, where people build their own shelters from corrugated iron, wood and plastic on land without formal services. Plots are small, houses are close together and many households share taps and toilets. These areas are often far from the CBD and the main areas of employment, so residents spend a lot of time and money travelling to work, mainly by minibus taxi, bus and train.

Residential areas change over time. Some suburbs become denser as large plots are subdivided and townhouses are built. Some townships have developed busy shopping centres, restaurants and middle-income housing. Informal settlements may be upgraded with roads, water, electricity and formal houses.

Backyard housing is an important and often overlooked part of low-income areas. In many townships, homeowners build rooms or shacks in their back yards and rent them out. This gives tenants a place to live close to transport and services, and gives owners an income, but it also increases density and puts pressure on water, sanitation and electricity that were designed for far fewer people. Hostels, built under apartheid for single male migrant workers, have in many cases been converted into family units, though many remain overcrowded.

## Subtopic: Apartheid Planning and the Unequal City

The pattern of residential areas in South African cities is not explained by income alone. It was deliberately shaped by segregation and apartheid.

Segregation of housing began before apartheid. From the early twentieth century, laws such as the Natives (Urban Areas) Act of 1923 controlled where African people could live in towns, usually in separate locations on the edge of the town. After 1948, the apartheid government made segregation stricter and more systematic. The Group Areas Act of 1950 divided every city into separate areas for each racial group: white, coloured, Indian and African. People who lived in an area declared for another group were forcibly removed. About three and a half million people were moved in forced removals of all kinds between 1960 and 1983. Well-known examples include District Six in Cape Town, Sophiatown in Johannesburg and Cato Manor in Durban.

The result was what geographers call the apartheid city. White suburbs were placed close to the CBD and the best land, with good services. Coloured and Indian areas were placed further out. African townships were placed furthest out, often beyond the industrial areas, and separated from other areas by buffer zones of open land, railways, highways or industry. Townships were built with few shops, few recreation facilities and limited services, because the government saw African people as temporary residents of the cities. Pass laws controlled who could live in the cities at all.

After 1994, the Group Areas Act and the pass laws were gone, and people could legally live anywhere. Many middle-class black families moved into formerly white suburbs, and some new developments have been built on land closer to the centre. But the pattern has been slow to change. Land and houses in well-located areas are expensive, so most poor households still live on the edges of the cities. Many new government housing projects were also built on cheap land far from jobs, which repeated the old pattern. Today, cities try to promote integration through mixed-income housing developments close to transport routes and jobs, such as Cosmo City north of Johannesburg, and by upgrading townships and informal settlements in place.

Understanding this history is essential for explaining why South African cities are among the most unequal in the world, with very rich and very poor areas only a few kilometres apart.

Geographers describe the remaining pattern using the idea of spatial inequality: differences in wealth, services and opportunities between places. In South African cities, spatial inequality means that where a child grows up still strongly affects the quality of schools, clinics and parks they can reach, the safety of their streets, and the time and money their family spends travelling to work. Reducing spatial inequality is one of the main aims of city planning today.

## Subtopic: Shopping Centres, Services and Recreation

Residential areas need shops, services and places for recreation, and these are arranged in a hierarchy, like settlements.

Shopping areas range from small to large. At the bottom are corner shops, spaza shops and street traders, selling low-order goods such as bread, milk, airtime and paraffin, which people buy often and want close to home. Neighbourhood shopping centres have a supermarket, a pharmacy and a few small shops, and serve the surrounding suburbs. Regional shopping centres, often called malls, are very large, with department stores, many specialised shops, cinemas, restaurants and huge car parks; they sell high-order goods, such as furniture, clothing and electronics, and draw customers from across the city. They are located where many people can reach them, at the junctions of highways and main roads. Examples include Canal Walk in Century City, Gateway in Umhlanga and the Mall of Africa in Midrand. Since the 1990s, large shopping centres have also been built in townships, such as Maponya Mall in Soweto, which opened in 2007, so that residents no longer have to travel to the city or the suburbs for many goods.

Services include education, health, safety and government services. Every residential area needs primary schools, clinics and police stations within reach. Higher-order services, such as high schools, hospitals, magistrates' courts and home affairs offices, serve larger areas, and the highest-order services, such as universities and specialist hospitals, serve the whole city or province. Chris Hani Baragwanath Hospital in Soweto is one of the largest hospitals in Africa. Under apartheid, townships were deliberately under-served, and many still have fewer and more crowded services than wealthier areas.

Recreation land is used for leisure. It includes parks and playgrounds, sports fields, stadiums, golf courses, swimming pools, community halls, beaches and nature reserves. Some recreation areas are large and serve the whole city, such as FNB Stadium in Johannesburg, the Durban beachfront, and Table Mountain National Park in Cape Town. Others are small and local, such as a neighbourhood park or soccer field. Recreation areas are often located on land that is not suitable for building, such as river banks that flood, steep slopes and wetlands. Wealthier areas usually have much more green space per person than poorer, crowded areas.

Informal businesses are also an important part of shopping in residential areas. Spaza shops, hair salons, car washes, shebeens, tailors and food stalls run from homes and street corners provide goods, services and jobs close to where people live, especially in townships, where formal shops were few for many decades.

## Subtopic: Density, Evidence and the Error Museum

Housing density helps to compare residential areas. A hectare is ten thousand square metres. Suppose, as an example for the method, that in a high-income suburb each plot is one thousand square metres. Ten thousand divided by one thousand is ten, so there are about ten houses per hectare, before allowing for roads. In a low-income area with plots of two hundred and fifty square metres, ten thousand divided by two hundred and fifty is forty, so there are about forty houses per hectare, four times as many. In an informal settlement, there may be many more shelters per hectare, packed together without formal plots. These example figures show how density increases as income decreases.

On a map or aerial photograph, you can identify residential areas by clues. High-income areas show large plots, big houses, swimming pools, curving streets, cul-de-sacs, and lots of trees and green space. Middle-income areas show medium plots in regular streets, townhouse complexes and blocks of flats. Low-income formal areas show many small houses in long, straight rows on small plots. Informal settlements show very dense, irregular clusters of small structures with narrow, unplanned paths and few roads.

When explaining residential patterns in South Africa, always consider both economic and historical factors. For example, a township on the edge of the city is low-income partly because of the income of its residents, and partly because apartheid planning placed it far from jobs and services.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying high-income areas have high density. They have low density, with large plots. Two: explaining South African residential patterns using income only. Apartheid laws such as the Group Areas Act were a major cause. Three: saying all townships are informal settlements. Most townships have formal houses with services; informal settlements are different. Four: confusing low-order and high-order services. Five: forgetting that patterns are changing since 1994, with movement into former white suburbs and growth of township businesses.

Layout for marks in a residential area question: identify the type of area, give two pieces of evidence from the map or photograph, explain its location using income, land value and history, and suggest one improvement.

## Subtopic: Where People Live

In a city, most of the land is used for homes.

In rich areas, houses are big, with big gardens and pools, and they are far apart. In middle-income areas, houses are smaller and closer together, with flats and townhouses. In poorer areas, houses are small and close together, and some people live in shacks in informal settlements, sharing taps and toilets.

Poorer areas are often far from the city centre and the jobs, so people spend a lot on taxis and buses.

Picture the city from above: big green plots in some places, tightly packed roofs in others.

Some families also rent a room in someone's back yard. This is called backyard housing.

Keep that picture in mind and try the questions for this part: residential areas.

## Subtopic: Why It Is So Unequal

Under apartheid, a law called the Group Areas Act decided where people could live, according to race. Many families were forced out of their homes, like the people of District Six in Cape Town and Sophiatown in Johannesburg.

White suburbs got the best land, near the centre. African townships were put far away, with few shops, parks or clinics.

Since 1994, anyone can live anywhere, but the old pattern is still there, because good land is expensive.

Remember: income and history together explain where people live.

Keep that in mind and try the questions for this part: the unequal city.

## Subtopic: Shops, Services and Fun

Every area needs shops, schools, clinics and places to play. Small shops like spaza shops sell everyday things close to home. Big malls sell things you buy less often, like clothes and furniture, and are near highways. Schools and clinics should be close to everyone; universities and big hospitals serve the whole city.

Parks, sports fields, stadiums and beaches are for recreation. They are often on land where you cannot build, like river banks that flood.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Residential areas take up the most land in a city. High-income areas have low density and large plots; middle-income areas have medium density; low-income areas, including townships and informal settlements, have high density and are often far from jobs. Apartheid laws such as the Group Areas Act shaped this pattern, which is slowly changing. Shopping centres, services and recreation areas form a hierarchy from local to city-wide.
