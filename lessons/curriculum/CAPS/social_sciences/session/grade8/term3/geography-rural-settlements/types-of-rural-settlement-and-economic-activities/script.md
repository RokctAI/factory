# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces settlement geography, beginning with rural settlements. The anchor is the settlement hierarchy at the rural end: the isolated farmstead, the hamlet and the village, each with its own size, pattern and services. The governing idea is that rural settlements are small, are supported mainly by primary economic activities that use natural resources directly, and are located where those resources, water and suitable land are found. Identifying types of rural settlement, describing their patterns and functions, and linking them to farming, mining, forestry and fishing are examinable this term, and these ideas run through the term project.

## Subtopic: What a Settlement Is

A settlement is any place where people live, from a single house to a huge city. Geographers describe settlements by their size, by their function, meaning the main activities that take place there, and by their shape or pattern. Settlements are divided broadly into rural settlements and urban settlements.

Rural settlements are found in the countryside. They are small, with relatively few people, and most people work in primary activities. Primary activities take resources directly from nature: farming, mining, forestry and fishing. Rural settlements usually have few services, so people travel to towns for hospitals, banks, larger shops and secondary schools. Urban settlements, towns and cities, are much larger and denser, and most people work in secondary activities, such as manufacturing, or tertiary activities, such as trade, transport, education and health.

When people first chose where to build a settlement, they looked at its site, the actual land on which it is built. Important site factors included a reliable water supply, such as a river, spring or well; flat or gently sloping land for building and farming; fertile soil; protection from flooding, so many settlements were built on slightly higher ground near, but not on, a river; shelter from strong winds; safety from attack, for example on a hill; and nearby building materials, such as wood, stone, clay or grass for thatch. Geographers also describe a settlement's situation, its position in relation to the surrounding area: roads, rivers, other settlements and resources.

Rural settlements have two main patterns. A dispersed pattern has buildings spread out, far from each other, as on large commercial farms in the Karoo or the Free State, where each farmhouse stands alone among its fields or grazing land. A nucleated pattern has buildings grouped closely together around a centre, such as a church, a crossroads, a water source or a harbour. Some settlements are linear, stretched out along a road, a river or a coastline.

In South Africa, the pattern of rural settlement was strongly shaped by history. Commercial farms owned mainly by white farmers produced a dispersed pattern of large farms. In the former reserves and homelands, where African people were confined by laws such as the Land Act of 1913, rural villages were often dense and crowded, and many were replanned by the government into lines of houses along roads.

Settlements also change over time. A farmstead may grow into a hamlet as workers' houses are added; a hamlet may grow into a village when a school or a shop opens; and a village at a crossroads or near a mine may grow into a town. Other settlements shrink when young people move to cities for work, leaving mainly older people and children behind. This movement from rural to urban areas is one of the most important changes in South Africa, and you will study it later this term.

## Subtopic: Farmsteads, Hamlets and Villages

Rural settlements are arranged in a settlement hierarchy, a ranking of settlements by size and the number of services they offer. At the bottom is the isolated farmstead, then the hamlet, then the village. Above them are urban settlements: towns, cities and conurbations.

An isolated farmstead is a single farmhouse with its outbuildings, such as sheds, kraals, stores and workers' houses, standing alone on a farm. It has no services for the public. The people who live there are the farmer's family and, often, farmworkers and their families. On large commercial farms in the Karoo, the Northern Cape or the Free State, the nearest neighbour may be many kilometres away, and the family may travel long distances to a town for shopping, school, church and medical care. Isolated farmsteads produce a dispersed settlement pattern.

A hamlet is a small group of houses, perhaps a few to around twenty households, with very few or no services. There may be a small spaza shop or a communal water tap, but usually no school or clinic. In many rural parts of KwaZulu-Natal and the Eastern Cape, small clusters of homesteads belonging to related families form hamlets on hillsides, with fields and grazing land around them.

A village is larger, with perhaps a few hundred to a few thousand people, and it offers some basic services: a primary school, a small shop or general dealer, a church, perhaps a clinic, a post office or a police station. Villages are often nucleated, built around a central point. Examples include fishing villages on the West Coast, such as Paternoster, and the rural villages of Limpopo, Mpumalanga and the Eastern Cape. Some villages began as mission stations, such as Genadendal in the Western Cape, founded in 1738, the oldest mission station in South Africa.

As you move up the hierarchy, the number of settlements decreases, the population of each settlement increases, the number and range of services increases, and the distance between settlements of the same rank increases. There are thousands of farmsteads, many hamlets, fewer villages, fewer towns and only a few large cities. Services follow the same rule. A low-order service, such as a small shop selling bread and milk, is needed often and found even in villages. A high-order service, such as a specialist hospital or a university, is needed less often and is found only in large towns and cities.

## Subtopic: Rural Economic Activities

Rural settlements exist because of the resources around them. The four main rural economic activities in South Africa are farming, mining, forestry and fishing.

Farming is the most widespread. Commercial farming produces crops and livestock to sell for profit, often on large farms using machinery, irrigation and paid workers. Examples include maize in the Free State and North West, sugar cane in KwaZulu-Natal, fruit and wine in the Western Cape, citrus in Limpopo and Mpumalanga, and sheep and cattle in the drier Karoo and Northern Cape. Subsistence farming produces food mainly for the farmer's own household, with any small surplus sold locally. It is common in the former homelands, where families grow maize, beans and vegetables and keep cattle, goats and chickens. Farming settlements may be isolated farmsteads on commercial farms, or villages and hamlets in subsistence areas.

Mining creates settlements where minerals are found. Mining villages and towns grew up near coal mines in Mpumalanga, platinum mines in North West and Limpopo, iron ore mines near Kathu and Sishen in the Northern Cape, and diamond mines on the West Coast and in the Northern Cape. Mining settlements are often planned by mining companies, with rows of similar houses, hostels, a clinic and a shop. Because minerals run out, some mining settlements decline or become ghost towns when the mine closes, while others grow into towns.

Forestry settlements are found near commercial plantations of pine and eucalyptus trees in the wetter, cooler parts of Mpumalanga, KwaZulu-Natal and the Eastern Cape, and near the indigenous forests around Knysna. Forestry villages house workers who plant, tend and fell the trees, and sawmills often provide jobs nearby.

Fishing villages are located on the coast, near safe harbours or sheltered bays. On the West Coast, the cold, nutrient-rich Benguela Current supports large fish stocks, and villages such as Paternoster, Lamberts Bay and Port Nolloth grew up around fishing for rock lobster, snoek and pilchards. Fishing communities also live along the False Bay coast, at places such as Kalk Bay, and many coastal communities depend on small-scale fishing.

Many rural settlements now also depend on tertiary activities such as tourism, for example game lodges, wine routes and coastal holiday villages, and on government grants and money sent home by family members working in cities.

Rural livelihoods are often fragile. Droughts, such as the severe drought in the Western Cape and Eastern Cape between 2015 and 2018, can destroy harvests and kill livestock. Falling prices for crops or minerals can close farms and mines. Fishing quotas and declining fish stocks can reduce the catch. Because rural settlements depend on a narrow range of activities, a single shock can affect the whole community, which is one reason why many people move to towns.

The questions for this section are with you now: farming, mining, forestry and fishing as rural economic activities.

## Subtopic: Investigating a Settlement and the Error Museum

Later this term you will hand in a project on settlement. A good settlement investigation follows the steps that geographers use. First, choose a settlement you can study, such as your own village, suburb, town or a settlement shown on a map. Second, find its location on a map and describe its site and situation: the land it is built on, the water supply, the slope, the roads and the nearby settlements. Third, identify its type and its position in the settlement hierarchy. Fourth, describe its pattern, dispersed, nucleated or linear, using a sketch map with a title, a key, a north arrow and an approximate scale. Fifth, identify its main economic activities and services. Sixth, explain the links between its location, its economic activities and its services. Finally, comment on problems and possible improvements. Keep your notes, sketches and sources in one place as you work.

A calculation helps to compare settlements. Population density is the number of people divided by the area they occupy. Suppose, as an example for the method, a village has one thousand two hundred people living on an area of three square kilometres. One thousand two hundred divided by three is four hundred, so its density is four hundred people per square kilometre. Suppose a farming district with forty farmsteads and about two hundred people covers four hundred square kilometres. Two hundred divided by four hundred is zero point five, or one person for every two square kilometres. The village is eight hundred times as dense as the farming district, because four hundred divided by zero point five is eight hundred. These are example figures, but they show why villages are nucleated and farming districts are dispersed.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: calling any small place a village. A hamlet has few or no services; a village has basic services. Two: confusing site and situation. Site is the land the settlement is built on; situation is its position relative to its surroundings. Three: saying that all rural people are farmers. Rural settlements also depend on mining, forestry, fishing, tourism and grants. Four: describing a pattern without evidence from the map. Five: forgetting the effect of history, such as the Land Act and the homelands, on rural settlement in South Africa.

Layout for marks in a settlement question: name the settlement type, give two pieces of map evidence, describe the pattern and explain it using site factors and economic activities.

The questions for this section are with you now: planning a settlement investigation, the density calculation and the error museum.

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: what rural settlements are, the three kinds, and why people live there.

## Subtopic: Living in the Countryside

A settlement is a place where people live. Rural settlements are in the countryside. They are small, and most people there work with the land or the sea: farming, mining, forestry or fishing.

People chose places with water, flat land, good soil and safety from floods.

On big farms, houses are far apart. That pattern is called dispersed. In villages, houses are close together around a centre. That is called nucleated.

Picture it from above: dots spread far apart on farms, dots bunched together in a village.

Some villages have houses in straight lines along a road. That pattern is called linear. It often follows a road, a river or the coast.

Keep those two pictures in mind and try the questions for this part: what a rural settlement is.

## Subtopic: Farm, Hamlet, Village

There are three kinds of rural settlement, from smallest to biggest.

An isolated farmstead is one farmhouse with its sheds, on its own.

A hamlet is a small group of houses, with almost no services.

A village is bigger and has some services: a school, a shop, a church, maybe a clinic.

Bigger places have more services. For a big hospital or a university, you must travel to a city.

Count them on your fingers: farmstead, hamlet, village, then town and city.

Keep that ladder in mind and try the questions for this part: types of rural settlement.

## Subtopic: Why People Live There

People live in rural settlements because of the work there. Farmers grow maize, fruit and sugar, or keep cattle and sheep. Miners dig coal, platinum and iron ore. Forestry workers plant and cut pine and gum trees. Fishers catch rock lobster and snoek on the West Coast.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Rural settlements are small and depend mainly on primary activities: farming, mining, forestry and fishing. Their location depends on water, land and resources. The isolated farmstead, hamlet and village form the rural end of the settlement hierarchy, with more services as settlements get bigger. Patterns can be dispersed, nucleated or linear. History, such as the Land Act and the homelands, shaped South Africa's rural settlements.
