### subtopic_1 — What a Settlement Is
**Assistant:** Why do some rural villages in South Africa have houses in straight lines along roads?

**Tutor:** That pattern is a result of history, specifically of government planning in the former reserves and homelands. Before the twentieth century, African rural settlements in many areas were made up of scattered homesteads, each with its own fields and grazing land, often on ridges or hillsides near water. From the late 1930s, and especially from the 1950s and 1960s, the government carried out what it called betterment planning in the reserves. Officials argued that the land was being overused and eroded, which was partly true because the reserves were far too small for the number of people forced to live in them by laws such as the Land Act of 1913. Their solution was to divide the land into separate zones: residential areas, fields and grazing land. People were moved from their scattered homesteads into new villages, where houses were laid out in rows along roads, sometimes in a grid. Many people resisted betterment because they lost land, their homes were moved and their way of life was disrupted, and there were uprisings against it in places such as Sekhukhuneland, Pondoland and Zeerust in the 1950s. So when you see a rural village with houses neatly lined up along roads on a map or aerial photograph, you are looking at the result of a planning policy, not only at the natural choices of the people who live there. It is a good example of how history shapes settlement patterns.

### subtopic_2 — Farmsteads, Hamlets and Villages
**Assistant:** Is a village with a clinic and a police station still rural?

**Tutor:** Yes, in most cases it is still rural, because geographers classify settlements by several features together, not by one service alone. The main questions are: How many people live there? What do most people do for a living? How dense is the settlement? What range of services does it offer? A village with a clinic, a primary school, a police station and a few shops is still a small settlement, and many of its people may still farm, fish, work in nearby mines or forests, or travel to towns for work. It offers low-order services, the ones people need often, but not high-order services like a hospital with specialists, a shopping mall, a university or a large bank branch. That places it near the top of the rural part of the settlement hierarchy, not in the urban part. As a village grows, it may gain more services and more people may work in trade, transport and offices. At some point it becomes a small town, and the line between a large village and a small town is not always sharp. Different countries and even different government departments use different definitions. In an exam, it is safest to explain your answer using evidence: the size of the settlement, its main economic activities and the order of its services. That shows that you understand how the classification works rather than just applying a label.

### subtopic_3 — Rural Economic Activities
**Assistant:** Why are many large farms in the Karoo so far apart?

**Tutor:** Because the land in the Karoo can only support a small number of animals per hectare, so each farm needs a very large area to make a living. The Karoo is a semi-desert. Rainfall is low, often below about two hundred and fifty millimetres a year in large parts of it, and unreliable, and the vegetation is mainly small shrubs and grasses. A sheep may need several hectares of grazing to find enough food over a year, so a farmer who wants to keep a flock of a few thousand sheep needs thousands of hectares. Crops can only be grown where there is irrigation from a river, a dam or boreholes. This means that each farmstead stands in the middle of a very large farm, and the next farmstead is many kilometres away. The result is a dispersed settlement pattern with very low population density. Compare this with the wetter parts of KwaZulu-Natal or the Western Cape, where rainfall is higher and soils are more fertile, so a farm can be smaller and still productive, and farms and settlements are closer together. History also plays a part: farms in the Karoo were granted in large blocks in the colonial period. So the spacing of farmsteads on a map is a clue to the climate, the vegetation and the type of farming in the area, which is a link examiners like you to make.

### subtopic_4 — Investigating a Settlement and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do I choose a good settlement for my project?

**Tutor:** Choose a settlement that you can find information about and that lets you show all the skills the project asks for. The best choice is often a settlement you know personally, such as your own village, suburb or town, because you can observe it directly, take notes and perhaps photographs where it is safe and allowed, and ask family members or neighbours about its history. If that is not possible, choose a settlement that appears clearly on a topographic map or aerial photograph available to you, or one for which there is good information in books or reliable websites. Make sure that you can answer the key questions: where it is located; what its site and situation are; what type of settlement it is; what pattern it has; what economic activities support it; what services it has; and what problems it faces. Before you start, write down your questions, so that your investigation has a clear focus. Keep a record of every source you use, including maps, interviews and websites, because you will need to acknowledge them. Draw your sketch map early and improve it as you learn more. Finally, remember that the strongest projects explain links rather than only listing facts: for example, the village is on the coast because of the harbour, so fishing is the main activity, so the services include a fish factory and a small boat repair yard.
