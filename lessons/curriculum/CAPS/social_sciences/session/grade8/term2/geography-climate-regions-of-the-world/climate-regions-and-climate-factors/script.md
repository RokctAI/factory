# Part 1 — Expert

This session links the climate regions of the world to the factors that influence temperature and rainfall. The anchor is a single question asked of every region: which factors explain its temperatures, and which factors explain its rainfall? The governing idea is that a climate region is not a random patch of colour but the combined result of latitude, the global pressure belts and winds, distance from the sea, ocean currents, altitude and relief. Usually one or two factors dominate, and the skill being examined is to name the dominant factors and explain how each one works. This brings together everything from the term's Geography: the factors, the kinds of climate, the climate graphs and the climate map.

## Subtopic: The Factors as a Toolkit

Begin by gathering the factors into a toolkit, with a short note on how each one works.

Latitude controls how much energy a place receives from the sun. Near the equator, the midday sun is high in the sky all year, so its energy is concentrated on a small area and temperatures are high. Towards the poles, the sun is lower in the sky, its energy is spread over a larger area and passes through more atmosphere, so temperatures are lower. Latitude also controls the length of daylight and the size of the seasonal change. You saw this when you compared Mbombela and George: Mbombela, further north, is warmer on average.

Latitude also places each region within the global pressure belts. Near the equator, rising warm air creates low pressure and heavy convectional rain. At about twenty to thirty-five degrees north and south, sinking air creates the subtropical high-pressure belts, with dry, clear conditions. In the middle latitudes, around forty to sixty degrees, westerly winds and cold fronts bring rain, especially in winter. Near the poles, cold sinking air creates high pressure and very little precipitation. These belts move a little towards the north in the southern winter and towards the south in the southern summer, following the overhead sun.

Distance from the sea controls the temperature range and often the rainfall. Water heats and cools slowly, so places near the sea have mild summers and mild winters. Places far inland heat and cool quickly, so they have hot summers, cold winters and a large range. Inland places also tend to be drier, because moist air from the sea has lost much of its moisture before it arrives. You saw this when you compared Johannesburg and Cape Town.

Altitude lowers temperature. On average, the temperature falls by about six and a half degrees Celsius for every thousand metres of height. Johannesburg, at about one thousand seven hundred and fifty metres, is noticeably cooler than a coastal town at the same latitude.

Ocean currents warm or cool the coasts they flow past. Warm currents, such as the Agulhas Current off South Africa's east coast, warm the air and supply moisture, so the coast is humid and rainy. Cold currents, such as the Benguela Current off the west coast, cool the air from below and make it stable, so fog is common but rain is rare. You saw this when you compared Durban and Port Nolloth.

Mountains, or relief, force moist air to rise on the windward side, where it cools and forms clouds and rain, called relief or orographic rain. On the leeward side, the air descends, warms and dries, creating a rain shadow. You saw this when you compared Umtata, today called Mthatha, inland of the escarpment, with Durban on the coast.

Think of these factors as tools. Every climate region can be explained by picking up the right tools.

The questions for this section are with you now: the six factors and how each one works.

## Subtopic: Explaining the Hot Regions

Take the tropical rainforest first. Its temperatures are high all year, around twenty-five to twenty-eight degrees, with a very small annual range, because it lies near the equator, where the midday sun is always high and day length hardly changes. Its rainfall is heavy, often more than two thousand millimetres, and falls in every month, because the equatorial low-pressure belt sits over it for most of the year: warm, moist air rises, cools and produces convectional thunderstorms, often in the afternoon. The dominant factors are latitude and the equatorial low pressure.

The tropical savanna lies further from the equator, at about five to twenty degrees. Temperatures are still high, but the range is a little larger. The key to the savanna is the movement of the pressure belts with the seasons. In summer, the rainy low-pressure belt moves over the savanna, bringing a wet season. In winter, the belt moves away towards the other hemisphere, and the subtropical high pressure brings a long dry season. That is why the Kruger National Park and much of Limpopo have summer rain and dry winters. The dominant factors are latitude and the seasonal shift of the pressure belts.

The hot deserts lie mainly between about fifteen and thirty-five degrees. Their dryness is explained by the subtropical high-pressure belts, where sinking air warms and dries, so clouds rarely form. Their very high daytime temperatures come from clear skies and strong sunshine, and their cold nights from rapid heat loss through the same clear skies, giving a large daily range. On the western sides of continents, cold currents make the deserts even drier and extend them right to the coast, as in the Namib and the Atacama. In the interior, distance from the sea adds to the dryness, as in the Kalahari. In some places, mountains create rain shadows that extend deserts. The dominant factors are the subtropical high pressure, cold currents and distance from the sea.

The semi-deserts, such as the Karoo, lie on the edges of the deserts. They receive a little more rain, between about two hundred and fifty and five hundred millimetres, because rain-bearing systems sometimes reach them. The Great Karoo is also in the rain shadow of the mountains of the Cape Fold Belt, and it is far from the moist Indian Ocean air that brings rain to the east.

The questions for this section are with you now: explaining the tropical rainforest, the savanna, and the hot deserts and semi-deserts.

## Subtopic: Explaining the Middle-Latitude, Cold and Mountain Regions

The Mediterranean climate, found around Cape Town, has hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters. Both seasons are explained by the movement of the pressure belts. In summer, the subtropical high pressure moves south over the south-western Cape, bringing dry, sunny weather and the south-easterly wind known as the Cape Doctor. In winter, the high pressure moves north, and the westerly winds and cold fronts from the Southern Ocean reach the Cape, bringing rain. The mountains of the Cape Fold Belt add relief rain, so the slopes above Cape Town receive much more rain than the Cape Flats. The sea keeps winters mild. The dominant factors are latitude, the seasonal shift of the pressure belts and the westerlies.

The humid subtropical climate, found on the KwaZulu-Natal coast, has hot, humid, wet summers and mild, drier winters. Its warmth and humidity are explained by the warm Agulhas Current and moist onshore winds from the Indian Ocean, which bring summer rain, including thunderstorms. The escarpment behind the coast lifts the moist air and adds relief rain. The sea keeps winters mild and frost-free on the coast.

The temperate oceanic climate of north-western Europe, around London or Dublin, has cool summers, mild winters and rain all year. It lies in the belt of westerly winds, which blow off the Atlantic Ocean, bringing a constant supply of moist air and depressions. The warm North Atlantic Drift, an extension of the Gulf Stream, keeps winters much milder than the latitude alone would suggest.

The continental climate of the interiors of North America and Eurasia, around Moscow or Winnipeg, has warm summers, very cold winters and a large annual range. The dominant factor is distance from the sea: these places are thousands of kilometres from the moderating influence of the ocean, so the land heats and cools strongly. Latitude adds long, dark winters. Rainfall is moderate and falls mainly in summer.

The tundra and polar climates are explained mainly by latitude. Near and beyond the polar circles, the sun is low in the sky or below the horizon for long periods, so the energy received is small, and the long polar night lets the surface cool intensely. Cold, sinking air creates high pressure and very little precipitation, so the polar regions are cold deserts. Ice and snow reflect most of the sunlight that does arrive, keeping them cold.

High mountain climates are explained by altitude. Temperatures fall with height, so climate changes up the slopes in bands, from warm at the base to cold, and sometimes snowy, at the top. Mountains also create relief rain on the windward side and rain shadows on the leeward side. The Drakensberg and the Lesotho highlands, above about three thousand metres in places, receive snow in winter, and Kilimanjaro, at about five thousand eight hundred and ninety-five metres and only three degrees south of the equator, still has small glaciers on its summit, although they are shrinking fast.

The questions for this section are with you now: explaining the Mediterranean, humid subtropical, temperate oceanic, continental, polar and high mountain climates.

## Subtopic: Linked Explanations and the Error Museum

Examiners reward explanations that link a factor to its effect through a process. A weak answer says: Cape Town has winter rain because of the westerlies. A strong answer says: Cape Town has winter rain because, in winter, the subtropical high-pressure belt moves north, allowing the westerly winds and cold fronts from the Southern Ocean to reach the south-western Cape, where moist air is forced to rise, cools and condenses to form rain. Notice the chain: factor, process, effect.

Here is a worked example using the lapse rate. Johannesburg lies at about one thousand seven hundred and fifty metres. Using a fall of six and a half degrees per thousand metres, the expected cooling compared with sea level is one point seven five times six point five, which is about eleven point four degrees. That is why the highveld is much cooler than its latitude alone would suggest, and why it has frost on winter nights.

A second example compares rainfall. Durban receives about one thousand and ten millimetres of rain a year, while Port Nolloth, on the west coast, receives about sixty millimetres. Durban's rainfall is roughly seventeen times Port Nolloth's, because one thousand and ten divided by sixty is about sixteen point eight. The factors that explain the difference are the warm Agulhas Current and moist easterly winds on the east coast, and the cold Benguela Current and subtropical high pressure on the west coast.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the equator is hot because it is closer to the sun. It is hot because the sun's rays strike it at a high angle, concentrating energy. Two: saying cold currents bring rain. Cold currents cool and stabilise the air and bring fog, not rain. Three: forgetting that the pressure belts move with the seasons, which is the key to both the savanna and the Mediterranean climates. Four: mixing up windward and leeward: rain falls on the windward side, the rain shadow is on the leeward side. Five: naming a factor without explaining the process.

Layout for marks in an explanation question: name the climate region, describe its temperature and rainfall, name the dominant factors, and for each factor write a linked chain of factor, process and effect, with a South African or world example.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: the toolkit of factors, why hot places are hot and wet or dry, and why the other regions are the way they are.

## Subtopic: Six Tools

To explain any climate, you have six tools. Latitude: near the equator the sun is high, so it is hot; near the poles it is low, so it is cold. Pressure belts and winds: rising air brings rain, sinking air brings dry weather, and the belts move with the seasons. The sea: it keeps coasts mild, while the interior has hot summers and cold winters. Height: every thousand metres up is about six and a half degrees cooler. Currents: warm currents bring humid, rainy coasts; cold currents bring dry, foggy coasts. Mountains: rain on the side facing the wind, a dry rain shadow on the other side.

Hold the six tools in mind and try the questions for this part: the factors.

## Subtopic: Hot, Wet and Dry

The rainforest is hot because it is near the equator, and wet every month because warm air rises there every afternoon and makes thunderstorms. The savanna gets rain only in summer, when the rainy belt moves over it, and is dry in winter. Deserts are dry because air sinks over them, so clouds cannot form; cold currents make west-coast deserts like the Namib even drier.

Think of the Kruger Park in the dry winter: the grass is brown, the waterholes shrink and the animals gather near the rivers. When the summer rain arrives, everything turns green. That change is the rainy belt arriving and leaving.

Hold the rising and sinking air in mind and try this part's questions: hot, wet and dry climates.

## Subtopic: Cool, Cold and High

Cape Town gets rain in winter, because in winter the dry high-pressure belt moves north and the cold fronts from the south can reach it. Durban is warm and humid because of the warm Agulhas Current. London is mild and wet because winds blow from the Atlantic all year. Moscow has very cold winters because it is far from the sea. The poles are cold because the sun is low or does not rise for months. Mountains are cold because temperature drops with height, which is why there is snow on Kilimanjaro near the equator.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Every climate region is explained by a few factors: latitude, pressure belts and winds, distance from the sea, altitude, ocean currents and mountains. Hot, wet rainforests lie under rising air at the equator; deserts lie under sinking air near the tropics; Mediterranean and savanna climates depend on the seasonal shift of the belts; coasts are mild and interiors extreme; height brings cold. Always explain with a chain: factor, process, effect.
