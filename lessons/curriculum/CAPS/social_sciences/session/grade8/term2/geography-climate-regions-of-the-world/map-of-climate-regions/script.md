# Part 1 — Expert

This session teaches how to read and describe a world map of climate regions. The anchor is a simplified world climate map, coloured by the climate types you studied in the last two lessons, with southern Africa as a detailed example. The governing idea is that climate regions form a broadly symmetrical pattern of belts on either side of the equator, controlled mainly by latitude and the global pressure belts, but that this pattern is distorted by the distribution of land and sea, by ocean currents on the eastern and western sides of continents, and by mountains and plateaus. Locating climate regions and describing their distribution are examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Reading a Climate Map

A climate map shows the world divided into climate regions, each shaded or coloured according to a key. Warm colours, such as dark green, light green and yellow, usually show tropical and dry climates; blues and purples show cold climates; and greys or browns often show high mountain climates. Always begin by reading the key carefully, because different atlases use different colours.

Next, find the main lines of latitude: the equator at zero degrees, the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn at about twenty-three and a half degrees north and south, and the Arctic and Antarctic Circles at about sixty-six and a half degrees. These lines help you describe where regions lie. For example, the tropical rainforest lies within about ten degrees of the equator, and the hot deserts lie mostly between about fifteen and thirty-five degrees.

Then identify the continents and oceans, and notice the side of each continent on which a climate occurs. Western and eastern sides of continents often have different climates at the same latitude because of ocean currents and prevailing winds, as you learnt in the lesson on Durban and Port Nolloth.

To describe the distribution of a climate region in an exam, use four kinds of information: the latitudes where it is found, the continents and countries it covers, the side of the continents, east, west or interior, and any link with physical features such as mountains or coasts. For example: the Mediterranean climate is found between about thirty and forty-five degrees north and south on the western sides of continents, around the Mediterranean Sea, in California, central Chile, the south-western Cape and south-western Australia.

Remember that a world climate map is a simplification. The boundaries are drawn where long-term averages cross certain values, and in reality one climate grades gradually into another. Small areas with different climates, such as mountain ranges or coastal strips, may be too small to show at a world scale. A larger-scale map of a single country shows more detail.

Maps of climate regions are closely related to maps of natural vegetation, called biomes, because vegetation responds to temperature and rainfall. Rainforest, savanna, desert, Mediterranean shrubland, temperate forest, taiga and tundra on a vegetation map match the climate regions closely. Geographers often compare the two maps to see the connection between climate and living things.

It also helps to know the scale of the map. A world map compresses huge distances, so a single colour may cover an area larger than South Africa. When you move to an atlas map of Africa or of South Africa, the regions become more detailed and boundaries more precise. Always state which map you are using when you describe a pattern, because a feature visible on a national map may disappear on a world map.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading the key and latitude lines, describing distribution, and the link between climate and vegetation maps.

## Subtopic: The Global Pattern of Belts

Start at the equator and move towards the poles, and you pass through a series of climate belts.

The tropical rainforest lies along the equator, roughly between ten degrees north and ten degrees south: in the Amazon Basin in South America, the Congo Basin in Central Africa, and Indonesia, Malaysia and Papua New Guinea in South-East Asia. In parts of South and South-East Asia and West Africa, the tropical monsoon climate replaces or borders it.

On either side, from about five to twenty degrees, lie the tropical savanna regions: much of Africa south of the Sahara and north of South Africa, including East Africa and the countries around the Zambezi; the Brazilian highlands; northern Australia; and much of India.

Next, roughly between fifteen and thirty-five degrees, lie the hot deserts and semi-deserts: the Sahara, the largest, stretching across North Africa; the Arabian Desert; the Thar in India and Pakistan; the Kalahari and Namib in southern Africa; the deserts of central and western Australia; the Atacama in Chile; and the deserts of northern Mexico and the south-western United States. Semi-desert belts, such as the Sahel and the Karoo, surround the deserts.

Between about thirty and forty-five degrees, the Mediterranean climate lies on the western sides of continents, and the humid subtropical climate lies on the eastern sides: south-eastern United States, south-eastern China, south-eastern Brazil, the coast of KwaZulu-Natal, and eastern Australia.

Between about forty and sixty degrees, temperate oceanic climates lie on the western sides of continents, in north-western Europe, the Pacific coast of North America, southern Chile and New Zealand. Continental climates cover the interiors of North America and Eurasia, with the vast taiga forest in the north.

Beyond about sixty degrees, the tundra borders the Arctic Ocean in northern Canada, Alaska, Scandinavia and Siberia, and polar ice caps cover Greenland and Antarctica.

High mountain climates cut across these belts wherever there are high mountains and plateaus: the Andes, the Rocky Mountains, the Alps, the Himalayas and the Tibetan Plateau, the Ethiopian Highlands, and the Lesotho highlands.

Notice two important asymmetries. First, the southern hemisphere has no large continental or tundra regions, because its continents narrow towards the south and the Southern Ocean surrounds Antarctica. Second, the northern hemisphere has far more land in the middle and high latitudes, so its continental climates are vast.

The belts also shift slightly with the seasons, because the pressure belts follow the overhead sun. That is why places on the edges of the belts, such as the Sahel or the south-western Cape, receive their rain only when a particular belt moves over them. A place on a boundary between two belts often has a climate that alternates between their characteristics, wet in one season and dry in the other.

The questions for this section are with you now: the sequence of belts from the equator to the poles, examples of each, and why the two hemispheres differ.

## Subtopic: Africa and Southern Africa on the Map

Africa is the most symmetrical continent on the climate map, because the equator crosses its middle. Starting from the Congo Basin, the pattern runs northward through savanna, the Sahel, the Sahara and the Mediterranean coast of North Africa, and southward through savanna, the Kalahari and Namib, and the Mediterranean south-western Cape. The symmetry is broken in the east, where the East African highlands have cooler climates because of altitude, and where the Horn of Africa is dry.

Southern Africa shows the influence of the two oceans clearly. On the west, the cold Benguela Current and the South Atlantic high pressure create the coastal Namib Desert, which extends into the Northern Cape as the Richtersveld and Namaqualand. Inland, the Kalahari is semi-desert, and the Karoo is semi-desert. Moving east, rainfall increases, and the climate changes to summer-rainfall grassland and savanna on the highveld and in Limpopo and Mpumalanga. Along the east coast, the warm Agulhas Current creates a humid subtropical strip in KwaZulu-Natal and the northern Eastern Cape. In the south-west, the Mediterranean climate covers the Cape Town region and the Boland. Along the southern coast, between about George and Gqeberha, rain falls in all seasons. The Drakensberg and Lesotho highlands have high mountain climates with winter snow.

South Africa's rainfall map shows a strong east-to-west gradient: more than one thousand millimetres a year in parts of the east and on the escarpment, falling to less than one hundred millimetres along parts of the west coast. A useful rule of thumb is that rainfall decreases from east to west across the country. The country's average annual rainfall is about four hundred and fifty to five hundred millimetres, well below the world average of roughly eight hundred and sixty millimetres over land and sea combined, which makes South Africa a water-scarce country.

This pattern shapes where people live and farm. Most of South Africa's population and its largest cities are in the wetter eastern half and the Western Cape. Maize is grown mainly in the summer-rainfall areas of the Free State, North West and Mpumalanga; wheat and wine in the winter-rainfall Western Cape; sugar cane on the subtropical KwaZulu-Natal coast; and sheep in the dry Karoo.

Altitude adds another layer. The highveld lies on a plateau between about one thousand two hundred and one thousand eight hundred metres, so although it receives summer rain like the savanna to its north, it is cooler, with frost in winter and grassland rather than savanna as its natural vegetation. On a detailed map of South Africa, the grassland biome covers most of the highveld, while savanna covers the lower, warmer north and east. This is a good example of how a national map reveals patterns that a world map hides.

The questions for this section are with you now: Africa's climate symmetry, southern Africa's climate regions, and the east-to-west rainfall gradient.

## Subtopic: Describing Distribution and the Error Museum

When you describe a climate region's distribution, avoid vague answers such as it is found in many places. Use the four kinds of evidence: latitude, continents and countries, side of the continent, and links to physical features. Use compass directions and named places. Two or three well-chosen examples are better than a long list.

Here is a model answer for the hot desert climate: hot deserts are found mainly between about fifteen and thirty-five degrees north and south of the equator, often on the western sides of continents beside cold currents; examples include the Sahara and Arabian deserts in the northern hemisphere and the Namib, Kalahari, Atacama and Australian deserts in the southern hemisphere.

A small calculation shows how much of the land the deserts cover. Deserts and semi-deserts together cover roughly one third of the earth's land surface. If the land surface is about one hundred and fifty million square kilometres, one third of that is about fifty million square kilometres, more than forty times the area of South Africa, which is about one point two million square kilometres.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying deserts lie on the equator; the equator is rainforest, and deserts lie near the tropics. Two: forgetting that the same latitude can have different climates on the east and west sides of a continent. Three: describing distribution with only one example. Four: claiming the southern hemisphere has large tundra regions; it does not, apart from small sub-Antarctic islands. Five: reading colours without checking the key.

Layout for marks in a map question: read the key, locate with latitude and continents, state the side of the continent, give two examples, and add a reason linked to a climate factor.

Here is a second model answer, this time for the Mediterranean climate, to show the same method working on a smaller region: the Mediterranean climate is found between about thirty and forty-five degrees north and south of the equator, on the western sides of continents; examples include the lands around the Mediterranean Sea, California, central Chile, the south-western Cape around Cape Town, and south-western Australia around Perth; these places lie under subtropical high pressure in summer, which keeps them dry, and receive rain from westerly winds and cold fronts in winter. Notice how the answer moves from latitude, to side of continent, to named examples, to a reason.

The questions for this section are with you now: describing distribution, the desert area calculation, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: reading the coloured map, the belts around the world, and southern Africa's own colours.

## Subtopic: The Coloured Map

A climate map colours the world by climate. Each colour stands for a climate type, and the key tells you which is which. Always look at the key first.

Then find the equator, the two tropics and the polar circles. These lines help you say where each climate is. For example, rainforest is near the equator, and deserts are near the tropics.

Also notice the side of a continent. The west side and the east side can be very different, because of cold and warm currents.

Hold the key and the latitude lines in mind and try the questions for this part: reading a climate map.

## Subtopic: Belts Around the Earth

Picture the earth wearing stripes. At the middle, along the equator, is a green stripe of rainforest. On each side, a stripe of savanna. Next, yellow stripes of desert near the tropics, like the Sahara in the north and the Kalahari and Namib in the south. Then come the Mediterranean and subtropical stripes, then temperate and continental, and at the top and bottom, tundra and ice.

The stripes are not perfect. Oceans, currents and mountains bend them. And the southern half has less land far from the equator, so it has no big continental or tundra areas.

You can test yourself by naming a place in each stripe: Kisangani in the rainforest, the Kruger Park in the savanna, the Sahara in the desert, Cape Town in the Mediterranean stripe, London in the temperate stripe, Moscow in the continental belt, and northern Canada in the tundra.

Hold the striped earth in mind and try this part's questions: the belts of climate.

## Subtopic: Southern Africa's Colours

Zoom in on South Africa. On the west coast, next to the cold current, it is desert. The middle, the Karoo and Kalahari, is semi-desert. The east is wetter, with summer rain on the highveld and savanna in the north. The KwaZulu-Natal coast is humid subtropical. Cape Town is Mediterranean, with winter rain. And the Drakensberg and Lesotho have cold mountain climates with winter snow.

The big rule: rain decreases from east to west.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A climate map shows climate regions in colours; read the key and the latitude lines. From the equator outwards: rainforest, savanna, desert, Mediterranean and subtropical, temperate and continental, tundra and ice, with mountains cutting across. Ocean currents make east and west coasts different. In South Africa, rainfall decreases from east to west, from humid subtropical and summer-rainfall regions to semi-desert and desert, with a Mediterranean south-west.

Hold the striped earth and the east-to-west rainfall line, and take the final questions of the lesson: the map of climate regions.
