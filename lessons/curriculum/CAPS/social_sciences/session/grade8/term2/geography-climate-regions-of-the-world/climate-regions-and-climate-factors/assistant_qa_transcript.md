### subtopic_1 — The Factors as a Toolkit
**Assistant:** Why does the temperature drop as you go higher, if the mountain top is closer to the sun?

**Tutor:** Because the atmosphere is heated mainly from below, by the ground, not directly from above by the sun. Most of the sun's energy passes through the air without warming it much. It is absorbed by the land and the sea, which warm up and then heat the air in contact with them. So the warmest air is usually near the ground, and the further you go from this heat source, the cooler the air becomes. The difference in distance to the sun is meaningless: the sun is about one hundred and fifty million kilometres away, and a mountain of a few kilometres makes no measurable difference. There is a second reason. Air pressure decreases with height, because there is less air above pressing down. When air rises, it expands into the lower pressure, and expanding air cools. When air sinks, it is compressed and warms. This is why rising air forms clouds and rain, and sinking air is dry and clear. Thin air at high altitude also holds less heat and lets heat escape quickly at night. On average, temperature falls by about six and a half degrees for every thousand metres. Johannesburg, at about one thousand seven hundred and fifty metres, is roughly eleven degrees cooler than sea level would be at the same latitude, which explains the frost on the highveld in winter. Kilimanjaro, at nearly five thousand nine hundred metres, should be about thirty-eight degrees cooler at the top than at sea level, which is why it can have glaciers just three degrees south of the equator. The same principle explains why Lesotho, where most of the country is above one thousand four hundred metres, is often called the kingdom in the sky and receives snow in winter.

### subtopic_2 — Explaining the Hot Regions
**Assistant:** If the Kalahari and the Kruger Park are both in the north of South Africa, why is one so much drier?

**Tutor:** Because the moist air that brings rain to the north of South Africa comes mainly from the Indian Ocean in the east, and it loses moisture as it travels west. In summer, warm moist air flows in from the Indian Ocean and is lifted by heating and by the escarpment, forming thunderstorms. The Lowveld, where the Kruger Park lies, is close to this source and receives about five hundred to seven hundred millimetres a year in many places. By the time the air has crossed the highveld and reached the Kalahari in the Northern Cape, Botswana and Namibia, much of its moisture has already fallen as rain. The Kalahari also lies closer to the influence of the subtropical high pressure over the South Atlantic and the interior, where sinking air suppresses rain. Annual totals there fall to about two hundred to four hundred millimetres, decreasing towards the west. So both places lie at similar latitudes and both receive summer rain, but the amount differs because of distance from the moisture source and the position of the high pressure. This is the same east-to-west decrease in rainfall that shows up on the national rainfall map. It also explains why the Kalahari is called a semi-desert rather than a true desert. It has grasses and thorny trees such as camel thorn, and animals such as gemsbok and springbok, adapted to long dry periods. True desert conditions, with less than about two hundred and fifty millimetres, appear further west towards the Namib, where the cold Benguela Current adds its own drying effect.

### subtopic_3 — Explaining the Middle-Latitude, Cold and Mountain Regions
**Assistant:** Does every climate region have just one main factor, or can several factors work together?

**Tutor:** Several factors almost always work together, and good geography answers show how they combine. Take the south-western Cape. Its latitude, about thirty-four degrees south, places it on the border between the subtropical high-pressure belt and the westerlies. The seasonal shift of these belts gives it dry summers and wet winters. The Cape Fold Mountains then add relief rain, which is why Newlands, below the eastern slopes of Table Mountain, receives far more rain than the Cape Flats, and why the mountain catchments supply Cape Town's dams. The cold Benguela Current to the west keeps summers cooler and drier. The sea keeps winters mild. So the Mediterranean climate of the Cape is explained by at least four factors. Now take Johannesburg. Its latitude, about twenty-six degrees south, places it in the summer rainfall region. Its altitude, about one thousand seven hundred and fifty metres, makes it cool, with frost in winter. Its distance from the sea gives it a larger temperature range than Durban and allows dry, clear winter skies. Its rain comes as afternoon thunderstorms when moist air from the Indian Ocean is heated and lifted in summer. In an examination, it is good practice to name the dominant factor first and then show how one or two others modify it. For example: Johannesburg's mild summers are explained mainly by altitude, while its summer rainfall is explained by the seasonal arrival of moist easterly air from the Indian Ocean and convection. This shows the examiner you understand that climate is the result of interacting factors, not a single cause.

### subtopic_4 — Linked Explanations and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why does it matter to know the factors behind climate regions if we can just look at the map?

**Tutor:** Because the map tells you what the climate is, but only the factors tell you why, and the why is what allows you to predict, plan and adapt. Farmers need to know why their region has summer or winter rain to choose crops and planting times. Wheat in the Western Cape is planted in autumn to use the winter rain; maize on the highveld is planted in spring to use the summer rain. Engineers designing dams need to understand rainfall patterns and evaporation. Town planners need to understand the risk of floods on the KwaZulu-Natal coast, where warm, moist air and the escarpment can produce intense rainfall, as in the floods of April 2022 that killed more than four hundred people. Understanding the factors also helps us understand climate change. If the pressure belts shift towards the poles as the world warms, as many scientists expect, the cold fronts that bring winter rain to the Cape may reach it less often, making droughts like the one of 2015 to 2018 more likely. If the sea warms, it may supply more moisture to storms on the east coast. A map cannot tell you these things, but an understanding of the factors can. It is also useful when you travel or read the news: when you hear of floods, droughts or heat waves anywhere in the world, you can ask which factors are involved. In short, the map is the answer to a question, and the factors are the explanation. Geography examinations reward explanation, because explanation is what turns information into understanding.
