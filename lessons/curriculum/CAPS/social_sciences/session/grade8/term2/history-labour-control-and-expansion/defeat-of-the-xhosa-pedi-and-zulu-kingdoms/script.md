# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the defeat of three African kingdoms by British forces between 1877 and 1879, and the further land dispossession that followed: the Xhosa in the Ninth Frontier War of 1877 to 1878, the Zulu in the Anglo-Zulu War of 1879, and the Pedi in 1879. The anchor is the British policy of confederation, which aimed to unite the colonies, the Boer republics and the African kingdoms of southern Africa under British control. The governing idea is that the mineral revolution changed Britain's interests in southern Africa: independent African kingdoms were now seen as obstacles to security, to a regular supply of labour and to British control of a wealthy region. Explaining the causes and results of these wars, and using sources to show different perspectives, is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Diamonds, Confederation and Conquest

Before 1870, Britain was a reluctant ruler in southern Africa. It held the Cape Colony and Natal, but it was unwilling to pay for expensive wars or to govern large new territories. The Boer republics, the Orange Free State and the South African Republic, also called the Transvaal, were independent, and so were many African kingdoms, including the Zulu, the Pedi, the Gcaleka Xhosa east of the Kei River, and others.

The discovery of diamonds changed this. Southern Africa now looked like a valuable region that could produce wealth for British investors. Britain annexed the diamond fields of Griqualand West in 1871. The British Colonial Secretary, Lord Carnarvon, came to believe that the best way to secure the region was a confederation: a single federation of the colonies and republics under British control, similar to the Confederation of Canada created in 1867. In 1877 Sir Bartle Frere was sent to the Cape as High Commissioner to carry out this plan.

Several reasons made the independent African kingdoms an obstacle to confederation. First, the British believed that strong African states threatened the security of the colonies and the Boer republics, and that a federation could not be stable while they remained powerful. Second, the mines and white farms needed a regular supply of labour. Kingdoms that were independent and had enough land did not need to send men to work for wages, or could control when and how they did so. Third, many Africans had bought guns at the diamond fields, and the colonial authorities feared that armed kingdoms could resist. Fourth, there was land, which settlers and speculators wanted.

In April 1877 Britain annexed the South African Republic, which was bankrupt and had failed to defeat the Pedi in a war in 1876. This annexation gave Britain responsibility for the Transvaal's conflicts with the Pedi and with the Zulu kingdom over disputed border land. Within two years, Britain was at war with all three kingdoms.

Historians debate how far these wars were caused by the search for labour, by the desire for land, by the ambitions of individuals such as Frere, or by fear of African military power. Most agree that the mineral revolution made all these factors stronger at the same time.

It also helps to remember who held power on the spot. Officials in southern Africa, such as Frere and Sir Theophilus Shepstone, for many years Natal's Secretary for Native Affairs, had their own ambitions and often acted ahead of instructions from London, where messages took weeks to arrive. Shepstone himself carried out the annexation of the Transvaal in 1877, with only a small escort of police. Decisions made by a few men far from London could commit Britain to war.

## Subtopic: The Xhosa and the Pedi

The Xhosa had resisted colonial expansion on the eastern frontier of the Cape for about a century, in a series of wars that began in 1779. By the 1870s much of their land had already been lost, and the cattle-killing of 1856 to 1857 had left many Xhosa communities weakened and dependent on wage labour. The Gcaleka Xhosa under their king Sarhili still held independent land east of the Kei River.

The Ninth Frontier War began in 1877 with a clash between the Gcaleka and the Mfengu, who were allies of the Cape government. Cape and British forces invaded Gcalekaland. The Ngqika Xhosa under Sandile, who lived inside the Cape Colony, joined the war. By 1878 the Xhosa had been defeated. Sandile was killed in 1878, and Sarhili was forced into hiding. Gcalekaland was taken over and later annexed to the Cape Colony, and land was given to white settlers and to colonial allies. Many Xhosa men were forced to seek work on farms, on the railways and in the mines.

The Pedi kingdom, under King Sekhukhune, lay in the north-eastern Transvaal, around the Leolo Mountains. The Pedi had been among the first to send migrant workers to the diamond fields, and they had bought many guns there. In 1876 the South African Republic attacked the Pedi and failed to defeat them, which contributed to the republic's collapse. After Britain annexed the Transvaal in 1877, it demanded that Sekhukhune accept British authority and pay a fine in cattle. He refused. A British attack in 1878 failed. In November and December 1879, after the Zulu war was over, a large British force under Sir Garnet Wolseley, together with thousands of Swazi allies, attacked the Pedi stronghold, known as Tsate. The Pedi were defeated, and Sekhukhune was captured and imprisoned in Pretoria.

The Pedi lost their independence, and much of their land was opened to white farmers and taxation. Like the Xhosa, they were increasingly drawn into migrant labour, now as a necessity rather than a choice.

The Pedi defence was strong because of their mountain strongholds, their guns and their experience. Their warriors used the rocky hills and caves of the Leolo range to shelter and to fire on attackers, and they had stored food to withstand sieges. It took a force of about two thousand British and colonial troops and many thousands of Swazi soldiers to overcome them, and the fighting at Tsate was fierce, with heavy losses on both sides. After his capture, Sekhukhune was held in Pretoria until 1881, when the Transvaal regained its independence; he was murdered in 1882 by a rival, his half-brother Mampuru.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Ninth Frontier War, the defeat of the Gcaleka and Ngqika Xhosa, and the defeat of the Pedi under Sekhukhune.

## Subtopic: The Anglo-Zulu War of 1879

The Zulu kingdom, ruled since 1872 by King Cetshwayo, was the most powerful independent African state in southern Africa. It had a large, well-organised army of age-regiments, called amabutho, which also served as a labour force for the king. Frere saw the Zulu kingdom as the greatest obstacle to confederation and as a threat to Natal and the Transvaal.

In 1878 a boundary commission investigated the land dispute between the Zulu and the Transvaal and largely supported the Zulu claim. Frere delayed announcing the decision. In December 1878 he combined the announcement with an ultimatum demanding, among other things, that Cetshwayo disband his army within thirty days. Frere knew the king could not accept this, because the amabutho system was the foundation of the Zulu state. When the deadline passed in January 1879, British forces under Lord Chelmsford invaded Zululand.

On 22 January 1879, at Isandlwana, a Zulu army of about twenty thousand men attacked a British camp and destroyed it. About one thousand three hundred soldiers on the British side, British and African, were killed. It was one of the heaviest defeats suffered by a British army in a colonial war. On the same day and night, a small garrison held off a Zulu attack at Rorke's Drift, which the British celebrated to soften the shock of Isandlwana.

Britain sent reinforcements. The Zulu won further engagements, but they lost heavily at battles such as Khambula in March 1879, where their attacks on fortified positions were met by rifles and artillery. On 4 July 1879, at Ulundi, near the royal homestead of oNdini, the British defeated the Zulu army and burned the royal homestead. Cetshwayo was captured in August 1879 and sent into exile in the Cape.

The British did not annex Zululand at first. Wolseley divided it into thirteen chiefdoms under appointed chiefs, which led to years of civil war. Cetshwayo was allowed to return in 1883 but was defeated by rivals and died in 1884. In 1887 Britain annexed Zululand, and in 1897 it was incorporated into Natal, after which large areas were opened to white settlement.

The Zulu army's tactics were well suited to open battle. The regiments advanced in a formation called the horns of the bull: the chest attacked from the front, while the horns swept round to surround the enemy, and the loins waited in reserve. At Isandlwana this formation, combined with speed and surprise, overwhelmed a camp that had not been entrenched. Against entrenched positions and massed rifle fire, the same tactics proved very costly.

## Subtopic: Dispossession, Labour and the Error Museum

The defeats of 1877 to 1879 had connected results. Land was taken for settlers or placed under colonial control. African rulers lost the power to decide how their people lived and worked. Colonial governments imposed hut taxes, payable in cash, which pushed men into wage labour. Gun laws, such as the Cape's Peace Preservation Act of 1878, tried to disarm Africans; when the Cape tried to apply it in Basutoland, the Sotho resisted in the Gun War of 1880 to 1881 and largely succeeded, keeping their guns and later coming under direct British rule rather than Cape rule. This is a reminder that resistance continued and sometimes succeeded.

A small calculation shows the scale of Isandlwana. If about one thousand three hundred men on the British side were killed out of about one thousand seven hundred in and around the camp, then about four hundred survived, because one thousand seven hundred minus one thousand three hundred is four hundred. About three out of four were killed.

Different groups remember these wars differently. In British accounts of the time, the wars were often presented as bringing order and civilisation; Rorke's Drift became a celebrated story of heroism. In Zulu, Xhosa and Pedi memory, the wars are remembered as wars of resistance to invasion, and Isandlwana is a symbol of African military skill and courage. Historians today use both kinds of sources and ask whose interests each account served.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the Zulu won the war because they won at Isandlwana. They won a great battle but lost the war at Ulundi. Two: saying Cetshwayo started the war. Frere's ultimatum and the British invasion started it. Three: forgetting the Pedi; Sekhukhune was defeated in 1879 by British and Swazi forces. Four: confusing the Ninth Frontier War of 1877 to 1878 with the cattle-killing of 1856 to 1857. Five: describing the wars without linking them to the mineral revolution, labour and confederation.

Layout for marks in an essay or paragraph: state the cause linked to diamonds and confederation, describe the key events of the war, give the result for the kingdom, and explain the link to land and labour.

## Subtopic: Why Britain Wanted Control

Before diamonds, Britain did not want to spend money ruling more of southern Africa. After diamonds, it did. Britain wanted to join all the colonies, Boer republics and African kingdoms into one country under British control, called confederation.

Strong, independent African kingdoms stood in the way. They had armies and guns, and they had land, so their men did not have to work on the mines and farms. The British wanted the land, the labour and the control.

Think of it as a jigsaw puzzle that Britain wanted to complete. The independent kingdoms were pieces that did not fit Britain's picture, so Britain tried to force them in.

Diamonds made the picture worth much more, so Britain was willing to pay for wars it had once avoided.

## Subtopic: Two Kingdoms Fall

The Xhosa had fought colonial forces for about a hundred years. In the war of 1877 to 1878, they were defeated. Their leader Sandile was killed, King Sarhili went into hiding, and their land was taken.

The Pedi, under King Sekhukhune in the north-east, beat the Boers in 1876 and beat back the British in 1878. But at the end of 1879, a large British army with Swazi allies attacked them. Sekhukhune was captured.

After their defeats, both peoples lost land and had to pay taxes, so more men had to work for wages.

The Pedi fought from their mountain strongholds, with caves and rocky hills to shelter in and guns bought with mine wages. It took thousands of British, colonial and Swazi soldiers to defeat them. Sekhukhune was held in prison in Pretoria until 1881.

## Subtopic: Isandlwana and Ulundi

In December 1878, the British told King Cetshwayo to break up his army in thirty days. He could not. In January 1879, the British invaded. On 22 January, the Zulu army destroyed a British camp at Isandlwana, killing about one thousand three hundred men. It was a great Zulu victory. But Britain sent more soldiers, and on 4 July 1879 they beat the Zulu at Ulundi. Cetshwayo was captured and sent away, and the kingdom was split into thirteen pieces.

Pocket summary of the lesson. After diamonds were found, Britain wanted to control all of southern Africa through confederation. Independent African kingdoms stood in the way. The Xhosa were defeated in 1878, the Zulu in 1879 despite their victory at Isandlwana, and the Pedi under Sekhukhune at the end of 1879. They lost land and independence, paid taxes and were pushed into labour on the mines and farms. Resistance continued, as the Sotho showed in the Gun War.
