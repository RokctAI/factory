# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the increasing control over black workers on the Kimberley diamond fields from the 1870s to the 1890s, through migrant labour, pass laws and the closed compound system. The anchor is the closed compound, introduced from 1885, in which black mineworkers were housed, fed and confined for the length of their contracts. The governing idea is that the mine owners and the colonial state built a system of labour control that kept black wages low, prevented workers from bargaining or deserting, and reduced diamond theft, and that this system became a model for later labour practices in South Africa. Explaining the reasons for the system and its effects on workers is examinable this term, using written and visual sources.

## Subtopic: Why Workers Came to Kimberley

When diamonds were found in the late 1860s and the dry diggings opened in 1870 and 1871, the mines needed huge numbers of workers to dig, haul and sort the diamond-bearing ground. By the mid-1870s, tens of thousands of black workers passed through the diamond fields every year. Most were young men who came for a few months and then returned home. This was the beginning of migrant labour on a large scale in South Africa.

Historians explain why they came using push and pull factors. The pull factors were the wages, which were higher than anything available in the rural areas, and the goods that could be bought with them. In the early years, many workers wanted guns, which were sold freely at Kimberley in the early 1870s, as well as ploughs, blankets, clothing and cattle. Chiefs, including the Pedi king Sekhukhune and Moshoeshoe's successors in Lesotho, sent young men in groups to earn money and buy guns to strengthen their kingdoms against Boer and British pressure.

The push factors grew stronger over time. Colonial governments introduced hut taxes, which had to be paid in cash, forcing families to send members to earn wages. Land was being lost to settlers, so many families no longer had enough land to support themselves by farming alone. Droughts, cattle diseases and wars added to the pressure. Young men also needed cattle for bridewealth, called lobola, in order to marry, and wages were a way to acquire them.

At first, workers had considerable bargaining power. They could choose employers, leave if conditions were bad, and go home when they had earned what they needed. Employers complained of a labour shortage and of workers leaving at the busiest times, such as the ploughing season. Wages rose in the early 1870s because diggers competed for workers. This independence was exactly what the employers and the colonial government set out to reduce.

The journey itself was dangerous. Workers walked hundreds of kilometres, often through Boer republics such as the Orange Free State and the South African Republic, where they could be stopped, taxed, robbed or forced to work on farms on the way. Some chiefs organised workers into groups for protection, and some colonial officials set up depots and routes. The journey was one more cost that workers bore in order to earn wages, and one more reason why they bargained hard once they arrived.

Kimberley grew quickly as a result. By the late 1870s it was one of the largest towns in southern Africa, a crowded, noisy settlement of tents and iron shacks around the great open pits, with people from many African societies, from Britain and Europe, from the Cape and from as far as America and Australia.

The questions for this section are with you now: why workers came to Kimberley, push and pull factors, and the early bargaining power of migrant workers.

## Subtopic: Pass Laws and Contracts

The first tool of control was the pass. After Britain annexed Griqualand West in 1871, the colonial authorities introduced pass laws for black workers in 1872. Every black worker arriving at the diamond fields had to register, obtain a pass and enter into a contract with an employer. The pass recorded the worker's name, employer and wage, and the worker had to carry it at all times. A worker found without a pass could be arrested, fined, imprisoned or forced to work. When the contract ended, the employer signed the pass, and only then could the worker leave legally or find another employer.

Masters and servants laws made it a criminal offence for a worker to break a contract, desert, refuse an order or be absent from work. An employer who broke the contract faced, at most, a civil claim, but a worker who broke it could be jailed. This inequality meant that the law was used to keep workers tied to their employers.

The pass laws had several aims from the employers' point of view. They aimed to stop workers deserting before the end of their contracts, to prevent competition between employers for workers, which pushed wages up, and to identify workers suspected of diamond theft. From the workers' point of view, passes limited their freedom of movement, exposed them to arrest and humiliation, and reduced their ability to bargain for better pay.

The pass system at Kimberley was not the first in South Africa; the Cape and Natal had earlier pass regulations. But Kimberley applied it on a larger scale to an industrial workforce, and it became a model for the much bigger pass system on the Witwatersrand gold mines after 1886 and, in the twentieth century, for the national pass laws of the segregation and apartheid eras. Historians therefore see Kimberley as an important early step in the history of pass laws.

Passes also shaped everyday life outside work. Workers could be stopped by police in the streets and asked to show their pass, and at night a curfew applied to black people in parts of the town. Arrests for pass offences were common, and the prison population of Kimberley grew. Prisoners were often hired out to the mining companies as convict labour, which gave the companies another source of cheap workers. Historians see this link between the law, the prison and the mine as part of a wider system of labour control.

The questions for this section are with you now: the pass laws of 1872, masters and servants laws, and the aims and effects of passes.

## Subtopic: The Closed Compounds

From 1885, the large mining companies began to house their black workers in closed compounds. A closed compound was a large rectangle of barracks built around an open yard, surrounded by a high wall or fence and covered in parts by wire mesh to stop diamonds being thrown out. Workers lived inside for the whole of their contract, usually three to six months, and went to and from the mine through tunnels or guarded passages. They could not leave the compound until their contract ended. Food, clothing and other goods were sold inside the compound by a company store.

When a worker's contract ended, he was kept in a detention room for several days and searched thoroughly, including being given purgatives, before he was allowed to leave, so that he could not carry out swallowed diamonds. Every day, workers were stripped and searched when they left the mine.

The companies gave three main reasons for the compounds. First, to stop the theft of diamonds and illicit diamond buying, known as IDB, which they believed cost them a large share of their output. Second, to stop workers drinking alcohol sold by canteen keepers in the town, which employers said made workers less productive. Third, to control the workforce: to stop desertion, keep workers at work for the whole contract and prevent them from moving to employers who paid more.

The compounds were applied only to black workers. In 1883 and 1884, the companies tried to introduce searching for white workers, and white overseers and miners went on strike. In April 1884, during the strike, a clash at the Kimberley Mine left six strikers dead. The companies then largely exempted white workers from strip-searching, and white workers continued to live in the town. This difference shows clearly that the system was built on a racial division of labour.

The compounds had serious effects. Workers lost the freedom to move, to choose where to buy goods and to live with their families. They paid company-store prices, so part of their wages flowed back to the company. Overcrowding spread diseases such as pneumonia, and death rates in the compounds were high. Townspeople and shopkeepers in Kimberley, who lost the trade of thousands of workers, protested against the compounds, but the government supported the mining companies.

Mine managers presented the compounds as orderly and even beneficial, pointing to regular meals, a hospital and protection from the canteens of the town. Critics, including some missionaries, journalists and members of the Cape Parliament, compared them to prisons. When you meet sources from either side in an examination, ask who wrote them, for whom and why, because both kinds of source were written to persuade.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a closed compound was, the reasons the companies gave for them, and their effects on workers.

## Subtopic: Effects, Legacy and the Error Museum

The labour system at Kimberley had long-term effects on South Africa. Migrant labour became the basis of the mining economy. Men worked in the mines on contracts and returned to rural homes, where their families farmed, cared for children and looked after the sick and old. This allowed employers to pay wages calculated for a single man rather than a family, because the rural household was expected to support itself. When the gold mines opened on the Witwatersrand from 1886, they copied the system of migrant labour, passes and compounds, although most gold-mine compounds were open rather than closed.

A simple calculation shows how a company store could recover part of a wage. Suppose a worker earned twenty shillings a week on a twelve-week contract, which is twenty times twelve, or two hundred and forty shillings. If he had to spend eight shillings a week in the company store, he would return ninety-six shillings to the company, because eight times twelve is ninety-six, leaving one hundred and forty-four shillings to take home. These are example figures, not records from a particular mine, but they show the mechanism.

Workers were not simply passive. They resisted in many ways: by deserting, by choosing employers with better conditions while they still could, by stealing and selling diamonds, which some saw as a fair share of the wealth they dug, by refusing work, and by organising in groups based on home areas. Some returned with guns that helped their kingdoms resist conquest, which is one reason the colonial authorities later restricted the sale of guns.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the compounds were introduced in 1867. Diamonds were found in the late 1860s, but closed compounds came from 1885. Two: saying white workers lived in closed compounds. They did not. Three: saying migrant workers came only because they were forced. Early migrants chose to come, but taxes and land loss increasingly forced them. Four: confusing passes with identity documents for everyone. Passes applied to black workers. Five: forgetting the companies' stated reasons. A strong answer gives the companies' reasons and the workers' experience.

Layout for marks in a source question on compounds: identify the source and its author, explain what it shows, state the companies' reasons, describe the effect on workers, and evaluate whose point of view the source represents.

The questions for this section are with you now: the legacy of the system, the company-store calculation, worker resistance and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Walking to the Diamonds

Picture a young man from Pedi country in the 1870s. He walks for weeks to Kimberley. He wants to earn money to buy a gun, cattle or a plough, or to pay the hut tax. He works for a few months and goes home. That is migrant labour: working far from home for a while, then returning.

At first, workers could choose their boss and leave if things were bad. The mine owners did not like that, because they wanted workers who would stay and accept low pay.

The walk was long and risky. On the way, workers could be robbed or stopped and made to work on farms, so many travelled in groups organised by their chiefs.

## Subtopic: Papers to Work

From 1872, every black worker had to carry a pass. It showed who he was, who he worked for and what he earned. Without it, he could be arrested. He could not leave his job until his employer signed the pass. Breaking a contract was a crime for a worker, but not for a boss.

Passes helped the owners keep workers from leaving and keep wages low. Later, the same idea spread to the gold mines and across the country.

Think about what that meant in daily life. A worker could be stopped in the street and asked for his pass. If he had left it in his hut, he could spend a night in jail. He could not walk away from a bad boss and look for a better one.

Hold the pass in mind and try this part's questions: passes and contracts.

## Subtopic: Behind the Fence

From 1885, black workers lived in closed compounds: big barracks behind high fences with wire over the top. They stayed inside for their whole contract, three to six months, and bought food at the company shop. They were searched every day, and at the end of the contract they were held and searched again so no diamonds left with them.

The companies said it was to stop diamond theft and drinking. It also stopped workers leaving or bargaining for better pay. White workers did not live in compounds.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Black workers came to Kimberley as migrants, first by choice and then more and more because of taxes and land loss. Pass laws from 1872 tied them to employers. Closed compounds from 1885 confined them for their contracts, searched them and sold them goods at company stores. The companies said this stopped theft and drinking; it also kept wages low and workers under control. The system was copied on the gold mines and shaped South Africa's later labour laws.

Hold the fence and the pass in mind, and take the final questions of the lesson: migrant labour and closed compounds.
