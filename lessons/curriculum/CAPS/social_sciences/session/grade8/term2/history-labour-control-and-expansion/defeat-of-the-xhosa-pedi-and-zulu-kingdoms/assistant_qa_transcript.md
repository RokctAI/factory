### subtopic_1 — Diamonds, Confederation and Conquest
**Assistant:** If the Zulu won at Isandlwana, why did they still lose the war?

**Tutor:** Because a single great victory could not overcome the military and economic power of the British Empire, and because of the way the Zulu were forced to fight after Isandlwana. At Isandlwana, the Zulu army of about twenty thousand attacked a camp that had not been fortified, and the British force was spread out. The Zulu used their famous tactic of the horns of the bull, with a central chest attacking from the front and horns encircling the flanks, and they overwhelmed the camp. But the victory came at a terrible cost: the Zulu also lost a large number of men, perhaps over a thousand, killed by rifle fire. Britain responded by sending thousands of reinforcements by sea, together with more artillery and Gatling guns, and the commanders changed their tactics. In the later battles, at Khambula and at Ulundi, the British fought from fortified positions or a tight square formation, with rifles and artillery firing at long range. The Zulu, armed mainly with spears and shields and some guns, had to cross open ground to reach them, and suffered heavy losses. The Zulu kingdom could not replace its losses, while the British Empire could keep sending soldiers. The Zulu army was also needed at home to plant and harvest crops, so a long war hurt the kingdom's economy. Cetshwayo had hoped that after Isandlwana Britain might negotiate, and he sent messages offering peace, but Chelmsford wanted a decisive victory to restore British prestige. So the answer is that Isandlwana showed Zulu courage and skill, but the war was decided by the industrial power, resources and determination of the British Empire, which is itself linked to the Industrial Revolution you studied in Term 1.

### subtopic_2 — The Xhosa and the Pedi
**Assistant:** Why did the Swazi help the British against the Pedi?

**Tutor:** Because the Swazi kingdom had its own interests, and helping the British served them at the time. The Swazi and the Pedi had a long history of rivalry and conflict over land, cattle and influence in the region north of the Vaal. The Swazi kings were also skilful diplomats who tried to keep their kingdom independent by making alliances with the most powerful outsiders, first with the Boers and later with the British. By helping Britain defeat the Pedi, the Swazi hoped to weaken a rival, gain cattle and captives as rewards, and win British goodwill and protection for their own independence. In 1879 the Swazi sent thousands of soldiers, and they played a major part in storming the Pedi stronghold at Tsate. This is an important point for historians, because it shows that African kingdoms were not a single group acting together against colonialism. Each kingdom had its own leaders, interests and history, and colonial powers often used rivalries between them, a strategy sometimes called divide and rule. The Mfengu in the Eastern Cape fought on the colonial side against the Xhosa for similar reasons. The Swazi strategy did help them keep a form of independence: Swaziland, today Eswatini, became a British protectorate rather than being absorbed into a settler colony, and it became independent in 1968. But in the late nineteenth century the Swazi also lost much land to concessions granted to white speculators. When you read sources about these wars, look for the interests of every group involved, not only the colonisers and the main kingdoms.

### subtopic_3 — The Anglo-Zulu War of 1879
**Assistant:** Did everyone in Britain support the Zulu war?

**Tutor:** No. The war was controversial in Britain, especially after Isandlwana. Frere had issued the ultimatum without clear approval from the government in London, which had told him it did not want a war in southern Africa at that time because it was already involved in a war in Afghanistan. When news of Isandlwana reached Britain in February 1879, it caused shock and anger. Some politicians and newspapers blamed Frere for starting an unnecessary war and Chelmsford for the defeat. William Gladstone, the leader of the Liberal opposition, attacked the government's imperial policies in his famous Midlothian campaign of 1879 to 1880, and the cost and disasters of the wars in southern Africa and Afghanistan helped the Liberals win the election of 1880. Frere was later recalled. Some people in Britain also sympathised with Cetshwayo. When he visited London in 1882, he was received by Queen Victoria and treated with curiosity and some respect, and Bishop John Colenso of Natal had argued for Zulu rights for years. However, support for the empire was still strong in Britain, and the defeat at Isandlwana was quickly followed by celebrations of Rorke's Drift, where eleven Victoria Crosses were awarded. This shows that British opinion was divided, and that public memory was shaped by what the authorities and newspapers chose to celebrate. For a historian, this is a useful example of how the same events can be presented in very different ways at the time they happen.

### subtopic_4 — Dispossession, Labour and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How are these wars connected to the land that people live on in South Africa today?

**Tutor:** They are a direct part of the history of land dispossession, which still shapes land ownership and land debates today. After the defeats of 1877 to 1879, large areas of Xhosa, Zulu and Pedi land were taken over by colonial governments. Some was given or sold to white settlers, some was given to colonial allies, and some was set aside as locations or reserves where African people were allowed to live under appointed chiefs. In Zululand, after annexation in 1887 and its incorporation into Natal in 1897, a land commission set aside about forty percent of Zululand for white settlement and sugar farming, and the rest became reserves. In Sekhukhuneland, the Pedi were confined to a smaller area, and Boer and British farmers took much of the surrounding land. In the Eastern Cape, Gcalekaland and other areas were annexed and divided. These reserves became too small and crowded to support the growing population, which pushed more men into migrant labour, as you will see in later lessons. The Natives Land Act of 1913, which you will study at the end of this term, built on this pattern by limiting African land ownership to the reserves, at first about seven percent of the country. These reserves later became the basis of the homelands of the apartheid era. Since 1994, the land restitution programme has allowed communities to claim land lost after 1913, and the debate about earlier losses, including those of the 1870s, continues. So the wars of 1877 to 1879 are not distant events: they are an important link in the chain of history that explains the distribution of land in South Africa today.
