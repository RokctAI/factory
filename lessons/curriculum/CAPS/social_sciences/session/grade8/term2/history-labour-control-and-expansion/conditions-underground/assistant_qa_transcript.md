### subtopic_1 — From Open Pits to Underground Mines
**Assistant:** Why did the mining companies not make the mines safer if so many workers were dying?

**Tutor:** Because safety cost money, and for many years the companies and the government did not treat the deaths of black workers as a serious enough problem to justify the cost. Better ventilation, more timber supports, water sprays to reduce dust, safer explosives, proper medical examinations and better food all required investment. On the gold mines especially, the companies faced a particular problem: the ore was low-grade, meaning there was only a small amount of gold in each tonne of rock, and the price of gold was fixed internationally, so they could not raise prices to cover higher costs. Their answer was to keep costs, especially labour costs, as low as possible. Because black workers were migrants on short contracts, a worker who became ill could be sent home and replaced by a new recruit, so the company did not bear the full cost of his illness. Black workers had no vote and no recognised unions to press for change. Safety did improve gradually, however, under pressure from several directions. Mine inspectors and government commissions reported on death rates. Scandals, such as very high death rates among workers recruited from tropical regions in the early 1900s, led to restrictions on recruitment. White workers, who had the vote and unions, pressed for compensation for miners' phthisis, and a Miners' Phthisis Act was passed in 1911. Medical research identified the causes of silicosis and pneumonia. But improvements often came first for white workers. So the answer is that profits, the migrant labour system and the lack of political power of black workers all slowed down safety improvements, and change came through pressure, not goodwill.

### subtopic_2 — The Dangers Underground
**Assistant:** How do we know what it was like underground if most miners could not write about it?

**Tutor:** Historians use a wide range of sources and listen carefully for workers' voices within them. One source is oral history: interviews with former miners and their families, recorded in the twentieth century, which preserve memories passed down from earlier generations. Another is song. Migrant workers composed songs about the journey to the mines, the darkness underground, the danger, the compounds and the longing for home, and some of these songs were recorded by researchers. Some workers did write letters, or had them written by others, and some gave evidence to government commissions, where their words were recorded, although often through interpreters and in a setting controlled by officials. Missionaries, doctors and journalists also described conditions, sometimes sympathetically. Official records provide another layer: mine inspectors' reports, records of accidents and deaths, and medical statistics on diseases, which allow historians to calculate death rates. Photographs show the tunnels, the compounds and the workers, though they were usually taken for the companies and may show a tidied picture. Historians compare these sources, look for agreement and contradiction, and ask who created each one and why. For example, a company report might stress improvements and good food, while a miner's song stresses hunger and fear. Neither alone is the full truth, but together they let us build a balanced picture. This is a key skill in Grade 8 History: identifying the type of source, its author and purpose, and judging how useful and reliable it is for a particular question.

### subtopic_3 — Disease, the Colour Bar and Unequal Treatment
**Assistant:** Did conditions underground ever improve for black workers?

**Tutor:** Yes, slowly, over many decades, and mostly because of pressure from workers, governments, doctors and public opinion. In the early twentieth century, very high death rates, especially from pneumonia among workers recruited from tropical regions, led the government to restrict recruitment from those regions for some years and to require better housing and food. Medical research led to vaccination against pneumonia from the 1910s and 1920s, improved diets in the compounds to prevent scurvy, and better ventilation and wet drilling to reduce dust. Death rates fell over time. However, inequality remained. Compensation for silicosis was much lower for black workers than for white workers, and many black miners who developed lung disease after returning home were never compensated at all. The colour bar continued for most of the twentieth century. Black mineworkers organised and struck, notably in the great African mineworkers' strike of 1946, which was crushed by the police. The National Union of Mineworkers was founded in 1982 and grew into one of the largest unions in the country, campaigning for safety, wages and an end to the colour bar, which was finally removed in 1987 and 1988. After 1994, a new Mine Health and Safety Act was passed in 1996. Even in the twenty-first century, former miners have gone to court to win compensation for silicosis, and a settlement was reached in 2018 with major gold-mining companies. So conditions did improve, but the history of the mines is also a history of long struggles for safety and fairness, many of them still continuing.

### subtopic_4 — Sources, Calculations and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why did white workers get the better jobs if black workers often had more experience?

**Tutor:** Because the division of labour on the mines was based on political power and racial ideas, not on experience alone. In the early years, many skilled jobs such as engine driving, blasting and machine repair were done by white immigrants from Britain, Cornwall and Australia, who had experience of mining elsewhere. They formed unions to protect their position. Over time, many black workers learned these skills on the job, often doing work that was officially done by a white supervisor, who might simply watch. Black workers were also cheaper to employ, so mine owners were sometimes tempted to give them more skilled work to reduce costs. White workers saw this as a threat to their jobs and wages, and they used their political power, because they had the vote, and their unions to demand protection. The government responded with laws and regulations such as the Mines and Works Act of 1911, which reserved certain certificates, such as blasting certificates, for white workers. When the mine owners tried to weaken the colour bar in 1921 and 1922, white workers rose in the Rand Revolt of 1922, an armed strike that was crushed by the government with many deaths. The colour bar was then reinforced. Racist ideas of the time claimed that black people were suited only to manual labour, and these ideas were used to justify the system. So historians explain the colour bar as the result of competition for jobs, the political power of white workers, government policy and racist ideology working together. It meant that black workers' skills and experience were not rewarded, which kept their wages low for decades.
