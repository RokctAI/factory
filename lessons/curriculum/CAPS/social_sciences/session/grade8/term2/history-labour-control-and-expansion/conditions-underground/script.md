# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the conditions underground in the diamond mines of Kimberley from the 1880s and in the early deep-level gold mines of the Witwatersrand. The anchor is the change from open-pit digging to underground mining, which made the work more dangerous and more tightly controlled. The governing idea is that the dangers of underground work fell most heavily on black migrant workers, who did the hardest and most dangerous jobs for the lowest pay, while a colour bar reserved skilled and supervisory work for white workers. Describing conditions underground, explaining why they were so dangerous and using sources to show the miners' experience are examinable this term.

## Subtopic: From Open Pits to Underground Mines

In the early 1870s, the diamond mines at Kimberley were open pits. Thousands of small claims were dug side by side, and the ground was hauled up by a web of ropes and buckets running to the edge of the pit. As the claims went deeper, the sides of the pits, made of loose, weathered rock that the miners called reef, began to collapse into the workings. Reef falls buried claims for weeks or months and killed workers. Water seeped in and flooded the deepest claims, so pumps became necessary.

By the early 1880s, the Kimberley Mine was more than one hundred metres deep, and open-pit mining was becoming impossible. From about 1885, the companies began underground mining. Shafts were sunk outside the pit, in solid rock, and tunnels were driven from them into the diamond-bearing blue ground of the volcanic pipe. Miners blasted and dug out the blue ground in underground chambers, and it was hauled to the surface in trucks and lifted by steam-powered winding engines.

Underground mining needed far more capital than open-pit digging, for engines, pumps, rails, cables and explosives. Only large companies could afford it, which is one reason why mining was consolidated into a few companies and finally into De Beers Consolidated Mines in 1888. It also changed the work. Instead of small groups of workers on individual claims, large gangs now worked underground under the direction of white overseers, at a pace set by the machines and the company.

The blue ground brought to the surface was spread out on large floors, sometimes for months, so that sun and rain could break it down, before being washed and sorted for diamonds. Workers on the surface floors were also closely watched to prevent theft.

The same pattern developed on the Witwatersrand after 1886, but on a much larger scale. The gold-bearing reefs were thin layers of rock that sloped down deep into the earth, so mines soon had to go hundreds and then thousands of metres underground. These deep-level mines were among the most expensive and dangerous mining operations in the world.

The size of the Kimberley Mine still astonishes visitors today. The open pit, now called the Big Hole, reached a depth of about two hundred and forty metres before underground working took over, and the underground mine was eventually worked to about one thousand and ninety-seven metres before it closed in 1914. The surface hole is roughly four hundred and sixty metres across. It was dug largely by hand, and the scale of the labour it represents is one of the reasons it is such a powerful historical site.

## Subtopic: The Dangers Underground

Underground mines were dangerous places. The main dangers were rockfalls, explosions, fires, flooding, falls down shafts and accidents with machinery and trucks.

Rockfalls were the most common cause of death. Blasting and the pressure of the rock above could bring down the roof of a tunnel or stope without warning. Timber props and pillars of unmined rock were used to hold up the roof, but they did not always hold.

Explosives were essential and dangerous. Miners drilled holes in the rock face by hand, using hammers and steel drills, and filled them with dynamite. Misfires, charges that did not go off and were later struck by a drill, killed and injured many workers. Fumes from the explosions filled the tunnels.

Fire was a terrible danger in mines where tunnels were lined with timber. On 11 July 1888, a fire started in the De Beers Mine, probably when timber caught alight near a paraffin lamp. Smoke spread quickly through the tunnels. Rescuers brought out some workers, but about two hundred miners died, most of them black workers who were deep in the mine and had no way to escape the smoke. The disaster led to new safety measures, but it showed that in an emergency, black workers were the most exposed.

The working environment itself was harmful. In deep mines, the temperature of the rock rises with depth, so the air underground was hot and humid. Ventilation was poor in the early years. Workers drilled and shovelled in heat and darkness, lit only by candles or lamps, often for ten or more hours a shift. They climbed long ladders or were lowered in cages, and in many mines they had to walk long distances underground to reach the working face before the shift even started.

Accidents were often blamed on the workers themselves. Company and official reports of the time frequently described deaths as the result of carelessness by individual miners, rather than as the result of the pace of work, the lack of safety equipment or the pressure to produce. Historians reading these reports ask who was writing them and whose interests they protected, because blaming workers reduced pressure on companies to spend money on safety.

The pace of work also increased the danger. On the gold mines, black workers drilling by hand were often set a daily target, such as a hole of a certain depth, and a worker who failed to finish it might not be paid for the shift. This system, called the ticket system, pushed workers to drill fast in difficult conditions.

## Subtopic: Disease, the Colour Bar and Unequal Treatment

Underground work damaged workers' health. Drilling into rock produced fine dust, and on the gold mines, where the rock contained a lot of quartz, the dust caused a lung disease called silicosis, known at the time as miners' phthisis. The dust scarred the lungs, made breathing difficult and made workers much more likely to catch tuberculosis. Pneumonia was a major killer, especially among workers who came up from the hot, damp mine into cold winter nights on the highveld, and among workers recruited from tropical regions such as northern Mozambique. Poor food in compounds caused scurvy, a disease caused by a lack of vitamin C. Death rates among black mineworkers in the early twentieth century were very high, and on some mines several out of every hundred workers died in a single year.

The work underground was divided by race. Black workers did the heavy manual labour: hand drilling, shovelling the broken rock, called lashing, pushing trucks, and timbering. White workers were mainly employed as skilled tradesmen, such as engine drivers, fitters and blasters, and as supervisors and overseers who directed gangs of black workers. This division was not based only on skill. Over time it was protected by custom and by law in what became known as the colour bar, which reserved certain jobs for white workers and prevented black workers from doing them, however experienced they became. On the gold mines, the Mines and Works Act of 1911 later gave legal force to the colour bar.

Pay and treatment were very unequal. White workers earned many times the wages of black workers, lived with their families in the town, and could organise in trade unions. Black workers were migrants on contracts, lived in compounds, had no vote and were not allowed to form recognised unions. When workers were injured or killed, compensation, where it existed, was much smaller for black workers than for white workers, and many injured black workers were simply sent home.

Workers recruited from far away were especially vulnerable. Many came from regions where pneumonia and tuberculosis were rare, so they had little resistance. They arrived after long journeys, sometimes poorly fed, and went straight into hard work underground. Medical officers in the early twentieth century recorded much higher death rates among these recruits than among workers from nearby areas, which became a political scandal and led the government to restrict recruiting from tropical regions for some years.

The questions for this section are with you now: diseases of the mines, the colour bar and the unequal treatment of black and white workers.

## Subtopic: Sources, Calculations and the Error Museum

Historians reconstruct conditions underground from many sources: reports of mine inspectors and government commissions, company records, newspapers, photographs, medical reports on death rates and diseases, and the accounts of miners themselves, including songs, oral histories and letters. Each kind of source has strengths and limits. Company records give numbers but rarely the voices of workers. Government reports may criticise conditions but were often written to defend the industry. Oral histories and songs give the workers' experience but may have been recorded many years later.

A calculation shows how historians use death rates. Suppose a mine employed four thousand black workers in a year and one hundred and twenty of them died. The death rate is one hundred and twenty divided by four thousand, which is three hundredths, or thirty deaths per thousand workers per year. Historians compare death rates like this between mines, between groups of workers and with the death rate of the population as a whole, to judge how dangerous the work was. These are example figures for the method, not the record of one mine.

Workers responded to these conditions in many ways. They deserted, refused dangerous work, avoided certain mines that had a bad reputation, and passed information about conditions to people at home, so that recruits would choose some mines over others. Songs sung by miners describe the darkness, the danger and the longing for home. Chiefs and families in rural areas sometimes complained to officials about the deaths and injuries of men who had gone to the mines.

Compare two short sources. A company annual report might state that the compounds provide wholesome food and medical care, and that the death rate has fallen. A miner's song might describe the cage going down into darkness, the falling rock and the friend who never came back up. The first source is useful for official numbers and the company's point of view; the second is useful for the feelings and fears of workers. A strong answer uses both and explains the purpose behind each.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the mines were always underground. They began as open pits and moved underground in the 1880s because of reef falls and depth. Two: saying white and black workers did the same jobs for different pay. The work itself was divided by race. Three: confusing silicosis, a dust disease of the lungs, with scurvy, a disease of poor diet. Four: forgetting that the De Beers fire of 1888 killed mainly black workers. Five: using a company source as if it were neutral.

Layout for marks in a source question on conditions underground: identify the source and author, list the dangers it shows, explain why black workers were most exposed, and comment on the usefulness and limits of the source.

## Subtopic: Going Down

At first, Kimberley was a huge open hole. As it got deeper, the sides kept falling in and water flooded the bottom. So from about 1885, the companies dug shafts beside the hole and tunnels into the diamond rock. Machines lifted the rock out. Only big companies could pay for this, so the small diggers disappeared.

On the gold mines of Johannesburg, the gold was in thin layers sloping deep underground, so the mines went down hundreds and then thousands of metres.

The Big Hole at Kimberley was dug to about two hundred and forty metres as an open pit, and the tunnels below it went down more than a thousand metres before the mine closed in 1914.

## Subtopic: Danger in the Dark

Underground, the roof could fall. Dynamite could go off at the wrong time. In 1888 a fire in the De Beers Mine filled the tunnels with smoke, and about two hundred miners died, most of them black workers.

It was hot, dark and dusty, and shifts were ten hours or more.

Imagine drilling a hole in rock with a hammer and a steel bar for hours, in the heat, by candlelight, knowing the roof might fall at any moment. That was a normal shift for many workers.

Accidents were often blamed on the workers, but the real causes were often the speed of work, the lack of safety equipment and the push to dig more rock every day.

Safety came slowly, and only after many deaths.

## Subtopic: Hard Work, Unfair Rules

Black workers did the heaviest and most dangerous work: drilling, shovelling and pushing trucks. White workers were bosses and skilled workers, and earned many times more. Rules called the colour bar kept the better jobs for white workers.

Dust caused lung disease, and pneumonia and poor food made many workers ill. Many died.

Pocket summary of the lesson. From the 1880s, mines moved underground because of reef falls, flooding and depth. Underground work was dangerous: rockfalls, explosives, fires such as the De Beers fire of 1888, heat, dust and long shifts. Black migrant workers did the hardest and most dangerous work for low pay, while a colour bar reserved skilled and supervisory jobs for white workers. Diseases such as silicosis, pneumonia and scurvy killed many. Historians use many kinds of sources to reconstruct these conditions.

Today you can stand at the edge of the Big Hole in Kimberley and look down. Almost all of it was dug by hand.
