# Part 1 — Expert

This session teaches the skill of reading, drawing and interpreting climate graphs, which combine a line graph of mean monthly temperature with a bar graph of mean monthly rainfall. The anchor is a pair of climate graphs for two South African stations: Station A, with figures like Cape Town's, and Station B, with figures like Pretoria's. The governing idea is that a climate graph is a summary of long-term averages, and that by reading the highest and lowest points of the temperature line, the size and timing of the rainfall bars, and doing a few simple calculations, you can describe a climate precisely, identify its type and explain it using the factors you have studied. Reading and interpreting climate graphs is examinable this term, and appears in almost every Geography test.

## Subtopic: The Parts of a Climate Graph

A climate graph has three axes. Along the bottom, the horizontal axis shows the twelve months, usually from January to December, labelled with their initial letters: J, F, M, A, M, J, J, A, S, O, N, D. On the left, a vertical axis shows temperature in degrees Celsius. On the right, a second vertical axis shows rainfall in millimetres. Some graphs swap the sides, so always read the axis labels first.

Temperature is shown as a line graph. For each month, a point is plotted at the mean monthly temperature, placed in the middle of the month's space, and the points are joined with a smooth line or straight segments. Temperature is drawn as a line because it changes continuously from one month to the next. Some graphs show two lines, one for the mean daily maximum and one for the mean daily minimum, in which case the gap between them shows the daily range.

Rainfall is shown as a bar graph, with one bar for each month. The height of each bar shows the mean total rainfall for that month. Rainfall is shown as bars because it is a total amount collected over each month, not a continuous quantity.

Every climate graph should have a title naming the place, and often its latitude, longitude and altitude, which help you explain the climate. Many graphs also show the annual rainfall total and the mean annual temperature in a box.

To read a value, find the month along the bottom, go up to the line or the top of the bar, then read across to the correct axis. Take care to read temperature from the temperature axis and rainfall from the rainfall axis; mixing them up is one of the most common errors. Use a ruler to read accurately, especially when the scales are fine.

Remember that the figures are averages over about thirty years. A particular January may be hotter or wetter than the graph shows. The graph describes what is typical, not what happened in a particular year.

Drawing a climate graph follows the same logic in reverse. Choose sensible scales that fit the largest values, label both vertical axes with units, draw the rainfall bars first in a light colour or shading, then plot the temperature points in the middle of each month and join them. Add a title and a key. A neatly drawn graph with correct scales and labels earns marks even before any interpretation.

Climate graphs are produced by the South African Weather Service for hundreds of stations, and similar graphs exist for every country. Once you know how to read one, you can understand the climate of any place on earth from a single page.

The questions for this section are with you now: the axes and labels of a climate graph, why temperature is a line and rainfall is bars, and how to read values accurately.

## Subtopic: Reading Station A

Here are rounded figures for Station A, a winter-rainfall station like Cape Town. Mean monthly temperatures from January to December: twenty-one, twenty-two, twenty, eighteen, sixteen, fourteen, thirteen, thirteen, fifteen, seventeen, nineteen and twenty degrees. Mean monthly rainfall from January to December: fifteen, seventeen, twenty, forty-one, sixty-nine, ninety-three, eighty-two, seventy-seven, forty, thirty, fourteen and seventeen millimetres.

Start with temperature. The highest point on the line is February, at twenty-two degrees. The lowest is July and August, at thirteen degrees. The annual temperature range is twenty-two minus thirteen, which is nine degrees. The mean annual temperature, found by adding all twelve values and dividing by twelve, is about seventeen degrees. Every month is above ten degrees, so winters are mild, and no month is above twenty-five, so summers are warm rather than hot.

Now rainfall. Add the twelve bars: fifteen plus seventeen plus twenty plus forty-one plus sixty-nine plus ninety-three plus eighty-two plus seventy-seven plus forty plus thirty plus fourteen plus seventeen gives a total of five hundred and fifteen millimetres. The wettest month is June, with ninety-three millimetres. The driest month is November, with fourteen. The four months from May to August together receive sixty-nine plus ninety-three plus eighty-two plus seventy-seven, which is three hundred and twenty-one millimetres. Divide three hundred and twenty-one by five hundred and fifteen, and you find that about sixty-two percent of the year's rain falls in those four winter months.

Put it together. Station A has warm, dry summers and mild, wet winters, with moderate annual rainfall and a moderate temperature range. That matches the Mediterranean climate type. Notice the key relationship on the graph: the tallest bars sit under the lowest part of the temperature line. When rain and cold coincide, the rain is falling in winter.

The explanation draws on what you learnt earlier in the term. In winter, the pressure belts shift north and cold fronts in the westerlies bring rain to the south-western Cape; in summer, the subtropical high pressure brings dry, sinking air. The moderate range reflects the influence of the nearby ocean.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading the highest and lowest temperatures, calculating the range, total and seasonal percentage, and identifying Station A's climate.

## Subtopic: Reading Station B and Comparing

Here are rounded figures for Station B, a summer-rainfall station like Pretoria. Mean monthly temperatures from January to December: twenty-two, twenty-two, twenty, seventeen, fourteen, eleven, eleven, fourteen, eighteen, twenty, twenty-one and twenty-two degrees. Mean monthly rainfall from January to December: one hundred and thirty-five, seventy-five, eighty, fifty, thirteen, seven, three, six, twenty-two, seventy, one hundred and one hundred and ten millimetres.

Temperature: the warmest months are January, February and December at twenty-two degrees; the coolest are June and July at eleven. The annual range is twenty-two minus eleven, which is eleven degrees, a little larger than Station A's nine degrees, which suggests a station further from the sea. The mean annual temperature is again about seventeen or eighteen degrees.

Rainfall: the total is six hundred and seventy-one millimetres. The wettest month is January, with one hundred and thirty-five millimetres; the driest is July, with only three. The six months from October to March receive seventy plus one hundred plus one hundred and ten plus one hundred and thirty-five plus seventy-five plus eighty, which is five hundred and seventy millimetres. Divide five hundred and seventy by six hundred and seventy-one and you find that about eighty-five percent of the rain falls in the summer half of the year. Winter is very dry.

Here the tallest bars sit under the highest part of the temperature line: rain and heat coincide, which means summer rainfall. Combined with warm summers, cool dry winters, an inland position and moderate rainfall, this points to the summer-rainfall temperate highland or subtropical climate of the South African interior. The explanation: summer heating causes convection, moist air flows from the Indian Ocean, and thunderstorms form; in winter, high pressure over the interior brings sinking, dry air.

Comparing the two graphs side by side is a powerful exam technique. Both stations have similar mean annual temperatures, about seventeen degrees, but their rainfall seasons are opposite. Station A has its rain when it is cool; Station B has its rain when it is warm. Station B's larger range suggests an inland position, and its higher rainfall total reflects moist air from the Indian Ocean in summer. Writing a comparison means using linking words such as whereas, while, both and however, and giving figures for both stations in the same sentence.

Climate graphs from other parts of the world follow the same method. A rainforest graph has a nearly flat temperature line near twenty-six degrees and tall bars in every month. A desert graph has tiny bars and often a high temperature line. A continental graph has a line that dips below zero in winter. A tundra graph has a line that only rises above zero for a few summer months.

One more habit makes interpretation easier: shade or bracket the wettest months on the graph before you write. Seeing the wet season marked visually helps you describe it accurately and avoid confusing it with the warm or cool season. Then write your answer in the order temperature, range, rainfall total, rainfall season, climate type, cause.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading Station B, calculating its seasonal percentage, and comparing the two stations.

## Subtopic: Writing the Answer and the Error Museum

Examiners reward answers that follow a clear structure. For a describe question, give four things with figures: the highest and lowest temperatures and the months, the annual range, the total rainfall, and the season of maximum rainfall with the wettest month. For an explain question, link each feature to a factor: latitude, distance from the sea, altitude, ocean currents, relief, or pressure belts and winds. For an identify question, name the climate type and justify it with two pieces of evidence from the graph.

Here is a model description for Station A: temperatures range from thirteen degrees in July and August to twenty-two degrees in February, an annual range of nine degrees; total rainfall is five hundred and fifteen millimetres, of which about sixty-two percent falls from May to August, with a peak of ninety-three millimetres in June; summers are warm and dry and winters mild and wet. And a model identification: this is a Mediterranean climate, because most rain falls in winter and summers are warm and dry.

Check your arithmetic. When you add twelve bars, add them in pairs or groups and check the total twice. When you calculate a percentage, divide the part by the whole and multiply by one hundred. When you calculate a range, always subtract the lowest from the highest, never the other way round.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: reading rainfall from the temperature axis or the other way round. Two: giving the range as the highest temperature only. Three: saying a station has summer rain just because it has some rain in summer; look for where most of it falls. Four: forgetting that the southern hemisphere's summer is December to February; a northern hemisphere graph has its warm months in June to August. Five: describing without figures; every description needs numbers with units.

Layout for marks in a climate graph question: one sentence for temperature, one for range, one for rainfall total and season, one identifying the type, and one or two explaining the cause, each with figures and units.

The questions for this section are with you now: structuring descriptions and explanations, checking arithmetic, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: what the graph shows, a winter-rain town, and a summer-rain town.

## Subtopic: A Line and Some Bars

A climate graph is a little picture of a place's weather over many years. Along the bottom are the twelve months. The line shows how warm it usually is each month. The bars show how much rain usually falls each month.

Read the line using the temperature numbers on one side. Read the bars using the rainfall numbers on the other side. Do not mix them up.

Look for two things: where is the line highest and lowest, and where are the tallest bars? Those two answers tell you most of the story of a place.

A tip: put your finger on a month at the bottom, slide it up to the line or the top of the bar, then slide sideways to the numbers. Use a ruler if the lines are close together. Always check you are reading the right side.

Hold the wavy line and the row of bars in mind and try the questions for this part: the parts of a climate graph.

## Subtopic: The Winter-Rain Town

Station A is like Cape Town. Its line goes from twenty-two degrees in February down to thirteen in July and August. Take thirteen away from twenty-two: the range is nine degrees.

Its tallest bars are in May, June, July and August, right where the line is lowest. So the rain comes when it is cool: winter rain. In total, it gets about five hundred and fifteen millimetres a year. That is a Mediterranean climate.

Why winter? In winter, cold fronts sweep in from the ocean and bring rain to the south-western Cape. In summer, high pressure sits over it, and the sky stays clear and dry. The nearby sea keeps the temperatures mild, so the range is small.

Hold the tall bars under the low line in mind and try this part's questions: reading Station A.

## Subtopic: The Summer-Rain Town

Station B is like Pretoria. Its tallest bars are from October to March, right under the highest part of the line. So the rain comes when it is hot: summer rain. Its winter bars are tiny, only three millimetres in July.

Its range is twenty-two minus eleven: eleven degrees. It gets about six hundred and seventy-one millimetres a year.

Why summer? In summer the ground heats up, warm wet air from the Indian Ocean rises, and afternoon thunderstorms pour down. In winter, dry high pressure sits over the interior, so there is almost no rain. Station B's bigger range shows it is further from the sea.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A climate graph shows mean monthly temperature as a line and mean monthly rainfall as bars. Read the highest and lowest temperatures, subtract to find the range, add the bars for the total, and look where the tallest bars fall. Tall bars under a low line mean winter rain, as at Station A with five hundred and fifteen millimetres. Tall bars under a high line mean summer rain, as at Station B with six hundred and seventy-one. Always give figures with units.

Hold the line, the bars and the two towns, and take the final questions of the lesson: reading climate graphs.
