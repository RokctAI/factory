### subtopic_1 — The Parts of a Climate Graph
**Assistant:** Why do some climate graphs show two temperature lines instead of one?

**Tutor:** Because two lines, one for the mean daily maximum and one for the mean daily minimum, give more information than a single mean line, especially about how much temperatures change between day and night. A single line usually shows the mean monthly temperature, which is the average of the daily maximums and minimums for that month. It tells you how warm a month is overall, but it hides the daily range. Two places can have the same mean temperature for July, say twelve degrees, but very different daily experiences. In a coastal town, July might have maximums of seventeen and minimums of seven, a daily range of ten degrees. In an inland desert town, July might have maximums of twenty and minimums of four, a daily range of sixteen degrees, with frost on some nights. Their single mean lines would look almost identical, but a graph with maximum and minimum lines would show a much wider gap for the inland town. This matters for interpretation. A wide gap between the lines suggests a continental or desert location, dry air and clear skies, and possibly a high altitude. A narrow gap suggests a maritime location, humid air and cloud cover. The minimum line is especially important for farmers, because if it falls near zero, frost is likely, which affects which crops can be grown. The maximum line matters for heat stress, water demand and comfort. When you are given a graph with two lines, describe both: give the highest maximum and the lowest minimum, the months in which they occur, and comment on the daily range in summer and winter. Then explain the pattern using factors such as distance from the sea and humidity. This extra detail can earn you additional marks in a describe-and-explain question.

### subtopic_2 — Reading Station A
**Assistant:** How can I tell from a climate graph whether a place is in the northern or southern hemisphere?

**Tutor:** By looking at when the warmest and coolest months occur on the temperature line. The seasons are reversed in the two hemispheres. In the southern hemisphere, including South Africa, summer is from December to February and winter from June to August, so the temperature line is highest at the beginning and end of the year and dips in the middle, forming a U shape or a valley. In the northern hemisphere, summer is from June to August and winter from December to February, so the temperature line is lowest at the beginning and end of the year and rises to a peak in the middle, forming an upside-down U, or a hill. If you see the warmest months in December, January and February, the station is in the southern hemisphere; if the warmest months are June, July and August, it is in the northern hemisphere. This test works well for places outside the tropics, where seasons are clear. It is harder for stations close to the equator, where the temperature line is almost flat, because the annual range may be only one or two degrees. In that case, look at the rainfall pattern instead. In tropical savanna climates, the wet season follows the overhead sun, so a southern hemisphere savanna station has its wet season from about November to March, while a northern hemisphere one has it from about May to September. A rainforest station near the equator may have two rainier periods, around March and September, when the sun is overhead at the equator, and you may not be able to tell the hemisphere from temperature at all. The title of the graph usually gives the latitude, which settles the question, but examiners sometimes remove it to test your reasoning. So always look for the shape of the line first, and then the timing of the rain.

### subtopic_3 — Reading Station B and Comparing
**Assistant:** Why do examiners care so much about giving figures with units?

**Tutor:** Because figures with units turn a vague description into precise, checkable evidence, and precise use of evidence is a core skill in Geography. Consider two answers. The first says: Station A is hot in summer and cold in winter and gets a lot of rain in winter. The second says: Station A's temperatures range from thirteen degrees Celsius in July and August to twenty-two degrees Celsius in February, an annual range of nine degrees; it receives five hundred and fifteen millimetres of rain a year, about sixty-two percent of it from May to August. The first answer is not wrong, but it is vague and partly misleading. Is twenty-two degrees hot? Compared with a desert, not really. Is thirteen degrees cold? Compared with Moscow, certainly not. Is five hundred and fifteen millimetres a lot of rain? Compared with a rainforest, it is quite little. The second answer lets the reader judge for themselves and shows that the student has actually read the graph accurately. Units matter because numbers without units are meaningless or ambiguous: twenty-two could be degrees, millimetres, or percent. Examiners usually award a mark for each correct figure, and often lose marks for missing units. Figures with units also allow comparison. If you say Station A has a range of nine degrees and Station B a range of eleven degrees, the reader can see immediately which has the more extreme climate. Finally, using figures is a habit that will serve you in many subjects and in life: scientists, economists, journalists and engineers all need to support claims with accurate, clearly labelled data. That is why climate graph questions are a good training ground for evidence-based thinking.

### subtopic_4 — Writing the Answer and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What can a climate graph not tell me?

**Tutor:** Quite a lot, which is why geographers use climate graphs alongside other information. First, a climate graph shows averages, so it hides variability. A station with an average of six hundred millimetres might receive four hundred in a drought year and nine hundred in a wet year. In southern Africa, where rainfall varies a great deal from year to year, this variability is often more important for farmers than the average. Second, it hides extremes. The graph does not show the hottest day on record, the most intense rainstorm, the longest dry spell, or how often frost or hail occur. Third, it usually does not show how the rain falls. Six hundred millimetres falling as gentle, soaking rain over many days is very different from the same amount falling in a few violent thunderstorms, which cause runoff, flooding and soil erosion. Fourth, a standard graph does not show other elements of climate such as humidity, wind, sunshine hours, evaporation or cloud cover, all of which affect how a climate feels and how much water is available to plants. For example, two places with the same rainfall may have very different evaporation rates, so one may be much drier in practice. Fifth, a climate graph applies to one point. It cannot show how climate changes across a valley or a mountain range nearby. Sixth, a graph based on one thirty-year period cannot by itself show long-term change. Comparing graphs for different periods, such as 1961 to 1990 and 1991 to 2020, can reveal trends like warming. So a climate graph is an excellent summary, but a full understanding of a place's climate needs data on variability, extremes and other elements, together with maps and local knowledge.
