# Part 1 — Expert

This session completes the survey of world climate types by examining the drier, colder and more extreme climates: desert, semi-desert, continental, Mediterranean, polar, tundra and high mountain. The anchor is a set of contrasting places: Upington and the Namib for dry climates, Cape Town for the Mediterranean climate, Moscow for the continental climate, northern Canada and Siberia for the tundra, Antarctica for the polar climate, and the Andes and the Lesotho highlands for high mountain climates. The governing idea is that each of these climates results from a particular combination of the factors you have studied, latitude, distance from the sea, ocean currents, pressure belts and altitude, and that each produces a characteristic environment and way of life. Describing and explaining these climate types is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Desert and Semi-desert Climates

A desert climate is one in which so little rain falls, usually less than about two hundred and fifty millimetres a year, that very little vegetation can grow, and in which evaporation is much greater than precipitation. Deserts can be hot or cold, but the best-known deserts are hot ones in the subtropics.

Hot deserts lie mainly between about fifteen and thirty-five degrees north and south, in the belts of subtropical high pressure where air sinks, warms and dries. Many lie on the western sides of continents, beside cold ocean currents, which add to the dryness. Examples include the Sahara, the largest hot desert in the world, the Arabian Desert, the Namib along the coast of Namibia, the Kalahari in its driest south-western part, the Atacama in Chile, and the deserts of central and western Australia. Rainfall is very low and unreliable: some years may have no rain at all, and then a single storm can bring a large part of the yearly total. Temperatures in hot inland deserts are extreme: summer days can exceed forty-five degrees, while clear skies and dry air let heat escape quickly at night, so the daily range can exceed twenty degrees. Coastal deserts beside cold currents, such as the Namib, are cooler and foggy.

Semi-desert, or steppe, climates lie on the edges of deserts, where a little more rain falls, typically between about two hundred and fifty and five hundred millimetres a year. Rain is still unreliable, and droughts are common. The Karoo and much of the Northern Cape are semi-desert, as is the Sahel, the belt south of the Sahara across West Africa. Natural vegetation is low shrubs, short grasses and succulents, which can survive long dry spells.

Upington, on the Orange River in the Northern Cape, has a hot semi-arid to arid climate. Its annual rainfall is about one hundred and ninety millimetres, falling mostly in late summer and autumn in a few storms. January days average about thirty-five degrees, while July nights average close to freezing, with frequent frost. Farmers there depend on irrigation from the Orange River to grow grapes for raisins and wine, while the surrounding dry land supports only sheep and goats at low densities.

Desert plants and animals have remarkable adaptations. Succulents store water in thick leaves or stems; many plants have deep roots, small leaves or waxy coatings; some seeds remain dormant for years until rain falls, then germinate, flower and set seed within weeks, which is why Namaqualand bursts into flower after good rains. Animals such as gemsbok can survive long periods without drinking, getting moisture from plants, and many desert animals are active only at night.

The questions for this section are with you now: what defines a desert and a semi-desert, why hot deserts form where they do, and how life adapts.

## Subtopic: Mediterranean and Continental Climates

The Mediterranean climate is found on the western sides of continents at about thirty to forty-five degrees north and south. It has hot or warm, dry summers and mild, wet winters. It is named after the lands around the Mediterranean Sea, such as Italy, Greece, Spain and the coast of North Africa, but it also occurs in California, central Chile, south-western Australia around Perth and Adelaide, and the south-western Cape of South Africa.

Cape Town is South Africa's best example. Its January mean is about twenty-one degrees and its July mean about twelve or thirteen degrees. Annual rainfall at the airport is about five hundred millimetres, with most of it falling from May to August, and summers are dry, sunny and often windy with the south-easter. The cause is the seasonal shift of the pressure belts. In summer, the subtropical high-pressure belt lies over the region, bringing sinking, dry air. In winter, the belt shifts north, and the westerly winds with their cold fronts reach the south-western Cape, bringing rain. The mountains around Cape Town greatly increase rainfall on their windward slopes.

Natural vegetation in Mediterranean regions is adapted to summer drought: evergreen shrubs with small, tough, leathery leaves, such as the fynbos of the Cape Floral Kingdom, the smallest but richest of the world's six floral kingdoms, with about nine thousand plant species, most found nowhere else. Fynbos depends on periodic fires to regenerate. Farming includes wheat in winter, and vineyards, olives and deciduous fruit such as apples, pears and plums, which need cold winters and dry summers.

The continental climate is found in the interiors of large northern hemisphere continents, in North America, Europe and Asia, between about forty and sixty-five degrees north, far from the moderating influence of the sea. It has very large annual temperature ranges: warm or hot summers and long, cold winters with snow. Moscow's January mean is about minus six degrees and its July mean about nineteen, a range of about twenty-five degrees, and annual precipitation is about seven hundred millimetres, with a summer maximum and winter snow. Further inland and further north, ranges grow even larger. The southern hemisphere has no true continental climate of this kind, because its continents are too narrow at these latitudes. Natural vegetation includes grasslands, such as the prairies and steppes, and the vast coniferous forests, called taiga, of Canada and Russia.

Mediterranean regions share particular challenges. Because rain falls in the cool season when plants grow slowly, and summers are long and dry, water must be stored in dams through the summer, and irrigation is essential for many crops. Hot, dry, windy summers also bring a high risk of wildfire, as seen in California, Greece, Australia and the mountains of the Western Cape, where fires in fynbos are a natural part of the ecosystem but can threaten homes built close to the vegetation. Tourists are drawn to the long, sunny summers, which supports the tourism industries of Cape Town and southern Europe.

The questions for this section are with you now: Mediterranean climates and Cape Town, fynbos, and continental climates and Moscow.

## Subtopic: Polar, Tundra and High Mountain Climates

Polar climates are found at high latitudes, in Antarctica, Greenland and the Arctic Ocean, where the sun is low in the sky or below the horizon for months. The ice cap climate has no month with a mean temperature above zero degrees. Antarctica is the coldest place on earth: the lowest temperature ever recorded there, at the Russian Vostok station in 1983, was about minus eighty-nine degrees. Precipitation is very low, often less than one hundred millimetres a year in the interior, falling as snow, so Antarctica is technically a cold desert. The ice that covers it has built up over hundreds of thousands of years because snow rarely melts. South Africa operates a research base in Antarctica called SANAE IV, and a research station on Marion Island in the sub-Antarctic.

The tundra climate lies between the ice caps and the forests, around the Arctic Ocean in northern Canada, Alaska, Scandinavia and Siberia. Its warmest month averages between zero and ten degrees, so there is a short, cool summer, and its winters are long and very cold. The ground below the surface stays permanently frozen, which is called permafrost; in summer only the top layer thaws, creating boggy ground, lakes and swarms of insects. Precipitation is low. Trees cannot grow because the summer is too short and cold and the roots cannot penetrate the permafrost, so vegetation consists of mosses, lichens, grasses, low shrubs and wild flowers. Animals include caribou, Arctic foxes, lemmings and many migrating birds. Indigenous peoples such as the Inuit and the Sámi have long lived in these regions, herding, hunting and fishing.

High mountain climates, also called highland climates, are found on high mountains and plateaus anywhere in the world, such as the Andes, the Himalayas, the Rocky Mountains, the Ethiopian Highlands and the Lesotho highlands. Because temperature falls with altitude, these climates change rapidly with height, creating zones of climate and vegetation stacked one above another, from forests at the base through grasslands to bare rock, ice and snow at the summit. Weather changes quickly, sunshine is intense, and nights are cold. In the Andes, farmers grow different crops at different heights: tropical fruit in the valleys, maize higher up, potatoes and quinoa higher still, and herders keep llamas and alpacas on the highest pastures.

Lesotho's highlands, above about two thousand five hundred metres, have cold winters with regular snow and cool summers with heavy thunderstorms, and they are the source of the Orange River and of water transferred to Gauteng through the Lesotho Highlands Water Project.

These cold regions matter to people far away. The ice sheets of Antarctica and Greenland store most of the world's fresh water, and if they melt, sea levels rise along every coast, including South Africa's.

The questions for this section are with you now: polar ice caps and Antarctica, the tundra and permafrost, and high mountain climates.

## Subtopic: Recognising Climates and the Error Museum

To recognise a climate from data, look first at temperature, then at rainfall. If the coolest month is above about eighteen degrees, it is tropical. If total rain is below about two hundred and fifty millimetres, it is desert; between about two hundred and fifty and five hundred, semi-desert. If the rain falls mainly in winter and summers are dry and warm, it is Mediterranean. If there is a large annual range with a cold winter well below zero and a warm summer, it is continental. If the warmest month is below ten degrees but above zero, it is tundra; if no month is above zero, it is polar. If temperatures are much lower than latitude suggests and change rapidly with height, think of high mountain.

Here is a quick check using figures. A station has January twenty-one degrees, July twelve degrees, and five hundred millimetres of rain, of which three hundred and sixty fall from May to August. That is seventy-two percent in four winter months, since three hundred and sixty divided by five hundred is zero point seven two: a Mediterranean climate, like Cape Town's. Another station has January minus six degrees, July nineteen degrees and snow in winter: a range of twenty-five degrees, a continental climate, like Moscow's.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying all deserts are hot; Antarctica and the Gobi are cold deserts. Two: saying Cape Town gets summer rain; it has a Mediterranean winter-rainfall climate. Three: thinking there are continental climates in South Africa like Moscow's; the southern hemisphere lacks the large landmasses at those latitudes, though the interior has continental features. Four: confusing tundra with polar; the tundra has a short summer above zero. Five: saying high mountain climates exist only in cold countries; they occur on high ground everywhere, even at the equator.

Layout for marks in a describe question: name the climate, locate it, describe temperatures and rainfall with figures, explain the main cause, and describe the vegetation and one human use.

The questions for this section are with you now: recognising climates from data, the percentage check, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: dry places, winter rain and cold insides, and the frozen and high places.

## Subtopic: Dry Places

A desert is a place with very little rain, less than about two hundred and fifty millimetres a year. The Sahara and the Namib are deserts. Hot deserts can reach over forty-five degrees in the day and then get cold at night, because there are no clouds to keep the heat in.

A semi-desert gets a little more rain, but still not much, like the Karoo. Upington gets about one hundred and ninety millimetres. Plants there store water in fat leaves, like succulents, and some seeds wait for years until rain comes, then flower in a rush.

People in dry places plan carefully for water. Farmers along the Orange River use its water to grow grapes, and in the dry Karoo, windmills pump water from underground for sheep.

Hold the cracked ground and the fat succulent leaves in mind and try the questions for this part: deserts and semi-deserts.

## Subtopic: Winter Rain and Frozen Winters

Cape Town is different from most of South Africa. It gets its rain in winter, from cold fronts, and its summers are dry and sunny. That is called a Mediterranean climate, like Italy and Greece. Fynbos grows there, and farmers grow grapes, wheat and apples.

Far away, in the middle of Russia, Moscow has a continental climate: summers are warm, about nineteen degrees, but winters are freezing, about minus six on average, with lots of snow. That happens because it is so far from the sea.

Hold the wet Cape winter and the snowy Moscow street in mind and try this part's questions: Mediterranean and continental climates.

## Subtopic: Ice, Tundra and Mountain Tops

At the poles, it is freezing all year. Antarctica once reached about minus eighty-nine degrees. Next to the ice is the tundra, a wide, flat land with a short, cool summer, frozen ground underneath, and only moss and small plants, no trees.

High mountains have their own climate, even near the equator: the higher you go, the colder it gets, from forests at the bottom to snow at the top. Lesotho's mountains get snow every winter.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Deserts get less than about two hundred and fifty millimetres of rain and semi-deserts about two hundred and fifty to five hundred. The Mediterranean climate, as in Cape Town, has dry summers and wet winters. Continental climates, as in Moscow, have warm summers and very cold winters. Polar climates are below freezing all year; the tundra has a short cool summer and permafrost. High mountain climates change with height.

Hold the desert, the winter rain, the snowy city and the ice, and take the final questions of the lesson: the world's other climates.
