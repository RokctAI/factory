### subtopic_1 — Desert and Semi-desert Climates
**Assistant:** If Antarctica is covered in ice, how can it be a desert?

**Tutor:** Because a desert is defined by how little precipitation falls, not by heat or sand, and Antarctica receives extremely little. Geographers define a desert as a region that receives very low precipitation, usually less than about two hundred and fifty millimetres a year, so that little vegetation can grow. Most people picture hot, sandy deserts like the Sahara, but the definition includes cold deserts too. In the interior of Antarctica, the annual snowfall is equivalent to less than fifty millimetres of water in many places, which is less than falls in parts of the Sahara. The reason is the extreme cold. Very cold air can hold very little water vapour, so there is little moisture available to form snow. The continent is also dominated by high pressure over the ice sheet, with dense, cold air sinking and flowing outward towards the coast, which prevents moist ocean air from penetrating inland. So why is Antarctica covered in ice up to more than four kilometres thick? Because although very little snow falls each year, almost none of it melts. The temperatures are so low that the snow accumulates year after year, compressing into ice over hundreds of thousands of years. Scientists drill ice cores from Antarctica that contain layers of snow going back more than eight hundred thousand years, with tiny bubbles of ancient air that reveal past climates. There are also the Dry Valleys of Antarctica, areas where almost no snow lies at all, because strong, dry winds sweep the snow away and the ice sheet is held back by mountains. These are among the driest places on earth. So Antarctica is both the coldest and, in its interior, one of the driest continents, a cold desert covered in ice that built up slowly over vast periods of time.

### subtopic_2 — Mediterranean and Continental Climates
**Assistant:** Why does Cape Town have a Mediterranean climate when the rest of South Africa gets summer rain?

**Tutor:** Because Cape Town lies at the south-western tip of the continent, the one part of South Africa that the westerly winds and their cold fronts regularly reach in winter, while the summer rain-bearing systems of the rest of the country rarely reach it. In summer, the subtropical high-pressure belt over the South Atlantic Ocean, called the South Atlantic High, extends towards the south-western Cape. Air sinks under high pressure, warming and drying, so clouds and rain are rare, and the south-easter blows strongly. The moist air from the Indian Ocean that brings summer thunderstorms to the interior and the east does not reach the south-west, because it has lost its moisture crossing the country and because the mountains of the Cape Fold Belt block it. The cold Benguela Current offshore also stabilises the air. In winter, the whole system of pressure belts moves north, following the sun. The westerly wind belt, with its series of cold fronts moving eastward across the Southern Ocean, moves far enough north to sweep across the south-western Cape. Each front brings north-westerly winds, cloud and rain, followed by cold south-westerly winds. The mountains around Cape Town greatly increase the rain through orographic lifting. So Cape Town receives most of its rain from May to August. The same arrangement occurs at similar latitudes on the western sides of other continents: California, central Chile, south-western Australia, and the Mediterranean itself. The pattern is so consistent that the Mediterranean climate is often called the west coast subtropical climate. For people in Cape Town, this means that dams fill in winter and must last through the dry summer, which is why the drought of 2015 to 2018, when several winters brought poor rain, created such a severe water crisis.

### subtopic_3 — Polar, Tundra and High Mountain Climates
**Assistant:** How is climate change affecting the tundra?

**Tutor:** The tundra is warming faster than almost anywhere else on earth, and this is causing major changes to the land, ecosystems and people, with effects that reach the whole world. Scientists have found that the Arctic has warmed about three to four times faster than the global average over recent decades. One reason is a feedback involving snow and ice. Bright snow and ice reflect much of the sunlight that falls on them; when they melt, they expose darker ground or ocean, which absorbs more sunlight, which causes more warming and more melting. As the tundra warms, permafrost begins to thaw. Thawing permafrost makes the ground unstable, damaging roads, buildings, pipelines and airstrips in northern towns, and causing land to slump and coastlines to erode. Permafrost also contains huge amounts of frozen organic matter, the remains of plants and animals that have been frozen for thousands of years. When it thaws, microbes break this material down and release carbon dioxide and methane, both greenhouse gases, which can add to global warming, another feedback. Ecosystems are changing too. Shrubs are spreading northward into areas that were once mosses and grasses, and the tree line, where forests give way to tundra, is moving north in some places. Animals adapted to cold, such as Arctic foxes and caribou, face new competitors and changing food supplies. Sea ice along the Arctic coast is forming later and melting earlier, affecting polar bears, seals and the traditional hunting of Indigenous communities such as the Inuit, whose knowledge of the ice is passed down through generations. The tundra's changes are monitored closely because they affect sea levels, ocean currents and the global climate, which in turn affect places as far away as South Africa.

### subtopic_4 — Recognising Climates and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why are mountain climates so different from one valley to the next?

**Tutor:** Because in mountains, several climate factors change over very short distances: altitude, slope aspect, exposure to wind and moisture, and the drainage of cold air. In flat country, a few kilometres make little difference to climate. In mountains, a short walk can take you up or down hundreds of metres, which changes the temperature by several degrees according to the lapse rate. One valley may face the moist wind and receive orographic rain, while the next, sheltered behind a ridge, lies in a rain shadow. A north-facing slope in the southern hemisphere receives far more sunshine than a south-facing slope nearby, especially in winter, so one side may be warm and grassy while the other is cool and shaded, with snow lasting longer. At night, cold, dense air drains down slopes into valley floors, creating frost hollows and inversions, so valley bottoms can be colder than slopes above. Narrow valleys can channel winds, making them stronger, while sheltered basins can be calm. In the Lesotho highlands and the Drakensberg, all of these effects can be seen within a day's walk: snow on high south-facing slopes, warm sunny north-facing slopes, misty windward valleys and dry leeward ones. People in mountain regions have long understood these microclimates. They choose sheltered, sunny slopes for villages and fields, place animal kraals where cold air does not collect, and move herds between high summer pastures and lower winter grazing, as Basotho herders do with their cattle posts in the high mountains. For geographers, mountain climates are a reminder that the climate types on a world map are generalisations, and that local relief can create a patchwork of different conditions within a single region.
