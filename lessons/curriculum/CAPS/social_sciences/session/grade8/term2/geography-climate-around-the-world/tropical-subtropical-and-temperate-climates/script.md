# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the main kinds of climate found around the world, beginning with tropical, subtropical and temperate climates. The anchor is a journey southwards from the equator: from the rainforest of the Congo Basin, through the savanna of southern Africa, to the humid subtropical coast of KwaZulu-Natal, and, for comparison, to the temperate climate of north-western Europe. The governing idea is that climates can be grouped into types based mainly on their temperature and rainfall patterns through the year, that these patterns are controlled largely by latitude and the other factors you have studied, and that each climate type supports characteristic vegetation and ways of life. Describing and recognising these climate types is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: How Climates Are Classified

There are thousands of weather stations around the world, each with its own climate statistics. To make sense of them, geographers group places with similar climates into climate types or regions. The most widely used system was developed by the scientist Wladimir Köppen in the early twentieth century and later improved. It is based on two main things: temperature, especially the mean temperature of the warmest and coolest months, and precipitation, especially the total amount of rain and the season in which it falls. Köppen also noticed that natural vegetation matches climate closely, so he used vegetation zones to help draw his boundaries.

School geography in South Africa uses a simplified version with names that describe each climate: tropical, subtropical, temperate, desert, semi-desert, continental, Mediterranean, polar, tundra and high mountain. This lesson deals with the first three, which together cover a large part of the world's inhabited land, and the next lesson deals with the others.

When classifying a climate, ask four questions. First, how hot is it, and does it have a cold season? Second, how large is the annual temperature range? Third, how much rain falls in a year? Fourth, when does the rain fall: all year, mainly in summer, or mainly in winter? The answers usually point to one climate type.

Remember that climate boundaries are not sharp lines drawn by nature. Climates change gradually across the land, and maps simplify this by drawing boundaries where averages cross certain values. Local factors, such as mountains, ocean currents and altitude, also create small pockets of different climate within a larger region. Johannesburg, for example, lies in a region that would be subtropical at sea level, but its altitude gives it a temperate highland climate.

Climate classification has many practical uses. Farmers and agricultural scientists use it to decide which crops and livestock suit a region. Architects use it to design buildings that stay comfortable. Health authorities use it to predict where diseases such as malaria, which depends on warm, wet conditions for mosquitoes, are likely to occur. Planners use it to estimate water supply.

## Subtopic: Tropical Climates

Tropical climates are found mainly between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn, at low latitudes, where the sun is high in the sky all year. Their defining feature is that every month is hot, with a mean temperature above about eighteen degrees, so there is no cold season. They differ mainly in their rainfall.

The tropical rainforest climate, also called the equatorial climate, is found close to the equator, in the Congo Basin, the Amazon Basin and parts of South-East Asia. Temperatures are high and very even, around twenty-six to twenty-seven degrees in every month, so the annual range is only one to three degrees, often smaller than the range between day and night. Rainfall is heavy, usually more than two thousand millimetres a year, and falls in every month, often in afternoon thunderstorms caused by intense heating and rising air. Kisangani in the Democratic Republic of the Congo receives about one thousand eight hundred millimetres a year with no dry month. The vegetation is dense, evergreen rainforest with very high biodiversity.

The tropical savanna climate, also called the tropical wet and dry climate, is found further from the equator, roughly between five and twenty degrees north and south, across much of Africa, including parts of Zambia, Zimbabwe, Mozambique, northern Botswana and the far north of South Africa, as well as northern Australia, India and Brazil. It is hot all year, though with a slightly cooler season, and it has a distinct wet summer and dry winter. The rain follows the overhead sun: when the sun is overhead in summer, rising air brings storms, and when it moves away, the dry season begins. Annual rainfall is typically five hundred to one thousand five hundred millimetres. Natural vegetation is savanna, grassland with scattered trees such as acacias and baobabs, home to large herds of grazing animals, as in the Serengeti and the Kruger National Park.

The tropical monsoon climate, found in parts of South Asia, South-East Asia and West Africa, has a very wet season caused by seasonal winds from the ocean and a short dry season. Mumbai in India receives more than two thousand millimetres, most of it between June and September.

People in tropical climates adapt in many ways: houses with wide eaves, raised floors and good ventilation; crops such as cassava, yams, bananas, cocoa, coffee and rice; and seasonal patterns of farming and herding in the savanna that follow the rains.

Tropical climates also contain some of the world's fastest-growing cities, such as Lagos, Kinshasa, Dar es Salaam and Mumbai. Heavy tropical rain can overwhelm drainage systems and cause floods, while heat and humidity increase the demand for water and electricity. Planning for these climates is a major challenge for governments.

The questions for this section are with you now: the characteristics of tropical climates, rainforest, savanna and monsoon types, and where they are found.

## Subtopic: Subtropical and Temperate Climates

Subtropical climates lie just outside the tropics, roughly between twenty and thirty-five degrees north and south. They have hot summers and mild winters, with a noticeable but not severe cool season. Winter months typically average between about ten and eighteen degrees, and frost is rare at low altitudes.

The humid subtropical climate is found on the eastern sides of continents in these latitudes, where warm ocean currents and onshore winds bring moisture. Examples include the KwaZulu-Natal coast and the north-eastern Eastern Cape in South Africa, south-eastern Brazil, the south-eastern United States, eastern Australia around Brisbane, and southern China around Hong Kong. Durban is a good South African example: January mean about twenty-four and a half degrees, July mean about seventeen degrees, and about one thousand millimetres of rain, mostly in summer but with some in every month. The natural vegetation includes subtropical forest and grassland, and crops include sugar cane, bananas, citrus, pineapples and subtropical fruit.

Temperate climates are found in the middle latitudes, roughly between thirty-five and sixty degrees. They have distinct seasons, with warm or cool summers and cool or cold winters, but without the extremes of continental or polar climates. The temperate oceanic climate, also called the maritime or west coast marine climate, is found on the western sides of continents in the belt of westerly winds, where moist air from the ocean brings rain throughout the year and the sea moderates temperatures. North-western Europe is the classic example. London has a January mean of about five degrees and a July mean of about nineteen degrees, with about six hundred millimetres of rain spread across all months, often as light rain or drizzle. Other examples include New Zealand, Tasmania and the Pacific coast of North America around Vancouver. Natural vegetation is deciduous or mixed forest, and farming includes dairy cattle, wheat, barley and vegetables.

In South Africa, the southern Cape coast around George and Knysna has a mild climate with rain in all seasons, sometimes described as warm temperate, and the highveld has a temperate highland climate, warm summers and cool to cold dry winters, caused by altitude rather than latitude.

Another subtropical type is worth knowing, although it is covered with the other climates in the next lesson: the Mediterranean climate, with hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters, found on the western sides of continents in these latitudes, including Cape Town. Notice the contrast: on the eastern side of a continent, the subtropics are humid with summer rain; on the western side, they are dry in summer with winter rain. The difference comes from the ocean currents and the seasonal shift of the pressure belts, factors you studied earlier in the term.

Temperate climates also include a cooler, drier type deeper inside continents, which grades into the continental climate. As you move inland from the coast of Europe towards Russia, winters become colder, summers warmer and rainfall lower, showing how distance from the sea gradually changes a temperate oceanic climate into a continental one.

## Subtopic: Comparing Climates and the Error Museum

The best way to understand these climates is to compare their figures side by side. Kisangani, tropical rainforest: warmest month about twenty-six degrees, coolest about twenty-five, range about one degree, rain about one thousand eight hundred millimetres, no dry season. A tropical savanna station such as Kano in northern Nigeria: warmest month about thirty-one degrees, coolest about twenty-two, range about nine degrees, rain about eight hundred millimetres, almost all from June to September. Durban, humid subtropical: warmest about twenty-four and a half, coolest about seventeen, range about seven and a half degrees, rain about one thousand millimetres, mostly summer. London, temperate oceanic: warmest about nineteen, coolest about five, range about fourteen degrees, rain about six hundred millimetres, spread evenly.

Notice the pattern: as latitude increases, the annual temperature range generally grows, and the rainfall pattern changes from all-year to a summer maximum and then, in the westerly belt, back to all-year rain of a different kind.

Climate types also shape vegetation, which you can see in a quick check. Rainforest needs heat and rain in all months; savanna grass and trees survive a long dry season; subtropical forests grow where frost is rare and rain is reliable; temperate deciduous trees drop their leaves to survive cold winters.

A useful exercise is to place South African towns into these families. Musina and Skukuza, in the hot far north and Lowveld, have savanna climates with summer rain and warm dry winters. Durban and Richards Bay have humid subtropical climates. George and Knysna have mild all-year rainfall climates. Johannesburg, Pretoria and Bloemfontein have temperate highland climates. Cape Town, Upington and Sutherland belong to other families, which you will meet in the next lesson.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying all tropical climates are wet all year; the savanna has a long dry season. Two: thinking tropical means only near the equator; tropical climates extend to about the tropics. Three: calling Johannesburg subtropical; its altitude gives it a temperate highland climate. Four: confusing subtropical with tropical; subtropical places have a mild cool season. Five: believing temperate means pleasant all year; temperate climates can have cold, grey winters.

Layout for marks in a climate type question: name the type, give its location by latitude and continent side, describe temperature with figures and the range, describe rainfall with total and season, explain the cause briefly, and give an example place and vegetation.

## Subtopic: Sorting Climates

There are thousands of weather stations on earth. To make sense of them, geographers sort places with similar weather patterns into families called climate types.

To sort a place, ask: Is it hot all year, or does it have a cold season? How big is the difference between summer and winter? How much rain falls? And when does the rain fall?

Think of it like sorting a pile of clothes. You do not need a separate drawer for every shirt; you group them by type. Geographers do the same with climates, so that a place in Brazil and a place in Mozambique with the same pattern end up in the same family, even though they are thousands of kilometres apart.

Hold the four questions, about heat, range, amount of rain and season of rain, in mind and try the questions for this part: how climates are classified.

## Subtopic: The Hot Tropics

Near the equator, in the Congo and the Amazon, it is hot and wet every single month, about twenty-six degrees, with storms most afternoons. That is the tropical rainforest climate, and it grows thick, green forest.

A little further from the equator, it is still hot, but there is a wet summer and a long dry winter. That is the tropical savanna climate: grassland with scattered trees, like the Kruger Park and the Serengeti, full of zebras and elephants.

People live differently in each. In the rainforest, houses are built on stilts to stay dry, and crops like cassava and bananas grow all year. In the savanna, farmers plant when the rains come, store grain for the dry months, and herders move cattle to find grass and water.

Hold the dripping rainforest and the dry savanna in mind and try this part's questions: tropical climates.

## Subtopic: Subtropical and Temperate

Just outside the tropics, it is hot in summer and mild in winter. That is subtropical, like Durban, with about one thousand millimetres of rain, mostly in summer, and sugar cane in the fields.

Further from the equator, in places like London, summers are cool, winters are chilly, and light rain falls in any month. That is temperate oceanic.

Altitude can change the family, too. Johannesburg is at the same latitude as some subtropical places, but because it is so high, it has cold winter nights and a temperate climate.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Climates are grouped by temperature and rainfall. Tropical climates are hot all year: rainforest is wet every month, savanna has wet summers and dry winters, and monsoon climates have a very wet season. Subtropical climates, like Durban's, have hot summers and mild winters. Temperate climates, like London's, have distinct seasons without great extremes, and oceanic types get rain all year. Altitude can change the type, as on the highveld.
