### subtopic_1 — How Climates Are Classified
**Assistant:** Why does the rainforest get rain almost every afternoon?

**Tutor:** Because the daily cycle of heating near the equator produces convection storms in a regular rhythm. In the equatorial regions, the sun is high in the sky every day of the year, and the air is very humid because of evaporation from the forest, rivers and wet ground, and because of moist winds converging from both hemispheres. Each morning, the sun heats the ground and the lowest layer of air. By late morning, warm, moist air begins to rise in strong currents. As it rises, it cools, and its abundant water vapour condenses into cumulus clouds, which grow rapidly into tall cumulonimbus clouds by early afternoon. Heavy downpours follow, often with thunder and lightning, typically in the mid to late afternoon. After the storm, and as the sun sets, the air cools and the clouds break up, and the night is often clear and calm, with high humidity and dew. The next morning the cycle begins again. Because the sun's position changes little through the year, this daily pattern happens in almost every month, which is why the rainforest has no real dry season and why its annual temperature range is so small. The forest itself helps the process: trees release enormous amounts of water vapour through their leaves, a process called transpiration, so a large share of the rain that falls in the Amazon and Congo basins is recycled from the forest itself. This is one reason scientists worry about deforestation: if large areas of forest are cleared, less water vapour is released, which can reduce rainfall and make the remaining forest drier. People who live in rainforest regions often plan their day around the afternoon rain, doing outdoor work in the morning.

### subtopic_2 — Tropical Climates
**Assistant:** Why is Durban subtropical but Johannesburg not, when Johannesburg is further north?

**Tutor:** Because altitude changes Johannesburg's climate so much that it no longer fits the subtropical type, even though its latitude would otherwise put it there. By latitude alone, both cities lie in the subtropical belt, roughly between twenty and thirty-five degrees south. Durban is at sea level, beside the warm Agulhas Current, with onshore winds bringing moist air. Its winters are mild, with July averaging about seventeen degrees and no frost, and its summers are hot and humid. That fits the humid subtropical climate perfectly. Johannesburg, at about one thousand seven hundred and fifty metres above sea level, is cooled by roughly eleven degrees compared with a sea-level place at the same latitude, using the lapse rate. Its July mean is only about eleven degrees, with frosty nights and occasional snow, and its summer days are warm rather than hot. It is also far from the sea, with a continental pattern of larger daily ranges and dry winters. These features are more like a temperate climate than a subtropical one. Geographers therefore describe the highveld as having a temperate highland climate, sometimes called a subtropical highland climate in Köppen's system, to show that it is the height, not the latitude, that makes it temperate. A similar pattern appears elsewhere in Africa: Nairobi in Kenya and Addis Ababa in Ethiopia lie close to the equator, but at about one thousand seven hundred and two thousand three hundred metres, so they have mild, spring-like climates rather than the hot tropical climates of the lowlands around them. In exams, if a place's climate does not match its latitude, check its altitude first, and then its distance from the sea and any ocean current.

### subtopic_3 — Subtropical and Temperate Climates
**Assistant:** Why does London get rain all year when Johannesburg gets almost none in winter?

**Tutor:** Because they lie in different wind and pressure belts, and the rain-bearing systems reach London in every season but reach Johannesburg only in summer. London lies at about fifty-one degrees north, in the belt of westerly winds that blow from the Atlantic Ocean towards Europe throughout the year. These westerlies carry a continuous series of mid-latitude low-pressure systems, called depressions, with their warm and cold fronts. Each depression brings cloud and rain as warm, moist air from the ocean is lifted over cooler air along the fronts. Because the westerlies and their depressions affect north-western Europe in every season, though they are stronger in autumn and winter, London receives rain in every month, typically in the form of light, steady rain and drizzle rather than heavy storms. The sea also keeps the air moist. Johannesburg, at about twenty-six degrees south, lies in a different belt. In winter, the subtropical high-pressure belt shifts north and sits over the interior of southern Africa. Under high pressure, air sinks, warms and dries, so clouds rarely form, and winter months often have no rain at all. The westerly winds and their cold fronts are further south in the Southern Ocean, and they reach only the south-western Cape. In summer, the high-pressure belt weakens over the hot interior, and moist air from the Indian Ocean flows in. Strong heating causes convection, and Johannesburg gets afternoon thunderstorms. So London's all-year rain is frontal rain from the westerlies, and Johannesburg's summer rain is convectional rain from heating and moist easterly air. Knowing which kind of rain a place gets helps explain its climate type and its rainfall season.

### subtopic_4 — Comparing Climates and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do people in the savanna cope with the long dry season?

**Tutor:** Through a wide range of strategies developed over generations, in farming, herding, water storage, building and the timing of activities, and animals and plants have their own adaptations too. In a tropical savanna climate, the dry season can last five to seven months, with almost no rain, high temperatures, dry winds and dust. Farmers who grow crops such as maize, sorghum, millet and groundnuts plant at the start of the rains and harvest before the dry season. Sorghum and millet are especially suited to the savanna because they tolerate drought better than maize. After harvest, grain is dried and stored in raised granaries that protect it from moisture, insects and rodents, so families have food through the dry months. Herders move cattle, goats and sheep to areas where grazing and water remain, sometimes over long distances; this seasonal movement is called transhumance, and it was practised by many communities in southern and eastern Africa. Water is stored in dams, wells and boreholes, and in some communities rainwater is collected from roofs into tanks. Houses with thick earth walls and thatched roofs stay cool in the heat. Fires are used to burn old grass at the end of the dry season, which encourages fresh growth when the rains return. The natural vegetation is adapted too: grasses die back above ground but survive through their roots; trees such as acacias have deep roots and small leaves that lose less water, and baobabs store water in their swollen trunks. Many animals migrate following the rains, as in the great wildebeest migration of the Serengeti and Maasai Mara. Today, climate change and population growth are putting pressure on these strategies, so governments and communities invest in irrigation, drought-resistant crops and early-warning systems for droughts.
