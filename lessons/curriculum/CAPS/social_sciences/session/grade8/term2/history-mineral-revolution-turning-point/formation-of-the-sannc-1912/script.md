# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the formation of the South African Native National Congress, the SANNC, in 1912. The anchor is the founding conference in Bloemfontein on 8 January 1912, less than two years after the Union of South Africa. The governing idea is that the Union united four colonies under a white minority government, and that African leaders answered by uniting across regions and ethnic groups into one national organisation, which used peaceful, constitutional methods to defend African rights. Explaining why the SANNC was formed, its aims, its leaders and its methods, and using sources about it, are examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Why Africans Needed a National Voice

By 1910, African people had lost most of their independent land and political power. The kingdoms had been defeated in the wars of the nineteenth century, and the mineral revolution had pulled hundreds of thousands of men into migrant labour. The Union of South Africa, formed on 31 May 1910, made matters worse. Only white men could be elected to Parliament. Outside the Cape, Africans had no vote at all, and in the Cape the qualified franchise was under threat. The new government was planning laws on land, labour and passes that would affect every African family.

Before 1910, African political organisations were regional. There were native congresses and associations in the Cape, Natal, the Transvaal and the Orange River Colony. Each petitioned its own colonial government. They had different leaders and sometimes different aims, and their voices were easy for governments to ignore.

The making of the Union showed what unity could achieve and what disunity cost. In March 1909, African leaders from all four colonies met in Bloemfontein as the South African Native Convention to protest against the draft constitution, which excluded Africans from Parliament. Later in 1909, a delegation went to London with W. P. Schreiner, a former Prime Minister of the Cape, to ask the British government and Parliament to change the draft. They failed, and the South Africa Act was passed. But the Convention showed that leaders from different regions could work together.

The leaders of these organisations were mostly educated at mission schools and colleges in South Africa, and some at universities abroad. Many were teachers, ministers of religion, lawyers, clerks, journalists and owners of small farms or businesses. They believed in education, Christianity and constitutional government, and in the British promise of equal rights for civilised men. They felt deeply betrayed by the Union, which made race, not education or property, the test of political rights.

African newspapers spread these ideas. Imvo Zabantsundu, edited by John Tengo Jabavu, had been published in King William's Town since 1884. Ilanga lase Natal, founded by John Dube, appeared in 1903. Tsala ea Becoana, edited by Sol Plaatje, was published in Kimberley. These newspapers allowed educated Africans to share news and arguments across the country and helped to create a sense of a national community.

Other groups had already shown the value of organisation. Coloured South Africans had formed the African Political Organisation in 1902, and Indian South Africans had organised in Natal from 1894 and in the Transvaal from 1903. African leaders watched these organisations, and some worked with them, as in the 1909 delegation to London, which included Dr Abdurahman of the APO. The lesson they drew was that a scattered set of local voices could be ignored, but a single national body speaking for millions of people would be much harder to dismiss.

## Subtopic: The Founding Conference of 1912

The main organiser of the founding conference was Pixley ka Isaka Seme, a young lawyer from Natal who had studied at Columbia University in New York and at Oxford, and trained as a lawyer in London. In 1911 he wrote in African newspapers calling for a national congress. He argued that the divisions between Xhosa, Zulu, Sotho, Tswana and other groups, and the jealousies between regions, were the cause of African weakness, and that Africans must now act as one people.

The conference met in Bloemfontein on 8 January 1912. Bloemfontein was chosen partly because it was central and partly because the 1909 Convention had met there. Several hundred delegates attended, including chiefs, lawyers, teachers, ministers, journalists, and representatives from the British protectorates of Basutoland, Bechuanaland and Swaziland. Seme opened the meeting with a famous speech in which he said that the white people of the country had formed the Union, in which Africans had no voice, and that Africans must therefore form their own union.

The conference elected its first leaders. John Langalibalele Dube, a teacher, minister and founder of the Ohlange Institute and of Ilanga lase Natal, was elected president, even though he was not present at the meeting. Sol Plaatje, a journalist and writer from Kimberley, became the first secretary-general. Seme became treasurer. The organisation's structure included an upper house of chiefs, showing that the founders wanted to unite traditional leaders and educated professionals.

The new organisation was named the South African Native National Congress. In 1923 it changed its name to the African National Congress, the ANC, which was banned in 1960, unbanned in 1990 and became the governing party after the first democratic election in 1994.

The founders were all men. Women were not full members in the early years. African women organised separately, for example in the campaign against passes for women in the Orange Free State in 1913, and in 1918 Charlotte Maxeke led the formation of the Bantu Women's League, linked to the Congress. Women were only admitted as full members of the ANC in 1943.

## Subtopic: Aims and Methods of the Early Congress

The SANNC set out to unite African people across ethnic and regional lines, to defend their rights and interests, and to oppose laws that discriminated against them. It aimed to be the voice of African people to the government, and to educate its members and the public about their rights.

Its first great battle came quickly. In 1913, Parliament passed the Natives Land Act, which limited the land Africans could own or rent to the reserves, about seven per cent of the country. The SANNC protested strongly. In 1914, it sent a delegation to London, including Dube, Plaatje, Walter Rubusana, Saul Msane and Thomas Mapikela, to ask the British government to intervene. The British government refused, saying that South Africa was self-governing. When the First World War began in August 1914, the delegation returned, and the Congress declared its loyalty to the British Empire during the war, hoping this would later be rewarded. Plaatje stayed in Britain and wrote a powerful book against the Land Act, Native Life in South Africa, published in 1916.

The early Congress used peaceful, constitutional methods: petitions, deputations to government ministers, letters to newspapers, public meetings and delegations to London. The founders believed that reasoned argument, loyalty to the Crown and appeals to Christian and British values could persuade the government to change course. They also launched a newspaper, Abantu-Batho, in 1912, which published in several languages.

These methods had limited success. Governments listened politely but passed the laws anyway. Critics, then and later, argued that the early Congress was too cautious, too dependent on the goodwill of Britain, and too far from the lives of ordinary workers and rural people. Others point out that, in the circumstances of 1912, with Africans defeated in war and without votes, peaceful organisation was the only realistic path, and that the Congress built a national identity and a tradition of organisation that later generations transformed into a mass movement.

The founding of the SANNC was a turning point. For the first time, Africans across South Africa had a national political organisation. It was one of the oldest liberation movements in Africa, and it lasted through all the changes of the twentieth century.

The Congress also took up other grievances. It protested against the pass laws, which forced African men to carry documents and could lead to arrest at any time, and it supported the women of the Orange Free State who resisted passes in 1913. It campaigned against the colour bar on the mines and against unequal treatment in the courts. In these early years its leaders often travelled long distances to meet communities, collect money and explain the Congress's aims, because most Africans had never heard of a national political organisation before.

## Subtopic: Reading Seme's Call and the Error Museum

In 1911 and at the conference in 1912, Pixley ka Isaka Seme called on Africans to unite. A summary of his argument runs like this: the demon of racialism, the divisions between Xhosa and Zulu, between Basotho and every other group, must be buried and forgotten. We are one people. These divisions and jealousies are the cause of all our woes and of all our backwardness and ignorance today. This source is very useful for understanding why the Congress was formed: its central aim was unity. When you use it, identify its author, a founder and organiser of the Congress; its date, just after the Union; its audience, African leaders; and its purpose, to persuade them to unite. Its limitation is that it shows the view of an educated leader, and not necessarily the views of rural people or workers.

A simple calculation helps to place the Congress in time. It was founded in 1912. It changed its name in 1923, eleven years later. It was banned in 1960, forty-eight years after its founding, and won the first democratic election in 1994, eighty-two years after its founding. Nineteen ninety-four minus nineteen twelve is eighty-two. So a person who was a young delegate at the founding conference would not have lived to see the goal of equal political rights achieved.

The founders' backgrounds also tell us about the organisation. Many were educated at mission institutions such as Lovedale, Healdtown or Ohlange, and some, like Seme and Dube, had studied in the United States. They were influenced by African American leaders such as Booker T. Washington, who stressed education and self-help. Their belief in education and constitutional methods shaped the early Congress.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the ANC was founded under that name in 1912. It was founded as the SANNC and renamed in 1923. Two: saying Nelson Mandela founded the ANC. He was not yet born; the founders included Seme, Dube and Plaatje. Three: saying the early Congress used armed struggle. It used petitions and delegations. Four: forgetting the link to the Union. The Congress was a direct response to the white-only Union of 1910. Five: saying women were full members from the start. They were admitted only in 1943, though they organised from 1913.

## Subtopic: One People

In 1910, white men formed the Union and kept Africans out of Parliament. African leaders realised they had to stand together. Before, each region had its own small organisation. Now they wanted one big one.

Pixley ka Isaka Seme, a young lawyer, called everyone to Bloemfontein. His message was simple: forget the old divisions; we are one people.

Before this, leaders had already tried. In 1909 they went to London to ask Britain to change the plan for the Union. Britain said no. They learned that small separate groups were easy to ignore.

Think of many small streams joining to make one river. That was the idea of the Congress.

## Subtopic: The Day in Bloemfontein

On 8 January 1912, leaders met in Bloemfontein and formed the South African Native National Congress. John Dube became president and Sol Plaatje became secretary-general. In 1923, it was renamed the African National Congress, the ANC.

Women were not full members at first, but they organised too, and Charlotte Maxeke led the Bantu Women's League from 1918.

The meeting had chiefs as well as teachers, lawyers and ministers. The founders wanted everyone, from the villages and the towns, to belong. That is why there was a special house for chiefs.

Date to remember: 8 January 1912, Bloemfontein.

## Subtopic: Asking Politely

The early Congress used peaceful methods: letters, meetings, newspapers and trips to London. In 1913 the Land Act took away most of Africans' land rights, and in 1914 the Congress sent leaders to London to complain. Britain said no. Sol Plaatje wrote a famous book against the Land Act.

Some people said the Congress was too polite and too slow. Others said that, in 1912, it was the only safe way. Both views have a point. What everyone agrees is that the Congress lasted, and grew, for more than a hundred years.
