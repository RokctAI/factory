### subtopic_1 — Gold, War and the Defeat of the Boer Republics
**Assistant:** If Britain said it fought for equal rights, why did the peace treaty leave black people without the vote?

**Tutor:** Because Britain's real priority after the war was not equal rights but a stable, loyal, profitable South Africa, and British leaders decided that this required the cooperation of the defeated Boers. During the war, British politicians had criticised the Transvaal for denying rights to uitlanders, and some had also spoken about the poor treatment of black people in the republics. Many black South Africans believed these statements and supported the British, hoping for the Cape franchise to be extended. But at the peace negotiations at Vereeniging in 1902, the Boer leaders insisted that black people must not get the vote, and the British negotiators, including Lord Milner, accepted a clause that postponed the question until after self-government. Milner himself later admitted that he regarded this as a mistake, but at the time Britain wanted the Boers to accept defeat, rebuild the farms and help run the colonies, and it wanted the gold mines working again with cheap labour. A franchise for black people would have angered almost all white voters in the north. So the outcome showed a pattern historians see many times: reconciliation between the two white groups was achieved at the expense of black South Africans. When you answer a question on this, it is useful to compare what Britain said during the war with what it agreed at the peace, and to explain the reasons for the difference.

### subtopic_2 — New Voices: the APO and the Transvaal Indian Congress
**Assistant:** Why did the APO and the Indian organisations use petitions and delegations rather than force?

**Tutor:** There were several reasons, and they tell us a lot about the leaders and their situation. First, the leaders were mostly educated professionals, such as Dr Abdurahman, a doctor, and Gandhi, a lawyer. They believed in the British idea of the rule of law and in the promise of equal rights for civilised men, and they thought that reasoned argument could persuade the British government and public. Second, they had good reason to think that force would fail. The colonial states had armies, police, machine guns and artillery, and the defeat of the African kingdoms, and later the crushing of the Bambatha Rebellion, showed what happened to armed resistance. Third, their communities were relatively small minorities, so they looked for allies, especially in Britain, where newspapers and members of Parliament could put pressure on colonial governments. Gandhi's satyagraha went a step further. It was still peaceful, but it was not merely asking: it meant deliberately breaking unjust laws and filling the jails, so that the government faced a moral and practical problem. Historians debate how effective these methods were. They won some limited concessions, but did not stop the Union constitution. They did, however, create organisations, newspapers and leaders that later movements built on.

### subtopic_3 — The Bambatha Rebellion and the Union of 1910
**Assistant:** Was the Bambatha Rebellion only about the poll tax?

**Tutor:** The poll tax was the trigger, but historians see deeper causes. By 1906, African people in Natal had lost much of their land to white settlers and the colonial government. Many lived as tenants on white-owned farms, paying rent or working for the farmer, or in crowded reserves. Taxes such as the hut tax were already heavy, and the poll tax of 1905 added one pound a year for young men who did not pay hut tax. This hit young men who had little money, and it was clearly meant to force them into wage labour on farms and in the mines. There were also other grievances: laws that allowed colonial officials to appoint and remove chiefs, as happened to Bambatha himself; the weakening of the authority of chiefs and elders; drought and cattle disease; and the memory of the destruction of the Zulu kingdom in 1879. Some people hoped that the Zulu king Dinuzulu would support the rebellion, which is one reason the colonial government later put him on trial. So a good answer explains the poll tax as the immediate cause and land loss, labour pressure and loss of political authority as the underlying causes. The harsh response, with three to four thousand Africans killed, also shows how the colonial state was willing to use overwhelming force.

### subtopic_4 — Sources, Calculations and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How can two sources about the Union of 1910 both be useful if they disagree?

**Tutor:** Because a source is useful not only for the facts it reports but also for what it tells us about the people who made it. Suppose one source is a speech by a white politician in 1910, praising the Union as the day Boer and Briton became one nation. This is useful for understanding how white leaders saw the Union: as reconciliation after a bitter war, and as the start of a strong, united state. It tells us about their hopes and priorities. Now suppose another source is a petition from African leaders, complaining that the Union constitution takes away their hope of political rights. This is useful for understanding how black South Africans experienced the same event: as exclusion and betrayal. Neither source gives the whole picture. A historian asks who made each source, when, for whom and why. The speech was aimed at white voters and the British public, so it stresses unity and leaves out the black majority. The petition was aimed at the British government, so it stresses injustice in the hope of change. By putting them together and checking them against other evidence, such as the franchise rules and the census figures, the historian can explain that the Union was both a reconciliation between whites and an exclusion of the majority. Saying this clearly is the kind of answer that earns the highest marks.
