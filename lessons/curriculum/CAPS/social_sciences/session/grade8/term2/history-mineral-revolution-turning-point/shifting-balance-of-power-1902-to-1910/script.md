# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines how the balance of power in South Africa shifted between 1902 and 1910, and why the mineral revolution is seen as a turning point in South African history. The anchor is the line from the gold of the Witwatersrand to the South African War of 1899 to 1902, and from the British victory to the Union of South Africa in 1910. The governing idea is that Britain fought to control the richest gold fields in the world, then made peace with the defeated Boers on terms that left political power in white hands, while black, coloured and Indian South Africans organised and resisted, but were shut out of the new state. Explaining these events and using sources about them are examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Gold, War and the Defeat of the Boer Republics

After 1886, the richest gold fields in the world lay inside the South African Republic, also called the Transvaal, an independent Boer republic led by President Paul Kruger. The mining industry was controlled mainly by British and other foreign investors, the Randlords, and many of the white miners and businessmen in Johannesburg were newcomers, called uitlanders, meaning foreigners. The Transvaal government taxed the industry, controlled the sale of dynamite through a concession, and made it difficult for uitlanders to get the vote.

Britain wanted the gold fields inside a British-controlled South Africa. The Cape Colony and Natal were British colonies already; the two Boer republics, the Transvaal and the Orange Free State, stood in the way. In 1895 and 1896, the Jameson Raid, an armed attempt organised with the help of Cecil John Rhodes to overthrow the Transvaal government, failed badly. Tension grew, and in October 1899 war broke out between Britain and the two republics. It is often called the Anglo-Boer War, but historians now usually call it the South African War, because black South Africans also fought, worked and died in it.

The war lasted until 1902. After the British captured the Boer capitals in 1900, the Boers fought on as guerrillas, in small mobile groups called commandos. Britain responded with a scorched-earth policy: farms were burned, livestock killed and civilians moved into concentration camps. About twenty-six to twenty-eight thousand Boer women and children died in the camps, mainly of disease. Black people were held in separate camps, where at least fourteen thousand died; historians now think the real number was higher than twenty thousand. Thousands of black men served the British and the Boers as scouts, drivers, labourers and, in some cases, armed soldiers.

On 31 May 1902, the Peace of Vereeniging ended the war. The Transvaal and the Orange Free State became British colonies, the Transvaal Colony and the Orange River Colony. Britain promised the Boers self-government in the future and money to rebuild farms. One clause was crucial for black South Africans: the question of giving black people the vote in the former republics would not be decided until after self-government had been granted. In practice, this meant that white voters would decide, and they would refuse.

After the war, the British administrator, Lord Alfred Milner, set out to rebuild the gold mines quickly. To overcome a shortage of cheap labour, more than sixty thousand Chinese indentured workers were brought to the Witwatersrand from 1904. They were repatriated by 1910 after opposition in both South Africa and Britain.

The questions for this section are with you now: why Britain went to war with the Boer republics, how the war was fought and what the Peace of Vereeniging decided.

## Subtopic: New Voices: the APO and the Transvaal Indian Congress

The British victory raised hopes among black, coloured and Indian South Africans. Britain had claimed that it was fighting for equal rights in the Transvaal, and many people expected that British rule would bring the more open franchise of the Cape Colony to the north. In the Cape, men of any race could vote if they owned property worth a certain amount or earned a certain wage and could write their name, address and occupation. The Peace of Vereeniging showed that these hopes would be disappointed, and people began to organise.

In 1902, coloured people in Cape Town formed the African Political Organisation, known as the APO. Its leader from 1905 was Dr Abdullah Abdurahman, a medical doctor who had trained in Scotland and who was elected to the Cape Town City Council. The APO worked to protect the rights of coloured people, especially the Cape franchise, and to win the same rights in the Transvaal and the Orange River Colony. It published a newspaper, also called the APO, petitioned governments and sent a delegation to London. Its methods were constitutional: meetings, petitions, newspapers and delegations, not violence.

Indian South Africans also organised. In Natal, the Natal Indian Congress had been founded in 1894, with Mohandas Gandhi, a young lawyer from India, as a leading figure. In the Transvaal, Indians faced laws that restricted where they could live and trade and required them to register. In 1903, Indians in the Transvaal formed a political organisation to defend their rights, the Transvaal British Indian Association, which later became the Transvaal Indian Congress. In 1906, the Transvaal government proposed a law that would force every Indian man to register and carry a certificate with his fingerprints. Gandhi and the Indian community met in Johannesburg and pledged to refuse to obey it. This was the beginning of the method that Gandhi called satyagraha, meaning truth-force: peaceful, deliberate refusal to obey unjust laws, accepting arrest and punishment.

African political organisations were growing too. Educated Africans published newspapers, such as Imvo Zabantsundu, edited by John Tengo Jabavu in the eastern Cape, and Ilanga lase Natal, founded by John Dube in Natal in 1903. Regional organisations, called native congresses or vigilance associations, petitioned governments about land, passes and the vote.

These organisations had something in common. They were mainly led by educated, Christian, professional men who believed in British promises of equal rights for civilised men. They used peaceful, constitutional methods and appealed to the British government. Their disappointment after 1902, and again in 1910, pushed them towards more united and more determined action.

The questions for this section are with you now: the APO, the Transvaal Indian Congress and the methods used by these new organisations.

## Subtopic: The Bambatha Rebellion and the Union of 1910

In Natal, African people faced growing pressure on the land, high rents on white-owned farms and heavy taxes. In 1905, the Natal government introduced a poll tax of one pound a year on every adult man who did not already pay hut tax, which meant mainly young unmarried men. The tax was meant to raise money and to push more young men into wage labour. Many refused to pay.

In 1906, resistance turned into rebellion. Bambatha kaMancinza, a chief of the Zondi people near Greytown, who had been removed from his position by the colonial government, led armed followers into the Nkandla forest. Colonial troops, using machine guns and artillery, crushed the rebellion. In June 1906, at the Mome Gorge, Bambatha and many of his followers were killed. Altogether, between three and four thousand Africans died in the rebellion, compared with a few dozen whites. Thousands were imprisoned and flogged, and homesteads were burned. The Zulu king, Dinuzulu, was arrested and convicted in 1909 of sheltering rebels. The rebellion was the last armed uprising against colonial rule in South Africa for many decades, and it showed the colonies how strong the state had become and how weak armed resistance now was.

At the same time, the four colonies were moving towards unity. Britain granted self-government to the Transvaal in 1906 and the Orange River Colony in 1907, and Boer leaders such as Louis Botha and Jan Smuts won elections there. White leaders in all four colonies wanted one government to control railways, customs duties, and above all what they called native policy: land, labour and the vote. A National Convention of white delegates met in 1908 and 1909 and drew up a constitution. The British Parliament passed it as the South Africa Act of 1909.

On 31 May 1910, the Union of South Africa came into being. The four colonies became provinces. Louis Botha became the first Prime Minister. The Cape kept its qualified non-racial franchise, so some black and coloured men there could still vote, but only white men could be elected to the Union Parliament. In the Transvaal, the Orange Free State and Natal, voting was in practice limited to white men. A delegation that included W. P. Schreiner, Dr Abdurahman and John Tengo Jabavu went to London in 1909 to protest, but the British government accepted the Act.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Bambatha Rebellion of 1906 and the making of the Union of South Africa in 1910.

## Subtopic: Sources, Calculations and the Error Museum

Historians studying this period have sources from many points of view: British government documents, Boer leaders' memoirs, newspapers such as Imvo Zabantsundu and the APO, petitions, colonial military reports, photographs and oral histories of the Bambatha Rebellion. A British official might describe the Union as a great act of reconciliation between Boer and Briton. A petition from African leaders might describe the same event as a betrayal of black South Africans. Both are true to their authors' experience, and a historian must explain why the views differ.

A calculation shows why the Union is seen as a turning point. The first census of the Union, in 1911, counted about five million nine hundred and seventy thousand people. About one million two hundred and seventy-six thousand of them were white. One million two hundred and seventy-six thousand divided by five million nine hundred and seventy-three thousand is about zero point two one four, or about twenty-one per cent. So roughly one person in five was white, yet white men held almost all the votes and every seat in Parliament. The other four people in five, African, coloured and Indian, had little or no say in the government that ruled them.

Why is the period from 1867 to 1910 called a turning point? Before the mineral revolution, southern Africa was divided into two British colonies, two Boer republics and many independent African kingdoms. Diamonds and gold brought investment, railways, cities and a huge demand for labour. To secure land and labour, Britain and the colonies defeated the remaining African kingdoms, and Britain conquered the Boer republics. By 1910, all of this territory, except the British protectorates of Basutoland, Bechuanaland and Swaziland, was united in one state, controlled by a white minority, with an economy built on mining and migrant labour.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the South African War was fought only between Boers and British. Black South Africans fought, worked and suffered in it. Two: saying the Union of 1910 gave everyone the vote. It entrenched white political power. Three: confusing the APO, a coloured organisation formed in 1902, with the ANC, formed in 1912. Four: saying Bambatha rebelled against the Boers. He rebelled against the British colony of Natal. Five: treating a British official's speech as a neutral description of the Union.

Layout for marks in a paragraph question: begin with a clear statement, for example that the Union of 1910 shifted power to a white minority, give three pieces of evidence, such as the Vereeniging clause on the vote, the franchise of 1910 and the crushing of the Bambatha Rebellion, and end by linking back to the question.

The questions for this section are with you now: sources on the period from 1902 to 1910, the census calculation and the error museum.

## Subtopic: War and Peace

The Boer republic of the Transvaal had the richest gold in the world. Britain wanted it. In 1899 war broke out, and it lasted until 1902. Farms were burned and many thousands of people, Boer and black, died in camps.

At the peace in 1902, Britain took over the two Boer republics. But Britain agreed that the vote for black people would wait, and that meant white voters would decide. Black South Africans felt betrayed.

Think of it like this: two groups fought over a house, made peace, and then agreed between themselves that the people who had built the house would get no say in how it was run.

Keep the year 1902 in mind and try the questions for this part: the war and the peace.

## Subtopic: People Get Organised

After 1902, people who were left out started to organise. In 1902, coloured people formed the APO, later led by Dr Abdullah Abdurahman. In 1903, Indians in the Transvaal formed their own organisation, later called the Transvaal Indian Congress. Gandhi taught them to refuse unfair laws peacefully. African leaders started newspapers and congresses.

In 1906, in Natal, Bambatha led an armed rebellion against a new poll tax. Soldiers with machine guns crushed it, and thousands of Africans were killed.

Most leaders chose peaceful methods: meetings, letters, newspapers and trips to London.

Keep these new voices in mind and try the questions for this part: the new organisations and Bambatha.

## Subtopic: One Country, Whose Country?

On 31 May 1910, the four colonies joined to become the Union of South Africa. Louis Botha became the first Prime Minister. Only white men could sit in Parliament, and outside the Cape almost only white men could vote, though white people were only about one in five South Africans.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Gold led Britain to war with the Boer republics from 1899 to 1902. The peace of 1902 left the vote for black people to white voters. Coloured people formed the APO in 1902, and Indians in the Transvaal organised from 1903. The Bambatha Rebellion of 1906 was crushed. The Union of 1910 united four colonies under a white minority government. This is why the mineral revolution is a turning point.
