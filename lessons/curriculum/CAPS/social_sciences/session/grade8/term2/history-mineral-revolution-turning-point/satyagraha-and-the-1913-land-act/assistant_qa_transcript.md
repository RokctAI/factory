### subtopic_1 — The Natives Land Act of 1913
**Assistant:** Why did white farmers want to stop sharecropping if sharecroppers made them money?

**Tutor:** It seems strange at first, but it makes sense once you look at which farmers benefited and what they wanted in the long run. Sharecropping worked well for white landowners who had land but little money, oxen or equipment: the African family brought the ploughs, oxen, seed and skill, and the landowner received half the harvest without spending much. But many other white farmers, especially those trying to farm commercially with their own equipment, saw successful African sharecroppers as competitors and complained that they could not find labourers because Africans preferred to farm for themselves. A family that could earn a living by sharecropping had little reason to work as poorly paid labourers. Politicians and officials who believed in segregation also disliked Africans living independently on white-owned land. The Land Act served the interests of the more powerful group: it turned sharecroppers into labour tenants or labourers, giving white farmers cheap labour and removing African competition. Mine owners benefited too, because people pushed into the overcrowded reserves had to send men to the mines. So the Act was not mainly about land for its own sake; it was about labour and power. That is a good point to make in an essay: the Land Act was as much a labour law as a land law.

### subtopic_2 — Indian South Africans and Satyagraha
**Assistant:** Why did Gandhi choose non-violence rather than fighting?

**Tutor:** Gandhi had both moral and practical reasons, and historians usually explain both. Morally, he believed that violence was wrong and that it corrupted the person who used it, and that it was possible to resist injustice through the force of truth and suffering, rather than by inflicting suffering. He drew on ideas from Hindu and Jain traditions, from the teachings of Jesus in the Sermon on the Mount, and from writers such as Leo Tolstoy and Henry David Thoreau, who argued that people should refuse to obey unjust laws. Practically, Indians in South Africa were a small minority without weapons, without votes and without an army, facing a state that had just crushed the Bambatha Rebellion. Violence would have given the government an excuse to use overwhelming force. Non-violent law-breaking, by contrast, put the government in a difficult position: if it did nothing, the law collapsed; if it arrested thousands of peaceful men, women and children, it looked cruel and unjust in the eyes of the world, especially in India and Britain. Satyagraha also required discipline, courage and organisation, and it involved ordinary people, including women and workers, not only leaders. That combination of moral conviction and practical strategy is why it worked in 1913 and why Gandhi later used it in India.

### subtopic_3 — The Great March and the Indian Relief Act
**Assistant:** Why does land restitution today use 19 June 1913 as its starting date?

**Tutor:** Because the Natives Land Act, which came into force on 19 June 1913, was the first national law of the Union that systematically took away African people's right to own and rent land in most of the country, and it is seen as the legal foundation of land dispossession in the twentieth century. After 1994, the new democratic government passed the Restitution of Land Rights Act of 1994, and the Constitution of 1996 gave people dispossessed of property after 19 June 1913 as a result of racially discriminatory laws or practices the right to restitution, meaning the return of land or compensation. Choosing 1913 meant that claims could cover dispossession under the Land Act, later segregation and apartheid laws, and forced removals such as those of District Six or Sophiatown. It also has a limitation that historians and communities often point out: much African land was lost before 1913, through the wars of conquest and colonial land policies of the nineteenth century, which you studied earlier this term. Those earlier losses are not covered by the restitution process. So the date is both historically meaningful, because the Land Act was a turning point, and a compromise, because it does not reach back to the earlier conquest. That is a good example of how history shapes present-day laws and debates.

### subtopic_4 — Figures, Sources and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Was the Indian Relief Act a victory or not?

**Tutor:** It was a real but limited victory, and a strong answer says both. It was a victory because the main demands of the 1913 campaign were met. The three-pound tax, which had taken a large share of poor families' income for nearly twenty years, was abolished. Marriages performed according to Hindu and Muslim rites were recognised again, so wives and children had legal status. And the campaign proved that ordinary workers, women and traders, acting together without violence, could force a government that had refused for years to change a law. The government of India and British public opinion had been mobilised, and Smuts had to negotiate. It was limited because it did not touch the deeper inequalities. Indians still had no vote in the Union, could not move freely between provinces, faced restrictions on trading licences and land ownership in the Transvaal, and later faced segregation laws, including the so-called Ghetto Act of 1946 and apartheid's Group Areas Act. Some Indian South Africans at the time felt that the settlement accepted too much. So a balanced judgement is that the Indian Relief Act was an important victory for the specific demands of 1913 and for the method of satyagraha, but not a victory over racial discrimination as a whole. That kind of balanced conclusion earns high marks in an essay.
