# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines two events of 1913 that show how the new Union government treated black South Africans, and how they responded. The anchor is the year 1913, when Parliament passed the Natives Land Act and Indian South Africans launched the satyagraha campaign that ran until 1914. The governing idea is that the Union used law to control land, labour and movement along racial lines, and that the responses differed: the SANNC protested by petition and delegation without changing the law, while Indian satyagraha, using mass peaceful law-breaking and strikes, won some concessions. Explaining both events and using sources and figures about them are examinable this term.

## Subtopic: The Natives Land Act of 1913

By 1913, many African families still farmed land outside the reserves. Some owned land, often bought jointly by groups of families. Many more lived on white-owned farms as tenants. Some paid rent in cash. Some were labour tenants, who worked for the farmer for part of the year in return for land to farm. Others were sharecroppers, who farmed the white farmer's land with their own oxen, ploughs and seed, and gave the farmer half of the harvest. In the Orange Free State and parts of the Transvaal, African sharecroppers were often successful farmers, and white farmers who had little capital depended on them.

Many white farmers wanted this to change. They wanted Africans to work for them as labourers rather than compete with them as farmers, and they complained of a shortage of farm labour. Mine owners also wanted a steady flow of migrant workers from the reserves. Segregationist politicians argued that Africans and whites should live in separate areas.

The Natives Land Act, Act 27 of 1913, came into force on 19 June 1913. It set aside certain areas, called scheduled areas, mainly the existing reserves, for African occupation. These made up only about seven per cent of the land of the Union. Outside these areas, Africans were forbidden to buy or rent land from non-Africans. Sharecropping was outlawed in the Orange Free State. Africans could still live on white-owned farms, but only as labourers or labour tenants. In the Cape, the Act could not take away Africans' existing right to buy land because land ownership was linked to the qualified franchise, and the courts later ruled that the Act did not apply there in the same way.

The effects were immediate and harsh, especially in the Orange Free State. Farmers told sharecroppers and rent-paying tenants that they must either become servants, giving their labour and often their cattle, or leave. Thousands of families were evicted. They wandered the roads in the middle of winter, with livestock dying, looking for land that they were no longer allowed to rent. Over time, the reserves became overcrowded, the soil was overused, and more and more men had to become migrant labourers.

The Land Act was the foundation of land segregation in South Africa. A later law in 1936 increased the reserved land to about thirteen per cent. The pattern of land ownership created by these laws lasted for most of the twentieth century, and land restitution after 1994 uses 19 June 1913 as its starting date for claims.

African leaders responded at once. The SANNC, only a year old, called the Act the most serious threat African people had faced since the Union. It held meetings, collected evidence of evictions and petitioned the government and the Governor-General. Supporters of the Act argued that it protected African land in the reserves from being bought by white people; critics replied that the reserves were far too small for the people expected to live in them, and that a promised commission to find more land would take years.

## Subtopic: Indian South Africans and Satyagraha

Indian South Africans had arrived in two main groups. Indentured labourers came to Natal from 1860 to work on the sugar plantations, and passenger Indians, mainly traders, came at their own expense from the 1870s. By 1911 there were about one hundred and fifty thousand people of Indian origin in the Union, most of them in Natal.

They faced growing discrimination. In 1895, Natal imposed an annual tax of three pounds on every Indian who had finished an indenture and chose to stay in the colony rather than return to India or sign a new contract. It applied to men, women and older children. In the Transvaal, Indians could not own land outside certain areas and had to register with the authorities. Movement between provinces was restricted.

Mohandas Gandhi, a lawyer who had arrived in South Africa in 1893, became a leader of the Indian community. In 1906, when the Transvaal introduced a law requiring every Indian man to register and give fingerprints, Gandhi and the community pledged at a mass meeting in Johannesburg to refuse to obey it. This began satyagraha, a word Gandhi formed from Sanskrit meaning truth-force or soul-force. Satyagraha meant non-violent resistance: deliberately breaking an unjust law, openly and peacefully, and accepting arrest and imprisonment without fighting back, so that the injustice became visible and the government was forced to respond. In August 1908, hundreds of Indians burned their registration certificates at a meeting outside the Hamidia Mosque in Johannesburg, and many, including Gandhi, went to prison.

In 1913, three grievances brought the campaign to a climax. First, the three-pound tax was still in force. Second, in March 1913, a judgment of the Cape Supreme Court, the Searle judgment, declared that marriages performed according to Hindu or Muslim rites were not legally valid, so many Indian wives had no legal status as wives. Third, the Immigrants Regulation Act of 1913 restricted the movement of Indians into the Union and between provinces.

Indian women played a leading part. Groups of women crossed the border between Natal and the Transvaal without permits and were arrested, and they encouraged coal miners in northern Natal to strike. Among them was Valliamma Munuswamy Mudaliar, a teenage girl who fell ill in prison and died in 1914, shortly after her release, and who is remembered as a heroine of the campaign.

## Subtopic: The Great March and the Indian Relief Act

In October 1913, Indian workers in the coal mines of northern Natal, around Newcastle, went on strike against the three-pound tax. The mine owners cut off water and electricity to the compounds. Gandhi led the strikers and their families on a march. On 6 November 1913, about two thousand men, women and children marched from Charlestown across the border into the Transvaal at Volksrust, deliberately breaking the law that restricted Indian movement between provinces. Gandhi and other leaders were arrested. The marchers were taken back to Natal by train and many were forced to work in the mines, which were declared prisons.

The strike then spread to the sugar plantations, mills and towns of the Natal coast. Tens of thousands of Indian workers stopped work. Police and soldiers were sent in, and in several clashes strikers were shot dead or wounded. The scale of the strike, the arrests and the violence were reported in India and in Britain, where there was public outrage. The government of India, which was British-ruled, protested to the South African government.

The government appointed a commission, and in 1914 Gandhi and Jan Smuts, the Minister of the Interior, reached an agreement. The Indian Relief Act of 1914 abolished the three-pound tax and recognised marriages performed according to Indian religious rites. Satyagraha was called off. In July 1914, Gandhi left South Africa for India, where he later led the struggle for Indian independence using the methods he had developed in South Africa.

The gains were real but limited. Indians still could not vote, could not move freely between provinces, faced restrictions on trading and land, and continued to face discrimination for decades. But the campaign showed that disciplined, non-violent mass action could force a government to change a law.

The two events of 1913 can be compared. Both were responses to Union laws that discriminated by race. The SANNC, against the Land Act, used petitions and a delegation to London, and failed to change the law. The Indian campaign used mass law-breaking and strikes and won concessions, helped by the involvement of the government of India and pressure from Britain. Later South African movements, including the ANC's Defiance Campaign of 1952, learned from satyagraha.

## Subtopic: Figures, Sources and the Error Museum

Figures help to show the impact of these laws. In the census of 1911, about four million of the Union's five million nine hundred and seventy thousand people were African. Four million and nineteen thousand divided by five million nine hundred and seventy-three thousand is about zero point six seven, or sixty-seven per cent. So about two-thirds of the population was African, yet the Land Act reserved only about seven per cent of the land for African occupation. Even with the addition of land under the 1936 law, the figure rose to only about thirteen per cent.

The three-pound tax can also be measured. Three pounds was sixty shillings. Suppose, as an example for the method, a worker who had finished his indenture earned about twenty shillings a month. Sixty divided by twenty is three. The tax would take three months' wages every year, for each adult in the family who had to pay it. These are example figures for the calculation, not the record of one worker, but they show why the tax was so hated.

Sources on the Land Act include the Act itself, parliamentary debates, petitions from the SANNC and Sol Plaatje's book Native Life in South Africa, published in 1916. Plaatje's book is a powerful source because he travelled and recorded the experiences of evicted families. As a historian, you should recognise that he was a founder and secretary-general of the SANNC, writing to persuade the British public to oppose the Act, so he chose the most moving stories. That makes the book persuasive, but it does not make it untrue; other evidence confirms the evictions. Sources on satyagraha include Gandhi's own writings, newspapers, government reports and photographs of the marchers.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the Land Act took away all land from Africans. It reserved about seven per cent and banned purchase and rental outside it. Two: saying Gandhi led an armed struggle. Satyagraha was non-violent. Three: saying the Indian Relief Act gave Indians full equality. It ended the tax and recognised marriages, but discrimination continued. Four: forgetting that women led key parts of the 1913 campaign. Five: treating Plaatje's book as worthless because it is one-sided. Its purpose must be explained, but its evidence is supported by other sources.

Layout for marks in an essay comparing the two campaigns: introduce both laws, explain the methods and results of each, compare why one won concessions and the other did not, and conclude with their long-term importance.

## Subtopic: Pushed Off the Land

In 1913 a new law, the Natives Land Act, said Africans could only own or rent land in the reserves, about seven parts out of every hundred of the country. Outside the reserves they could only stay on white farms as workers.

Families who had farmed for years were told: work for the farmer for nothing much, or leave. Thousands left, in the middle of winter, with their cattle dying on the roads.

Sol Plaatje wrote that Africans had become outcasts in the land of their birth.

Why did the government do it? Many white farmers wanted Africans to work for them, not farm for themselves. The mines wanted men from the crowded reserves too.

## Subtopic: Truth-Force

Indians in South Africa faced unfair laws too: a tax of three pounds a year in Natal, marriages that the courts would not recognise, and rules stopping them moving between provinces.

Gandhi's answer was satyagraha, truth-force. Break the unfair law openly and peacefully. Do not fight back. Go to jail. Make the injustice impossible to ignore.

In 1913, women crossed the border without permits, coal miners went on strike, and about two thousand people marched into the Transvaal. Sugar workers struck too.

Women were brave leaders in 1913. Some went to prison. A young woman named Valliamma fell ill in jail and died soon after she was released.

Keep that peaceful march in mind and try the questions for this part: satyagraha.

## Subtopic: What Changed, What Did Not

In 1914, the government agreed to end the three-pound tax and recognise Indian marriages. Gandhi went home to India. But Indians still could not vote. The Land Act stayed in place, and the SANNC's trip to London did not change it.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Natives Land Act of 1913 reserved about seven per cent of the land for Africans, banned them from buying or renting land elsewhere and outlawed sharecropping in the Orange Free State, causing mass evictions. Indian South Africans, led by Gandhi, used satyagraha, peaceful law-breaking, strikes and the great march of November 1913. The Indian Relief Act of 1914 ended the three-pound tax and recognised Indian marriages. Both events show how the Union used laws based on race, and how people resisted.
