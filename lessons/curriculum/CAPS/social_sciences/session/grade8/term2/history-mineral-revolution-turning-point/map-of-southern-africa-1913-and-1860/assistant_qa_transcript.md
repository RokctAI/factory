### subtopic_1 — Southern Africa in 1860
**Assistant:** Why were Basutoland, Bechuanaland and Swaziland not included in the Union?

**Tutor:** Because they were under direct British protection, and the British government was not willing to hand them over without the agreement of their people. Each case had its own history. The Basotho, under Moshoeshoe, fought several wars against the Orange Free State in the 1850s and 1860s and lost much fertile land. To save his kingdom from being swallowed by the Free State, Moshoeshoe asked for British protection, and Basutoland became British territory in 1868. In Bechuanaland, Tswana leaders such as Khama III wanted protection against Boer expansion from the Transvaal and against the companies of Cecil Rhodes, and Britain declared a protectorate in 1885. Swaziland came under British administration in 1903, after the South African War. When the Union was formed in 1910, the South Africa Act allowed for these territories to be transferred to the Union in the future, and South African governments asked for them many times. But African leaders in all three territories strongly opposed transfer, especially as they watched laws like the Land Act of 1913 being passed in the Union, and the British government did not agree to it. After the 1940s, apartheid made transfer even less acceptable. The three territories became independent in the 1960s and 1970s: Botswana and Lesotho in 1966 and Swaziland in 1968. So their existence today is a direct result of choices made by African leaders and Britain during the period you are studying.

### subtopic_2 — Southern Africa in 1913
**Assistant:** Did the African kingdoms just disappear from the map?

**Tutor:** As sovereign states, yes, but as societies, no, and that difference matters. A sovereign state rules itself: it makes its own laws, controls its land and army, and deals with other states. By 1913, the Zulu, Pedi, Xhosa and other kingdoms inside the Union had lost that sovereignty. Their land had been annexed, their armies disbanded, their people taxed and ruled by colonial officials and later by the Union government. But the people, languages, cultures and many of the royal families remained. Colonial governments often ruled through chiefs and kings, who kept some authority over local matters such as land allocation and disputes, while answering to colonial magistrates. The Zulu royal house continued, and Dinuzulu and his successors remained important figures even under colonial rule. Many Africans kept a strong sense of identity and history connected to their kingdoms. A political map shows sovereignty and borders, so it cannot show this continuity. That is one of the limitations of using maps as historical sources. When you compare the maps of 1860 and 1913, it is accurate to say that the African kingdoms lost their independence, but not that they ceased to exist. Many of today's traditional authorities and kingdoms in South Africa trace their history to the states that appear on the map of 1860.

### subtopic_3 — Explaining the Change
**Assistant:** Why did colonial maps show African land as empty?

**Tutor:** There were a few reasons, and they tell us about the purpose of the maps. Some mapmakers simply did not know much about the interior and filled in only what European travellers had recorded. But often the emptiness was not just ignorance. Colonial governments and settlers wanted land, and a map that showed land as empty or unoccupied supported the argument that it was available for settlement, farming or mining. Maps labelled areas with the names of colonial districts, farms and mission stations, and left out African settlements, kingdoms and place names, even when they knew they were there. The idea that the interior had been largely empty when settlers arrived became a powerful myth that was used to justify land dispossession. Historians and archaeologists have shown that it was false: African farming communities had lived in the interior for well over a thousand years, and kingdoms such as the Pedi, Zulu, Basotho and Tswana held the land in the nineteenth century. So when you read a colonial map as a source, ask: who drew it, for whom, and why? What does it show, and what does it leave out? A map is not just a picture of the land; it is also an argument about who the land belongs to. That kind of critical comment earns marks when a question asks about the usefulness or reliability of a map.

### subtopic_4 — Map Skills and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do I write a good answer comparing two maps?

**Tutor:** Use a clear structure, and always link what you see to what you know. Start by identifying both maps: their titles, dates and what they show. Then describe similarities briefly. For example, the coastline, the main rivers and the positions of Cape Town and Durban are the same on both maps, and the Cape Colony and Natal appear on both, though they are much larger in 1913. Next, describe the differences carefully, one by one. For example: in 1860 the Zulu kingdom was independent north of the Thukela, but in 1913 Zululand is part of Natal; in 1860 the Transvaal was a Boer republic, but in 1913 it is a province of the Union; Basutoland appears on both maps, but in 1860 it was an independent kingdom and in 1913 a British protectorate. Then explain each difference using dates and events: the defeat of the Zulu in 1879 and annexation in 1887 and 1897; the South African War and the Union of 1910; Moshoeshoe's request for British protection in 1868. Finally, write a short conclusion that links the change to the mineral revolution: diamonds and gold drove conquest and union, turning a region of many states into one white-ruled state. Examiners reward answers that are specific, use dates, and explain rather than only describe.
