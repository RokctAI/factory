# Part 1 — Expert

This session compares the political map of southern Africa in 1860 with the map in 1913, and uses the comparison to sum up the mineral revolution as a turning point. The anchor is the pair of maps: a region of many separate states in 1860, and a region dominated by one white-ruled state, the Union of South Africa, in 1913. The governing idea is that diamonds and gold drove British expansion, the conquest of the African kingdoms and the defeat of the Boer republics, so that in about fifty years the independent African states of the interior lost their sovereignty and the region was united under white minority rule. Reading, comparing and explaining these maps is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Southern Africa in 1860

In 1860, before diamonds were discovered, southern Africa was divided among many political units of different kinds.

There were two British colonies. The Cape Colony stretched from Cape Town to the Orange River in the north and to the Keiskamma and Kei rivers in the east. Next to it lay British Kaffraria, the land between the Keiskamma and the Kei, then a separate British colony that was joined to the Cape in 1866. Natal, on the east coast, had been British since 1843 and a separate colony since 1856. Both colonies depended mainly on farming, wool in the Cape and, increasingly, sugar in Natal, where indentured Indian labourers began arriving in 1860.

There were two Boer republics. The Orange Free State, between the Orange and Vaal rivers, had been recognised by Britain in 1854. The South African Republic, the Transvaal, north of the Vaal River, had been recognised in 1852. Both were poor farming states with small white populations and weak governments.

Most of the land and most of the people, however, were still in independent African states. The Zulu kingdom, under King Mpande, lay north of the Thukela River. The Pedi kingdom, under Sekwati and from 1861 Sekhukhune, held the land around the Leolu Mountains in the eastern Transvaal. The Swazi kingdom, under Mswati II, was a strong state in the east. The Basotho kingdom of Moshoeshoe held the fertile lands of the Caledon River valley and the mountains. Beyond the Kei River, the Gcaleka Xhosa, the Thembu and the Mpondo were independent. To the north and west lived the Tswana chiefdoms, and in the far north the Venda. The Griqua had communities at Philippolis, moving to Griqualand East in the early 1860s, and at Griquatown in what became Griqualand West.

On a map of 1860, therefore, the colonies and republics occupied large areas, but their borders were often unclear, disputed and changing, and much of the region was still ruled by Africans. Many African kingdoms had lost land in frontier wars, but they still had their own rulers, armies and laws.

Relations between these states were complex. They traded with each other, made alliances and fought wars. The Basotho fought the Orange Free State over land in the Caledon valley. The Swazi sometimes allied with the Transvaal. Missionaries, traders and hunters moved between them. Some African kingdoms already had guns and horses, bought with ivory, cattle or wages earned in the colonies. Many people moved between the kingdoms and the colonies for work, and some Africans in the Cape and Natal had become Christian farmers and traders. The region was linked, but no single power controlled it.

## Subtopic: Southern Africa in 1913

By 1913, the map had been transformed. The Union of South Africa, formed in 1910, covered almost the whole of the region south of the Limpopo River, from the Atlantic to the Indian Ocean. It had four provinces: the Cape Province, Natal, the Transvaal and the Orange Free State.

The independent African kingdoms inside this area were gone as sovereign states. Their land had been annexed, step by step, to the colonies and then included in the Union. Griqualand West, with the diamond fields, was annexed by Britain in 1871 and joined to the Cape in 1880. The Transkei territories beyond the Kei were annexed to the Cape between 1879 and 1894, ending with Pondoland. The Zulu kingdom, defeated in 1879, was divided, and Zululand was annexed by Britain in 1887 and joined to Natal in 1897. The Pedi were defeated in 1879 and their land became part of the Transvaal. The Venda were conquered by the Transvaal in 1898.

The Boer republics had also lost their independence. After the South African War, they became British colonies in 1902 and provinces of the Union in 1910.

Around the Union lay other territories. Three were British High Commission territories, also called the protectorates: Basutoland, now Lesotho, under British protection since 1868; the Bechuanaland Protectorate, now Botswana, from 1885; and Swaziland, now Eswatini, under British administration from 1903. These were not part of the Union, though white South African leaders hoped to include them. To the north-west lay German South West Africa, now Namibia, a German colony from 1884. To the north lay Southern Rhodesia, now Zimbabwe, ruled by the British South Africa Company of Cecil Rhodes since 1890. To the north-east was Portuguese Mozambique.

Some African rulers survived as chiefs and kings under colonial authority, and African people still made up the large majority of the population. But they now lived under laws made by a white Parliament, including the Land Act of 1913, which confined their land rights to about seven per cent of the Union.

Railways tell the story of the new map too. In 1860 there was almost no railway in southern Africa. By 1913, lines ran from Cape Town, Port Elizabeth, East London and Durban to Kimberley and Johannesburg, and from Johannesburg to Delagoa Bay in Mozambique. A railway also ran north through Bechuanaland to Southern Rhodesia. The railways followed the minerals, and they tied the region's economy to the mines of the Witwatersrand.

## Subtopic: Explaining the Change

How did the map change so much in about fifty years? The answer lies in the mineral revolution and the forces it set in motion.

First, diamonds. After diamonds were found in 1867 and 1870, Britain annexed Griqualand West in 1871, ahead of claims by the Orange Free State, the Transvaal and African groups. The diamond fields made the interior valuable to Britain for the first time.

Second, confederation and conquest. In the 1870s, Britain tried to unite the colonies and republics in a confederation under British control, which would make it easier to build railways and organise labour. Britain annexed the Transvaal in 1877. To make confederation work, British officials believed that the independent African kingdoms, which had land and men the mines and farms needed, had to be broken. In 1878 and 1879, colonial forces fought the Xhosa, the Pedi and the Zulu. The Zulu won a famous victory at Isandlwana in January 1879 but were defeated at Ulundi in July. The Pedi were defeated at the end of 1879 by British and Swazi forces. Their defeat opened the way for annexation and for taxes that pushed men into migrant labour.

Third, gold. After 1886, the Witwatersrand became the economic heart of the region, and railways from the Cape, Natal and Delagoa Bay competed to reach it. Control of the Transvaal became the main aim of British policy, leading to the South African War of 1899 to 1902 and the conquest of the Boer republics.

Fourth, union. After the war, the four British colonies were united as the Union in 1910, under a white minority government that controlled land, labour and the vote.

African societies did not simply disappear. Some, like the Basotho, fought hard and then asked for British protection, which is why Lesotho, Botswana and Eswatini are independent countries today and not provinces of South Africa. Others resisted for years, as the Pedi did. The way each kingdom was conquered or protected shaped the borders of southern Africa that still exist.

Historians debate how much weight to give each cause. Some stress economic forces: the mining industry needed land, labour, railways and a single government, and conquest and union delivered them. Others stress political and strategic aims: Britain wanted to protect its sea route to India and keep out rival powers such as Germany, which had taken South West Africa in 1884. Others stress the choices and resistance of African leaders, which shaped where and when conquest happened. Most historians agree that all of these played a part.

The questions for this section are with you now: how diamonds, conquest, gold and union changed the map between 1860 and 1913.

## Subtopic: Map Skills and the Error Museum

History examinations often give you two maps and ask you to compare them. Follow a method. First, read the title, date and key of each map. Second, list what has stayed the same, for example the coastline, the major rivers such as the Orange, Vaal, Limpopo and Thukela, and the position of Cape Town and Durban. Third, list what has changed: which states have disappeared, which have been joined together and which new territories appear. Fourth, explain the changes using your knowledge, linking each change to an event and a date.

A simple calculation helps to show the speed of change. From 1860 to 1913 is fifty-three years, because nineteen thirteen minus eighteen sixty is fifty-three. A person born in an independent Pedi or Zulu kingdom in 1860 would, by the age of fifty-three, have lived through conquest, annexation, the mineral revolution, a war between Britain and the Boers, and the creation of a white-ruled Union. In counting political units, the map of 1860 shows two British colonies, two Boer republics and many independent African states. The map of 1913 shows one Union with four provinces, three British protectorates and the colonies of three European powers around it.

Maps are sources and have authors and purposes. Colonial maps of the nineteenth century often showed African land as empty, or labelled it only with the names of colonial districts, which suggested that the land was available for settlement. Boundaries drawn on maps were often imposed without the agreement of the people who lived there. Historians therefore read maps critically, asking who drew them and why.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the Union of 1910 included Lesotho, Botswana and Eswatini. They were British protectorates. Two: saying the Zulu kingdom was part of Natal in 1860. It was independent until 1879. Three: forgetting that the Transvaal and Orange Free State were independent republics in 1860. Four: treating a colonial map as a neutral picture of who lived where. Five: describing the change without explaining it. Each change must be linked to diamonds, conquest, gold or union.

Layout for marks in a map comparison: two or three similarities, three or four differences with dates, an explanation of each difference, and a conclusion on why the change shows a turning point.

## Subtopic: Many Pieces

In 1860, southern Africa was like a puzzle with many pieces. Two British colonies, the Cape and Natal. Two Boer republics, the Orange Free State and the Transvaal. And many African kingdoms ruling their own land: the Zulu, the Pedi, the Swazi, the Basotho, the Xhosa chiefdoms and the Tswana.

Each had its own leaders and its own rules.

Draw it in your mind: two colonies at the edges, two republics in the middle, and kingdoms all around.

## Subtopic: One Big Piece

By 1913, the puzzle had become one big piece: the Union of South Africa, with four provinces. The African kingdoms inside it had been conquered and their land added to the colonies. The Boer republics had been beaten in war.

Around it were three British protectorates, Basutoland, Bechuanaland and Swaziland, which are today Lesotho, Botswana and Eswatini. German South West Africa, now Namibia, was to the west.

Draw it again: one big shape, and a few small pieces around it.

Look at where the railway lines went by 1913: from the ports to the diamond and gold mines. The map followed the minerals.

Keep that one big piece in mind and try the questions for this part: the map of 1913.

## Subtopic: Why It Changed

Why did the puzzle change? Diamonds and gold. To get the minerals, the land and the workers, Britain conquered the African kingdoms and then the Boer republics, and in 1910 joined everything together as the Union, run by white men.

Pocket summary of the lesson. In 1860, southern Africa had two British colonies, two Boer republics and many independent African kingdoms. By 1913, the Union of South Africa covered most of the region, with British protectorates and other colonies around it. The change was driven by the discovery of diamonds and gold, the wars of conquest of 1879, the South African War of 1899 to 1902 and the Union of 1910. This is why the mineral revolution was a turning point in South African history.
