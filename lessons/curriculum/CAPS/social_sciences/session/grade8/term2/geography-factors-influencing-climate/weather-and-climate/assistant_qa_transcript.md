### subtopic_1 — Weather Versus Climate
**Assistant:** If weather forecasts are sometimes wrong, how can scientists predict climate decades ahead?

**Tutor:** Because predicting the exact weather on a particular day and predicting long-term average conditions are different kinds of problems, and the second can be more reliable than the first. Weather forecasting tries to predict the precise state of the atmosphere at a particular place and time: whether it will rain in Soweto at three o'clock next Thursday. The atmosphere is a chaotic system, which means that tiny differences in the starting conditions grow over time into large differences. Even with excellent observations and powerful computers, forecasts become less accurate after about a week, and beyond about two weeks, the detailed day-to-day weather cannot be predicted. Climate projections ask a different question: what will the average conditions be over many years, given changes in factors that control the climate, such as the amount of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere? An analogy helps. You cannot predict whether the next roll of a die will be a six, but you can predict that about one in six rolls will be a six over thousands of rolls, and if someone adds weight to one side of the die, you can predict that sixes will become more common. Climate scientists use models based on physics, tested against past climate records, to estimate how averages, such as mean temperature, and the frequency of extremes, such as heat waves, will change. They give ranges rather than single numbers, and they check their models by seeing whether they can reproduce the climate of the past century. Projections made decades ago about global warming have largely matched what has been observed since. So a forecast that says it will rain tomorrow might be wrong, but a projection that summers in the interior of South Africa will on average become hotter is based on robust physics and evidence. Both are useful, for different decisions.

### subtopic_2 — Temperature and Humidity
**Assistant:** Why is wind direction named after where it comes from, not where it goes?

**Tutor:** Mainly because, for people observing the weather, the source of the wind tells them most about what the wind will bring, and because the convention has been used by sailors, farmers and weather observers for centuries. When you stand outside and feel the wind, you naturally face into it to judge its direction, and you notice where it is coming from. The source of the air determines its characteristics. A wind from the sea is usually moist and mild; a wind from a desert or the interior is usually dry and may be hot or cold depending on the season; a wind from the polar regions is cold. In South Africa, people know that a north-westerly wind in the Western Cape in winter often comes ahead of a cold front, bringing clouds and rain from the Atlantic, while a south-easter in summer brings clear, dry, windy weather. A berg wind blowing from the interior brings hot, dry air. In each case, naming the wind by its source tells you what kind of air is arriving and what weather to expect. Sailors in the age of sail depended on knowing the direction from which winds blew, and the names of winds in many languages reflect their sources. Wind vanes are designed accordingly: the arrow, or the heavy end, points into the wind, towards the direction it is coming from. Ocean currents use the opposite convention, named for the direction they flow towards, so a northward-flowing current flows towards the north. That difference often confuses students, so remember: winds come from, currents go to. In exams, check whether the question asks about a wind or a current before answering.

### subtopic_3 — Wind and Precipitation
**Assistant:** Why does the highveld get so much hail?

**Tutor:** Because it combines the main ingredients that hailstorms need: strong summer heating, enough moisture, unstable air, and a high altitude at which the freezing level is closer to the ground. Hail forms inside tall thunderstorm clouds called cumulonimbus. On summer afternoons on the highveld, the sun heats the ground strongly, and warm, moist air, partly brought from the Indian Ocean, rises in powerful updrafts. As it rises, it cools and forms towering clouds that can extend more than ten kilometres high, well above the level where temperatures drop below freezing. Inside the cloud, there are supercooled water droplets, water that remains liquid below zero degrees. When a small ice particle forms, strong updrafts carry it upward, and supercooled droplets freeze onto it, adding layers of ice. The particle may be carried up and down several times, growing larger with each trip, which is why a hailstone cut open often shows rings like an onion. When the hailstone becomes too heavy for the updraft to support, it falls. Because the highveld is about one and a half to two kilometres above sea level, the hailstones fall a shorter distance through warm air before reaching the ground, so less of the ice melts on the way down. That allows larger hailstones to reach the surface than in a similar storm at sea level. The highveld is one of the most hail-prone regions in the world, with storms most common from October to January. Hail can destroy crops such as maize and sunflowers within minutes, dent cars and break roofs and windows. Insurance companies and farmers pay close attention to hail warnings from the South African Weather Service, and some farmers buy hail insurance for their crops every season.

### subtopic_4 — Weather Records, Forecasts and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How can a school set up its own weather station?

**Tutor:** Quite simply, with a few instruments, a good site and a routine for daily observations, and the records can be used for Geography projects, Mathematics and Natural Sciences. First, choose a site. It should be an open, level area of grass, away from buildings, walls, trees and tarred surfaces, which can affect temperature, wind and rainfall readings. Second, set up a Stevenson screen, or a simple homemade version: a white, ventilated box on a stand, with its door facing south in the southern hemisphere so that the sun does not shine onto the thermometers when the door is opened. Inside, place a maximum-minimum thermometer and, if possible, a wet-and-dry bulb hygrometer. Third, place a rain gauge in the open, with its rim about thirty centimetres above the ground, firmly fixed so it does not tip over. A simple rain gauge can be made from a straight-sided bottle with a funnel, marked in millimetres, though a commercial gauge is more accurate. Fourth, mount a wind vane and, if possible, an anemometer on a pole a few metres high, clear of obstacles, with the compass directions marked correctly. Fifth, establish a routine. Readings should be taken at the same time every day, for example at eight in the morning, and recorded in a logbook or spreadsheet: maximum and minimum temperatures for the previous twenty-four hours, rainfall in the past twenty-four hours, humidity, wind direction and strength, and cloud cover. Reset the thermometer and empty the rain gauge after each reading. Over weeks and months, students can calculate averages and ranges, draw graphs, compare their data with the South African Weather Service, and look for patterns, such as the start of the rainy season. It is a hands-on way to see how weather records become climate statistics.
