### subtopic_1 — Orographic Rain and the Rain Shadow
**Assistant:** Why does air warm up when it comes down the other side of a mountain?

**Tutor:** Because it is compressed as it sinks into higher pressure, and compression raises the temperature of a gas, the reverse of the cooling that happens when air rises and expands. The air pressure at any height is caused by the weight of the air above. Near sea level the pressure is greatest; on a mountain top it is lower. When a parcel of air descends from the summit towards the valley, it moves into air with higher pressure, which squeezes it into a smaller volume. Squeezing a gas pushes its molecules closer together and the work done on it increases their energy, so the temperature rises. You can feel the same effect when you pump up a bicycle tyre quickly: the pump gets warm because you are compressing the air. For dry air, the warming is about ten degrees for every thousand metres of descent. Now, there is an important difference between the two sides of the mountain. On the windward side, the air rising and forming cloud cools more slowly, at roughly five to six degrees per thousand metres, because as its water vapour condenses it releases heat, which partly offsets the cooling. On the leeward side, the air has lost most of its moisture as rain, so it descends as dry air and warms at the faster dry rate. As a result, when it reaches the same height on the leeward side, it is warmer than it was on the windward side, and much drier. This is the explanation for föhn winds in the European Alps and for berg winds in South Africa, which flow from the high interior plateau down to the coast. Berg winds can make a winter day in Durban, East London or Cape Town feel like midsummer, with temperatures above thirty degrees and very low humidity, and they greatly increase the risk of veld and mountain fires.

### subtopic_2 — Comparing Mthatha and Durban
**Assistant:** How can two towns only ninety kilometres apart have such different rainfall?

**Tutor:** Because a mountain range between them changes everything about the air that reaches each town, and orographic effects work over short distances. Take Newlands and Worcester in the Western Cape. In winter, the main rain-bearing systems are cold fronts approaching from the south-west and north-west, bringing moist air from the Atlantic. Newlands lies on the eastern slopes of the Table Mountain range, close to the mountain, where the moist air is forced to rise sharply. The air cools rapidly, cloud forms over the mountain, and heavy rain falls on its slopes and immediately downwind, especially during cold fronts. Newlands receives around one thousand five hundred millimetres a year, among the highest totals in any South African suburb. As the air moves further east, it meets more ranges of the Cape Fold Mountains, including the Du Toitskloof and Hex River mountains, which rise to around two thousand metres. Each time, it rises and loses more moisture. By the time the air descends into the Breede River valley at Worcester, it has been drained of most of its water and is warming as it sinks. Worcester receives only about two hundred and fifty millimetres, which is semi-arid. Further inland, the Karoo receives even less. This pattern repeats across the mountains of South Africa and the world. In Hawaii, the windward side of a mountain on Kauai is one of the wettest places on earth, while the leeward coast nearby is dry. For people, this matters a lot. The wet mountains of the Western Cape are the catchments for the dams that supply Cape Town and the irrigation that makes Breede valley farming possible. During the drought of 2015 to 2018, when cold fronts brought less rain to the mountains, Cape Town's dams fell to dangerously low levels, showing how dependent the region is on orographic rain.

### subtopic_3 — Mountains as Barriers, and Slope Aspect
**Assistant:** Why does the KwaZulu-Natal coast get rain from the sea all year, while the interior mainly gets summer storms?

**Tutor:** Because the coast receives moist onshore air that is lifted by the coastal hills and escarpment in many seasons, while the interior depends mostly on the summer heating that produces thunderstorms. Along the KwaZulu-Natal coast, the warm Agulhas Current keeps the air moist throughout the year. Winds frequently blow onshore from the east and south-east, especially behind coastal low-pressure systems and cold fronts that move up the coast. When this moist air meets the rising land of the coastal hills, it is lifted, cools and produces rain or drizzle, even in winter when the interior is dry. That is why Durban receives some rain in every month, although most falls in summer. Moving inland, the land rises in steps towards the Drakensberg escarpment. In summer, moist air from the Indian Ocean reaches the Midlands and the escarpment, where it is lifted both by the slopes and by strong solar heating, producing heavy afternoon thunderstorms and high totals on the eastern slopes. In winter, however, high pressure dominates the interior. The air sinks and is dry, and the escarpment helps block the moist coastal air from penetrating far inland. So the interior, including much of the highveld, gets very little rain in winter. Places such as Mthatha, inland and behind higher ground in the Eastern Cape, lie in between: they get less steady coastal rain than Durban and rely more on summer storms. The combination of relief, the warm current and the seasonal pressure systems explains these patterns. A good answer on this topic combines factors: the source of moisture, the lifting mechanism, and the season.

### subtopic_4 — Diagrams, Explanations and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Does the direction of a slope really make that much difference to farming?

**Tutor:** Yes, in many places it makes a large difference, especially for crops that are sensitive to temperature, sunshine or frost, and farmers have used aspect for centuries. In the southern hemisphere, a north-facing slope receives the sun's rays more directly for most of the day, especially in winter when the sun is low in the northern sky. On a slope tilted towards the sun, the rays strike the ground closer to a right angle, concentrating the energy, just as at lower latitudes. A south-facing slope, tilted away from the sun, receives slanting rays and may lie in shadow for part of the day. Measurements show that on a steep slope in winter, a north-facing surface can receive several times as much solar energy as a south-facing one. The north-facing soil warms more, dries faster and is less likely to have frost. Farmers use these differences carefully. In the vineyards of the Western Cape, the aspect of a slope influences how ripe the grapes become and how much sugar they develop; winemakers choose cooler, south-facing slopes for some grape varieties and warmer, north-facing slopes for others. In fruit orchards, north-facing slopes can produce earlier harvests, while sloping land in general allows cold air to drain away, reducing frost damage. In the Drakensberg and Eastern Cape, herders know that north-facing grassland greens earlier in spring. In Europe, the famous vineyards along the Rhine and Mosel rivers in Germany are on steep south-facing slopes, the sunny side in the northern hemisphere. Builders use aspect too, placing houses so that living rooms face the sun, and engineers place solar panels facing north in South Africa, tilted at roughly the angle of latitude, to capture the most energy. So aspect is a small-scale factor with large practical effects.
