# Part 1 — Expert

This session sets out the key difference between weather and climate and describes the elements of weather and how they are measured. The anchor is a simple weather station of the kind found at many schools and at the South African Weather Service's stations across the country, with a thermometer screen, a rain gauge, a wind vane and an anemometer. The governing idea is that weather is the condition of the atmosphere at a particular place and time, made up of measurable elements, while climate is the average pattern of weather over a long period, normally thirty years, and that climate is calculated from weather records. Using these terms precisely and reading weather data are examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Weather Versus Climate

Weather is the state of the atmosphere at a particular place and time. It describes conditions over hours or days: today is hot and sunny with a light north-westerly wind; tomorrow a cold front will bring rain and strong winds. Weather changes from hour to hour and from day to day, sometimes very quickly. A summer afternoon on the highveld can go from bright sunshine to a violent thunderstorm with hail in less than an hour.

Climate is the average weather conditions of a place over a long period of time, usually at least thirty years, together with the usual range of variation and extremes. It tells you what to expect, not what will happen on a particular day. Johannesburg's climate can be described as warm summers with afternoon thunderstorms, and dry, sunny winters with cold nights and frost. That description stays true even if a particular July day happens to be rainy.

A useful saying is that climate is what you expect, and weather is what you get. Another way to think about it: weather is like your mood on a particular day, while climate is like your personality over many years.

The difference matters in practice. A farmer decides which crops to grow and when to plant using knowledge of the climate, such as the average start of the rainy season and the average date of the first frost. The same farmer decides whether to spray crops or harvest this week using the weather forecast. Engineers designing a bridge or a stormwater drain use climate statistics about extreme rainfall over many years, while a construction team checks the weather forecast before pouring concrete. Tourists choose the season for a holiday by climate, and pack their bags by weather.

Climate is calculated from weather records. Each day, weather stations measure the temperature, rainfall and other elements. Over thirty years, these daily measurements are averaged to give, for example, the mean January temperature or the mean annual rainfall. That is why climate information is always given as averages, and why reliable climate statistics need long, careful records.

Climate also changes over long periods. Scientists use weather records going back more than a century, together with evidence such as ice cores and tree rings, to show that the earth's average temperature has risen by more than one degree since the late nineteenth century. That is climate change, a change in the long-term average, not a change in a single season's weather.

It also helps to know the scales involved. A single thunderstorm lasts an hour or two and covers a few kilometres. A cold front crosses the country over two or three days. A season lasts about three months. A climate average covers thirty years. And natural climate patterns such as El Niño, a periodic warming of the eastern Pacific Ocean, shift weather patterns for a year or more; in South Africa, El Niño years often bring drought to the summer-rainfall region, as in the severe drought of 2015 and 2016. So weather and climate sit at opposite ends of a long scale of time, with seasons and patterns like El Niño in between.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definitions of weather and climate, examples of each, and why the difference matters.

## Subtopic: Temperature and Humidity

Temperature measures how hot or cold the air is. It is measured in degrees Celsius with a thermometer. To get accurate readings of air temperature, thermometers are kept in a Stevenson screen, a white box with slatted sides, standing on legs about one and a quarter to two metres above the ground. The white paint reflects sunlight, the slats let air flow freely, and the height keeps the thermometers away from the heat of the ground, so they measure the temperature of the air in the shade.

Weather stations record the maximum temperature, the highest of the day, and the minimum temperature, the lowest, usually just before sunrise. A maximum-minimum thermometer, such as Six's thermometer, records both. From these you can calculate the daily mean, by adding the maximum and minimum and dividing by two, and the daily range, by subtracting the minimum from the maximum. For example, if the maximum is twenty-eight degrees and the minimum is twelve, the mean is twenty and the range is sixteen.

Humidity measures the amount of water vapour in the air. The most common measure is relative humidity, which compares the amount of water vapour in the air with the most the air could hold at that temperature, as a percentage. At one hundred percent relative humidity, the air is saturated, and condensation will form fog, cloud or dew. Warm air can hold much more water vapour than cold air, so as air cools, its relative humidity rises even if the actual amount of water vapour stays the same.

Humidity is measured with a hygrometer. A simple type is the wet-and-dry bulb thermometer, or psychrometer. It has two thermometers side by side; one has its bulb wrapped in a wet cloth. Water evaporating from the cloth cools the wet bulb. In dry air, evaporation is fast and the wet bulb shows a much lower temperature than the dry bulb; in humid air, evaporation is slow and the difference is small. Using a table, you convert the difference into relative humidity.

Humidity affects how we feel. In Durban's humid summers, sweat evaporates slowly from the skin, so the body struggles to cool itself, and thirty degrees feels oppressive. In Upington's dry heat, sweat evaporates quickly, so the same temperature feels more bearable, although dehydration becomes a danger.

Dew forms when air near the ground cools at night to its dew point and water vapour condenses on cool surfaces such as grass. If the temperature is below zero, frost forms instead. Fog is a cloud at ground level, formed when air near the surface cools to its dew point.

The questions for this section are with you now: measuring temperature in a Stevenson screen, calculating daily mean and range, and relative humidity.

## Subtopic: Wind and Precipitation

Wind is the movement of air from areas of higher pressure to areas of lower pressure. Differences in pressure are caused mainly by uneven heating of the earth's surface. Wind is described by its direction and its speed.

Wind direction is always given as the direction the wind is blowing from. A north-westerly wind blows from the north-west towards the south-east. Direction is measured with a wind vane, which swings so that its arrow points into the wind, and a windsock at an airport shows direction and approximate strength. Wind speed is measured with an anemometer, usually three or four cups on arms that spin faster as the wind gets stronger. Speed is measured in kilometres per hour, metres per second or knots. The Beaufort scale describes wind strength from zero, calm, when smoke rises straight up, to twelve, hurricane force, using observations such as the movement of leaves, branches, dust and waves.

In South Africa, some winds have local names. The south-easter, sometimes called the Cape Doctor, blows strongly across the Cape Peninsula in summer and is said to blow away pollution. The berg wind, hot and dry, flows from the interior down to the coast, mostly in winter. The north-westerly winds that come before cold fronts bring rain to the south-western Cape in winter.

Precipitation is any form of water that falls from the atmosphere to the ground: rain, drizzle, snow, sleet and hail. It forms when water droplets or ice crystals in clouds grow heavy enough to fall. Hail forms in tall thunderstorm clouds, where strong updrafts carry ice pellets up and down through layers of cold water droplets, so they grow layer by layer until they are heavy enough to fall. The highveld has some of the most frequent hailstorms in the world, which can damage crops, cars and roofs.

Precipitation is measured with a rain gauge, which collects rain in a funnel and container. The amount is measured in millimetres, meaning the depth of water that would cover flat ground if none drained away or evaporated. One millimetre of rain on one square metre of ground equals one litre of water. A rain gauge should stand in an open place, away from buildings and trees that could block or drip into it.

Air pressure, measured with a barometer in hectopascals, is not one of the four elements in the syllabus list, but it is closely linked to weather. Falling pressure often warns of approaching low-pressure systems and rain, while rising pressure often brings settled, fine weather.

Cloud cover is often recorded alongside these elements. Observers estimate how much of the sky is covered by cloud, in eighths called oktas, from zero for a clear sky to eight for a completely overcast one, and they note the type of cloud, such as fluffy cumulus, layered stratus, wispy high cirrus or towering cumulonimbus. Cloud type is a good clue to the weather that is coming.

The questions for this section are with you now: wind direction and speed, local winds, forms of precipitation and the rain gauge.

## Subtopic: Weather Records, Forecasts and the Error Museum

The South African Weather Service, a national agency, runs a network of weather stations, weather radars, upper-air stations that launch weather balloons, and lightning detectors. It also uses satellite images. Its forecasters combine these observations with computer models of the atmosphere to produce forecasts and warnings, such as warnings of severe thunderstorms, floods, heat waves and strong winds. Weather records collected over many years become the climate statistics that you read on climate graphs.

Weather maps, called synoptic charts, summarise the weather over a large area at a particular time. They show isobars, lines joining places of equal air pressure, with high-pressure and low-pressure systems, cold fronts and warm fronts, and station symbols showing temperature, wind, cloud cover and precipitation. Closely spaced isobars mean strong winds. You will practise reading synoptic charts in later grades, but the elements they show are the elements you are learning now.

Here is a short calculation using weather data. Suppose a school weather station records rainfall of twelve, zero, five, twenty-three and zero millimetres on five days. The total is forty millimetres. The mean daily rainfall over the five days is forty divided by five, which is eight millimetres. Now suppose maximum temperatures over the same days were twenty-seven, thirty, thirty-one, twenty-two and twenty-five degrees. The total is one hundred and thirty-five, so the mean maximum is one hundred and thirty-five divided by five, which is twenty-seven degrees. Over thirty years, the same kind of calculation gives climate averages.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying that climate is the weather today; climate is the long-term average. Two: giving wind direction as where the wind is going; it is where it comes from. Three: thinking relative humidity of one hundred percent means it is raining; it means the air is saturated, and fog or dew may form. Four: measuring air temperature in direct sunlight; thermometers must be in the shade of a Stevenson screen. Five: calling fog precipitation; fog is cloud at ground level, while precipitation falls from clouds.

Layout for marks in a definitions question: give the definition, add a time scale, and give a South African example. For example: weather is the condition of the atmosphere at a place over a short time, such as a thunderstorm in Pretoria this afternoon; climate is the average weather of a place over about thirty years, such as Pretoria's summer rainfall and dry winters.

The questions for this section are with you now: the weather service and synoptic charts, calculating averages from weather data, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: what you get versus what you expect, how hot and how damp, and wind and rain.

## Subtopic: What You Get, What You Expect

Weather is what is happening outside right now or this week: sunny, rainy, windy, cold. It can change in an hour.

Climate is what usually happens in a place, worked out from about thirty years of weather. Johannesburg's climate: summer storms in the afternoon, dry winters with cold nights.

An easy way to remember: climate is what you expect, weather is what you get. You choose your holiday month by the climate, and you pack your bag by the weather.

Hold the umbrella and the calendar in mind and try the questions for this part: weather versus climate.

## Subtopic: How Hot and How Damp

Temperature tells us how hot or cold the air is, measured in degrees Celsius with a thermometer. The thermometer sits in a white box with slatted sides, called a Stevenson screen, so that it measures the air in the shade, not the sunshine.

Humidity tells us how much water vapour is in the air. In Durban the air is humid, so you feel sticky. In Upington it is dry, so sweat dries fast. When air is completely full of water vapour, we get fog, dew or clouds.

Hold the white box and the sticky Durban afternoon in mind and try this part's questions: temperature and humidity.

## Subtopic: Wind and Rain

Wind is moving air. A wind vane shows where it comes from, and spinning cups called an anemometer measure how fast it blows. A north-westerly wind comes from the north-west.

Precipitation is water falling from the sky: rain, drizzle, snow and hail. A rain gauge catches it, and we measure it in millimetres.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Weather is the state of the atmosphere at a place and time; climate is the average weather over about thirty years. The elements of weather are temperature, measured by a thermometer in a Stevenson screen; humidity, measured by a hygrometer; wind, with a wind vane for direction and an anemometer for speed; and precipitation, measured in millimetres with a rain gauge. Climate statistics are calculated from weather records.
