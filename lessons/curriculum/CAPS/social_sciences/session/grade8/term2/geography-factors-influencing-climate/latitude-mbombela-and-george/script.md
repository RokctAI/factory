# Part 1 — Expert

This session begins the study of the factors that influence temperature and rainfall, starting with latitude, the distance of a place north or south of the equator. The anchor is a comparison between Mbombela, at about twenty-five and a half degrees south, and George, at about thirty-four degrees south, roughly nine hundred and fifty kilometres apart. The governing idea is that places nearer the equator receive more concentrated solar energy through the year, so they are generally warmer, and that latitude also affects the seasons and the pattern of rainfall, but that latitude is never the only factor, so every comparison must consider others too. Explaining the effect of latitude using data is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: What Latitude Measures and Why It Matters

Latitude measures how far north or south of the equator a place is, in degrees. The equator is zero degrees, the poles are ninety degrees north and ninety degrees south. South Africa lies between about twenty-two degrees south, at the Limpopo River, and about thirty-five degrees south, at Cape Agulhas, so the whole country is in the southern hemisphere, in the zone between the Tropic of Capricorn at about twenty-three and a half degrees south and the latitude of thirty-five degrees.

Latitude matters for climate because the sun heats the earth unevenly. Near the equator, the midday sun is high in the sky all year, and its rays strike the ground almost vertically. Further from the equator, the sun is lower in the sky, and its rays strike the ground at a slant. A slanting beam of sunlight spreads the same energy over a larger area, so each square metre receives less heat.

You can test this with a torch. Shine it straight down onto a table and you see a small, bright circle. Tilt the torch and the light spreads into a larger, dimmer oval. The torch gives the same amount of light in both cases, but when it is tilted, each part of the table gets less. That is exactly what happens to sunlight at higher latitudes.

A second reason is the thickness of the atmosphere. A slanting ray passes through more air before it reaches the ground than a vertical ray does. On the way, some energy is absorbed or reflected by gases, dust and clouds. So at higher latitudes, less energy reaches the surface.

A third reason is the length of day and night. Near the equator, day and night are close to twelve hours each throughout the year. Further from the equator, summer days are longer and winter days shorter. This gives higher latitudes bigger differences between summer and winter, so the seasons become more pronounced as you move away from the equator.

Together, these effects create broad temperature zones. The tropics, between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn, are hot all year. The middle latitudes, roughly between thirty and sixty degrees, have warm summers and cool or cold winters. The polar regions, beyond about sixty-six and a half degrees, are cold all year, with months of continuous daylight in summer and continuous darkness in winter.

You can see the effect within South Africa on any midsummer day. On about the twenty-first of December, the sun is directly overhead at noon on the Tropic of Capricorn, which crosses Limpopo just north of Polokwane. At noon that day, a pole standing upright near Musina casts almost no shadow, while a pole in Cape Town, about eleven degrees further south, still casts a short shadow towards the south. On about the twenty-first of June, the midday sun in Cape Town rises only about thirty-two degrees above the northern horizon, so shadows are long even at noon and the day lasts under ten hours. Ask people who have lived in both Limpopo and the Western Cape, and they will tell you how differently the seasons feel.

The questions for this section are with you now: what latitude measures, the angle of the sun's rays, and how latitude creates temperature zones.

## Subtopic: Comparing Mbombela and George

Mbombela, formerly called Nelspruit, lies in the Lowveld of Mpumalanga at about twenty-five and a half degrees south, very close to the Tropic of Capricorn, at a height of about six hundred and sixty metres. George lies on the coastal plateau of the southern Cape at about thirty-four degrees south, at a height of about two hundred metres, a few kilometres from the sea.

Look at typical climate figures, which are approximate long-term averages. Mbombela's mean temperature in January, its warmest month, is about twenty-four degrees Celsius, and in July, its coolest, about fifteen degrees. George's mean temperature in January is about twenty degrees, and in July about thirteen degrees. Mbombela is therefore about four degrees warmer in summer and about two degrees warmer in winter.

Calculate the annual temperature range, which is the difference between the warmest and coolest monthly means. For Mbombela it is twenty-four minus fifteen, which is nine degrees. For George it is twenty minus thirteen, which is seven degrees. George's smaller range is partly because it lies close to the sea, which moderates temperatures, a factor you will study in the next lesson.

The difference in warmth fits the effect of latitude. At Mbombela the midday summer sun is almost directly overhead; in late December it is close to vertical. At George, more than eight degrees further south, the sun is lower in the sky every day of the year, so its energy is spread more thinly.

But notice that latitude is not the only factor. Mbombela is higher above sea level than George, which on its own would make it cooler, about two and a half to three degrees cooler for the extra four hundred and sixty metres. That means the effect of latitude is actually larger than the raw comparison suggests: Mbombela is warmer despite being higher. Geographers always look for such complicating factors before drawing conclusions.

Rainfall shows another pattern. Mbombela receives about seven hundred and seventy millimetres of rain a year, falling mostly in summer, from about October to March, often as afternoon thunderstorms. George receives about eight hundred millimetres, spread across all months, with slightly more in spring and autumn. The totals are similar, but the seasons are very different. That difference is linked to latitude through the movement of the global wind and pressure belts, which the next subtopic explains.

The questions for this section are with you now: the locations of Mbombela and George, comparing their temperatures and ranges, and why altitude complicates the comparison.

## Subtopic: Latitude, Seasons and Rainfall

Latitude affects rainfall indirectly, through the pattern of global winds and air pressure. Near the equator, intense heating makes air rise. Rising air cools, and its water vapour condenses into clouds and heavy rain, so the equatorial region is wet. The rising air spreads towards the poles high in the atmosphere and sinks again at around twenty to thirty-five degrees north and south, creating belts of high pressure. Sinking air warms and becomes drier, so clouds rarely form. That is why many of the world's great deserts, such as the Sahara and the Kalahari and Namib, lie near these latitudes. Further towards the poles, around forty to sixty degrees, cold and warm air masses meet along fronts, bringing rain from mid-latitude cyclones, also called cold fronts.

These belts move north and south with the seasons, following the sun. In the southern hemisphere summer, from about November to February, the sun is overhead near the Tropic of Capricorn, and the belts shift south. Moist air from the Indian Ocean flows over the eastern and northern parts of South Africa, and strong heating causes convection, producing thunderstorms. This is why Mbombela, Johannesburg and most of the interior get summer rain.

In the southern hemisphere winter, from about June to August, the belts shift north. The high-pressure belt sits over the interior of South Africa, bringing clear, dry, sunny days, and cold fronts from the Southern Ocean reach the southern tip of Africa. This is why the south-western Cape around Cape Town gets winter rain.

George lies between these two systems. It receives some rain from cold fronts in winter, some from coastal low-pressure systems and onshore winds in spring and autumn, and some from summer weather, so its rain is spread through the year. Geographers call the southern Cape coast an all-year rainfall region.

So latitude influences not only how warm a place is, but also which weather systems reach it, and when. South Africa's three rainfall regions, summer rainfall in most of the country, winter rainfall in the south-west, and all-year rainfall along the southern coast, can be explained largely by latitude and the seasonal movement of pressure belts.

A practical consequence follows for farming. In the summer-rainfall Lowveld around Mbombela, warm temperatures and summer rain suit subtropical crops such as citrus, mangoes, bananas, avocados and macadamia nuts. In the all-year rainfall region around George, milder temperatures suit dairy farming on green pastures, hops and timber plantations. Climate, shaped by latitude, influences what people grow and how they earn a living.

The questions for this section are with you now: global pressure belts, how they move with the seasons, and South Africa's rainfall regions.

## Subtopic: Using Climate Data and the Error Museum

Climate is described using averages calculated over many years, normally thirty, so that a single unusual year does not distort the picture. A climate graph shows the mean monthly temperature as a line and the mean monthly rainfall as bars, with the months along the bottom. When you compare two places, read the hottest and coolest months, calculate the annual range, add the monthly rainfall to find the annual total, and identify the season when most rain falls.

When explaining differences, always name the factor, describe the mechanism, and then mention any other factors that may also play a part. For example: Mbombela is warmer than George mainly because of its lower latitude, since the sun's rays strike it more directly and pass through less atmosphere; this difference exists even though Mbombela is higher above sea level, which would otherwise make it cooler.

A quick numerical check is useful. Each degree of latitude is about one hundred and eleven kilometres. Mbombela and George differ by about eight and a half degrees of latitude, so they are about nine hundred and fifty kilometres apart from north to south. Over that distance, the January mean temperature changes by about four degrees, or roughly half a degree for each degree of latitude, although local factors change this a great deal.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying that places further from the equator are always colder; altitude, the sea and ocean currents can change this. Two: thinking that the earth is closer to the sun in summer; the seasons are caused by the tilt of the earth, not by distance. Three: forgetting that Mbombela is higher than George; that makes the effect of latitude stronger, not weaker. Four: confusing the annual temperature range with the mean temperature. Five: saying that all of South Africa gets summer rain; the south-west has winter rain and the southern coast has rain all year.

Layout for marks in a data comparison: state the two figures with units, calculate the difference, name the factor, explain the process in one or two sentences, and mention any complicating factor. For example: Mbombela's January mean is about twenty-four degrees and George's about twenty, a difference of four degrees; Mbombela is at a lower latitude, so the sun's rays are more direct and concentrated; this is despite Mbombela being higher above sea level.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading climate graphs, writing data comparisons, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: The Torch Test

Take a torch and shine it straight down on a table. You get a small, bright spot. Now tilt it. The light spreads out into a big, dim patch. Same torch, same light, but now it is spread thin.

The sun works the same way. Near the equator, the sun shines almost straight down, so the heat is concentrated. Further from the equator, the sun shines at a slant, so the same heat is spread over more ground. The slanting light also has to travel through more air, which soaks up some of the heat. That is why places near the equator are hot and places near the poles are cold.

Latitude is the number that tells you how far from the equator you are. South Africa runs from about twenty-two degrees south to about thirty-five degrees south.

## Subtopic: Mbombela Versus George

Mbombela is up north in Mpumalanga, close to the Tropic of Capricorn. George is down south near the coast. In January, Mbombela averages about twenty-four degrees, and George about twenty. Mbombela is warmer because it is closer to the equator.

Here is a twist. Mbombela is higher above sea level than George, and higher places are usually cooler. So Mbombela is warmer even though its height should cool it down. That shows how strong the latitude effect is.

Hold the two thermometers, twenty-four and twenty, in mind and try this part's questions: comparing Mbombela and George.

## Subtopic: When the Rain Comes

Both towns get about the same total rain in a year, but at different times. Mbombela gets almost all its rain in summer, from big afternoon thunderstorms. George gets rain in every month.

Why? In summer, warm wet air from the Indian Ocean flows over the north and east of the country, and the heat makes it rise into storm clouds. In winter, dry high pressure sits over the north, while cold fronts from the south bring rain to the south-west. George sits in between, so it catches a bit of everything.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Latitude is the distance from the equator. Near the equator, the sun's rays are more direct, pass through less air and heat the ground more, so places are warmer. Mbombela, at about twenty-five and a half degrees south, is warmer than George, at about thirty-four degrees south, even though it is higher. Latitude also controls which weather systems arrive, giving Mbombela summer rain and George rain all year.
