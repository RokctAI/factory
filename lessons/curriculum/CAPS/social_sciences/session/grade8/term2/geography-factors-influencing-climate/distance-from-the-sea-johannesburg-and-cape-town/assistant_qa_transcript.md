### subtopic_1 — Why Land and Sea Heat Differently
**Assistant:** If the sea keeps the coast mild, why are some coastal places in South Africa still very hot?

**Tutor:** Because distance from the sea is only one factor, and other factors can make a coastal place hot, especially latitude, warm ocean currents and humidity. Durban, for example, lies on the coast at about thirty degrees south, next to the warm Agulhas Current, which flows south from the tropics along the east coast. The warm current means the sea next to Durban is about twenty-four to twenty-six degrees in summer, so sea air does not cool the city much. Durban's summer days average about twenty-eight degrees, and the high humidity, often around eighty percent, makes it feel hotter, because sweat evaporates slowly when the air is already full of moisture. Further north, the coast of KwaZulu-Natal near Richards Bay and the coast of Mozambique are hotter still. So the sea moderates extremes, but it moderates them around its own temperature. A warm sea keeps a coast warm and humid, while a cold sea keeps a coast cool. Compare Durban with Port Nolloth on the west coast, at a similar latitude: Port Nolloth sits beside the cold Benguela Current, with sea temperatures around twelve to fifteen degrees, and its summer days average only about twenty degrees. Both are coastal, and both have small annual ranges, but their average temperatures are very different because of the currents. That is the subject of a later lesson. In an exam, if you are asked why a coastal town is hot, consider its latitude and the current offshore, and remember that the sea's moderating effect is about the range, the difference between hot and cold, more than about the average itself. Durban's annual range is only about seven or eight degrees, smaller than Johannesburg's, even though it feels much hotter in summer.

### subtopic_2 — Comparing Johannesburg and Cape Town
**Assistant:** Why does Johannesburg get frost but not much snow, even though it is cold in winter?

**Tutor:** Because frost and snow need different conditions, and Johannesburg's winters have the conditions for frost but usually not for snow. Frost forms when the temperature of the ground and of surfaces near it falls to zero degrees or below, so that water vapour in the air turns directly into ice crystals on the grass, cars and roofs. This happens on clear, calm, dry nights, when heat escapes quickly from the ground into space and there are no clouds to trap it. Johannesburg's winter is dominated by high pressure over the interior, which brings exactly these conditions: clear skies, light winds and very dry air. So even when the air temperature measured at about one and a half metres above the ground is a few degrees above zero, the grass surface can drop below zero, and frost forms. Snow, on the other hand, needs moisture in the air and clouds, together with temperatures low enough for precipitation to fall as ice crystals that do not melt on the way down. In Johannesburg's winter, the air is usually too dry for clouds to form, so there is no precipitation at all, frozen or otherwise. Snow falls in Johannesburg only rarely, when an unusually strong cold front brings cold, moist air far inland. This has happened a handful of times in living memory, for example in 2007 and 2012, when snow fell briefly on the highveld. Snow is more common on the high mountains of the Drakensberg, the Eastern Cape and the Western Cape, which are higher and closer to the moist air of cold fronts. So frost is a sign of clear, dry, continental winter nights, and snow is a sign of moist, cold air meeting high ground.

### subtopic_3 — Continental and Maritime Climates Around the World
**Assistant:** Do land and sea breezes really make a difference to people on the coast?

**Tutor:** Yes, they make a noticeable difference to temperature, comfort and even daily routines along many coasts. On a sunny day, the land heats up faster than the sea. The air over the land warms, expands and rises, creating slightly lower pressure near the ground. The air over the cooler sea is denser and has slightly higher pressure, so it flows towards the land to replace the rising air. This is the sea breeze. It usually begins in the late morning, is strongest in the afternoon, and can reach several kilometres or even tens of kilometres inland. It can lower the temperature on the coast by several degrees compared with places further inland, and it brings moist, fresh air. People in coastal cities often notice that the afternoon is cooler and breezier than the morning on a hot summer day. At night the process reverses. The land cools faster than the sea, so the air over the land becomes cooler and denser than the air over the sea, and a gentle land breeze flows out towards the sea. Land breezes are usually weaker than sea breezes. Fishermen in many parts of the world have traditionally used land breezes to sail out early in the morning and sea breezes to sail back in the afternoon. Sea breezes also affect weather: where sea breezes from two coasts meet over a peninsula or island, rising air can create afternoon thunderstorms. They can also carry air pollution inland or trap it near the coast. In South Africa, sea breezes affect Durban, the Cape Peninsula and the west coast, though in Cape Town the strong summer south-easter often dominates. So yes, land and sea breezes are a small daily example of the same physics that creates maritime and continental climates.

### subtopic_4 — Writing Explanations and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why is the interior of South Africa less extreme than Siberia or Canada?

**Tutor:** Mainly because southern Africa is narrower, closer to the equator, and surrounded by oceans on three sides, so no part of it is very far from the sea's influence. Continentality increases with distance from the sea, especially from the sea that the prevailing winds blow from. North America and Asia are huge landmasses. Yakutsk in Siberia is more than one thousand kilometres from the nearest open ocean, and in winter its air comes from the frozen interior of the continent. Winnipeg is more than one thousand kilometres from the Pacific and the Atlantic. South Africa, by contrast, is roughly one thousand six hundred kilometres wide, and no place is more than about six hundred kilometres from a coast. Latitude matters too. Siberia and central Canada lie at fifty to sixty-five degrees north, where winter days are very short and the sun is very low, so there is little incoming energy in winter and temperatures fall extremely low. South Africa lies between about twenty-two and thirty-five degrees south, where even winter sunshine is fairly strong. The oceans around southern Africa also have a great influence: the warm Agulhas Current along the east coast and the cold Benguela Current along the west coast affect the air that flows inland. Nevertheless, the interior of South Africa does show clear continental features. Places like Upington, Kimberley and Bloemfontein have hot summers, cold winter nights, frost, and large daily ranges, sometimes twenty degrees or more between dawn and mid-afternoon in winter. The high altitude of the plateau adds to the cold nights. So South Africa has a mild form of continentality, which makes sense once you consider its size, its latitude and the oceans around it.
