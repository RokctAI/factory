### subtopic_1 — What Ocean Currents Are
**Assistant:** Why does cold air over a cold current not rise and make rain?

**Tutor:** Because air cooled from below becomes stable, meaning that it resists rising, and without rising air, the clouds that produce rain cannot form. To understand stability, think about what makes air rise. Air rises when it is warmer, and therefore less dense, than the air around it, like a hot-air balloon. On a hot day over warm land or warm sea, the air near the surface is heated, becomes lighter than the air above, and rises in thermals. As it rises, it cools, its water vapour condenses, and tall clouds can grow and produce rain. That is an unstable situation, because a small push upwards leads to more rising. Now think about air above the cold Benguela. The sea surface is cold, so it cools the lowest layer of air. That layer becomes colder and denser than the air above it. Dense air at the bottom and lighter air on top is a very stable arrangement, like oil floating on water. Even if something pushes the cold air upwards a little, it is heavier than its surroundings and sinks back down. There is often also a temperature inversion above the cold layer, with warmer air sitting on top, which acts as a lid. Under these conditions, deep clouds cannot develop. Instead, the moisture in the cold layer condenses close to the surface as fog or low stratus cloud. That cloud can be thick and grey, and it can make the coast feel damp and gloomy, but it produces only light drizzle at most. In addition, high pressure dominates the west coast for much of the year, bringing sinking air, which also suppresses rain. So the combination of cold water, stable air and high pressure explains why the west coast is so dry despite lying beside the ocean.

### subtopic_2 — Comparing Durban and Port Nolloth
**Assistant:** If Durban and Port Nolloth are on the same latitude, why is the sea so different on each side of the country?

**Tutor:** Because the currents come from different places and are driven by different wind systems, and because of upwelling on the west coast. The oceans on either side of southern Africa belong to two different circulation loops, or gyres. In the southern Indian Ocean, the gyre turns anticlockwise. Water warmed in the tropics flows westward towards Africa, then turns south along the east coast through the Mozambique Channel and past KwaZulu-Natal as the Agulhas Current. It is carrying warm tropical water southwards, so the sea off Durban is warm. In the South Atlantic Ocean, the gyre also turns anticlockwise. Water flows eastward across the Southern Ocean, which is cold, then turns north along the west coast of Africa as the Benguela Current. It carries cold water northwards from high latitudes. On top of this, the south-easterly winds blowing along the west coast push the surface water away from the shore, because the earth's rotation deflects the moving water to the left in the southern hemisphere. Cold water from depths of a few hundred metres rises to replace it, which is upwelling. That makes the coastal water off Port Nolloth even colder, sometimes below ten degrees in summer. So although the two towns are the same distance from the equator, one is bathed by water flowing down from the tropics and the other by water flowing up from the Southern Ocean and the deep sea. If you swim at Durban's beaches and then at Port Nolloth's, the difference can be fifteen degrees or more on the same day. This is one of the clearest examples in the world of how ocean currents create sharply different climates at the same latitude, and it is why South African geography textbooks use this comparison.

### subtopic_3 — Currents, Deserts, Fog and Fishing
**Assistant:** How does fog help plants and animals survive in the Namib and Namaqualand?

**Tutor:** Fog provides water in places where rain is rare, and many living things have adapted to capture it. Along the coasts of Namibia and the Northern Cape, rain may total only a few tens of millimetres a year, and in some years almost none falls. But fog forms over the cold Benguela on many mornings, especially in the cooler months, and drifts inland, sometimes reaching fifty kilometres or more from the coast in the Namib. The fog consists of tiny water droplets suspended in the air. When fog meets a surface, droplets collect, merge and drip. Several Namib beetles stand on the crests of sand dunes facing the foggy wind, with their bodies tilted forward. Droplets collect on their bumpy backs and run down into their mouths. Some beetles dig trenches in the sand to collect fog water. Lizards lick moisture from their skin and from plants. Plants have adaptations too. Many succulents in Namaqualand, such as vygies, have small, fleshy leaves that store water and surfaces that absorb dew and fog moisture. The remarkable Welwitschia plant of the Namib, which can live for more than a thousand years, may absorb some moisture from fog through its leaves, although it also relies on groundwater through its roots. Lichens, which cover rocks and soil in parts of the fog belt, absorb fog water directly. People have learned from this too. In parts of Chile, Peru and Morocco, and in experiments in South Africa, large mesh nets called fog collectors are set up on hillsides to catch fog droplets, which drain into tanks to provide drinking water for villages. So fog, which does not show up much in rainfall statistics, is a vital resource in coastal deserts created by cold currents.

### subtopic_4 — Writing Explanations and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Could climate change alter South Africa's ocean currents?

**Tutor:** Scientists are studying this closely, and there is evidence that the oceans around southern Africa are already changing, although the details are still being researched. As the earth warms, the oceans absorb most of the extra heat, and sea surface temperatures have risen in many regions. Research suggests that the Agulhas Current has become broader and more turbulent in recent decades, rather than simply faster, and that the waters along parts of the east and south coasts have warmed. Changes to the Agulhas matter beyond South Africa, because some of its warm, salty water leaks into the South Atlantic around the Cape, in what scientists call Agulhas leakage, and this may affect the larger circulation of the Atlantic Ocean, which helps keep Europe mild. On the west coast, the picture is complex. Some studies suggest that stronger south-easterly winds could increase upwelling in parts of the Benguela, cooling the coastal water and possibly affecting fog, while other changes may reduce it elsewhere. Changes in the Benguela can affect the fishing industry. For example, the distribution of sardines and anchovies has shifted eastward along the South African coast since the 1990s, which affected seabirds such as African penguins whose breeding colonies are on the west coast; changes in ocean conditions are one suspected cause, along with fishing pressure. Marine heatwaves, short periods of unusually warm water, have also become more frequent globally and can damage coral reefs, such as those at Sodwana Bay. Because ocean currents influence rainfall, temperature and fisheries, changes to them could affect farming, water supply and food security in South Africa. That is why South African scientists monitor the oceans with satellites, research ships and moored instruments, and why understanding ocean currents is not only about textbooks but about planning for the future.
