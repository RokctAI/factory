# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the third factor that influences temperature: altitude, or height above sea level. The anchor is a comparison between Johannesburg, at about one thousand seven hundred and fifty metres above sea level on the highveld, and Cape Town, at sea level. The governing idea is that air temperature generally falls as height increases, at an average rate called the environmental lapse rate of about six and a half degrees Celsius for every thousand metres, so high places are cooler than low places at the same latitude, and that South Africa's high interior plateau has a cooler climate than its latitude alone would suggest. Explaining and calculating the effect of altitude is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Why It Gets Colder Higher Up

Many people expect that high places should be warmer, because they are closer to the sun. But the difference in distance is tiny compared with the hundred and fifty million kilometres between the earth and the sun. Mountaineers know the opposite is true: the higher you climb, the colder it gets.

The main reason is that the atmosphere is heated mainly from below, not from above. Most sunlight passes through the air without warming it much. The sunlight is absorbed by the ground and the sea, which warm up and then heat the air in contact with them, through conduction, rising currents and the long-wave heat radiation the ground gives off. The air closest to the ground is therefore usually the warmest, and the air gets cooler the further it is from this heat source.

The second reason is the density of the air. Near sea level, the air is pressed together by the weight of all the air above it, so it is dense and holds more water vapour, carbon dioxide and dust, which absorb heat radiated from the ground and act like a blanket. Higher up, the air is thinner, with fewer molecules in each cubic metre, so it holds less heat and lets heat escape more easily into space. Air pressure halves for roughly every five and a half kilometres of height.

The third reason is that rising air expands and cools. When air rises, the pressure around it falls, so the air expands. Expanding air uses energy, so its temperature drops, even though no heat is taken away. This is called adiabatic cooling, and it is the same process that cools air coming out of a pump or spray can. Rising air therefore cools as it climbs, which helps explain why mountain summits are cold and why clouds often form over them.

On average, the temperature in the lower atmosphere falls by about six and a half degrees for every thousand metres of height. This average rate is called the environmental lapse rate. It varies from day to day and place to place, but it is a good rule for comparing places at different altitudes.

Altitude also affects other aspects of climate. High places receive stronger sunlight, because there is less air to absorb it, so sunburn happens more quickly at altitude. They often have larger daily temperature ranges, because the thin, dry air lets heat in easily by day and out easily at night. And they can receive snow, because low temperatures at height mean that precipitation falls as snow rather than rain.

You can see the lapse rate on any clear day from a high viewpoint. In the Drakensberg in winter, the valleys around the foothills can be pleasant in the afternoon sun while the escarpment above is white with snow. Pilots know the rule too: an aircraft cruising at about eleven thousand metres flies through air that is typically around minus fifty degrees, even over the tropics, which is why aircraft windows are double-glazed and cabins are heated and pressurised. Weather balloons, released twice a day from weather stations including several in South Africa, carry instruments upward to measure temperature at every height, and the readings show the steady fall in temperature with altitude, interrupted only now and then by inversions.

The questions for this section are with you now: why air is heated from below, thinner air and expanding air, and the environmental lapse rate.

## Subtopic: Johannesburg and Cape Town Compared

Johannesburg lies on the highveld at about twenty-six degrees south and about one thousand seven hundred and fifty metres above sea level. Cape Town lies at about thirty-four degrees south at sea level. Johannesburg's lower latitude should make it warmer, but its altitude should make it cooler.

Look at approximate mean annual temperatures. Johannesburg's mean annual temperature is about sixteen degrees Celsius. Cape Town's is about seventeen degrees. So despite being much closer to the equator, Johannesburg is slightly cooler on average.

Now use the lapse rate to estimate how much cooler Johannesburg is because of its height. One thousand seven hundred and fifty metres is one point seven five thousand metres. Multiply by six and a half degrees per thousand metres, and you get about eleven point four degrees. So if Johannesburg were at sea level, with the same latitude, we would expect it to be roughly eleven degrees warmer than it is, with a mean annual temperature of about twenty-seven degrees, which is warmer than Durban's twenty-one. That estimate is too high, because a high plateau heats up more than free air at the same height, so the real sea-level effect is smaller. But it shows that altitude is a powerful cooling factor, strong enough to outweigh more than eight degrees of latitude.

Winter shows altitude most clearly. Johannesburg's July average minimum is about four degrees, and frost is common; on some nights the temperature falls below zero. Cape Town's July minimum is about seven degrees. Summer days are similar in both cities, around twenty-six degrees, because Johannesburg's strong summer sun at its lower latitude makes up for its height during the day.

Height also affects daily life. At Johannesburg's altitude, air pressure is about eighty-two percent of sea-level pressure, so water boils at about ninety-four degrees instead of one hundred, which is why cooking times are slightly longer. Visitors from the coast often feel short of breath when they first arrive, and athletes sometimes train at altitude to build stamina, because their bodies adapt to having less oxygen in each breath.

The highveld's altitude explains much of its climate: warm summer days with afternoon thunderstorms, cool nights, dry sunny winters with cold nights and frost, and occasional snow. Pretoria, about fifty kilometres north and about four hundred metres lower than Johannesburg, is noticeably warmer, showing altitude at work over a short distance.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing Johannesburg and Cape Town, using the lapse rate, and how altitude shapes the highveld climate.

## Subtopic: Altitude Across South Africa and the World

South Africa's relief makes altitude a major factor in its climate. The country has a narrow coastal plain, a steep escarpment called the Great Escarpment, and a high interior plateau, mostly between about one thousand and two thousand metres. The Drakensberg, the highest part of the escarpment, reaches over three thousand metres; its highest point in South Africa, Mafadi, is about three thousand four hundred and fifty metres, and Thabana Ntlenyana in Lesotho reaches about three thousand four hundred and eighty metres.

Because so much of the country is high, large parts of South Africa have cooler climates than their latitudes would suggest. The Lowveld of Mpumalanga and Limpopo, below about six hundred metres, is hot and subtropical, while the highveld, a few hundred kilometres away, is temperate. Lesotho, which lies entirely above about one thousand four hundred metres, has the highest lowest point of any country in the world, and its mountains receive snow every winter.

The town of Sutherland in the Northern Cape, at about one thousand four hundred and fifty metres, is known as one of the coldest towns in South Africa, with winter nights often well below zero. Its clear, dry, high-altitude air also makes it an ideal place for astronomy, which is why the Southern African Large Telescope was built nearby.

Around the world, altitude can create cool or even icy climates at the equator. Quito, the capital of Ecuador, lies almost on the equator but at about two thousand eight hundred and fifty metres, so its temperatures average around fourteen degrees all year. Kilimanjaro's summit, at about five thousand eight hundred and ninety-five metres, is about thirty degrees cooler than the plains around its base, which lie at roughly one thousand metres, using the lapse rate, which is why ice can exist there.

Altitude creates zones of vegetation on mountains, from tropical forest at the base through cooler forests and grasslands to bare rock and ice at the top. Climbing a high mountain is like travelling from the equator towards the poles. Farmers in mountainous regions use these zones, growing different crops at different heights. In South Africa, the cooler highveld suits maize, wheat, sunflowers and cattle, while the warm Lowveld suits citrus and subtropical fruit.

Altitude also influences where people have chosen to live. Many of South Africa's largest inland cities, including Johannesburg, Pretoria and Bloemfontein, lie on the plateau, where the climate is mild and free of the diseases of hot, humid lowlands, such as malaria, which historically affected low-lying parts of Limpopo, Mpumalanga and northern KwaZulu-Natal. In tropical East Africa, highland cities such as Nairobi and Addis Ababa grew for similar reasons, because their altitude gives a comfortable climate close to the equator.

The questions for this section are with you now: South Africa's relief and altitude, Sutherland and Lesotho, and altitude at the equator.

## Subtopic: Calculations and the Error Museum

Altitude questions often require a calculation. Use this method. First, find the difference in height between the two places in metres. Second, divide by one thousand to change it to thousands of metres. Third, multiply by six and a half to find the expected difference in temperature. Fourth, subtract that from the temperature of the lower place to estimate the temperature of the higher place, or add it to go the other way.

Example one: the temperature at the coast is twenty-four degrees. What is the expected temperature at a place two thousand metres higher? Two thousand metres is two thousand-metre units; two times six and a half is thirteen; twenty-four minus thirteen is eleven degrees.

Example two: a weather station at one thousand five hundred metres records eighteen degrees. What would you expect at sea level nearby? One thousand five hundred metres is one and a half thousand-metre units; one and a half times six and a half is nine and three quarter degrees, about ten; eighteen plus ten is about twenty-eight degrees.

Remember that these are estimates. Real lapse rates vary with weather, time of day and humidity. On some calm, clear winter nights, cold air sinks into valleys, so the valley floor is colder than the slopes above, which is called a temperature inversion. Farmers plant frost-sensitive crops on slopes rather than valley floors for this reason, and smoke can be trapped under inversions in winter, causing smog over cities.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying high places are warmer because they are closer to the sun. Two: forgetting to divide the height by one thousand before multiplying by six and a half. Three: subtracting when you should add; going up, temperatures fall, going down, they rise. Four: ignoring latitude; Johannesburg is cooler than its latitude suggests, not colder than everywhere. Five: assuming the lapse rate is exact; it is an average.

A third example uses South African places. Durban, at sea level, has a mean annual temperature of about twenty-one degrees. Pietermaritzburg is about six hundred and fifty metres higher, so we expect it to be about four degrees cooler, around seventeen degrees, which is close to its actual average.

Layout for marks in an altitude question: state the heights, calculate the difference, apply the lapse rate with working shown, give the estimated temperature, then explain why in one or two sentences, naming heating from below and thin air.

The questions for this section are with you now: lapse-rate calculations, inversions, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Colder as You Climb

You might think climbing a mountain brings you closer to the sun, so it should be warmer. But the sun is so far away that a few kilometres make no difference. Climbers know it gets colder as they go up.

Why? The sun heats the ground first, and the ground heats the air just above it. The higher you go, the further you are from that warm ground. Up high, the air is also thinner, so it cannot hold heat like a thick blanket. And air that rises spreads out and cools down.

The rule to remember: about six and a half degrees colder for every thousand metres you go up.

## Subtopic: Johannesburg Up High

Johannesburg is about one thousand seven hundred and fifty metres above the sea. Cape Town is right at sea level. Johannesburg is much closer to the equator, so you would expect it to be warmer. But over a whole year, it is a little cooler: about sixteen degrees on average, against about seventeen in Cape Town.

The height is the reason. One and three quarter thousand metres times six and a half is about eleven degrees of cooling. That is why Johannesburg has frosty winter nights, why water boils there at about ninety-four degrees, and why visitors from the coast feel short of breath at first.

## Subtopic: Snow on the Equator

Mount Kilimanjaro is almost on the equator, but its top is covered in ice, because it is nearly six thousand metres high. In South Africa, the Drakensberg and Lesotho get snow every winter, and Sutherland in the Karoo is one of our coldest towns.

Air is heated from below by the ground, and higher air is thinner and expands as it rises, so temperatures fall by about six and a half degrees for every thousand metres. Johannesburg, at about one thousand seven hundred and fifty metres, is slightly cooler on average than Cape Town despite its lower latitude. Much of South Africa is a high plateau, so altitude makes its climate cooler than its latitude suggests.
