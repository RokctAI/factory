# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the fourth factor that influences temperature and rainfall: ocean currents. The anchor is a comparison between Durban, on the east coast at about thirty degrees south, beside the warm Agulhas Current, and Port Nolloth, on the west coast at about twenty-nine degrees south, beside the cold Benguela Current. Because the two towns share almost the same latitude and altitude, and both are on the coast, the comparison isolates the effect of the currents very clearly. The governing idea is that warm currents warm the air above them and supply moisture, encouraging rainfall, while cold currents cool the air, stabilise it and discourage rainfall, producing fog and dry coasts. Explaining these effects using data is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: What Ocean Currents Are

An ocean current is a large, continuous movement of seawater in a particular direction, like a river within the ocean. Surface currents are driven mainly by the prevailing winds blowing over the sea, and their paths are turned by the rotation of the earth and by the shapes of the continents. In each major ocean, the surface currents form great circular loops called gyres, which turn anticlockwise in the southern hemisphere and clockwise in the northern hemisphere.

Currents are described as warm or cold according to where they come from compared with the water around them. Warm currents flow from the tropics towards the poles, carrying warm equatorial water to cooler latitudes. They are usually found along the eastern coasts of continents. Cold currents flow from polar or higher latitudes towards the equator, carrying cool water, and are usually found along the western coasts of continents.

Southern Africa is a perfect example. Along the east coast, the Agulhas Current flows southward from the Mozambique Channel and the tropical Indian Ocean, past KwaZulu-Natal and the Eastern Cape. It is one of the fastest currents in the world, reaching speeds of about two metres per second, and it is roughly one hundred kilometres wide. Its surface water off Durban is typically about twenty-one degrees in winter and about twenty-six in summer.

Along the west coast, the Benguela Current flows northward from the Southern Ocean past the Western Cape, the Northern Cape and Namibia. Its water is cold, typically about ten to fifteen degrees off Port Nolloth, even in summer. Part of the reason is that the south-easterly winds blowing along the west coast push surface water away from the shore, and cold, nutrient-rich water rises from the depths to replace it. This process is called upwelling, and it makes the coastal water even colder than the current alone would.

Because the two currents meet near the southern tip of Africa, the sea around the Cape Peninsula can differ by several degrees between False Bay, which receives some warmer water, and the Atlantic side at places like Clifton, which is famously cold for swimming.

Scientists track the currents using satellites that measure sea surface temperature and the height of the sea surface, drifting buoys that report their positions, and research ships that lower instruments into the water. Satellite images coloured by temperature show the warm Agulhas as a bright band hugging the east coast and the cold Benguela as a blue strip along the west coast, a picture that makes the contrast obvious at a glance.

## Subtopic: Comparing Durban and Port Nolloth

Durban lies at about twenty-nine point nine degrees south. Port Nolloth lies at about twenty-nine point two degrees south, less than one degree further north. Both are coastal towns at sea level. If latitude, altitude and distance from the sea were the only factors, their climates would be almost the same. They are not.

Look at approximate long-term averages. Durban's mean temperature in January is about twenty-four and a half degrees, and in July about seventeen degrees. Port Nolloth's mean temperature in January is about sixteen and a half degrees, and in July about twelve and a half. So in January Durban is about eight degrees warmer, and in July about four and a half degrees warmer, at the same latitude.

The rainfall difference is even more striking. Durban receives about one thousand millimetres of rain a year, most of it in summer, with some in every month. Port Nolloth receives only about sixty millimetres a year, mostly in winter. Divide one thousand by sixty and you find that Durban gets roughly sixteen times as much rain. Port Nolloth lies on the edge of the Namaqualand coastal desert, while Durban's surroundings were once covered in subtropical coastal forest.

The explanation lies in the currents. Off Durban, the warm Agulhas warms the air above it. Warm air can hold more water vapour, and warm water evaporates more easily, so the air flowing onshore from the Indian Ocean is warm and moist. When this air is forced to rise, by heating over land, by hills, or by weather systems, it cools and its moisture condenses into clouds and rain. Durban's humidity is high, often around seventy to eighty percent in summer, which makes the heat feel sticky.

Off Port Nolloth, the cold Benguela cools the air above it from below. Air that is cooled from below becomes stable: the cool, dense air near the surface does not want to rise, so the towering clouds that produce rain rarely form. Cold water also evaporates less, so the air holds little moisture. Instead, when moist air from the ocean passes over the cold water, it is cooled until its water vapour condenses into fog, which drifts onshore, especially in the mornings. Port Nolloth has fog on many days each year, but very little rain.

The vegetation along each coast tells the same story. Driving north from Durban, you pass sugar cane fields, banana plantations and patches of dune forest with tall trees, monkeys and many bird species. Driving north from Port Nolloth, you pass low, scattered shrubs and succulents on sandy plains, with almost no trees at all. In spring, after good winter rains, parts of Namaqualand inland burst into brief carpets of wild flowers, which draw tourists from around the world, but for most of the year the land is dry. Farmers in Namaqualand keep sheep and goats that can survive on sparse grazing, while farmers on the KwaZulu-Natal coast grow water-hungry crops. The currents shape not only weather but also land use and livelihoods.

## Subtopic: Currents, Deserts, Fog and Fishing

The link between cold currents and deserts appears around the world. The Namib Desert, along the coast of Namibia, is one of the driest places on earth, lying beside the cold Benguela Current. In South America, the Atacama Desert in Chile, beside the cold Humboldt Current, has places where rain has hardly ever been recorded. In both, fog from the cold ocean is often the main source of moisture. Some Namib beetles collect fog droplets on their backs to drink, and plants in the Namaqualand coastal zone, such as succulents, use fog and dew to survive.

Warm currents have the opposite effect. The Gulf Stream and its extension, the North Atlantic Drift, carry warm water from the Gulf of Mexico across the Atlantic to north-western Europe. They help keep Ireland, Britain and Norway much warmer in winter than places at the same latitude in Canada, and they supply moisture for frequent rain. Along South Africa's east coast, the Agulhas helps make the coastal belt of KwaZulu-Natal warm enough to grow sugar cane, bananas and other subtropical crops, which, as you saw in Term 1, is why sugar planting began there.

The currents also shape marine life and fishing. Upwelling in the Benguela brings nutrients from the deep ocean to the sunlit surface, where they feed huge amounts of plankton. Plankton feeds fish such as pilchards, anchovies and hake, which feed seals, seabirds such as Cape gannets and African penguins, and people. The west coast is therefore one of the world's richest fishing grounds, and towns such as Saldanha, St Helena Bay and Port Nolloth grew around fishing and fish processing. Rock lobster, also called crayfish, is an important catch. The warm Agulhas has fewer nutrients, but it supports coral reefs further north, at Sodwana Bay, and a great variety of tropical fish.

Each winter, along the east coast, millions of sardines move north from the cooler waters of the Eastern Cape into KwaZulu-Natal, in an event called the sardine run. A band of cooler water close to the shore, which forms in winter when the Agulhas moves slightly offshore, allows the sardines to travel up the coast. Dolphins, sharks, gannets and fishermen follow them.

Currents can also create hazards. Where the fast Agulhas meets strong winds and swells from the south-west, freak waves can form off the Wild Coast, and ships have been damaged or lost. Sailors and shipping routes take the current into account.

The questions for this section are with you now: cold currents and coastal deserts, warm currents and mild climates, and how currents shape fishing and marine life.

## Subtopic: Writing Explanations and the Error Museum

When you explain a difference caused by ocean currents, use this chain: identify the current and whether it is warm or cold; explain how it affects the temperature of the air; explain how it affects humidity and the stability of the air; explain the result for rainfall; and mention any related feature, such as fog or high humidity. For example: Port Nolloth lies beside the cold Benguela Current, which cools the air above it from below; cool air is stable and holds little moisture, so rain clouds rarely form and annual rainfall is only about sixty millimetres; instead, the cold water causes frequent fog.

Use the figures carefully. Durban's January mean is about twenty-four and a half degrees and Port Nolloth's about sixteen and a half, a difference of eight degrees. Durban's annual rainfall is about one thousand millimetres and Port Nolloth's about sixty, a difference of about nine hundred and forty millimetres.

Remember that ocean currents also affect the annual temperature range. Both towns, being on the coast, have small ranges: Durban about seven and a half degrees and Port Nolloth about four degrees. Port Nolloth's range is especially small because the cold sea keeps summer temperatures down, while winter temperatures are moderated by the ocean.

If a question gives you a third town, such as Richards Bay or Lüderitz, apply the same chain: identify the current offshore first, then reason about air temperature, humidity and rain.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the Benguela is cold because it is in the Atlantic; it is cold because it flows from the Southern Ocean and is strengthened by upwelling. Two: mixing up the currents; the warm Agulhas is on the east coast and the cold Benguela on the west coast. Three: saying fog is the same as rain; fog adds little measurable rainfall. Four: forgetting that Durban and Port Nolloth share the same latitude; that is what makes the comparison so useful. Five: saying cold currents make the coast cold and wet; they make it cool and dry.

Layout for marks in a comparison question: give both towns' figures for temperature and rainfall with units, calculate the differences, identify both currents and their direction, and explain the effect of each on temperature, humidity, air stability and rainfall. Finish by noting that latitude and altitude are the same, so the currents explain the difference.

The questions for this section are with you now: writing explanations, using the figures, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Rivers in the Sea

The ocean is not still. Huge streams of water, called currents, flow through it like rivers. Winds push them, and the turning earth bends them.

South Africa has two famous currents. On the east coast, the Agulhas Current rushes south from the warm tropics, so the sea at Durban is warm, about twenty-one to twenty-six degrees. On the west coast, the Benguela Current flows north from the cold Southern Ocean, and cold water also rises up from the deep, so the sea at Port Nolloth is cold, about ten to fifteen degrees.

Hold the warm stream on the right and the cold stream on the left in mind and try the questions for this part: what ocean currents are.

## Subtopic: Two Towns, One Latitude

Durban and Port Nolloth are at almost the same distance from the equator, and both are on the beach. So they should feel the same, right? Not at all.

Durban in January averages about twenty-four and a half degrees, warm and sticky, and gets about one thousand millimetres of rain a year. Port Nolloth in January averages about sixteen and a half, cool and grey, and gets only about sixty millimetres. That is about sixteen times less rain.

Why? The warm Agulhas warms the air and fills it with moisture, so clouds and rain form. The cold Benguela cools the air from below, so it stays still and dry. Instead of rain, Port Nolloth gets fog rolling in off the cold sea.

Hold the rainy green coast and the foggy desert coast in mind and try this part's questions: comparing Durban and Port Nolloth.

## Subtopic: Deserts, Fog and Fish

Cold currents make dry coasts all over the world: the Namib Desert next to the Benguela, the Atacama next to the cold current off Chile. Warm currents make coasts warm and wet, like Durban, where sugar cane grows.

But the cold water brings a gift: food. Cold water rising from the deep carries nutrients that feed plankton, which feed fish, seals, penguins and people. That is why the west coast has big fishing towns.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Ocean currents are flows of water in the sea. The warm Agulhas flows south along the east coast, warming the air and adding moisture, so Durban is warm, humid and wet. The cold Benguela flows north along the west coast, with upwelling, cooling the air and keeping it stable and dry, so Port Nolloth is cool, foggy and very dry. Both towns share the same latitude, so the currents explain the difference. Cold upwelling also makes the west coast rich in fish.
