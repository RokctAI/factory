# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the fifth factor that influences temperature and rainfall: relief, meaning the shape and height of the land, especially mountains and hills. The anchor is a comparison between Durban, on the coast at sea level, and Mthatha, formerly called Umtata, about seventy-five kilometres inland at about seven hundred metres above sea level in the Eastern Cape. The governing idea is that mountains force moving air to rise, which cools it and causes orographic rain on the windward side, while the sinking air on the leeward side warms and dries, creating a rain shadow; mountains also act as barriers, and the direction a slope faces affects how much sunshine it receives. Explaining the effect of relief using data and diagrams is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Orographic Rain and the Rain Shadow

When wind carrying moist air reaches a mountain range, the air cannot pass through it, so it is forced to rise over it. As the air rises, the pressure around it falls, it expands, and it cools, as you learnt in the lesson on altitude. Cool air can hold less water vapour than warm air. When the rising air has cooled enough, it reaches its dew point, the temperature at which its water vapour condenses into tiny droplets, and clouds form on the mountain slopes. As the air continues rising, the droplets grow and fall as rain, or as snow on high mountains. This is called orographic or relief rain, from the Greek word oros, meaning mountain.

The side of the mountain facing the wind is called the windward side. It receives the most rain, and it is often green and forested. The air that crosses the summit has lost much of its moisture. As it descends the far side, called the leeward side, it is compressed by the increasing pressure and warms up. Warming air can hold more moisture, so clouds evaporate and rain is unlikely. The leeward side is therefore drier, often much drier. This dry area is called a rain shadow.

Air descending a mountain warms at about ten degrees per thousand metres when it is dry, while rising air that is forming cloud cools more slowly, at about five to six degrees per thousand metres, because condensation releases heat. The result is that air descending the leeward side can end up warmer, as well as drier, than it was at the same height on the windward side. In some regions this produces hot, dry, gusty winds called föhn winds. In South Africa, the berg wind is a similar wind: hot, dry air flowing down from the interior plateau towards the coast, often in winter, which can raise temperatures on the coast to over thirty degrees and increase the risk of fires.

A classic South African example is the Cape Fold Mountains. Winter cold fronts bring moist north-westerly winds to the south-western Cape. At Newlands, on the windward slopes near Table Mountain, the annual rainfall is around one thousand five hundred millimetres, and in the Jonkershoek valley near Stellenbosch it can exceed two thousand. Beyond the mountains, in the Breede River valley at Worcester, rainfall drops to about two hundred and fifty millimetres, and the Karoo beyond receives even less. Farmers in the Breede valley depend on irrigation from rivers and dams fed by the wet mountains.

The Drakensberg shows the same pattern on a larger scale. In summer, moist air from the Indian Ocean flows from the east and south-east and rises up the escarpment. The eastern slopes and the KwaZulu-Natal Midlands receive heavy summer rain, often over one thousand millimetres a year, with more than one thousand five hundred on the high slopes. Behind the escarpment, the western parts of Lesotho and the eastern Free State are drier.

## Subtopic: Comparing Mthatha and Durban

Durban lies on the KwaZulu-Natal coast at sea level, at about thirty degrees south, beside the warm Agulhas Current, with hills rising behind the city towards the interior. Mthatha lies inland in the Eastern Cape at about thirty-one and a half degrees south, at about seven hundred metres above sea level, behind higher ground that rises from the coast.

Look at approximate averages. Durban's annual rainfall is about one thousand millimetres. Mthatha's is about six hundred and fifty millimetres. Durban's January mean temperature is about twenty-four and a half degrees and its July mean about seventeen. Mthatha's January mean is about twenty-one degrees and its July mean about twelve. So Mthatha is drier, cooler, and has a larger annual range, nine degrees against Durban's seven and a half.

How does relief explain the rainfall difference? The moist air that brings rain to this part of South Africa comes mostly from the Indian Ocean, from the south-east and east. When this air meets the coastal hills and the rising land behind them, it is forced upward, cools, and drops much of its moisture on the seaward slopes and coastal belt. By the time the air has crossed the higher ground and reaches places inland, it has lost some of its moisture. Mthatha, inland of this higher ground, receives less rain than the coast, especially from the steady onshore flow. Most of its rain comes from summer thunderstorms rather than from moist coastal air.

Other factors add to the difference, and a careful geographer separates them. Mthatha's altitude of seven hundred metres makes it about four and a half degrees cooler than a sea-level place, using the lapse rate. Its distance from the sea gives it a slightly larger range, with colder winter nights and occasional frost. Durban's warm current adds moisture and warmth. Mthatha's latitude is slightly further south. So the temperature difference is explained mainly by altitude, distance from the sea and the warm current, while the rainfall difference is explained largely by relief and distance from the moist air source.

Local relief also explains variation within each area. In KwaZulu-Natal and the Eastern Cape, the hills and valleys mean that rainfall can change significantly over just a few kilometres: windward hilltops receive more rain than sheltered valleys, and valleys running inland can channel moist air further than flat land would.

The landscape between the two places shows the effect. Travelling inland from the coast of the former Transkei region, the land climbs through a series of rolling hills and deeply cut river valleys, such as those of the Mthatha and Mzimvubu rivers. The coastal belt is greener, with patches of forest in sheltered valleys, while the higher interior has more open grassland. Farmers in the inland areas plant maize and keep cattle, relying on the summer rains, and dry winters mean that grazing becomes sparse by August and September. Water supply for Mthatha comes from dams on the Mthatha River, whose catchment receives more rain on its higher slopes.

## Subtopic: Mountains as Barriers, and Slope Aspect

Mountains affect climate in other ways besides orographic rain. They act as barriers that block or redirect air masses. The Great Escarpment helps keep the moist air of the east coast from penetrating far into the interior in some conditions, and it shelters the interior from some coastal influences. On a much larger scale, the Himalayas block cold air from central Asia from reaching India, which keeps India warmer in winter, and they force the summer monsoon winds to rise, giving north-eastern India some of the highest rainfall on earth.

The direction a slope faces, called its aspect, affects how much sunshine and heat it receives. In the southern hemisphere, the sun is in the northern part of the sky at midday for most of the year, especially in winter. North-facing slopes therefore receive more direct sunlight and are warmer and drier, while south-facing slopes receive slanting light or are in shadow for long periods, so they are cooler and damper. In the northern hemisphere, the opposite is true: south-facing slopes are the sunny ones.

People use aspect in many ways. In the Western Cape, vineyards are often planted on slopes chosen for the right amount of sun. On the highveld, houses are designed with living rooms and windows facing north to catch the winter sun. In mountain valleys, south-facing slopes may keep snow longer and carry forest or thicker vegetation, while north-facing slopes are covered in grass or scrub. In the Drakensberg, you can often see the difference clearly: grassy, sunny north slopes and shady, bushier south slopes.

Mountains also create local winds. During the day, the sun warms mountain slopes, and air flows up the valleys as valley breezes. At night, the slopes cool quickly, and cold, dense air flows down into the valleys as mountain breezes. As you learnt in the altitude lesson, this cold air can collect in valley bottoms, causing frost and temperature inversions, which is why fruit farmers avoid planting frost-sensitive orchards on valley floors.

Snow shows the combined effect of relief and altitude very clearly. When a strong cold front reaches South Africa in winter, the moist air is lifted over the high ground of the Eastern Cape, the Drakensberg and the Cape Fold Mountains, and at those heights the precipitation falls as snow. Roads such as the passes over the Drakensberg and the Eastern Cape highlands can be closed for days, while towns on the coast below receive only cold rain.

## Subtopic: Diagrams, Explanations and the Error Museum

Relief questions often ask you to draw or label a diagram of orographic rain. A good diagram shows the mountain in cross-section, with the sea or a source of moisture on one side; an arrow showing moist wind blowing towards the mountain; arrows showing the air rising up the windward side; clouds forming and rain falling on the windward slope; arrows showing air descending the leeward side; and labels for windward side, leeward side, rain shadow, condensation level, and warm dry descending air. Add the words cools as it rises on the windward side and warms as it sinks on the leeward side.

When you write an explanation, use the full chain: moist air from the sea is forced to rise over the mountains; it expands and cools; at the dew point it condenses into cloud; rain falls on the windward side; the air descending the leeward side is compressed and warms, so it can hold more moisture and clouds evaporate; the leeward side is in a rain shadow and is drier.

A quick calculation supports the explanation. Newlands receives about one thousand five hundred millimetres a year and Worcester about two hundred and fifty. Divide one thousand five hundred by two hundred and fifty and you get six: the windward station receives about six times as much rain as the leeward one, even though they are less than one hundred kilometres apart.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the leeward side is dry because the mountain blocks the clouds like a wall; it is dry because the descending air warms and the moisture has already fallen. Two: confusing windward and leeward. Three: saying north-facing slopes are colder in South Africa; in the southern hemisphere they are the sunnier, warmer slopes. Four: explaining Mthatha's lower temperature only by relief; altitude, distance from the sea and the current matter. Five: calling a berg wind a cold wind; it is hot and dry because the air warms as it descends.

Layout for marks in a relief question: draw and label the diagram, then explain in five steps, rising, cooling, condensing, raining, descending and warming, and give a real example with figures.

## Subtopic: The Wet Side and the Dry Side

Picture wet air blowing in from the sea towards a mountain. It cannot go through, so it climbs up. As it climbs, it cools. Cool air cannot hold as much water, so clouds form and rain pours down on that side of the mountain. That side, facing the wind, is called the windward side. It is green and wet.

The air goes over the top, now with less water, and sinks down the other side. Sinking air warms up, and warm air soaks up the clouds instead of making rain. So that side, the leeward side, is dry. We call it a rain shadow.

In the Western Cape, Newlands, on the windward side near Table Mountain, gets about six times as much rain as Worcester on the other side of the mountains.

## Subtopic: Mthatha Behind the Hills

Durban sits on the coast, and the wet air from the Indian Ocean reaches it first. It gets about one thousand millimetres of rain a year.

Mthatha sits inland, about seven hundred metres up, behind higher ground. The wet sea air drops much of its rain on the slopes nearer the coast before it gets there. So Mthatha gets about six hundred and fifty millimetres. It is also cooler, because it is higher and further from the warm sea.

## Subtopic: Sunny Slopes and Shady Slopes

In South Africa, the sun is in the northern part of the sky. So slopes that face north get more sun and are warmer and drier. Slopes that face south are cooler and shadier. That is why highveld houses put their big windows on the north side.

Mountains also block winds, and at night cold air slides down into valleys, bringing frost.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Moist air forced over mountains rises, cools, condenses and rains on the windward side; air sinking on the leeward side warms and dries, creating a rain shadow. Newlands gets about six times as much rain as Worcester. Durban gets about one thousand millimetres; Mthatha, inland behind higher ground and about seven hundred metres up, gets about six hundred and fifty and is cooler. In the southern hemisphere north-facing slopes are sunnier and warmer.
