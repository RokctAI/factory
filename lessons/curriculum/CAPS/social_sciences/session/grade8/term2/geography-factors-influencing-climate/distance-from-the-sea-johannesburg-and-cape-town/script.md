# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the second factor that influences temperature: distance from the sea. The anchor is a comparison between Johannesburg, which lies inland on the highveld, roughly five hundred kilometres from the nearest coast at Durban or Maputo, and Cape Town, which lies on the coast where the Atlantic meets the edge of the continent. The governing idea is that the sea heats up and cools down much more slowly than land, so it moderates the temperatures of nearby coastal places, giving them smaller daily and yearly ranges, while inland places, far from this influence, experience greater extremes. Geographers call the inland effect continentality and the coastal effect maritime influence. Explaining these effects with data is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Why Land and Sea Heat Differently

On a sunny day at the beach, the sand can become too hot to walk on by midday, while the water still feels cool. At night the sand cools quickly, but the sea stays almost the same temperature. This everyday experience shows the key principle: land heats and cools quickly, water heats and cools slowly.

There are four main reasons. First, water has a high specific heat capacity, which means it takes much more energy to raise the temperature of a kilogram of water by one degree than to raise the temperature of a kilogram of rock or soil by one degree. Water needs about four times as much energy as dry soil for the same rise in temperature. So the same sunshine warms the land far more than the sea.

Second, water is transparent, so sunlight penetrates several metres below the surface and its heat is spread through a large volume. On land, sunlight is absorbed in the top few centimetres of soil, which heat up quickly.

Third, water moves. Waves, currents and mixing carry warm surface water down and bring cooler water up, spreading heat through a deep layer. Land cannot mix in this way.

Fourth, some of the sun's energy at the sea surface is used to evaporate water rather than to raise its temperature, which further slows warming.

The result is that the sea acts like a giant storage heater. In summer it absorbs heat slowly and stays cooler than the land; in winter it releases its stored heat slowly and stays warmer than the land. Winds blowing from the sea carry this moderating influence onto nearby land. Coastal places therefore have cooler summers and milder winters than inland places at a similar latitude and altitude, and smaller differences between day and night.

Inland places, far from the sea's influence, heat up strongly in summer and during the day, and cool down strongly in winter and at night. The air inland is also usually drier, with fewer clouds at night, and clear skies allow heat to escape quickly into space after sunset, which makes nights colder still. That is why the interior of a continent has greater temperature ranges.

The questions for this section are with you now: why water heats and cools more slowly than land, the sea as a storage heater, and continentality.

## Subtopic: Comparing Johannesburg and Cape Town

Johannesburg lies at about twenty-six degrees south, about one thousand seven hundred and fifty metres above sea level, far inland. Cape Town lies at about thirty-four degrees south, at sea level, on the coast. The two cities differ in three factors at once: distance from the sea, altitude and latitude. A geographer must therefore be careful about which differences to explain by which factor.

Look at approximate long-term averages. In January, Johannesburg's average daily maximum is about twenty-six degrees and its average minimum about fifteen degrees. Cape Town's January maximum is also about twenty-six degrees and its minimum about sixteen degrees. In July, Johannesburg's average maximum is about seventeen degrees and its minimum about four degrees. Cape Town's July maximum is about eighteen degrees and its minimum about seven degrees.

The clearest effect of distance from the sea is on the daily temperature range, the difference between the maximum and minimum on an average day. In July, Johannesburg's daily range is seventeen minus four, which is thirteen degrees. Cape Town's is eighteen minus seven, which is eleven degrees. In Johannesburg, the dry, clear winter air lets heat escape quickly at night, and there is no sea nearby to supply warmth, so winter nights are cold and frost is common. Cape Town's nights are kept milder by the ocean, and frost almost never occurs in the city.

The annual range, the difference between the mean temperatures of the warmest and coolest months, is similar in the two cities, about nine degrees in both, if you calculate the means from the figures above. This is where the other factors complicate things. Johannesburg's lower latitude gives it more summer sunshine and warmer winters than its continental position alone would suggest, while its high altitude cools it all year. Cape Town's position next to the cold Benguela Current keeps its summers cooler than they would otherwise be. Geographers therefore look at several measures, not just one.

Humidity also differs. Cape Town's air is moister because of the nearby sea; in summer, the cold ocean and the south-easterly wind often produce cloud over Table Mountain, called the tablecloth. Johannesburg's winter air is very dry, with relative humidity often below thirty percent in the afternoons, which is one reason for the large daily range.

A cleaner South African test of distance from the sea uses two places at similar latitude and similar height, one coastal and one inland. Compare Port Nolloth on the west coast with Springbok, about one hundred and twenty kilometres inland, although Springbok is about one thousand metres high. Or compare Durban with Pietermaritzburg, about eighty kilometres inland and about six hundred and fifty metres high. In each pair, the inland town has hotter summer afternoons, colder winter nights and a larger daily range, even after allowing for its height. Pietermaritzburg's summer afternoons are often hotter than Durban's, and its winter nights are several degrees colder, sometimes with frost in the valleys, while Durban never has frost. Short distances inland are enough for the sea's influence to weaken noticeably.

The questions for this section are with you now: the locations of Johannesburg and Cape Town, comparing daily ranges, and why the comparison is complicated by other factors.

## Subtopic: Continental and Maritime Climates Around the World

The effect of distance from the sea is clearer on a larger scale. Compare two places at similar latitudes and low altitude, one on a coast and one deep inside a continent. In the northern hemisphere, Vancouver on the Pacific coast of Canada and Winnipeg in the middle of North America both lie at about fifty degrees north. Vancouver's January mean temperature is about four degrees and its July mean about eighteen, an annual range of about fourteen degrees. Winnipeg's January mean is about minus sixteen degrees and its July mean about twenty, an annual range of about thirty-six degrees. Winnipeg has a continental climate with very cold winters and warm summers, while Vancouver has a maritime climate with mild winters and cool summers.

The most extreme continental climates are found in the interior of Asia. In eastern Siberia, the town of Yakutsk has winter temperatures that average around minus thirty-eight degrees in January, while summer days in July average around nineteen degrees, an annual range of more than fifty degrees.

Southern Africa is narrower than these continents, and no place in South Africa is more than about six hundred kilometres from the sea, so the continental effect is less extreme. Even so, the interior of South Africa shows it clearly. Towns in the Northern Cape and Free State, such as Upington and Bloemfontein, have very hot summer days, often above thirty-five degrees in Upington, and cold winter nights with frost, while coastal towns such as Durban and Gqeberha have milder extremes.

The sea also affects rainfall indirectly. Coastal places usually have more moisture in the air, while air travelling a long way inland loses moisture as it rains on the way. That is one reason why the eastern half of South Africa, which receives moist air from the Indian Ocean, is wetter than the western interior, and why rainfall generally decreases from east to west across the country.

Land and sea breezes are a small-scale example of the same principle. On a sunny day, the land heats faster than the sea, the air over the land rises, and cooler air flows in from the sea as a sea breeze, often in the afternoon. At night, the land cools faster, and a gentle land breeze can blow towards the sea. People on the coast feel these breezes as a natural air conditioner on hot days.

Daily life adapts to these patterns. Houses on the highveld are often built with thick walls and north-facing windows to catch the winter sun and keep warmth in at night, and many families use heaters on winter evenings. Coastal houses in Durban are designed for airflow and shade, because the problem there is humidity and steady heat rather than cold nights. Farmers inland must plan for frost, which can destroy crops such as tomatoes and young citrus trees, so frost-sensitive crops are grown mainly near the coast or in warm, low valleys.

The questions for this section are with you now: maritime and continental climates around the world, the South African interior, and land and sea breezes.

## Subtopic: Writing Explanations and the Error Museum

When you explain a temperature difference caused by distance from the sea, use a clear chain: state the data, name the factor, explain the property of water, explain the result. For example: in July Johannesburg's daily range is about thirteen degrees and Cape Town's about eleven; Cape Town is next to the sea, which cools and heats slowly and releases stored warmth at night, so its nights are milder; Johannesburg is far inland, with dry, clear air that lets heat escape, so its nights are colder and frost is common.

Always check other factors. If two places differ in altitude, remember that temperatures fall by about six and a half degrees for every thousand metres of height. If they differ in latitude, remember that the sun's rays are more concentrated nearer the equator. If one is on a coast with a cold or warm current, remember that the current affects the temperature of the sea air. A strong answer names the main factor and acknowledges the others.

A numerical check helps. Johannesburg is about one thousand seven hundred and fifty metres higher than Cape Town. At about six and a half degrees per thousand metres, that height alone would make Johannesburg about eleven degrees cooler if all else were equal. Yet Johannesburg's January maximum is about the same as Cape Town's, which shows that its lower latitude and inland heating are making up for most of the altitude effect.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the sea makes coastal places hotter; it makes them milder, cooler in summer and warmer in winter. Two: thinking water heats slowly because it is wet; the reasons are its high specific heat, transparency, mixing and evaporation. Three: comparing only average temperatures; the sea's effect shows most in the ranges. Four: forgetting that Johannesburg and Cape Town also differ in altitude and latitude. Five: saying that inland places are always drier; it depends on the direction of moist winds.

Layout for marks in a describe and explain question: describe the pattern with figures, then give the explanation using the properties of land and water, then add a qualifying sentence about other factors. Spend about one line on the description, two or three on the explanation and one on the qualification.

The questions for this section are with you now: writing explanations, separating the factors, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: hot sand and cool water, Johannesburg and Cape Town, and coasts versus inland around the world.

## Subtopic: Hot Sand, Cool Water

Think about a summer day at the beach. By lunchtime the sand burns your feet, but the water still feels cool. At night the sand goes cold, but the water stays about the same.

That is the big idea: land heats up fast and cools down fast; water heats up slowly and cools down slowly. Water needs much more energy to warm up, sunlight sinks into it, and waves keep mixing it.

So the sea is like a giant heater that stores warmth. In winter it gives warmth back to the coast, and in summer it keeps the coast cooler. Places far from the sea do not get this help, so they get hotter days and colder nights.

Hold the hot sand and the cool water in mind and try the questions for this part: why land and sea heat differently.

## Subtopic: Frost in Johannesburg, Dew in Cape Town

On a July night, Johannesburg can drop to about four degrees on average, and frost covers the grass. Cape Town drops to about seven, and frost is very rare. In the afternoon, both reach about seventeen or eighteen.

So the difference between day and night in Johannesburg is bigger: about thirteen degrees, against about eleven in Cape Town. Johannesburg is far from the sea, and its dry, clear winter air lets the heat escape at night. Cape Town has the sea right next to it, keeping its nights milder.

Remember, Johannesburg is also much higher and further north, so not every difference comes from the sea.

Hold the frosty lawn and the damp one in mind and try this part's questions: comparing Johannesburg and Cape Town.

## Subtopic: Coasts and Insides

Look at bigger continents and the pattern is huge. In Canada, Vancouver on the coast has mild winters, while Winnipeg in the middle of the continent drops to about minus sixteen in January. In Siberia, winters reach about minus thirty-eight.

In South Africa, places like Upington in the Northern Cape get very hot days and frosty nights, while Durban on the coast stays mild.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Water heats and cools more slowly than land because of its high specific heat, its transparency, its mixing and evaporation. So the sea keeps coastal places mild, with smaller daily and yearly ranges. Inland places have greater extremes: continentality. Johannesburg's July daily range is about thirteen degrees and Cape Town's about eleven, but altitude and latitude also affect the comparison.

Hold the sand, the frost and the giant continent, and take the final questions of the lesson: distance from the sea.
