### subtopic_1 — What Latitude Measures and Why It Matters
**Assistant:** If the earth is closer to the sun in January, why is it winter in the northern hemisphere then?

**Tutor:** Because the seasons are caused by the tilt of the earth's axis, not by the distance from the sun, and the change in distance is too small to matter much. The earth's orbit around the sun is almost a circle, but not quite. In early January the earth is at its closest point, about one hundred and forty-seven million kilometres from the sun, and in early July it is at its furthest, about one hundred and fifty-two million kilometres. That difference changes the energy reaching the earth by only a few percent. What matters far more is that the earth's axis is tilted by about twenty-three and a half degrees, and it stays pointing in the same direction in space throughout the year. In December and January, the southern hemisphere is tilted towards the sun. The sun is high in our sky at midday, its rays strike us more directly, and our days are long, so we have summer. At the same time, the northern hemisphere is tilted away from the sun, its days are short and the sun is low, so it has winter, even though the whole earth is slightly closer to the sun. In June and July the situation reverses. This is also why the seasons are opposite in the two hemispheres: when it is summer in Johannesburg it is winter in London. The tilt is also the reason why the overhead sun moves between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn during the year, and why the global pressure belts shift north and south with the seasons. So if someone says summer happens because we are closer to the sun, you can ask them why Australia and Canada have opposite seasons at the same moment. That single question shows that distance cannot be the explanation.

### subtopic_2 — Comparing Mbombela and George
**Assistant:** How do we know that the difference between Mbombela and George is caused by latitude and not something else?

**Tutor:** We cannot be completely certain from one comparison, which is why geographers think carefully about other factors and compare many places. A good scientific comparison changes only one factor at a time, but real places differ in many ways at once. Mbombela and George differ in latitude, about twenty-five and a half against thirty-four degrees south, but also in altitude, about six hundred and sixty metres against two hundred metres, and in distance from the sea, since Mbombela is far inland and George is near the coast. They also face different ocean influences. To judge latitude's role, a geographer asks what each other factor would do. Higher altitude makes places cooler, roughly six and a half degrees for every thousand metres, so Mbombela's extra four hundred and sixty metres would make it about three degrees cooler if all else were equal. Yet Mbombela is warmer, which suggests that latitude is having a strong warming effect. Distance from the sea mainly affects the range of temperature rather than the average; George's position near the sea helps explain its smaller annual range and its milder summers. Then the geographer checks the pattern elsewhere. Across South Africa, and across the world, places at lower latitudes are generally warmer when other factors are similar. For example, at sea level, Durban at about thirty degrees south is warmer than Port Elizabeth, now Gqeberha, at about thirty-four degrees. When the same pattern appears in many comparisons, we can be confident that latitude is an important cause. In an exam answer, name latitude as the main factor, explain how it works, and then mention other factors that may also contribute. That shows the examiner you understand that climate has many causes.

### subtopic_3 — Latitude, Seasons and Rainfall
**Assistant:** Why does the summer-rainfall region get its rain from thunderstorms in the afternoon?

**Tutor:** Because the rain is produced by convection, which depends on the ground heating up during the day. In summer, the sun is high over the interior of South Africa, and the ground absorbs a lot of energy during the morning. The ground heats the air in contact with it. Warm air is less dense than the cooler air around it, so it begins to rise, in columns called thermals. At the same time, in summer, moist air flows in from the warm Indian Ocean over the eastern and northern parts of the country, so the rising air contains plenty of water vapour. As the air rises, it expands and cools, about ten degrees for every thousand metres while it is dry. Eventually it cools to the temperature at which its water vapour condenses into tiny droplets, forming a cloud. Condensation releases heat, which keeps the air rising further, so the cloud grows taller and taller into a towering cumulonimbus cloud, sometimes more than ten kilometres high. Inside, droplets collide and grow, and ice forms in the cold upper parts. When the drops or hail become too heavy, they fall as heavy rain, often with lightning, thunder and strong gusts. Because the heating builds up through the morning, storms usually form in the early to late afternoon, and they often die down after sunset when the ground cools. That is why people in Gauteng and Mpumalanga often see clear mornings and storms in the afternoon in summer. This kind of rain falls heavily over short periods, which can cause flash floods and soil erosion, and some of it runs off before soaking in. Farmers and town planners must take this into account when designing drainage and protecting soil.

### subtopic_4 — Using Climate Data and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What would happen to South Africa's climate if the country were at the same latitude as Europe?

**Tutor:** It would be much colder, and the pattern of seasons and rainfall would be quite different, which is a useful thought experiment for seeing how much latitude matters. Most of Europe lies between about thirty-five and seventy degrees north. If South Africa were moved to, say, forty-five to fifty-eight degrees, roughly the latitudes of France to Scotland, the sun would be much lower in the sky all year. In winter the midday sun would rise only a little above the horizon, and days would be very short, perhaps eight hours or less in the north of that range. Summers would be milder and days much longer. Average temperatures would fall sharply; the highveld, at about one and a half to two kilometres above sea level, would have long, cold winters with regular snow, as high plateaus in Europe do. The rainfall pattern would also change. Instead of summer thunderstorms fed by warm Indian Ocean air, the country would be in the belt of westerly winds and mid-latitude cyclones, receiving rain from fronts throughout the year, more in winter. The vegetation would change too: no subtropical fruit in the Lowveld, no savanna, but mixed forests and grasslands suited to cooler climates. Crops like maize and sugar cane would struggle, while wheat, potatoes and barley might do better. Interestingly, Western Europe is actually warmer than other places at the same latitude because of the warm North Atlantic Drift, an ocean current, and westerly winds from the ocean, which shows again that latitude works together with other factors. The thought experiment helps us appreciate that South Africa's mild, sunny climate owes a great deal to its position between about twenty-two and thirty-five degrees south.
