### subtopic_1 — Why It Gets Colder Higher Up
**Assistant:** If heat rises, why is it colder at the top of a mountain?

**Tutor:** This is a very common puzzle, and the answer is that warm air does rise, but as it rises it expands and cools, and a mountain top is surrounded by that cooled, thin air, not by the warm air from the valley. Start with what heat rising really means. Warm air is less dense than the cooler air around it, so it floats upwards, like a hot-air balloon. But the atmosphere is not a closed room with a ceiling where warm air collects. As a parcel of air rises, the pressure around it falls, because there is less air above pressing down. The parcel expands, and when a gas expands, its molecules spread out and do work pushing against the surrounding air, which uses energy and lowers the temperature. This cooling happens even though no heat is taken away; scientists call it adiabatic cooling. Dry rising air cools by about ten degrees for every thousand metres. So by the time valley air has risen to a high mountain top, it has become much colder. In a house, warm air does collect near the ceiling, because the ceiling stops it rising further and the height difference is only a couple of metres, too small for expansion to matter. Another part of the answer is that the air is heated by the ground. A mountain top is a small area of ground surrounded mostly by free air at that height, while a valley or plain has a large surface heating the air around it. Finally, high air is thin, holding less water vapour and carbon dioxide to trap heat, so heat escapes easily. Put together, these explain why heat rises and yet mountain tops are cold. Clouds forming over mountains are a visible sign of rising air cooling until its water vapour condenses.

### subtopic_2 — Johannesburg and Cape Town Compared
**Assistant:** Why does South Africa have such a high interior plateau?

**Tutor:** Because of the geological history of southern Africa over hundreds of millions of years, involving the breakup of an ancient supercontinent, uplift of the land and erosion along the edges. About one hundred and eighty million years ago, southern Africa was part of a huge supercontinent called Gondwana, joined to South America, Antarctica, India and Australia. Great outpourings of lava, which formed the basalt of the high Drakensberg and Lesotho, accompanied the beginning of the breakup. As Gondwana split apart and the oceans opened, the edges of the African landmass were affected by uplift, and over tens of millions of years southern Africa as a whole was raised. Geologists believe that hot material deep in the earth's mantle beneath southern Africa helped push the region upwards, creating what is sometimes called the African superswell. Once the land was raised, rivers began cutting back into it from the coast, eroding the edges more quickly than the interior. This created the Great Escarpment, the steep edge that separates the high plateau from the coastal lowlands, and that runs in a great arc from the Limpopo through Mpumalanga, the Drakensberg, the Eastern Cape and the Karoo to Namibia. Behind the escarpment, the interior has remained high and relatively flat, gently tilted, with large rivers such as the Orange and Vaal flowing westward across it. The result is a basin-like shape: a high interior plateau surrounded by a narrow coastal strip. This shape influences climate in many ways: altitude makes the interior cooler; the escarpment forces moist air to rise on its eastern side, causing rain, while leaving drier areas behind it; and the plateau's height helps create frost and large daily ranges. Geography and geology are deeply connected.

### subtopic_3 — Altitude Across South Africa and the World
**Assistant:** Do people really get altitude sickness in Johannesburg?

**Tutor:** Mild effects are common for visitors from the coast, but serious altitude sickness is unusual at Johannesburg's height; it becomes a real danger higher up, usually above about two thousand five hundred metres. At about one thousand seven hundred and fifty metres, the air pressure is about eighty-two percent of that at sea level, so each breath contains about eighteen percent fewer oxygen molecules. The body responds by breathing faster and making the heart work a little harder, and over a few weeks it produces more red blood cells to carry oxygen. Visitors arriving from Durban or Cape Town may notice that they get out of breath more quickly when climbing stairs or exercising, that they feel a little tired, sleep less well for the first night or two, or get a mild headache. Drinking enough water helps, because the dry air and faster breathing cause the body to lose more moisture. Sports teams sometimes arrive early before matches at the highveld so that players can adjust, and it is often said that visiting teams tire faster in the second half on the highveld. On the other hand, athletes who live and train at altitude, such as many runners from the highlands of Kenya and Ethiopia, develop strong endurance, which may help them when they race at lower altitudes. Real altitude sickness, with severe headaches, nausea, dizziness and, in rare cases, dangerous swelling of the lungs or brain, mainly affects people climbing high mountains such as Kilimanjaro too quickly. The rule for climbers is to ascend slowly and to descend if symptoms become severe. So for most people, Johannesburg's altitude is noticeable but harmless, and it is a good example of how climate factors also affect human bodies.

### subtopic_4 — Calculations and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why is Sutherland so cold when it is not in the mountains like the Drakensberg?

**Tutor:** Because Sutherland combines several factors that all push its winter nights down: high altitude, a continental inland position, very dry clear air, and its location in a basin where cold air collects. Sutherland lies on the Roggeveld plateau in the Northern Cape at about one thousand four hundred and fifty metres above sea level, which by itself makes it about nine degrees cooler than a sea-level place nearby. It is about two hundred and fifty kilometres from the nearest coast, so the sea's moderating influence does not reach it, and it has a continental pattern of large daily and yearly ranges. Its air is extremely dry, especially in winter, when high pressure dominates the interior, so skies are usually clear and there is little water vapour to trap heat at night. On calm winter nights, heat radiates away from the ground very quickly, and cold, dense air drains downhill into the low ground where the town lies, a local temperature inversion. Together these factors produce winter minimums that are regularly several degrees below zero; temperatures around minus ten degrees have been recorded, and snow falls in some winters when cold fronts bring moisture. Interestingly, Sutherland's summer days can be quite warm, so its annual and daily ranges are large. The same clear, dry, dark skies that make its nights cold make it one of the best places in the world for astronomy. That is why the Southern African Large Telescope, the largest single optical telescope in the southern hemisphere, and many other telescopes are located near the town. Sutherland is a good example of how the factors you study in this unit, altitude, distance from the sea, humidity and local relief, combine to create a local climate.
