### subtopic_1 — Gold on the Witwatersrand
**Assistant:** Why was the price of gold fixed when other prices could rise and fall?

**Tutor:** Because in the late nineteenth century, most major countries used a system called the gold standard, in which their money was officially defined as a fixed amount of gold. Britain, the most important trading nation, had been on the gold standard for most of the nineteenth century, and Germany, the United States and others joined in the 1870s and later. Under this system, the Bank of England promised to exchange its banknotes for gold at a fixed rate, so one ounce of standard gold was worth a fixed amount of pounds, about three pounds seventeen shillings and ten and a half pence. Because the value of money itself was tied to gold, the price of gold in pounds could not rise. For the Witwatersrand mines, this had huge consequences. A mine producing copper or wool could hope that the price would rise if demand increased, but a gold mine could not. Its income depended only on how much gold it produced, while its costs, such as wages, dynamite, coal, machinery and railway charges, could rise. The only way to increase profits was to produce more gold more cheaply. This is why historians say that the fixed gold price was one of the most important reasons for the mining industry's determination to keep black wages low and to recruit large numbers of migrant workers. It also explains why the mine owners were so angry about the Kruger government's dynamite monopoly and railway rates, which added to their costs. The world left the gold standard during the twentieth century, and the gold price was freed in the early 1970s, after which it rose sharply, but for the first eighty years of Rand mining, the fixed price shaped everything.

### subtopic_2 — The Randlords
**Assistant:** Were the Randlords heroes who built Johannesburg, or villains who exploited workers?

**Tutor:** Historians try to avoid simple labels like heroes and villains, and instead look at what the Randlords did, why they did it and what the consequences were for different people. On one side, the Randlords raised enormous amounts of capital, organised complex mining operations and turned the Witwatersrand into the world's largest gold-producing region. Johannesburg grew into a major city, railways and industries developed, and the South African economy was transformed. Some Randlords also gave money to universities, hospitals, art galleries and libraries, such as the gifts that helped found the University of the Witwatersrand. On the other side, their wealth was built on a labour system that paid black workers very low wages, housed them in compounds, controlled them with pass laws and exposed them to dangerous conditions in which many died. Through the Chamber of Mines, the Randlords deliberately cooperated to push black wages down. Some were also involved in political schemes, such as the Jameson Raid, that helped lead to a destructive war. Their contemporaries criticised them too: the British writer J. A. Hobson argued in 1900 that the war served the interests of mining capitalists, and some newspapers attacked the Randlords' wealth and influence. A balanced historical judgement acknowledges their role in building a modern industry, while recognising that the costs were borne mainly by black workers and their families, and that their decisions served the interests of shareholders above all. In an essay, you can show this balance by giving evidence for both sides and then reaching your own reasoned conclusion.

### subtopic_3 — The Chamber of Mines
**Assistant:** Why did so many workers come from Mozambique?

**Tutor:** Because of a combination of conditions in southern Mozambique, long-standing patterns of migration, and agreements between the mining industry and the Portuguese colonial government. Southern Mozambique had a history of migration to South Africa that began before gold was found: men from the area had worked on the Natal sugar plantations and the Kimberley diamond fields in the 1870s. When the Rand mines opened, they continued this pattern. Conditions at home pushed them. Southern Mozambique had poor soils and frequent droughts, and the Portuguese colonial authorities imposed taxes that had to be paid in cash, as well as forced labour on public works. Wages on the Rand were higher than anything available locally. The mining industry wanted these workers because South African sources did not supply enough labour at the low wages the mines offered, and because workers from Mozambique tended to sign longer contracts and return repeatedly, gaining experience. Recruiters from WNLA operated in Mozambique under agreements with the Portuguese government, which benefited by receiving fees for each worker recruited and by arrangements that sent a share of Transvaal railway traffic through the port of Lourenço Marques, today Maputo. In the early twentieth century, workers from Mozambique made up a large share of the black workforce on the gold mines. This shows that the Witwatersrand drew labour from the whole of southern Africa, and that the mining economy connected South Africa with its neighbours, a connection that continues in migration between the countries today.

### subtopic_4 — Profits, Politics and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What was the Jameson Raid and why did it fail?

**Tutor:** The Jameson Raid was an armed invasion of the South African Republic launched at the end of December 1895 by about five hundred mounted men of the British South Africa Company's police, led by Dr Leander Starr Jameson, a close friend of Cecil John Rhodes. The plan was that a group of leading Johannesburg residents, called the Reform Committee and including several Randlords, would start an uprising of the uitlanders, the foreign immigrants who were denied the vote, at the same moment that Jameson's force rode in from the border to support them. Together they hoped to overthrow Kruger's government and replace it with one friendly to the mining industry and to Britain. Rhodes, who was Prime Minister of the Cape Colony, supported the plan secretly, and there is evidence that the British Colonial Secretary, Joseph Chamberlain, knew something of it. The raid failed for several reasons. The uprising in Johannesburg did not happen, because the conspirators disagreed among themselves, especially about whether the new government would be under the British flag. Jameson rode in anyway, against advice. The Boer forces were warned, tracked the raiders, and forced them to surrender at Doornkop, near Krugersdorp, on 2 January 1896. The consequences were serious. Rhodes was forced to resign as Prime Minister. The Reform Committee leaders were tried and sentenced, though later released on payment of large fines. Jameson was sent to Britain for trial and briefly imprisoned. Kruger's position was strengthened, and distrust between the Boer republics and Britain deepened. Historians see the raid as an important step towards the South African War of 1899 to 1902, which you will study in the next lesson on the turning point.
