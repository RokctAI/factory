Imagine a village in the Eastern Cape, Lesotho or southern Mozambique in about 1910. Most of the young and middle-aged men are away, working on the gold mines of the Witwatersrand, hundreds of kilometres from home. They will be gone for nine months, a year, sometimes longer. At home, women plough the fields, herd the cattle, build and repair the houses and raise the children, while grandparents help where they can. Money arrives from the mines, sometimes. So does illness. Today we study how the migrant labour system on the gold mines affected families: the absence of men, the extra work of women, the money sent home, the spread of disease, and the long-term effects on family life in South Africa and its neighbours.
