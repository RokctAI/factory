### subtopic_1 — How the Migrant System Worked
**Assistant:** Why didn't the mineworkers just bring their families to Johannesburg?

**Tutor:** Because the law, the mining companies and their own circumstances all worked against it. The mining companies housed black workers in single-sex compounds and did not provide family housing, except for a very small number of long-serving workers. Building family houses, schools and clinics would have cost money, and the whole point of the migrant system, from the companies' point of view, was that families stayed in the rural areas and supported themselves. The state backed this with pass laws, which controlled where black people could live and work, and later with laws such as the Natives Urban Areas Act of 1923, which treated black people in towns as temporary residents who were there only to work. Black families who did move to the towns often had to live in crowded, unhealthy locations or informal settlements, and they could be removed. There were also reasons on the side of the workers themselves. Many wanted to keep their land and cattle in the rural areas, because land was security, a place to retire and a link to their community and ancestors. Life in the towns was expensive and dangerous, and wages were too low to support a family there. So many workers saw migrancy as a way to earn cash while keeping their rural home, at least in the early years. Over time, however, as rural land became crowded and less productive, more families did try to move to the towns, and the struggle over the right of black families to live together in cities became a central issue in South African history, especially under apartheid.

### subtopic_2 — Women, Children and the Rural Household
**Assistant:** Did women ever go to the mines or towns themselves?

**Tutor:** Women did not work underground on the mines in this period, but many women did migrate to the towns, especially from the early twentieth century, and their experience is an important part of the story. Some women went to find husbands who had stopped sending money, or to escape poverty, difficult marriages or the control of elders in rural areas. Others went to earn money for their families. In the towns, women found work as domestic workers in white households, as washerwomen, and in small businesses such as selling food or brewing beer, which was often illegal and led to police raids. Women in the towns faced great difficulties. They had few legal rights to live there, and the authorities often tried to send them back to the rural areas. When the government tried to extend passes to black women in the Free State in 1913, women resisted with protests, and the campaign was so strong that the plan was dropped for many years. Much later, in 1956, about twenty thousand women marched to the Union Buildings in Pretoria to protest against passes for women, an event remembered today on National Women's Day, 9 August. The experience of women in the migrant labour system was therefore twofold. Many stayed at home and carried the burden of rural life, and many others migrated and built new lives in the towns under difficult conditions. Both groups showed great determination. Historians have increasingly studied women's experiences, using oral histories and songs, because earlier histories often focused only on men and the mines.

### subtopic_3 — Health, Money and Long-Term Effects
**Assistant:** How did families stay in touch when the men were away for so long?

**Tutor:** In several ways, though none of them was easy or quick. Letters were one way. Some workers could read and write, often from mission schools, and others asked friends, clerks or letter writers to write for them. At home, letters were read aloud by a literate family member, teacher or minister. Because letters passed through other hands, people sometimes could not say everything they wanted to. Another way was through other workers. Men from the same village or district often worked on the same mine and lived in the same compound rooms, because they had travelled together and were recruited together. When one man went home, he carried news, messages, money and gifts for the families of the others, and brought back news from home. Home-area networks were very important in compound life and in keeping links with home. Money could be sent through recruiting agencies, post offices or trusted friends, though there was always a risk of loss or theft. Workers also returned home at the end of their contracts, and these returns were important family events, sometimes celebrated with feasts. Over time, migrant workers created their own culture, including songs, dances, styles of dress and music that combined rural and urban influences. Some of this culture, such as the songs of Basotho migrant workers called lifela, describes the journey between home and the mines and the feelings of those who made it. Historians use these songs as sources to understand how migrants saw their lives.

### subtopic_4 — Sources, Calculations and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Is migrant labour still a part of South African life today?

**Tutor:** Yes, in different forms. Many mineworkers still come from rural areas of South Africa and from neighbouring countries such as Lesotho and Mozambique, though the numbers from outside South Africa have fallen since the 1990s. Many mines have converted single-sex hostels into family housing or paid living-out allowances so that workers can live with their families, but migration between rural homes and mining areas continues. The legacy of the system can be seen in the platinum mining areas of North West and Limpopo, where the Marikana tragedy of 2012, in which thirty-four striking mineworkers were killed by police, drew attention to the poor living conditions of migrant workers in informal settlements around the mines. More broadly, many South African families still have members working far from home, in cities, mines and farms, and sending money back to relatives in rural areas. Grandmothers often raise grandchildren while parents work elsewhere. Circular migration, in which people move between rural homes and urban work, remains common. Migration from other African countries to South Africa for work also continues. The pass laws and influx control of the apartheid era were abolished in 1986, and people are now free to live where they choose, but economic pressures and the shape of the economy still lead many to migrate. Understanding the history of migrant labour helps explain why. It also helps us understand debates about wages, housing for workers, and the responsibilities of employers to the families of their workers, which are still important issues in South Africa.
