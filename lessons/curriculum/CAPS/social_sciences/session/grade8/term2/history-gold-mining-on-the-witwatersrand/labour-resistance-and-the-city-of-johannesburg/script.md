# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines two linked developments on the Witwatersrand from 1886 to about 1914: the forms of resistance used by workers against the conditions of mining labour, and the growth of Johannesburg as a divided industrial city. The anchor is the contrast between the power of the mining industry and the many ways, large and small, in which workers pushed back. The governing idea is that black, Chinese, Indian and white workers all resisted, using methods suited to the power they had, and that the city's form, with its mines, compounds, suburbs, slums and locations, both reflected and shaped these struggles. Identifying forms of resistance and describing the growth and character of early Johannesburg are examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Everyday Resistance by Black Workers

Black mineworkers had very little formal power. They could not vote, were controlled by pass laws and contracts, lived in compounds under the watch of compound managers and police, and were not allowed recognised trade unions. Open strikes were risky: strikers could be arrested, fined, imprisoned or sent home without pay. So most resistance in the early years took less visible forms, which historians call everyday resistance.

The most common form was desertion: leaving the mine before the end of the contract. Despite pass laws, thousands of workers deserted every year, some to return home, others to work on another mine or in the town where pay or conditions were better. Desertion was a way of voting with one's feet, and it was one reason why the mine owners demanded stricter pass laws, such as the pass law of 1895.

Workers also chose between mines. Information about which mines were dangerous, had cruel compound managers, or paid poorly spread through home-area networks and returning workers. Mines with bad reputations struggled to recruit. This was a form of collective bargaining without a union.

Other forms included working slowly, known as go-slows; refusing to go underground after accidents; complaining to recruiting officials or to chiefs at home; sabotage of equipment; and, in the compounds, protests over food, ill-treatment or the loss of wages through fines. Workers sometimes refused to accept new rules, such as changes to how drilling targets were measured. Many of these actions were small and short, but they were constant, and the mine owners spent a great deal of effort trying to prevent them.

Workers also built their own communities and cultures inside the compounds and towns: burial societies, home-area associations, churches, dance teams and music. These gave workers mutual support and dignity in a system designed to treat them only as labour, and some later became the basis of political organisation.

The mine owners and the state tried to close these gaps. In the 1890s and early 1900s, the pass department of the Transvaal kept registers of workers, and from the early twentieth century fingerprints were taken so that deserters who gave false names could be identified. Compound managers used fines, the withholding of wages and, in many cases, physical punishment, even though beating was officially illegal. Compound police, often recruited from among the workers themselves, watched for trouble. Each new control was a response to resistance, and each was met by new ways of avoiding it.

Historians have found evidence of this resistance in the records of the authorities themselves. Reports of the pass department list thousands of desertions each year. Court records show prosecutions for breaking contracts. Compound managers' reports complain about go-slows and absenteeism. Because workers left few written records, these official sources, read carefully, are an important way to recover the actions of people who were otherwise silenced in the archives.

## Subtopic: Strikes and Organised Resistance

As the industry grew, organised strikes became more common. White workers, who had unions and the vote, used strikes most openly. In 1907, white miners struck against changes that would have reduced the number of white jobs; the strike was broken. In 1913, a strike that began at the New Kleinfontein mine spread across the Rand. It became violent, and in July 1913, soldiers fired on a crowd in Johannesburg, killing about twenty people. The government was forced to negotiate. In January 1914, a general strike of white workers led to the declaration of martial law and the deportation of nine union leaders to Britain.

Black workers also struck, often learning from these events. During the white strike of 1913, about nine thousand black workers on several mines went on strike, demanding higher wages and better conditions. The strike was put down by armed police and soldiers, and the leaders were punished. Further black strikes followed, including the strike of 1920, when about seventy thousand black mineworkers stopped work, the largest strike of its time, and the great African mineworkers' strike of 1946.

Chinese workers, brought to the Rand between 1904 and 1910, also resisted. They protested against harsh treatment, refused to work, struck over wages and fines, and some deserted the mines. A number of riots and attacks on supervisors took place, and the authorities responded with harsh punishments. Their resistance added to the controversy about the Chinese labour scheme.

Indian workers in Natal, including those on the coal mines, joined the great strike of 1913 led by Gandhi, which you will study in the lesson on the satyagraha campaign. This shows that resistance in the mining economy crossed the boundaries of race and region, even if the different groups rarely acted together.

The strikes of this period also show how the state's response depended on who was striking. White strikers were met with soldiers in 1913 and martial law in 1914, but the government also negotiated with them and gradually extended protections for white workers. Black strikers were met with force and the prosecution of leaders, and their demands were rarely discussed. Comparing these responses is a useful way to understand how race shaped power in early twentieth-century South Africa.

The questions for this section are with you now: white workers' strikes of 1907, 1913 and 1914, the black miners' strike of 1913, and resistance by Chinese and Indian workers.

## Subtopic: The Growth of Johannesburg

Johannesburg began in 1886 as a mining camp on a triangle of government land called Randjeslaagte. The government of the South African Republic laid out stands, and within months there were shops, hotels, bars, banks and a stock exchange. The town grew at an astonishing speed. A census in 1896 counted about one hundred and two thousand people. By 1911, after the war, the population was about two hundred and thirty-seven thousand.

The population was extremely mixed. There were black workers from all over southern Africa; Afrikaners who had moved from the countryside; immigrants from Britain, Ireland, Europe, America and Australia; Jewish immigrants from Eastern Europe; Indian and Chinese traders; and coloured and Cape Malay artisans and workers. Men greatly outnumbered women in the early years, because so many came as workers without their families.

The city soon divided by wealth and by race. The Randlords and wealthy residents built grand houses on the ridges to the north, in Parktown, away from the dust and noise of the mines. White working-class families lived in suburbs closer to the mines, such as Fordsburg and Jeppestown. Many poor people of all backgrounds lived in crowded areas near the mines and the town centre, such as Brickfields and Vrededorp, where poor Afrikaners, black, coloured and Indian residents lived side by side.

The authorities increasingly tried to separate people by race. Locations were set aside for black, Indian and coloured residents. In 1904, after an outbreak of bubonic plague in the crowded area then called the Coolie Location, the town council burned the area and moved many of its black residents to Klipspruit, about twenty kilometres from the centre, which later became part of Soweto. Sophiatown, established in 1903 as a freehold suburb, became one of the few places where black people could own property in Johannesburg, until it was destroyed by forced removals in the 1950s.

The physical landscape of the city was shaped by gold. The mines ran in a line from east to west along the reef, and the town grew to the north of them. Huge yellow-white mine dumps of crushed rock rose beside the headgear. Railways linked the mines to the coal fields, the ports and the towns. Water was a constant problem in the early years, because Johannesburg is not on a large river, and supplies had to be brought from boreholes, dams and later from the Vaal River. Disease spread easily in crowded areas without proper sanitation, which the authorities later used as a reason for removing poor residents.

The questions for this section are with you now: the growth of Johannesburg, its mixed population, and its division by wealth and race.

## Subtopic: City Life, Calculations and the Error Museum

City life offered opportunities as well as hardship. People found work not only on the mines but also as domestic workers, washermen, cart drivers, traders, builders and in many small businesses. A group of Zulu-speaking washermen, called the amawasha, did the laundry of much of the town along the streams. Women sold food and brewed beer, often illegally. Churches, schools and newspapers grew. New forms of music and culture developed, mixing rural traditions with urban influences, which later produced styles such as marabi. Johannesburg became a centre of political organisation, where many of the people who later formed African, Indian and labour organisations lived and worked.

A calculation shows the speed of growth. If the population grew from about one hundred and two thousand in 1896 to about two hundred and thirty-seven thousand in 1911, the increase was two hundred and thirty-seven thousand minus one hundred and two thousand, which is one hundred and thirty-five thousand people in fifteen years. That is an average of nine thousand more people every year, because one hundred and thirty-five thousand divided by fifteen is nine thousand. The real growth was uneven, because the war of 1899 to 1902 emptied much of the town for a time.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying black workers did not resist because there were few strikes. Everyday resistance, such as desertion and choosing mines, was constant. Two: saying Johannesburg was planned as a great city. It grew from a mining camp. Three: confusing the white miners' strikes of 1913 and 1914 with the black miners' strike of 1913, which took place at the same time but was separate. Four: saying the population was only white and black. It was extremely mixed. Five: forgetting that segregation in Johannesburg began before apartheid.

Johannesburg's story also connects with Geography. The push and pull factors that drew people to the city in the 1890s, such as land loss, taxes and drought on one side and wages and opportunity on the other, are the same factors geographers use to explain urbanisation today. The divisions of the early city into rich suburbs, working-class areas and segregated locations are the roots of the land use zones and residential patterns you will study in Term 3.

## Subtopic: Quiet Ways of Fighting Back

Black mineworkers could not vote or form unions, and strikes were very dangerous. So they found quieter ways to resist. They ran away from mines before their contracts ended. They told friends at home which mines were bad, so fewer people went there. They worked slowly, refused to go underground after accidents, and complained to officials and chiefs.

They also built their own support: burial societies, churches, music and dance groups.

The owners fought back with fingerprints, registers and fines, and the workers found new ways around them. It was a long, quiet tug of war.

## Subtopic: Strikes

White workers had unions and votes, so they went on strike openly, in 1907, 1913 and 1914. In 1913, soldiers fired on a crowd in Johannesburg, and about twenty people died.

In 1913, about nine thousand black miners also went on strike, but police and soldiers forced them back to work. In 1920, about seventy thousand black miners struck. Chinese workers protested and refused to work too, and Indian workers joined Gandhi's great strike in 1913.

Notice how the government treated the groups differently. It talked with the white strikers, who had votes, but used force against the black strikers.

## Subtopic: The City of Gold

Johannesburg started as a camp of tents in 1886. By 1896 it had about one hundred and two thousand people, and by 1911 about two hundred and thirty-seven thousand. People came from all over the world.

The rich lived on the ridges in Parktown. Workers lived near the mines. Poor people of all races lived in crowded areas. The council began to separate people by race, moving black residents to places like Klipspruit, which later became part of Soweto.

Many people kept coming to the city because it offered wages and new chances, even though life there was hard, and because life in many rural areas was getting harder. Women sold food and brewed beer, washermen did the town's laundry, and new kinds of music grew in the crowded streets.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Black workers, with little power, resisted mainly through everyday actions: desertion, choosing mines, go-slows and complaints, while building their own communities. White workers, with unions and votes, struck in 1907, 1913 and 1914; black miners struck in 1913 and 1920; Chinese and Indian workers resisted too. Johannesburg grew from a mining camp into a large, mixed city divided by wealth and race, where segregation began long before apartheid.

Today you can still see the old mine dumps along the line of the reef in Johannesburg, reminders of how the city began.

The city was both a place of hardship and a place where people built new lives.
