# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the beginning of deep-level gold mining on the Witwatersrand from 1886, the rise of the Randlords who controlled it, and the formation of the Chamber of Mines in 1887. The anchor is the geology of the Witwatersrand: a vast amount of gold, spread thinly through hard rock that slopes deep underground. The governing idea is that this geology, together with a fixed world price for gold, made the industry depend on huge investments of capital and on large supplies of cheap labour, and that the mine owners organised themselves through the Chamber of Mines to secure both. Explaining who the Randlords were, why they formed the Chamber and how it shaped labour and politics is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Gold on the Witwatersrand

Gold had been found in small amounts in the Transvaal before, at places such as Pilgrim's Rest and Barberton in the eastern Transvaal. But in 1886, prospectors found the outcrop of a gold-bearing reef on the farm Langlaagte on the Witwatersrand, the ridge of white waters, about fifty kilometres south of Pretoria. The South African Republic, under President Paul Kruger, proclaimed public diggings in September 1886, and a mining camp grew rapidly. It was named Johannesburg. By 1896 a census counted more than one hundred thousand people in the town.

The Witwatersrand gold was unlike the gold of earlier rushes in California and Australia, where individual diggers could find gold in riverbeds by washing gravel. On the Rand, the gold was contained in very fine particles in a hard rock called conglomerate, made of pebbles cemented together, which the Dutch-speaking diggers called banket because it looked like a kind of almond pastry. The reef came to the surface in a long line, the outcrop, and then sloped steeply down into the earth, continuing for many kilometres.

Three features of this gold shaped the whole industry. First, it was low-grade: each tonne of rock contained only a small amount of gold, so huge amounts of rock had to be mined and crushed. Second, it was deep: after the outcrop mines had been worked, the reef had to be followed hundreds and then thousands of metres underground, in deep-level mines. Third, it was hard to extract. Below a certain depth, the gold was locked in a mineral called pyrite, and the usual method of trapping gold with mercury recovered only part of it. In 1890 the MacArthur-Forrest cyanide process was introduced on the Rand, which used a cyanide solution to dissolve the gold from the crushed rock, greatly increasing the amount that could be recovered.

All of this needed machinery: stamp batteries to crush the rock, cyanide plants, winding engines, pumps and compressors. Individual diggers could not afford it. Gold mining on the Witwatersrand was an industry for companies from the beginning.

Johannesburg grew in a disorderly way. Within months, the dusty camp of tents and wagons became a town of brick and iron buildings, stock exchanges, banks, hotels and bars. The population was extremely mixed: black workers from across southern Africa, Afrikaner families who had lost their farms, immigrants from Britain, Europe, America and Australia, Indian and Chinese traders, and Cape Malay and coloured artisans. Water, sanitation and housing could not keep up, and disease was common. The town was a mining camp first, and a city only later.

The discovery also changed the balance of power in southern Africa. Before 1886, the South African Republic was a poor, mostly farming state that had been bankrupt in 1877. Within a few years, taxes and concessions from the gold industry made it the richest state in the region, richer than the Cape Colony. Britain, which had ruled the Transvaal from 1877 until the Boers regained independence in 1881, now found that the most valuable part of southern Africa was outside its control.

The questions for this section are with you now: the discovery of gold in 1886, the nature of the Witwatersrand reef, and why mining needed companies and capital.

## Subtopic: The Randlords

The term Randlords was used for the small group of very wealthy men who controlled the gold mines through mining houses, which were groups of companies under common management. Many of them came from the diamond fields, where they had learned how to raise capital, organise companies and control labour.

Cecil John Rhodes and his partner Charles Rudd formed Gold Fields of South Africa in 1887, later called Consolidated Gold Fields. Alfred Beit and Julius Wernher, who had made fortunes in Kimberley diamonds, controlled the largest group of mines through the firm of Hermann Eckstein, known as the Corner House, and its London partner Wernher, Beit and Company; Lionel Phillips was a leading partner. Barney Barnato invested in Johannesburg property and mines. Joseph Robinson, another Kimberley magnate, bought land on the western Rand early and became very rich. George Farrar, Abe Bailey and others were also prominent. The mining houses raised money from investors in London, Paris and Berlin by selling shares, and they directed it into sinking shafts and building plants.

The Randlords were powerful in several ways. Economically, they controlled the largest industry in southern Africa, which by the late 1890s was producing more than a quarter of the world's gold. Socially, they built grand houses on Parktown ridge in Johannesburg and in London. Politically, they had close links with the British government and with the press, and several held public office.

The Randlords were not all the same. Some, such as Robinson, got on relatively well with the Kruger government; others, such as Rhodes and the Corner House partners, became its fierce opponents. Historians also note that they were businessmen focused on profits for shareholders, so their decisions about wages, safety and politics were shaped above all by the need to keep costs down in an industry with low-grade ore.

Not all the investors were wealthy. In the share booms of 1889 and 1895 on the London and Johannesburg stock exchanges, ordinary people bought shares in Rand gold companies hoping to get rich quickly. Many companies had little gold and collapsed when the booms ended, and many small investors lost their money. The big mining houses survived these crashes and bought up weaker companies, so control became even more concentrated.

## Subtopic: The Chamber of Mines

In 1887 the mine owners formed the Witwatersrand Chamber of Mines, reorganised in 1889. It was an organisation of the mining companies, through which they could act together on matters that affected the whole industry.

The Chamber's most important tasks were about costs, because the price of gold was fixed internationally by the gold standard. A gold mine could not sell its gold for more when its costs rose. If wages, dynamite, coal or railway charges went up, profits fell, and some low-grade mines might close. So the mine owners tried in every way to cut costs.

The largest cost was labour. The Chamber tried to stop the mines competing with one another for black workers, because competition pushed wages up. In 1890 and again in 1896, it agreed on maximum wage levels for black workers, and in 1896 it imposed a substantial cut in black wages. It set up recruiting organisations to bring workers to the mines: the Rand Native Labour Association in 1896, and after the South African War, the Witwatersrand Native Labour Association, known as WNLA or Wenela, from 1900 to 1901. These organisations recruited workers across southern Africa, and especially from southern Mozambique, which supplied a large share of the workforce, under an agreement with the Portuguese colonial government.

The Chamber also lobbied the government. It complained about the Kruger government's dynamite concession, which gave one company a monopoly over the supply of explosives and made dynamite expensive, about high railway rates, about the sale of liquor to black workers, which the owners said reduced productivity, and about the failure to enforce pass laws strictly enough to stop workers deserting. A stricter pass law for the mining districts was introduced in 1895 at the Chamber's request.

The Chamber continued as the voice of the mining industry throughout the twentieth century; today the industry is represented by the Minerals Council South Africa.

The Chamber's wage agreements did not always hold. Individual mines that were short of workers sometimes broke ranks and paid more to attract them, and workers chose mines with better reputations. Over time, however, the recruiting monopoly of WNLA made it much harder for workers to bargain, because there was effectively only one buyer of their labour. Historians call this a monopsony: one buyer facing many sellers.

The questions for this section are with you now: why the Chamber of Mines was formed, how it tried to control labour costs, and its lobbying of the government.

## Subtopic: Profits, Politics and the Error Museum

A simple calculation shows why low-grade ore made costs so important. Suppose a mine crushes ten thousand tonnes of rock a month and recovers ten grams of gold from each tonne. It produces ten thousand times ten, which is one hundred thousand grams, or one hundred kilograms of gold. If the gold is worth three pounds a gram and costs are two pounds fifty for each gram produced, the profit is fifty pence a gram, or fifty thousand pounds a month. If costs rise by just fifty pence a gram, the profit disappears entirely. These are example figures for the method, but they show why the Chamber fought so hard to keep wages and other costs down.

The Randlords' conflict with the Kruger government became part of the road to war. Many mine owners believed that the republic's taxes, monopolies and inefficiency raised their costs, and they supported the demands of the uitlanders, the foreign, mostly British, immigrants who were denied the vote. At the end of 1895, Rhodes, then Prime Minister of the Cape, backed an armed invasion of the Transvaal led by Leander Starr Jameson, which was supposed to coincide with an uprising in Johannesburg organised by a Reform Committee that included leading Randlords such as Lionel Phillips. The Jameson Raid failed in January 1896. Rhodes had to resign, and the Reform Committee leaders were tried and fined. Tension between Britain and the republic grew, and in 1899 the South African War broke out.

Notice the link between this lesson and the last. On the diamond fields, the mine owners had learned to use passes, compounds, contracts and recruitment to control labour. On the Rand, they used the same tools on a far larger scale. The compounds of the gold mines were mostly open, because there were no diamonds to steal, but the migrant labour system, the pass laws and the cooperation between mine owners to hold wages down were carried over directly from Kimberley.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying individual diggers made fortunes from Rand gold like in Kimberley. From the start, the Rand needed companies and capital. Two: forgetting that the price of gold was fixed. That is why costs mattered so much. Three: calling the Chamber of Mines a trade union. It was an employers' organisation. Four: saying the gold was found in rivers. It was in conglomerate rock sloping deep underground. Five: assuming all Randlords opposed Kruger. Some did, some did not.

Layout for marks in a paragraph question: explain the nature of the gold, the need for capital, the role of the Randlords, and how the Chamber of Mines controlled labour costs and lobbied government.

## Subtopic: Gold in Hard Rock

In 1886 gold was found on the Witwatersrand, near where Johannesburg is today. But it was not lumps of gold in a river. It was tiny bits of gold in hard rock, and the rock went deep underground. To get the gold, you had to dig the rock, crush it to powder and use chemicals to pull the gold out.

That needed big machines and lots of money, so only companies could do it.

Johannesburg grew from a camp of tents in 1886 to a town of more than one hundred thousand people ten years later, with people from all over southern Africa and the world.

## Subtopic: The Rich Men of the Rand

The Randlords were a small group of very rich men who owned the gold mines: men like Cecil John Rhodes, Alfred Beit, Julius Wernher, Barney Barnato and Joseph Robinson. Many had become rich from diamonds in Kimberley. They raised money from investors in London and Europe to build the mines.

They became very powerful, with big houses, newspapers and friends in government.

Not everyone who bought shares in gold companies became rich. In the share booms, many people bought shares in companies that had little gold, and lost their money when the booms ended. The big mining houses survived and grew bigger.

## Subtopic: The Owners' Club

In 1887 the mine owners formed the Chamber of Mines. It was like a club for owners. Because they could not raise the price of gold, they worked together to keep costs low. They agreed to pay black workers low wages, and they set up groups to recruit workers from all over southern Africa, especially Mozambique. They also pushed the government for cheaper dynamite and stricter pass laws.

Some Randlords wanted to get rid of Kruger's government, and in 1895 Rhodes backed the Jameson Raid, which failed.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Gold was found on the Witwatersrand in 1886. It was low-grade and deep, so mining needed big companies and lots of money. The Randlords, many from Kimberley, controlled the mining houses. In 1887 they formed the Chamber of Mines to keep costs down, especially black wages, to recruit workers and to lobby government. Their conflict with Kruger's republic helped lead to the Jameson Raid and the South African War.

The Chamber still exists in a new form today, as the Minerals Council South Africa, which speaks for mining companies.

Cheap labour was the key to their profits.
