# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the impact of the migrant labour system on families, from the opening of the Witwatersrand gold mines in 1886 into the twentieth century. The anchor is the split household: one part, mostly men, working on the mines and living in compounds, and the other part, mostly women, children and older people, living in rural areas and depending on both farming and wages. The governing idea is that migrant labour was built on this split, because it allowed the mines to pay wages for a single man while the rural household fed and cared for his family, and that this placed heavy burdens on women and children and changed family life across southern Africa for generations. Explaining the effects of migrant labour on families, using sources such as oral testimony, songs and official reports, is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: How the Migrant System Worked

By 1910, the Witwatersrand gold mines employed about two hundred thousand black workers. Almost all of them were migrants: men recruited on contracts of several months to a year or more, who lived in single-sex compounds at the mines and returned home at the end of the contract. Many returned to the mines again and again throughout their working lives.

The workers came from a huge area: the Eastern Cape, Natal and Zululand, Lesotho, Botswana, Eswatini, the northern Transvaal, and above all southern Mozambique, which for many years supplied the largest single share. Recruiters of the Witwatersrand Native Labour Association, known as Wenela, operated across this region, often with the help of chiefs and colonial officials, and sometimes advanced money or cattle to recruits, which tied them to a contract before they left home.

Several forces kept the system going. The loss of land after the conquests of the nineteenth century, which you studied in the lesson on the defeat of the African kingdoms, left many families with too little land to live from farming alone. Colonial taxes, such as the hut tax and poll tax, had to be paid in cash. Population growth in crowded reserves made land shortage worse. Then the Natives Land Act of 1913 limited African land ownership to the reserves, at first about seven percent of the country's land, so there was no escape from wage labour for most families.

From the mining companies' point of view, the migrant system had several advantages. They did not have to pay for family housing, schools or pensions in the towns. They could pay wages calculated for a single man rather than a family, because the rural household was expected to grow food and support the worker's dependants. When workers became ill, injured or old, they returned home, and the cost of caring for them fell on their families. The state supported the system through pass laws, which prevented black families from settling permanently in the towns.

Historians describe this as a system in which the rural areas subsidised the mines. The unpaid work of women and the land of the reserves paid part of the real cost of the mining workforce, allowing the mines to keep wages low.

The journey itself became part of the system. Early migrants walked for weeks; later, recruits travelled by rail from depots run by the recruiting organisation, with their contracts, passes and medical examinations organised along the way. Recruiting depots in places such as Ressano Garcia on the Mozambique border, Maseru, and towns in the Transkei processed thousands of men each year. Before they went underground, new recruits were examined, given numbers and allocated to mines. For many young men, the first contract was a rite of passage, a sign of becoming an adult who could contribute to the family and later marry.

## Subtopic: Women, Children and the Rural Household

With so many men away, the burden of rural life fell more heavily on women. Women had always done much of the agricultural work in many southern African societies, but now they also took on tasks that men had usually done, such as ploughing with oxen, herding cattle, building and repairing houses, and dealing with chiefs and officials. They raised children largely alone, cared for the sick and the elderly, and fetched water and firewood, often over long distances.

Women also managed the household economy. They decided how to use the money that arrived from the mines, when it arrived, and they often had to make difficult choices between food, school fees, taxes and lobola. Some women brewed beer for sale, grew vegetables for market or did other work to earn cash. Many women showed great resilience and resourcefulness, holding families and communities together.

Children grew up with fathers who were absent for most of the year. Boys often herded cattle from a young age and might leave for the mines themselves as teenagers. Girls took on household work early. Older relatives, especially grandmothers, played a central role in raising children, a pattern that is still common in South Africa today.

Relationships between husbands and wives came under strain. Long separations made communication difficult; letters were often written and read by others, and news could take weeks to arrive. Some men formed new relationships in the towns and stopped sending money home. Some women, facing poverty and loneliness, also left their villages. Historians and anthropologists in the twentieth century recorded the pain of these separations, as well as the efforts many families made to stay connected.

As the reserves became more crowded and less productive, more and more households came to depend mainly on the money sent home, called remittances. When remittances failed to arrive, because a man was ill, injured, unemployed or had abandoned his family, the household could fall into severe poverty.

Older women and chiefs also tried to manage the system. In some areas, chiefs negotiated with recruiters and received fees for the men who went, and families decided together which son would go and when, so that the fields would not be left without labour at ploughing time. This shows that rural communities were not simply victims; they made choices within a system that gave them few good options.

The questions for this section are with you now: the burden on women, the experience of children, and the strain on relationships.

## Subtopic: Health, Money and Long-Term Effects

Migrant labour carried disease from the mines to the rural areas. Tuberculosis, which spread easily in crowded compounds and was made worse by silicosis from rock dust, was the most serious. Mineworkers who became sick were often sent home, where they infected family members and neighbours. Doctors in the early twentieth century observed that tuberculosis was spreading through rural areas of the Eastern Cape, Lesotho and Mozambique that sent large numbers of men to the mines. Later in the twentieth century, other diseases also spread along the routes of migration.

Injury and death on the mines also struck families directly. When a man was killed underground, his family lost its main source of cash. Compensation for black workers, where it existed, was small. A widow might depend on relatives or try to find work herself.

Money from the mines was important to rural families. It paid taxes, bought food in years of drought, paid lobola, bought ploughs, cattle and school fees, and sometimes allowed a family to build a better house. Some workers saved carefully and used their earnings to improve their homesteads. For workers from Mozambique, an arrangement from 1928 paid part of their wages only when they returned home, which ensured that money reached their home areas but also benefited the Portuguese colonial authorities.

Over generations, migrant labour changed the structure of families and communities. Many households came to be headed by women. Hostels in the towns became home to men separated from their families for most of their lives. The system was later extended to other industries and became a central part of apartheid, which used pass laws and influx control to stop black families from living together in the towns. Many South Africans today grew up in families shaped by migrant labour, and migration between rural areas and cities remains a feature of life.

The effect on the rural economy was also deep. With fewer men to plough and fewer resources to invest, harvests in many reserves fell over the twentieth century, and families bought more of their food with wages. Government reports from as early as the 1930s described soil erosion, overgrazing and declining harvests in the reserves. The reserves increasingly became places where people lived and raised children but could not make a living, which deepened their dependence on migrant labour. Historians describe this as a downward spiral, in which migration weakened rural farming and weak farming increased migration.

The questions for this section are with you now: the spread of disease, the role of remittances, and the long-term effects on family structure.

## Subtopic: Sources, Calculations and the Error Museum

Historians study the impact of migrant labour using many sources. Oral histories record the memories of former miners and their wives and children. Songs composed by women in Lesotho and Mozambique describe the absence of husbands and sons, the waiting, and the fear of bad news from the mines. Letters, where they survive, show how families tried to stay in touch. Government reports and the records of doctors and missionaries describe conditions in rural areas. Novels and life stories, such as Peter Abrahams's novel Mine Boy of 1946, give a sense of how the system felt to those who lived through it.

A calculation shows how a remittance might be divided. Suppose a worker sends home six pounds after a contract. If one pound ten shillings goes to the hut tax, that leaves four pounds ten shillings. If half of what is left buys maize meal after a drought, two pounds five shillings is left for everything else, such as seed, clothing and school fees. Remember that one pound is twenty shillings, so one pound ten shillings is thirty shillings, and six pounds is one hundred and twenty shillings; one hundred and twenty minus thirty is ninety shillings, and half of ninety is forty-five shillings, which is two pounds five shillings. These are example figures for the method, but they show how quickly a remittance could be used up.

When you read a source about migrant families, ask about its perspective. A report by a mining company official might describe the system as good for rural areas because it brought cash. A song by a woman in Lesotho might describe the loneliness of waiting. A doctor's report might focus on disease. Each is true from its own point of view, and a strong answer uses more than one.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying migrant workers brought their families to live at the mines. Most could not; the compounds were single-sex, and pass laws restricted family settlement. Two: saying women in the rural areas did nothing while men worked. Women carried a very heavy burden of work. Three: saying the system affected only South Africa. It affected Lesotho, Mozambique, Eswatini, Botswana and Malawi too. Four: forgetting that remittances were important; many families depended on them. Five: describing only suffering and forgetting resilience. Families and communities also showed great strength.

Layout for marks in an empathy or paragraph question: describe how the system worked, explain the effects on women, children and men, give evidence from a source, and explain the long-term consequences.

## Subtopic: Two Halves of One Family

Under the migrant labour system, a family was split into two halves. The men worked on the mines for months or a year, living in compounds with other men. The women, children and old people stayed at home in the rural areas, farming and looking after one another.

The mines liked this because they could pay a wage for one man, not a whole family. The family at home was expected to grow its own food. When a worker got sick or old, he went home, and his family looked after him.

People were pushed into this system because they had lost land, had to pay taxes in cash, and after 1913 could own land only in small reserves.

For many young men, going to the mines for the first time was a sign of becoming an adult.

## Subtopic: Life at Home

At home, women did almost everything: farming, herding, building, raising children, caring for the sick and old, and dealing with officials. Grandmothers helped raise the children. Children often grew up hardly knowing their fathers. Boys herded cattle and later went to the mines themselves.

Families waited for money and letters from the mines. Sometimes the money did not come, and the family went hungry.

Think of a girl of twelve in such a village. Before school she fetches water, after school she helps in the fields and cooks for her younger brothers and sisters, while her mother ploughs and her grandmother watches the baby. Her father has been away since the last harvest.

## Subtopic: What the Mines Sent Home

The mines sent home money, which paid for taxes, food, lobola, ploughs and school fees. But they also sent home sickness. Workers sick with tuberculosis from the crowded compounds and dusty mines went home and infected their families. Some men were injured or killed, and their families lost their income.

Over time, many households were headed by women, and the system became part of apartheid.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Migrant labour split families: men worked on the mines in single-sex compounds, while women, children and old people stayed in the rural areas. The mines paid low wages because rural families were expected to support themselves. Women carried a heavy burden, children grew up with absent fathers, and relationships were strained. Remittances helped families survive but could fail, and diseases such as tuberculosis spread to rural areas. The system affected all of southern Africa and shaped family life for generations.
