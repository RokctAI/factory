### subtopic_1 — Skilled White Workers
**Assistant:** Why did skilled white workers not join with black workers to demand better pay for everyone?

**Tutor:** Because, in the conditions of the time, most white workers saw black workers as competitors rather than allies, and racial ideas reinforced that view. The mine owners' main strategy for cutting costs was to replace expensive white labour with cheaper black labour wherever possible. If black workers were allowed to do skilled jobs, white workers feared that they would lose their jobs or that their wages would be pushed down. White workers had political power, because they could vote, and their unions could put pressure on the government. Black workers could not vote and were controlled by pass laws, contracts and compounds, so they had much less power to bargain. From the point of view of white workers, it seemed easier to protect their own position through the colour bar than to build a movement with black workers. Racist ideas of the time, shared by many white people across all classes, held that black people were inferior and should not hold skilled or supervisory positions, and this made cooperation even less likely. There were some exceptions. A few socialists, such as members of the International Socialist League formed in 1915, argued that all workers should unite regardless of race, and some later trade unions organised across racial lines. But these were small minorities in this period. Historians point out that this division benefited the mine owners in some ways, because a divided workforce was easier to control, although the colour bar also raised their costs. The result was a labour system in which race determined a worker's job, pay and rights, a pattern that lasted for most of the twentieth century.

### subtopic_2 — Unskilled White Workers and the Poor White Question
**Assistant:** How could a three-pound tax cause so much hardship?

**Tutor:** Because it was large compared with the wages of the people who had to pay it, and because it applied to whole families, not just individual workers. Indentured labourers on the Natal sugar plantations earned very low wages, starting at around ten shillings a month in the early years, together with rations and housing. When their five-year contract ended, they could re-indenture, return to India, or stay in Natal as free people. Many chose to stay, working as market gardeners, fishermen, hawkers, labourers or small farmers, often earning little. From 1895, those who stayed without re-indenturing or returning had to pay three pounds a year, and from 1903 the tax was extended to their sons from the age of sixteen and daughters from thirteen. Three pounds was sixty shillings, which could be half a year's income or more for a poor labourer. For a family with several members liable for the tax, the total could be more than the family's annual income. People who could not pay faced fines and imprisonment, and many were forced to re-indenture, which was exactly what the planters wanted, because it kept a cheap labour force on the plantations. Women were especially hard hit, and the tax was linked to cases of family breakdown and severe poverty. The tax was a major grievance of the Indian community, and its repeal became one of the key demands of the satyagraha campaign. In 1913, Indian workers in Natal, including miners and plantation labourers, went on strike in thousands, partly in protest against the tax. It was finally abolished in 1914 under the Indian Relief Act, as you will see in a later lesson.

### subtopic_3 — Anti-Indian Legislation
**Assistant:** Why did the mine owners bring in Chinese workers instead of employing poor whites?

**Tutor:** Because the Chinese workers were cheaper and the mine owners believed they would be more productive than unskilled white workers. After the South African War of 1899 to 1902, the gold mines needed to restart production quickly, but many black workers did not return, because wages had been cut, conditions were poor, and other work, such as railway building and farm work, was available. The mines were short of tens of thousands of workers. Some people suggested employing unskilled white workers, and an experiment was tried, but the mine owners found that white workers expected much higher wages, which the low-grade mines could not afford. The owners also argued that white men would not accept being treated like black workers, in compounds and under contracts. So they turned to China, which had a long history of supplying indentured labour to other parts of the world. Between 1904 and 1907, about sixty-three thousand Chinese workers were brought to the Rand on three-year contracts, under strict controls: they lived in compounds, could not leave the mine property without permits, and were restricted to unskilled work by law. The experiment was very controversial. White workers saw the Chinese as competitors. In Britain, the Liberal Party attacked the scheme as Chinese slavery and won the election of 1906, partly on this issue. In the Transvaal, the new self-governing government elected in 1907 decided to end the scheme, and the last Chinese workers left in 1910. By then, recruitment of black workers from Mozambique and elsewhere had recovered. The episode shows how the search for cheap labour shaped decisions on the mines, and how racial politics shaped the response.

### subtopic_4 — Divisions, Calculations and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Were Indian South Africans all the same, or were there different groups?

**Tutor:** There were several different groups, and their experiences and interests were not the same. The largest group were indentured labourers and their descendants, who came mainly from southern and northern India, speaking languages such as Tamil, Telugu and Hindi, and who were mostly Hindu, with Muslim and Christian minorities. They came under contract to work on the sugar plantations, railways, docks and coal mines of Natal, and after their contracts many stayed as free people. They were generally poor and faced harsh treatment. A second group were passenger Indians, who paid their own fares and came as free immigrants, mainly from 1870s onwards. Many were traders and merchants, often Muslim, from Gujarat in western India. Some became prosperous, opening shops in Durban, the towns of Natal and the Transvaal. Their success was one reason why white traders demanded restrictions. A third group, which grew over time, were South African-born Indians, who had never lived in India and saw South Africa as their home. Some became teachers, clerks, interpreters and other professionals. These groups had different concerns. Traders worried most about trading licences, immigration and property rights, while ex-indentured families worried most about the three-pound tax, wages and working conditions. The early Natal Indian Congress, founded by Gandhi and others in 1894, was led mainly by traders, and the inclusion of indentured and ex-indentured workers in the struggle came later, especially in the great strike of 1913. Understanding these differences helps historians explain why some laws affected one group more than another and how political organisation developed.
