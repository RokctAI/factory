### subtopic_1 — Everyday Resistance by Black Workers
**Assistant:** Was desertion really resistance, or just people leaving a bad job?

**Tutor:** It was both, and that is exactly why historians see it as a form of resistance. In a free labour market, leaving a bad job is simply a choice. But the mining labour system was designed to take that choice away. Pass laws required workers to stay with their employer until the contract ended, masters and servants laws made breaking a contract a crime, and compounds made it physically harder to leave. In that situation, leaving before the end of a contract meant breaking the law and risking arrest, fines and imprisonment. When thousands of workers took that risk every year, they were defying a system of control, even if each one acted alone and for personal reasons. Desertion also had real effects on the mining industry. It made labour more expensive, because recruiters had to replace workers, and it put pressure on mines with bad conditions. The mine owners recognised this, which is why they spent so much effort tightening pass laws, fingerprinting workers to identify deserters, and coordinating through the Chamber of Mines so that deserters could not easily find work at another mine. Historians such as Charles van Onselen and others have argued that these everyday forms of resistance were the most important weapons of workers who had little formal power. They point out that focusing only on strikes and organisations can make it seem that black workers were passive, when in fact they were constantly finding ways to protect themselves. So when you answer a question about resistance, it is good to include everyday forms, explain why they were used, and show their effects.

### subtopic_2 — Strikes and Organised Resistance
**Assistant:** Why did white workers and black workers not strike together in 1913?

**Tutor:** Because they had different interests in the short term, different levels of power, and were divided by racial attitudes and the colour bar. The white strike of 1913 began over a dispute about working hours and union recognition at one mine and spread into a general strike about the rights of white unions. The white strikers wanted to protect their own position, including the colour bar that reserved skilled jobs for them. Most white workers saw black workers as cheap competition rather than as fellow workers. Black workers, for their part, had grievances about very low wages, harsh compound conditions and the pass system, which were quite different from those of the white strikers. When black workers on several mines struck during the white strike, demanding wage increases, they acted separately, and the white unions did not support them. The government and the mine owners treated the two strikes very differently. They negotiated with the white strikers, who had votes and whose strike had become a major political crisis, while they crushed the black strike with armed force. This contrast shows how racial divisions shaped the response of the state. Some historians argue that the black workers' decision to strike at the same time shows that they had learned from the white workers about the power of collective action. A few socialists at the time called for all workers to unite, but they were a small minority. The lack of cooperation between white and black workers in this period helped the mine owners and the state, and it set a pattern of separate struggles that lasted for decades.

### subtopic_3 — The Growth of Johannesburg
**Assistant:** Why did the council burn part of Johannesburg in 1904?

**Tutor:** Because of an outbreak of bubonic plague, but the way the authorities responded tells us a lot about their attitudes and intentions. In March 1904, cases of pneumonic and bubonic plague broke out in an area of the town known at the time as the Coolie Location, a crowded neighbourhood where many Indian, black and coloured residents lived. Plague is a very serious disease spread by fleas on rats and, in its pneumonic form, from person to person. Several dozen people died. The town council decided to burn down the whole location to destroy the rats and fleas, and moved its residents. Black residents were moved to a site at Klipspruit, about twenty kilometres from the town centre, near a sewage farm. Many Indian residents were moved elsewhere. Historians point out that health concerns were real, but that the authorities also used the plague as an opportunity to carry out a long-planned goal: to remove black residents from the town centre and separate them from white residents. The idea that black people were a source of disease, sometimes called the sanitation syndrome, was used in several South African cities at this time to justify segregation. Klipspruit, also called Pimville, later became part of Soweto, the vast township that grew in the south-west of Johannesburg. So the fire of 1904 is an early example of forced removal in Johannesburg, decades before the removals of the apartheid era, such as the destruction of Sophiatown in the 1950s. It shows how disease, fear and policy combined to shape the city.

### subtopic_4 — City Life, Calculations and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** If Johannesburg was so harsh, why did people keep coming?

**Tutor:** Because, for all its hardships, Johannesburg offered opportunities that were not available elsewhere, and because conditions in many rural areas were becoming worse. For black migrants, the city and mines offered cash wages, which were needed for taxes, lobola, cattle, ploughs and school fees, at a time when land loss and crowded reserves made farming alone insufficient. Some found better-paid work outside the mines, as domestic workers, washermen, drivers or in small businesses, and some built new lives in the city with their families, despite restrictions. For poor Afrikaners pushed off the land by drought, rinderpest and war, the city was a place where they hoped to find work. For immigrants from Britain, Europe and elsewhere, Johannesburg offered high wages for skilled workers and the chance of business success. For Indian and Chinese traders, it offered customers. The city also offered freedom of a kind: escape from the control of rural elders, landlords or chiefs, new ideas, schools, churches, music and entertainment, and the excitement of a fast-changing place. Many young people were drawn by this, as well as by necessity. People also kept coming because of push factors: they had few alternatives. This combination of push and pull factors is the same one geographers use to explain urbanisation today, which you will study in Geography in Term 3. Historians who have studied Johannesburg describe it as a city of both suffering and creativity, where people faced exploitation and segregation but also built communities, cultures and movements that changed South Africa.
