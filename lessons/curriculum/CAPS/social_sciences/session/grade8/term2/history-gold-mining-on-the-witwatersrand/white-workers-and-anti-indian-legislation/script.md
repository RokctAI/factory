# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines two kinds of division created during the mineral revolution: the division of the workforce on the Witwatersrand into skilled white workers, unskilled white workers and black workers, and the laws passed against Indian South Africans in the Boer republics, Natal and the Transvaal from the 1880s to the early 1900s. The anchor is the question of who was protected and who was excluded as the new industrial society took shape. The governing idea is that white workers used their political power to protect their jobs and wages through a colour bar, while governments passed laws to restrict the rights of Indian South Africans, and that both developments were steps towards the racial segregation of the twentieth century. Explaining the position of different groups of workers and the purpose and effects of anti-Indian legislation is examinable this term.

## Subtopic: Skilled White Workers

When deep-level gold mining began on the Witwatersrand, the industry needed workers with skills that very few people in southern Africa had: experience of sinking shafts, timbering, blasting, operating and repairing steam engines and machinery, and managing underground work. These skills were found among miners in Britain, especially in Cornwall, where tin and copper mines were in decline, and in Australia and the United States. Thousands of skilled miners and artisans emigrated to the Rand in the 1880s and 1890s, attracted by high wages.

Skilled white workers were in a strong bargaining position. They were few in number and difficult to replace. They earned high wages, many times those of black workers, and lived with their families in the towns. They formed trade unions, such as the Transvaal Miners' Association, founded in 1902, and unions of engine drivers and other artisans, to protect their wages and conditions.

The skilled workers' position was threatened in two ways. First, many of them suffered from miners' phthisis, the lung disease caused by rock dust from drilling, especially after machine drills were introduced. Many Cornish miners died young; a mine inspector's report in the early 1900s found that rock drillers on the Rand were dying at an average age in their thirties. Second, the mine owners wanted to reduce costs by replacing expensive white workers with cheaper black workers, who over time learned many of the same skills.

The white workers responded by demanding a colour bar: rules that reserved certain jobs for white workers only. Some of these rules were set by custom and agreements; others were written into law, such as the regulations of the Transvaal and later the Mines and Works Act of 1911, which allowed the government to restrict certificates of competency, for example for blasting and engine driving, to white workers. The colour bar protected white workers' jobs, but it also meant that black workers, however experienced, could not rise into skilled positions or earn skilled wages.

Skilled workers also took part in some of the earliest large strikes in South African history. In 1907, white miners struck when the mine owners tried to make each white miner supervise more machine drills operated by black workers, which would have reduced the number of white jobs. The owners broke the strike by employing unemployed Afrikaners as strike-breakers, which showed how the existence of poor whites could be used against skilled workers. Further strikes followed in 1913 and 1914, and the largest, the Rand Revolt of 1922, was crushed by the army.

The questions for this section are with you now: where skilled white workers came from, their unions, and the colour bar.

## Subtopic: Unskilled White Workers and the Poor White Question

A second group of white workers was very different. In the late nineteenth century, many Afrikaner families in the Transvaal and Free State were losing their place on the land. Farms had been divided among sons for generations until they were too small to support a family. Many families lived as bywoners, tenants on other people's farms, and were pushed off as landowners began to farm commercially for the new urban markets. The rinderpest epidemic of 1896 to 1897 killed most of the cattle in southern Africa. Droughts struck repeatedly. Then the South African War of 1899 to 1902 destroyed farms, as British forces burned homesteads and crops and moved women and children into concentration camps.

Thousands of impoverished Afrikaners moved to the towns, especially to Johannesburg. They usually had little education or industrial skill and often spoke little English, the language of the mines. Most of the unskilled jobs on the mines were done by black workers at very low wages, and the mine owners were unwilling to pay white workers more for the same work. This created what was called the poor white problem: a growing number of white people living in poverty in the towns, alongside black people.

Governments and churches saw the poor white problem as a political danger, because these were voters, and because poverty among white people seemed to them to undermine the idea of white superiority on which colonial society was built. Solutions included relief works, training schemes, and later, policies that reserved certain jobs on the railways and in government service for white workers at higher wages, sometimes called the civilised labour policy of the 1920s.

The mine owners, for their part, experimented with employing unskilled white workers but generally found them more expensive than black workers. During the shortage of black labour after the South African War, they chose instead to import about sixty-three thousand Chinese indentured workers between 1904 and 1907, who worked on the Rand until the last were sent back in 1910. White workers and many white voters strongly opposed Chinese labour, and it became a major election issue in Britain and the Transvaal.

Poverty among white people was not only an Afrikaner experience; there were also poor English-speaking whites. But the Afrikaner poor were the largest group, and their situation became an important part of Afrikaner nationalist politics in the twentieth century. A major study of the poor white problem by the Carnegie Commission, published in 1932, estimated that about three hundred thousand white people were very poor. Afrikaner nationalist leaders later used the memory of this poverty to win support for policies that protected white workers.

The questions for this section are with you now: why Afrikaners became poor whites, the poor white problem, and the experiment with Chinese labour.

## Subtopic: Anti-Indian Legislation

As you learned in Term 1, Indian indentured labourers began arriving in Natal in 1860 to work on the sugar plantations, and from the 1870s passenger Indians, mostly traders, also arrived at their own expense. By the 1890s, many Indians lived in Natal and in the Transvaal, working as market gardeners, hawkers, traders, waiters, railway workers and labourers. Some ex-indentured Indians bought land, and Indian traders competed successfully with white traders.

White settlers, especially white traders, increasingly demanded laws to restrict Indians. Governments responded with a series of laws.

In the South African Republic, Law 3 of 1885 prevented Indians and other Asians from owning land except in streets, wards and locations set aside for them, required them to register and pay a fee, and denied them citizenship. In the Orange Free State, a law of 1891 effectively banned Indians from living there, except as labourers.

In Natal, which received responsible government in 1893, the white parliament passed several laws aimed at Indians. A law of 1895 imposed an annual tax of three pounds on every ex-indentured Indian who chose to remain in Natal without re-indenturing or returning to India; it applied to men, women and older children, a heavy burden when a labourer's wage was about ten shillings a month. A franchise law of 1896 effectively removed Indians from the voters' roll by excluding people from countries that did not have elected parliaments. In 1897 the Immigration Restriction Act used a test in a European language to keep out new Indian immigrants, and the Dealers' Licences Act allowed town councils to refuse trading licences, which was used against Indian traders.

In the Transvaal, after the South African War, the British administration enforced the old republic's laws more strictly. In 1906 it proposed the Asiatic Law Amendment Ordinance, passed in 1907 as the Asiatic Registration Act, which required all Indians to register and give their fingerprints, and to carry registration certificates. Indians called it the Black Act. It led to the beginning of the satyagraha campaign led by Mohandas Gandhi, which you will study in the lesson on the turning point.

Indian South Africans did not accept these laws quietly. In 1894, Mohandas Gandhi, a young lawyer who had come to Natal in 1893 to work on a legal case for an Indian trading firm, helped to found the Natal Indian Congress. It organised petitions to the Natal parliament and to the British government, wrote letters to newspapers and published pamphlets explaining Indian grievances. These methods won some sympathy in Britain and India, but they did not stop the laws, which led later to more forceful forms of protest.

The questions for this section are with you now: the reasons for anti-Indian laws and the laws of the Transvaal, the Free State and Natal.

## Subtopic: Divisions, Calculations and the Error Museum

A calculation shows how heavy the three-pound tax was. If a labourer earned ten shillings a month, his yearly wage was ten times twelve, which is one hundred and twenty shillings, or six pounds. A tax of three pounds, which is sixty shillings, took half of that wage, because sixty is half of one hundred and twenty. For a family with a wife and a son over the age at which the tax applied, the tax could be nine pounds a year, more than a whole year's wage. The figures for wages varied, but this calculation shows why the tax caused such hardship and was one of the causes of the great strike of 1913.

The divisions created in this period were not natural or inevitable; they were made by people with interests and power. Skilled white workers used unions and votes. Mine owners wanted cheap labour. White traders wanted to limit competition. Governments responded to the demands of white voters. Black workers, Indians and Chinese had little or no political power, so laws were made about them, not by them.

The divisions also prevented workers from uniting. Instead of all workers bargaining together for better pay and conditions, white workers often saw black, Indian and Chinese workers as threats to their jobs, and the colour bar set them against one another. Historians debate whether white workers or mine owners were more responsible for the colour bar; most agree that both played a part.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying all white workers were rich. Skilled workers earned high wages, but many Afrikaner poor whites lived in poverty. Two: saying the colour bar was introduced to help black workers. It protected white workers. Three: confusing indentured Indians with passenger Indians. Indentured labourers came under contract to work on plantations; passenger Indians paid their own way, many as traders. Four: saying the three-pound tax applied to all Indians. It applied to ex-indentured Indians and their families who stayed in Natal. Five: forgetting the Chinese labourers of 1904 to 1910.

Layout for marks in a paragraph: describe each group, explain its position and interests, give an example of a law or rule, and explain the effect on relations between groups.

The questions for this section are with you now: the tax calculation, the making of divisions, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: The Skilled Workers

The gold mines needed people who knew how to sink shafts, use dynamite and run engines. Many such miners came from Cornwall in Britain, from Australia and from America. They were paid very well and formed trade unions.

But they were afraid that the mine owners would replace them with black workers, who were paid much less. So they demanded the colour bar: rules that kept certain jobs for white workers only. This kept their jobs safe but stopped black workers from getting better jobs.

Many of these skilled miners got a lung disease from rock dust and died young.

In 1907, when white miners went on strike, the owners hired poor Afrikaners to replace them. This shows how one group of white workers could be used against another.

Hold the colour bar in mind and try the questions for this part: skilled white workers.

## Subtopic: Poor Whites

Many Afrikaner families lost their farms: farms got too small, cattle died in the rinderpest of 1896 and 1897, droughts struck, and the war of 1899 to 1902 burned farms. They moved to Johannesburg but had few skills, so they struggled to find work. This was called the poor white problem.

After the war, the mine owners brought in about sixty-three thousand Chinese workers instead, which made white workers angry. The Chinese workers were sent home by 1910.

In 1932, a study counted about three hundred thousand very poor white people in South Africa.

Hold the poor white families in mind and try this part's questions: unskilled white workers.

## Subtopic: Laws Against Indians

Indian South Africans faced laws that limited their rights. In the Transvaal, from 1885, they could only own land in special areas. In the Free State, they were banned from living there from 1891. In Natal, ex-indentured Indians who stayed had to pay a tax of three pounds a year from 1895, Indians lost the vote in 1896, and new immigrants were kept out from 1897. In 1907, the Transvaal made all Indians register with fingerprints, which they called the Black Act. This led to Gandhi's campaign of satyagraha.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Skilled white workers from Britain and elsewhere earned high wages, formed unions and demanded a colour bar to protect their jobs. Unskilled white workers, mostly Afrikaners who had lost their land, became poor whites in the towns. The mine owners preferred cheap black labour and brought in Chinese workers from 1904 to 1910. Indian South Africans faced laws that restricted land, trade, the vote, immigration and movement, including Natal's three-pound tax and the Transvaal's Black Act. These divisions were made by people with power and laid foundations for later segregation.

Indian South Africans protested from the start. In 1894 Gandhi and others founded the Natal Indian Congress, which sent petitions and letters against these laws.
