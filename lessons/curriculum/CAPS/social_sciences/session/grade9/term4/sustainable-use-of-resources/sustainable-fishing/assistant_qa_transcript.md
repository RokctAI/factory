### subtopic_1 — What Sustainable Fishing Means
**Assistant:** Why do fishery managers aim to catch less than the maximum sustainable yield?

**Tutor:** The maximum sustainable yield is the largest catch that can, in theory, be taken from a fish stock every year without reducing the stock in the long run. In practice, fishery managers usually aim to catch less than this, for several reasons. First, estimates of fish stocks are uncertain. Scientists cannot count fish directly in the ocean, so they estimate stock sizes from catch data, research surveys and mathematical models, and these estimates can be wrong. If the stock is smaller than estimated, a catch set at the supposed maximum could actually cause over-fishing. Second, fish populations vary naturally. Changes in ocean temperatures, currents, winds and food supply can cause the number of young fish surviving each year to rise or fall sharply. Small pelagic fish such as sardines are especially variable. A catch that is sustainable in a good year may be too high in a bad year. Third, other species depend on the fish. A stock fished at its maximum sustainable yield may leave too little food for predators such as seabirds, penguins and seals, so an ecosystem approach to fisheries may require smaller catches of species that are important prey. Fourth, catches may be higher than reported, because of illegal fishing, unreported bycatch or fish that are thrown back dead, so actual fishing pressure may be greater than the official catch. Fifth, the consequences of a mistake are serious and may be long-lasting, as the Newfoundland cod collapse showed. For all these reasons, managers follow the precautionary principle, setting catch limits with a safety margin. This may reduce catches in the short term, but it makes it much more likely that the fishery will survive and provide food and jobs in the long term.

### subtopic_2 — Managing Fisheries: Science, Rules and Enforcement
**Assistant:** How do marine protected areas help fishers if fishing is banned inside them?

**Tutor:** At first, it might seem that marine protected areas simply take fishing grounds away from fishers, and fishers have sometimes opposed them for this reason. However, well-designed protected areas can benefit fisheries in several ways. Inside a no-take protected area, fish are not caught, so they live longer and grow larger. Large, older fish produce many more eggs than small, young fish of the same species, and often healthier eggs, so a protected area can become a powerful breeding source. Many fish species release eggs and larvae that drift with ocean currents for days or weeks before settling, so some of the young produced inside the protected area are carried into surrounding waters, where they grow up and can be caught. In addition, as the number of fish inside the protected area increases, some adult fish move out across its boundaries into fished areas. These effects are called spillover. Studies in many parts of the world have found that catches near the edges of well-protected areas can be higher than elsewhere. Protected areas also act as insurance. If management outside them fails and stocks decline, the protected population can help the stock recover. They protect important habitats, such as reefs, kelp forests and spawning grounds, that fish need at different stages of life. In South Africa, research in the Tsitsikamma Marine Protected Area has shown that species such as roman and red steenbras are much more abundant and larger inside the protected area than in nearby fished waters, and that some of these fish move out to surrounding areas. Protected areas work best when they are large enough, well enforced, connected in networks and supported by local communities, who need to share in the benefits, for example through tourism jobs.

### subtopic_3 — Protected Areas, Better Methods and Fish Farming
**Assistant:** How can one person's fish choice really make a difference?

**Tutor:** One person buying one piece of fish will not save a fish stock by themselves, but individual choices make a difference when many people make them together, and they influence the way businesses behave. Fishing companies, wholesalers, supermarkets and restaurants want to sell what customers want to buy. If many customers ask whether fish is sustainable and refuse to buy over-fished species, shops and restaurants notice that demand is falling and that their reputation is at risk. They then have an incentive to change their suppliers and to sell green-listed or certified fish instead. This is what has happened in South Africa. After SASSI was launched in 2004, several major supermarket chains and many restaurants committed to selling only sustainable seafood, partly because of customer pressure. When large buyers demand sustainable fish, fishing companies have an incentive to improve their practices and to seek certification, so that they can keep selling to those buyers. Consumer choices also send a message to governments that people care about the oceans, which supports stronger management. In addition, individual choices can help to stop illegal trade. Buying red-listed fish, such as protected line fish, or buying abalone from unknown sources, may support illegal fishing and poaching, which damages stocks and coastal communities. Asking questions about where fish comes from, checking SASSI, and choosing alternatives make illegal fish harder to sell. Finally, talking to family and friends about sustainable choices spreads the idea further. So, while governments, scientists and fishers carry the main responsibility for managing fisheries, consumers can help create the demand that makes sustainable fishing worthwhile.

### subtopic_4 — Consumers, Communities and SASSI, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why did South Africa's small-scale fishers need a new policy?

**Tutor:** Small-scale fishers are people who fish from the shore or from small boats, often using simple gear such as hand lines, nets and traps, mainly to feed their families and sell their catch locally. Many live in coastal communities in the Western Cape, Eastern Cape, Northern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal, and some families have fished in the same areas for generations. Under apartheid, black and coloured fishing communities were often excluded from fishing rights, which were dominated by large, mainly white-owned companies, and some communities were forcibly removed from the coast under the Group Areas Act. After 1994, the government introduced a new system of fishing rights under the Marine Living Resources Act of 1998, which aimed to transform the industry and include historically disadvantaged people. However, the system mainly divided rights into commercial and recreational categories and allocated individual rights through an application process. Many traditional small-scale fishers could not meet the requirements, or were left out, and some lost access to resources they had always used. This led to hardship and also to some fishing without permits, which the law treated as poaching. In 2005, a group of fishers and supporting organisations went to court, and in 2007 the Equality Court ordered the government to develop a new policy that would recognise small-scale fishers. The result was the Small-Scale Fisheries Policy of 2012, and the Marine Living Resources Act was amended in 2014. It recognised small-scale fishing as a separate sector, allowed rights to be given to community cooperatives rather than only to individuals, and aimed to involve communities in managing nearby resources. Implementation has been slow and disputed, but the policy shows that sustainable fishing must also be fair.
