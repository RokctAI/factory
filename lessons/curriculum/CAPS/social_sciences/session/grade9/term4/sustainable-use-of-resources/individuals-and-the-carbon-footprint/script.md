# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the role of individuals in more sustainable resource use. It covers how the choices of individuals and households add up to pressure on resources; what a carbon footprint is, how it is measured and why it matters for climate change, including the size of footprints in South Africa and other countries; practical ways in which individuals can reduce pressure on resources and lower their carbon footprint, including using less, reusing and recycling, and saving water, energy and food; and the importance and limits of individual action, alongside the responsibilities of governments and businesses. The anchor idea is that every person uses resources and produces waste and greenhouse gases, and that small changes made by millions of people add up to large effects. The governing idea is that individual action matters most when it is combined with action by communities, businesses and governments, which shape the choices people are able to make.

## Subtopic: How Individuals Put Pressure on Resources

Every day, each person uses natural resources. We drink water and use it for washing, cooking and flushing toilets. We eat food grown on farmland, using soil, water, fertiliser and energy. We use electricity, much of which in South Africa comes from burning coal. We travel in cars, taxis, buses and trains that burn petrol or diesel. We buy clothes, phones, furniture and packaged goods, each of which required raw materials, energy and transport to make. Then we throw things away, producing waste that must be collected and disposed of.

The amount of resources a person uses depends greatly on their wealth and way of life. A household with a car, many electrical appliances, a large house and frequent flights overseas uses far more resources than a household that walks or uses public transport, cooks on a single stove and buys few goods. Around the world, the richest people use a very large share of the world's resources and produce a large share of its greenhouse gas emissions, while the poorest use very little. The same pattern exists within South Africa, which is one of the most unequal countries in the world.

The pressure from individuals adds up. If one person leaves a tap dripping, the waste is small. If millions of people do so, the waste is enormous. South Africa is a dry country, and municipalities lose a large share of treated water through leaks in pipes and households. South Africans also throw away a great deal of food; studies have estimated that about a third of the food produced in South Africa, around ten million tonnes a year, is lost or wasted along the way from farm to plate, including in homes. And many South African landfill sites are filling up, with plastic packaging and organic waste making up a large part of household rubbish.

At the same time, the choices of individuals are not made in a vacuum. They are shaped by prices, by what is available in shops, by whether there is safe public transport, reliable electricity, recycling collection or a clean water supply. A person who lives far from work in a place with no safe public transport may have no choice but to use a car or taxi. This is important when we think about what individuals can and should do.

So the next question is: what is a carbon footprint?

## Subtopic: The Carbon Footprint

The burning of fossil fuels such as coal, oil and gas releases carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas. Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere trap heat from the sun, in the same way that the glass of a greenhouse keeps the inside warm. This is natural and necessary: without any greenhouse effect, the earth would be too cold for most life. However, since the Industrial Revolution, people have added large amounts of carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases, such as methane from livestock, rice fields and landfills, to the atmosphere. This has strengthened the greenhouse effect, causing global warming and climate change. The earth's average surface temperature has already risen by more than one degree Celsius since the late nineteenth century. Effects include more frequent heatwaves, droughts and floods, rising sea levels and damage to ecosystems. South Africa is expected to become hotter, and parts of it drier, with more severe droughts, like the one that nearly caused Cape Town's Day Zero.

A carbon footprint is the total amount of greenhouse gases produced, directly and indirectly, by the activities of a person, household, organisation, product or country. It is usually measured in tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent per year, which means that other greenhouse gases are converted into the amount of carbon dioxide that would have the same warming effect.

A person's carbon footprint includes direct emissions, such as the petrol burnt in the family car or the gas burnt in a stove, and indirect emissions, such as the emissions produced by the power station that generates their electricity, by the farms and factories that produced their food and goods, and by the trucks, ships and planes that transported them. The main parts of most people's footprints are energy use at home, transport, food and the goods they buy.

Footprints differ hugely between countries and people. On average, a person in South Africa is responsible for roughly six to eight tonnes of carbon dioxide a year from energy use, which is higher than the world average of about four to five tonnes and much higher than in most other African countries, mainly because South Africa's electricity comes largely from coal and its economy relies on energy-intensive mining and industry. A person in the United States produces, on average, more than twice as much as a South African, while a person in many countries of sub-Saharan Africa produces well under one tonne. Within South Africa, wealthier households have much larger footprints than poorer ones.

Calculators available online can estimate a household's carbon footprint by asking about electricity use, transport, diet and shopping. Calculating a footprint helps people see where their largest emissions come from, so they can focus on the changes that make the most difference.

Other footprints work in a similar way. A water footprint measures the total amount of fresh water used to produce the goods and services a person consumes, including the hidden water used to grow food and make products. For example, producing a kilogram of beef requires far more water than producing a kilogram of vegetables or grain. An ecological footprint measures the area of land and sea needed to supply a person's resources and absorb their waste.

So the next question is: what can individuals do?

## Subtopic: Practical Actions for Individuals and Households

Individuals can reduce pressure on resources and lower their carbon footprint in many practical ways. A useful guide is the waste hierarchy, often summarised as refuse, reduce, reuse, repair and recycle, in that order of importance. The best action is to avoid using a resource at all if it is not needed; the next best is to use less; then to reuse items, repair them when they break, and finally to recycle materials when they can no longer be used.

At home, saving electricity reduces both costs and carbon emissions, especially in South Africa, where most electricity comes from coal. People can switch off lights and appliances when not in use, use energy-efficient LED light bulbs, use a geyser blanket and a timer or lower the geyser temperature, boil only as much water as needed, dry clothes outside instead of in a tumble dryer, and cook with lids on pots. Where families can afford it, solar water heaters and solar panels reduce reliance on the grid.

Saving water is vital in a dry country. People can take short showers instead of baths, turn off the tap while brushing their teeth, fix dripping taps and leaking toilets, reuse grey water from washing to water gardens or flush toilets, collect rainwater in tanks, and plant indigenous, water-wise plants. During the Cape Town crisis, residents were asked to use no more than fifty litres each per day, and many succeeded.

Wasting less food saves the land, water and energy used to grow, transport and cook it, and reduces methane from food rotting in landfills. People can plan meals, buy only what they need, store food properly, use leftovers, and compost food scraps to make fertiliser for gardens. Eating more plant-based meals and less red meat also lowers carbon footprints, because cattle produce methane and need large amounts of land and feed.

Transport choices matter too. Walking, cycling, using public transport, sharing lifts and combining trips all reduce fuel use. For longer journeys, trains and buses produce fewer emissions per passenger than cars, and flights produce the most.

When shopping, people can carry reusable bags, avoid unnecessary packaging and single-use plastics such as straws, buy durable goods and repair them, buy second-hand clothes and pass on items they no longer need, and choose sustainable products, such as SASSI green-listed fish. Recycling paper, glass, cans and plastics reduces the need for new raw materials and saves energy. In South Africa, informal waste pickers collect a large share of the packaging that is recycled, so separating recyclables at home also supports their livelihoods.

Learners can also act at school, for example by starting recycling or food gardens, saving electricity in classrooms, and raising awareness in their communities.

So the final question is: how much can individuals really achieve?

## Subtopic: Individuals, Society and Government, and the Error Museum

Individual action is important for several reasons. Millions of small changes add up, as the Cape Town water crisis showed, when households reduced the city's total water use by about half in a few years. Individual choices also influence businesses, because companies respond to what customers want to buy. People who change their own behaviour often influence family, friends and neighbours, spreading new habits. And individuals can act as citizens, by voting, writing to leaders, joining community organisations and supporting policies that protect resources.

However, individual action alone cannot solve problems such as climate change or over-use of resources. The largest sources of greenhouse gas emissions in South Africa are coal-fired power stations, industry, mining and transport systems, which are controlled by governments and large companies. Individuals cannot choose clean electricity if it is not available, or use public transport that does not exist. Many South Africans already use very few resources because they are poor, and it would be unfair to expect them to carry the same responsibility as wealthy households or large polluters.

This is why governments and businesses also have responsibilities. Governments can make laws and set standards, invest in renewable energy and public transport, provide recycling services, put a price on pollution and protect natural resources. South Africa introduced a levy on plastic shopping bags in 2003, which reduced the use of thin bags and raised funds for environmental projects, and a carbon tax in 2019, which makes large emitters pay for their pollution. Businesses can reduce waste and emissions, design products that last and can be recycled, and source materials sustainably. Internationally, countries have agreed under the Paris Agreement of 2015 to limit global warming, and South Africa has committed to reducing its emissions.

The most effective approach combines all of these levels: individuals making sustainable choices, communities working together, businesses changing how they operate, and governments creating rules and systems that make sustainable choices easier and cheaper for everyone.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "One person's actions make no difference." Millions of small actions add up, as Cape Town's water savings showed, and individuals influence others and businesses.

Exhibit two: "Recycling is the most important thing to do." Refusing and reducing come first in the waste hierarchy; recycling is the last step.

Exhibit three: "A carbon footprint only includes the fuel in a car." It includes direct and indirect emissions from electricity, food, goods and transport.

Exhibit four: "Individuals alone can solve climate change." Governments and businesses control the largest sources of emissions, so action is needed at every level.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a bucket with a hole, a muddy footprint, and many hands.

## Subtopic: A Bucket With a Hole

Imagine carrying water in a bucket with a small hole. A few drops leak out with every step. One bucket does not lose much. But imagine millions of people, every day, all with leaking buckets.

That is how our everyday habits work. Every time we leave a light on, let a tap run, throw away food or use a plastic bag once and bin it, a little bit of our resources leaks away. Water, electricity, food and materials all come from nature, and in a dry country like South Africa, we cannot afford to waste them.

People with more money usually use far more: bigger houses, more cars, more gadgets, more flights. So they have bigger holes in their buckets.

In 2018, Cape Town nearly ran out of water. People took two-minute showers, saved bath water and fixed leaks. Together, they cut the city's water use by about half, and the taps kept running.

So how do our habits affect the climate?

## Subtopic: A Muddy Footprint

When you walk through mud, you leave footprints behind. In the same way, everything we do leaves an invisible footprint on the air, called a carbon footprint.

Burning coal, petrol and gas puts carbon dioxide into the air. It acts like a blanket around the earth, trapping heat and making the planet hotter. That brings more droughts, floods and heatwaves.

Your carbon footprint is all the greenhouse gas your life causes. It comes from the electricity you use, the car or taxi you ride in, the food you eat and the things you buy.

South Africans have bigger footprints than most Africans, because most of our electricity comes from burning coal. Americans have even bigger ones.

So what can we do?

## Subtopic: Many Hands

There is a saying: many hands make light work. If everyone does a little, a lot gets done.

You can switch off lights and chargers. Take short showers and fix dripping taps. Walk, cycle or share lifts. Take a reusable bag to the shop. Finish your food and compost the scraps. Fix things instead of throwing them away, and recycle bottles, cans and paper.

But some things are too big for one family. The government must build clean power, safe buses and trains, and recycling services. Companies must cut pollution and waste. When everyone plays their part, from families to factories, the changes become huge.

Here is the line to remember: every person leaves a footprint on the earth's resources and climate, and when individuals, communities, businesses and governments all act together, that footprint can be made much smaller.
