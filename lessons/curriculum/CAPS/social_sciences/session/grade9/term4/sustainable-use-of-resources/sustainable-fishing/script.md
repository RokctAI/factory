# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains sustainable fishing as an example of using a resource sustainably. It covers the meaning of sustainable resource use and of a sustainable fishery; the ways in which governments manage fisheries, including scientific stock assessments, catch limits, fishing rights, closed seasons, size limits and gear rules, as well as enforcement; marine protected areas and better fishing practices, including ways to reduce bycatch, and the role and limits of fish farming; and the role of consumers, certification and communities, including South Africa's SASSI programme and its small-scale fisheries policy. The anchor idea is that a sustainable fishery catches no more fish each year than the stock can replace, so that it can provide food and jobs indefinitely. The governing idea is that sustainable fishing needs good science, fair rules that are enforced, protected areas where fish can breed, and the cooperation of fishers and consumers.

## Subtopic: What Sustainable Fishing Means

Sustainable use of a resource means using it in a way that meets the needs of people today without reducing the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. This idea comes from the definition of sustainable development in the Brundtland Report of 1987, which you met when you studied development in Term 2. For renewable resources such as fish, sustainable use means using them at a rate no faster than they can be replaced.

A sustainable fishery is one that can continue to provide a catch year after year, without the fish stock declining. Three conditions are usually needed. First, the fish stock must be kept at a healthy level, with enough adult fish to breed. Second, fishing must not cause serious damage to the wider ecosystem, including other species, through bycatch or habitat damage. Third, the fishery must be well managed, with rules that are based on science and that are respected and enforced.

Sustainable fishing also has a social and economic side. A sustainable fishery should provide decent jobs and food, and the right to fish should be shared fairly, including with small-scale fishers and coastal communities who have depended on fish for generations. Sustainability is not about stopping fishing altogether; it is about fishing in a way that can last.

The key scientific idea is the maximum sustainable yield, which is the largest catch that can be taken from a fish stock each year without reducing its size in the long run. If a stock is fished below this level, it can grow; if it is fished above it, it shrinks. Because scientists cannot count fish directly, and because fish populations vary naturally from year to year with changes in ocean conditions, fishery managers usually aim to catch somewhat less than the estimated maximum, to leave a safety margin. This careful approach is called the precautionary principle: when there is uncertainty, it is better to be cautious than to risk a collapse.

So the next question is: how do governments manage fisheries?

## Subtopic: Managing Fisheries: Science, Rules and Enforcement

Fishery management begins with science. Fisheries scientists collect data on catches, the size and age of fish caught, and the results of research surveys at sea, and use these to estimate the size of each fish stock and how it is changing. These estimates are called stock assessments. In South Africa, this work is done by scientists in the national Department of Forestry, Fisheries and the Environment, working with universities and other researchers. South Africa's main fisheries are managed under the Marine Living Resources Act of 1998.

Based on stock assessments, the government sets a total allowable catch, or TAC, for many species each year. This is the maximum amount of a species that may be caught by all fishers together in that year. For some fisheries, a limit is placed on the amount of fishing effort instead, such as the number of boats, the number of days at sea or the amount of gear used. The total allowable catch is then divided among fishing companies and individuals as fishing rights or quotas. When the catch limit is reached, fishing for that species must stop.

Governments also use other rules. Closed seasons ban fishing at certain times of the year, especially during the breeding season, so that fish can spawn. Closed areas ban fishing in nursery areas where young fish grow. Size limits set a minimum size for fish that may be kept, so that young fish are returned to the sea and have a chance to breed at least once. Bag limits restrict how many fish a recreational angler may keep in a day. Gear restrictions control the type of nets and hooks that may be used, for example by setting a minimum size for the holes in nets, called mesh size, so that small fish can escape.

Rules only work if they are obeyed. Enforcement means checking that fishers follow the rules and punishing those who do not. Fishery control officers inspect catches when boats land, patrol vessels check boats at sea, and some boats are required to carry observers or satellite tracking systems that show where they are fishing. South Africa has patrol vessels for this purpose. Enforcement is expensive and difficult, especially along long coastlines and against well-organised poachers, which is why cooperation from fishers and communities is so important.

South Africa's hake trawl fishery shows how management can work. In the 1960s and 1970s, foreign fleets fished heavily off South Africa's coast, and hake stocks declined. After South Africa declared a 200-nautical-mile fishing zone in 1977 and excluded most foreign fleets, it introduced strict catch limits based on scientific assessments. The stock recovered, and in 2004 the hake trawl fishery became the first hake fishery in the world to be certified as sustainable by the Marine Stewardship Council.

So the next question is: what else can be done to protect fish?

## Subtopic: Protected Areas, Better Methods and Fish Farming

Marine protected areas, or MPAs, are areas of the sea where fishing and other activities are restricted or banned to protect marine life. In no-take zones, no fishing at all is allowed. Inside these areas, fish can grow large and breed, and many studies show that the number and size of fish inside well-protected areas increase. Fish and fish larvae can then spread into surrounding waters, where they can be caught, which is called the spillover effect. Protected areas also protect habitats such as reefs, kelp forests and seagrass beds, and they provide places for scientific research and tourism. In 2019, South Africa declared twenty new marine protected areas, bringing the total to forty-one and increasing the area of its mainland ocean territory under protection to about 5 percent. One of the oldest is the Tsitsikamma Marine Protected Area, declared in 1964, which has been a no-take zone for decades and has much larger and more numerous fish than nearby fished areas.

Better fishing methods can reduce damage. Using hooks and lines, or traps, instead of large nets can target particular species and reduce bycatch. Turtle excluder devices are grids fitted inside trawl nets that allow turtles to escape. On longline boats, bird-scaring lines, called tori lines, keep seabirds away from baited hooks, and setting lines at night or weighting them so they sink quickly reduces the number of albatrosses and petrels that are caught. South Africa's trawl and longline fisheries have greatly reduced seabird deaths by introducing such measures. Fishing gear can be marked so that it can be recovered if lost, reducing ghost nets.

Fish farming, also called aquaculture, means raising fish, shellfish or seaweed in tanks, ponds or enclosures in the sea. Today, roughly half of the fish and seafood that people eat comes from aquaculture. Farming can reduce pressure on wild stocks and provide jobs. In South Africa, abalone is farmed successfully along the Western Cape coast, and mussels and oysters are farmed in Saldanha Bay. However, aquaculture has its own problems. Farmed fish such as salmon and prawns are often fed with fish meal made from wild fish, so they can still add to pressure on the oceans. Fish farms can pollute water with waste and chemicals, spread diseases and parasites to wild fish, and, in some countries, mangrove forests have been cleared to make prawn ponds. Sustainable aquaculture must manage these risks.

Another approach is to manage the ecosystem as a whole rather than one species at a time. This is called the ecosystem approach to fisheries. It means taking into account the needs of predators such as seabirds and seals when setting catch limits for their prey, protecting important habitats, and reducing bycatch. In South Africa, for example, scientists have experimented with closing areas around African penguin breeding islands to sardine and anchovy fishing, to see whether this helps penguins find enough food.

So the final question is: what can consumers and communities do?

## Subtopic: Consumers, Communities and SASSI, and the Error Museum

Consumers, the people who buy and eat fish, can make a difference by choosing fish from sustainable sources. If consumers refuse to buy over-fished species, the demand for those fish falls, and businesses have an incentive to sell sustainable fish instead.

In South Africa, the WWF South African Sustainable Seafood Initiative, called SASSI, was launched in 2004 to help consumers, restaurants and shops make good choices. It classifies fish into three lists. The green list includes species from healthy, well-managed stocks, which are the best choice. The orange list includes species with some concerns, such as declining stocks or high bycatch, which should be bought with care. The red list includes species that are illegal to sell in South Africa, such as some protected line fish, and it is illegal to buy or sell them. SASSI provides a website and a mobile phone service, where consumers can type in the name of a fish and receive its status. Several major supermarket chains have committed to selling only sustainable seafood.

Certification schemes, such as the Marine Stewardship Council for wild fisheries and the Aquaculture Stewardship Council for farms, give an ecolabel to fisheries and farms that meet sustainability standards, so consumers can identify sustainable products in shops.

Communities also play a vital role. In many parts of the world, including Africa, local communities have managed fishing grounds sustainably for generations through traditional rules. When communities have secure rights to fish and a say in management, they have an incentive to protect the resource for the future. In South Africa, small-scale fishers, many of them from communities that were excluded from fishing rights under apartheid and in the early allocation of rights after 1994, campaigned for recognition, including through court action. The Small-Scale Fisheries Policy of 2012, and later changes to the Marine Living Resources Act, recognised small-scale fishing communities and allowed them to receive collective fishing rights. Implementing the policy has been slow and controversial, but it aims to combine sustainability with fairness.

International cooperation is also needed, because many fish migrate across borders and the high seas belong to no single country. Regional fisheries management organisations set rules for shared stocks such as tuna, and in 2023 United Nations members agreed on a High Seas Treaty to allow protected areas to be created on the high seas.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Sustainable fishing means banning all fishing." It means catching no more than the stock can replace, so that fishing can continue indefinitely.

Exhibit two: "Fish farming always takes pressure off the oceans." Some farmed fish are fed with fish meal from wild fish, and farms can cause pollution and disease.

Exhibit three: "Marine protected areas are useless for fishers." Fish grow and breed inside them and spill over into nearby fishing areas.

Exhibit four: "What I buy cannot affect fish stocks." Consumer choices, guided by SASSI and ecolabels, reduce demand for over-fished species.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a referee at a match, a nursery in the sea, and a traffic light in the fish shop.

## Subtopic: A Referee at a Match

A football match without a referee would be chaos. Players would push, grab and cheat, and the game would fall apart. Fishing is the same. Without rules, everyone grabs as many fish as they can, and the fish run out.

So governments act like referees. First, scientists study the fish and work out how many there are. Then the government sets a limit on how many fish of each kind may be caught each year, and divides that limit among the fishers. When the limit is reached, fishing stops.

There are other rules too. No fishing in the breeding season. No keeping fish that are too small, so they can grow up and have babies first. Nets must have holes big enough for small fish to escape.

Inspectors check the boats and the catches. People who break the rules can be fined or lose their right to fish.

South Africa's hake fishery followed rules like these, and the hake came back. In 2004 it was the first hake fishery in the world to be called sustainable.

So what else helps?

## Subtopic: A Nursery in the Sea

Some parts of the sea are like nurseries. In marine protected areas, fishing is limited or not allowed at all. Fish can grow big and have lots of babies there. Some of those fish then swim out into the open sea, where they can be caught.

South Africa now has forty-one marine protected areas, like Tsitsikamma on the Garden Route.

Fishers can also use smarter gear. Special grids let turtles escape from nets. Streamers on long fishing lines scare seabirds away from the hooks.

Some fish are now grown on farms, like abalone along the Western Cape coast. This can take pressure off wild fish, but fish farms must be careful not to pollute the sea or spread disease.

So what can you do?

## Subtopic: A Traffic Light in the Fish Shop

In South Africa, a system called SASSI works like a traffic light for fish.

Green means go: the fish comes from a healthy stock, so it is a good choice. Orange means think twice: there are some worries about that fish. Red means stop: that fish is protected, and it is illegal to buy or sell it.

You can check a fish on SASSI's website or by sending its name in a text message on a phone. When lots of people choose green, shops and restaurants start selling more sustainable fish.

Fishing communities matter too. When small-scale fishers have fair rights to fish, they have good reason to look after the sea for their children.

Here is the line to remember: sustainable fishing means taking no more fish than the sea can replace, using science, fair rules, protected areas and smart choices by everyone who catches, sells or eats fish.
