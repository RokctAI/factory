### subtopic_1 — How Individuals Put Pressure on Resources
**Assistant:** Why is South Africa's carbon footprint per person higher than that of most African countries?

**Tutor:** South Africa's average carbon footprint per person is much higher than that of most other African countries for several connected reasons. The most important is the way South Africa generates electricity. Most of its electricity comes from coal-fired power stations, and coal produces more carbon dioxide for each unit of electricity than any other major source. Because almost every activity that uses electricity in South Africa, from lighting homes to running factories, mines and trains, is powered largely by coal, the emissions linked to energy use are high. Second, South Africa's economy is more industrialised than most African economies, and much of its industry is energy-intensive. Mining, especially deep gold and platinum mining, uses large amounts of electricity for ventilation, cooling and hoisting. Smelters that process iron, steel, aluminium, chrome and manganese use enormous amounts of energy. Sasol's plants at Secunda, which make fuel and chemicals from coal, are among the largest single sources of greenhouse gas emissions in the world. Third, many South Africans, especially wealthier households, use cars for transport and live in large houses with many appliances, which increases their personal footprints. In many other African countries, most people have little or no access to electricity, use small amounts of energy and rarely travel by car, so average emissions are very low. It is important to remember that averages hide inequality: many poorer South Africans have very small footprints, while wealthier households and large industries account for a large share of the country's emissions. This is why the energy transition, which replaces coal with renewable energy such as solar and wind, is the most important way to reduce South Africa's footprint.

### subtopic_2 — The Carbon Footprint
**Assistant:** Why does the waste hierarchy put recycling last?

**Tutor:** The waste hierarchy ranks ways of dealing with resources and waste from the most effective to the least effective. It usually starts with refusing things you do not need, then reducing the amount you use, then reusing items, repairing them, and finally recycling materials. Recycling is very useful, but it is placed last among these options for several reasons. First, recycling still uses energy, water and other resources. Collecting materials, transporting them, sorting them and processing them into new products all require trucks, machines and electricity. Avoiding the use of a product in the first place saves all of the resources that would have been needed to make it and to recycle it. Second, not everything that could be recycled actually is. Many items are contaminated, mixed with other materials or thrown into general rubbish, and some materials, especially many types of plastic, are difficult or uneconomical to recycle, so much ends up in landfills or the environment. Third, recycling often produces lower-quality materials. Paper fibres become shorter each time they are recycled, and many plastics can be recycled only a few times, a process sometimes called downcycling. Glass and metals such as aluminium can be recycled many times without losing quality, which makes them better candidates. Fourth, focusing only on recycling can make people feel that consumption is not a problem, as long as they put items in the recycling bin. Reducing and reusing tackle the root of the problem. For example, carrying a reusable water bottle avoids buying many plastic bottles, which is better than recycling them. Recycling remains important for materials that cannot be avoided or reused, and it creates jobs, including for informal waste pickers, but it should be the last line of defence, not the first.

### subtopic_3 — Practical Actions for Individuals and Households
**Assistant:** How did Cape Town avoid Day Zero?

**Tutor:** Cape Town experienced a severe drought from 2015 to 2018, with three years of very low winter rainfall in a region that gets most of its rain in winter. The dams that supply the city fell to very low levels, and in January 2018 the city announced that, if water use did not fall, it would reach Day Zero, when most taps would be turned off and residents would have to collect a limited daily ration of water from about two hundred collection points guarded by police. Several actions helped to avoid this. First, residents dramatically reduced their water use. The city set strict limits, eventually asking each person to use no more than fifty litres a day, and households took very short showers, reused grey water for flushing toilets, stopped watering gardens, washed cars with buckets or not at all, and fixed leaks. Total consumption fell to roughly half of what it had been before the drought. Second, the city introduced much higher water tariffs for heavy users, installed water management devices on the properties of very high users, reduced water pressure in pipes to cut leaks, and publicised the water use of different areas, which encouraged people to save. Third, agriculture, which shares the same dams, received less water, as farmers in the region were also restricted. Fourth, the city began emergency projects to find new sources, such as groundwater from aquifers and small desalination plants, although these took time. Finally, good winter rains returned in 2018, helping the dams to refill. The crisis showed both the power of individual action and its limits: residents saved a huge amount of water, but long-term water security also requires government planning, better infrastructure and diverse water sources.

### subtopic_4 — Individuals, Society and Government, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Is it fair to ask poor households to lower their carbon footprint?

**Tutor:** This is an important question of fairness, often called climate justice. Many poor households in South Africa and around the world already have very small carbon footprints. They may not own cars, may use very little electricity, may buy few manufactured goods and may never travel by plane. Some families do not even have reliable access to electricity or clean water. For these households, the priority is often to improve their quality of life, which may mean using more energy and resources, not less, for example by gaining access to electricity for lighting, cooking and studying. It would therefore be unfair to expect them to make the same sacrifices as wealthy households, whose footprints may be many times larger, or as large industries and power companies, which produce most of the country's emissions. At the same time, poor communities are often the most vulnerable to the effects of climate change and resource shortages, such as droughts, floods, heatwaves and rising food prices, because they have fewer resources to protect themselves. So they have a strong interest in seeing emissions reduced. A fair approach would expect the largest reductions from those who emit the most, both within countries and between them, and would help poorer households to gain access to clean, affordable energy, such as solar power, rather than to more polluting sources. Some sustainable actions, such as saving water and electricity, reducing food waste and repairing goods, also save money, so they can benefit poorer households directly. Internationally, poorer countries have argued that wealthy countries, which have produced most of the historical emissions, should take the lead and provide finance for clean development. Fairness, in short, means that everyone has a role, but responsibilities differ.
