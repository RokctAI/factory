### subtopic_1 — The Negotiations: From CODESA to the Interim Constitution
**Assistant:** Why did the negotiations nearly fail several times?

**Tutor:** The negotiations between 1990 and 1994 nearly failed several times because the parties had very different goals, because violence kept undermining trust, and because groups outside the talks tried to wreck them. The first problem was the difference in goals. The National Party government wanted a new constitution, but it hoped to protect the interests of white South Africans and other minorities through measures such as power-sharing, strong regional governments and high majorities for changing the constitution, which would effectively give minorities a veto. The ANC wanted a democracy based on majority rule, with one person, one vote in a single, undivided country, and a Bill of Rights to protect individuals rather than special rights for groups. This disagreement caused CODESA 2 to deadlock in May 1992. The second problem was violence. Each massacre, such as Boipatong in June 1992, made the ANC suspect that the government was using the violence to weaken it, and made it difficult for the ANC's leaders to justify continuing to negotiate to their angry supporters. The government, in turn, accused the ANC of using mass action and armed groups to put pressure on it. The third problem was opposition from outside the talks. Right-wing groups, such as the Conservative Party and the AWB, rejected majority rule, and some were prepared to use violence. The IFP and some homeland leaders wanted more regional power and threatened to boycott the election. Extremists on the left, such as members of APLA, also carried out attacks. In the end, the negotiations succeeded because leaders on both sides, such as Nelson Mandela, F.W. de Klerk, Cyril Ramaphosa and Roelf Meyer, the chief negotiators, decided that the alternative, a long civil war, would be far worse, and they were willing to compromise.

### subtopic_2 — Violence: Boipatong, Bisho and the Assassination of Chris Hani
**Assistant:** What were the sunset clauses, and why were they important?

**Tutor:** The sunset clauses were a set of compromises proposed in 1992 by Joe Slovo, the chairperson of the South African Communist Party and a senior leader of the ANC. Their name suggested that they would apply only for a limited time, until the sun set on them. Slovo proposed that, for a period after the first democratic election, there should be a Government of National Unity in which all parties with a significant share of the vote would be represented in the cabinet, rather than the winning party governing alone. He also proposed that existing civil servants, including members of the police and army, should keep their jobs and pensions, and that there should be an amnesty for political crimes. These proposals were important for several reasons. First, they reassured the National Party and many white South Africans, as well as the security forces, that they would not lose everything immediately if the ANC won the election. This reduced the risk that the security forces or civil service would try to sabotage the transition. Second, they gave the ANC time to learn how to govern a complex modern state, with the help of experienced officials. Third, they helped to break the deadlock in the negotiations, which had collapsed in 1992, and they led to the Record of Understanding and the Multi-Party Negotiating Process. The proposals were controversial within the ANC, because some members believed that they gave away too much to the old regime. However, Slovo, who was known as a radical, had strong credibility with ANC supporters, and his support for compromise helped to persuade them. The Government of National Unity formed in 1994 was based on these ideas.

### subtopic_3 — The Crises of Early 1994
**Assistant:** Why was Mandela's speech after Chris Hani's assassination so important?

**Tutor:** When Chris Hani was assassinated on 10 April 1993, South Africa came closer to a full-scale explosion of violence than at any other time during the transition. Hani was one of the most popular leaders in the country, especially among young people and former Umkhonto we Sizwe soldiers, and his killing by a right-wing extremist, linked to a senior member of the Conservative Party, caused enormous anger. There was a real danger that people would take revenge on white South Africans, that the security forces would respond with force, and that the negotiations would collapse. On the evening of the killing, Nelson Mandela addressed the nation on television and radio. At that time, he was not yet President; De Klerk was still State President. Yet it was Mandela who spoke to the country as a national leader. He expressed grief and anger, but he called for calm and discipline, and he urged people not to allow those who wanted to destroy the negotiations to succeed. He pointed out that a white man had committed the crime, but that a white woman, an Afrikaner, had risked her life to help bring the killer to justice. This showed that the conflict was not simply black against white, but between those who wanted democracy and those who wanted to stop it. The speech did not prevent all violence: there were riots and some deaths in the days that followed. But many historians believe that it helped to prevent a much bigger catastrophe. It also showed that real power was shifting towards Mandela and the ANC. Soon afterwards, the negotiators agreed on the election date of 27 April 1994, which gave people hope that change was coming through the ballot box.

### subtopic_4 — The Election of 1994 and Its Results, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why is the 1994 election seen as such a historic moment?

**Tutor:** The election of April 1994 is seen as one of the most important moments in South African history for many reasons. For the first time, all adult South Africans, regardless of race, could vote in a national election. For more than three hundred years, since the beginning of colonial rule, the majority of people in South Africa had been excluded from political power. Under apartheid, Africans had been denied the vote in Parliament and told that their rights belonged in the homelands. On 27 April 1994, nearly twenty million people voted, many of them elderly people who had waited their whole lives for this day. The long queues of people of all races, standing together peacefully, became a powerful symbol of a new nation. The election was also historic because it marked the end of the long struggle against apartheid, in which many people had been imprisoned, exiled, injured or killed. It brought Nelson Mandela, who had spent twenty-seven years in prison, to the presidency, and it led to a Government of National Unity that included parties that had been bitter enemies. Internationally, the election was celebrated as a miracle, because many people had predicted that South Africa would collapse into civil war. However, historians also point out that the election was not the end of the story. Political violence continued in some areas, especially KwaZulu-Natal, and the new government faced enormous challenges, including poverty, unemployment, inequality, housing and education, many of which remain today. The election created political freedom and equality before the law, but achieving social and economic equality has proved a much longer task. Today, 27 April is celebrated as Freedom Day.
