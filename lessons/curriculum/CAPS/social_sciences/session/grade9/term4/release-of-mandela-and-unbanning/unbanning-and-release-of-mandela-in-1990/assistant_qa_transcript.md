### subtopic_1 — Why De Klerk Unbanned the Liberation Movements
**Assistant:** Why did De Klerk act in February 1990 and not later?

**Tutor:** Historians have asked why De Klerk moved so quickly, within five months of becoming State President, and why he made such sweeping announcements all at once rather than step by step. Several reasons help to explain the timing. First, the pressures on the government had been building for years and had reached a high point by late 1989. The Mass Democratic Movement's defiance campaign showed that the states of emergency had not crushed resistance, and the economy was suffering under sanctions and a shortage of foreign investment. Second, the international situation had changed dramatically. The fall of the Berlin Wall in November 1989 removed one of the government's main arguments, that the ANC was part of a communist threat, and it meant that the ANC could no longer rely on Soviet support. De Klerk later said that the collapse of communism created a window of opportunity. Third, the peaceful transition in Namibia, which held elections in November 1989, showed that a negotiated settlement could work. Fourth, De Klerk believed that it was better to act while the government was still strong, with its army, police and economy intact, so that it could negotiate from a position of strength and protect the interests of its supporters. If he waited, the situation might get worse, and the government might be forced to negotiate on weaker terms. Finally, the secret talks with Mandela had prepared the ground. De Klerk had met Mandela in December 1989 and had formed the view that he was a leader with whom he could negotiate. Making all the announcements at once also had a strategic advantage: it showed the world that the changes were real and irreversible, and it surprised opponents on the right before they could organise resistance.

### subtopic_2 — The Speech of 2 February 1990
**Assistant:** Why did Mandela say the armed struggle would continue in his first speech?

**Tutor:** In his speech from the balcony of Cape Town City Hall on 11 February 1990, Mandela said that the factors that had made the armed struggle necessary still existed, and that the ANC had no option but to continue it, although he also expressed the hope that a climate for a negotiated settlement would soon be created, so that there might no longer be a need for armed struggle. Some people were surprised by this, because they had expected a message of peace and reconciliation. To understand his reasons, we need to think about his position. Mandela was speaking as a loyal member of the ANC, and he wanted to show his supporters that he had not made secret deals with the government in prison, or abandoned the movement's policies. Many activists were suspicious about his talks with the government, and he needed to reassure them. He also knew that apartheid laws were still in force, that black South Africans still had no vote, that political prisoners remained in jail and that the state of emergency had not been fully lifted. If the ANC gave up the armed struggle immediately, it would lose an important bargaining tool in negotiations, before the government had made any concessions on the central issue of political power. So his statement was partly a matter of principle and partly a matter of strategy. In his speech, he also spoke about peace, reconciliation and a democratic, non-racial South Africa, and he called on white South Africans to join in building the future. In August 1990, in the Pretoria Minute, the ANC agreed to suspend the armed struggle, which showed that it was willing to compromise once negotiations had begun in earnest.

### subtopic_3 — The Release of Nelson Mandela on 11 February 1990
**Assistant:** What challenges did returning exiles face?

**Tutor:** The return of exiles after 1990 was a joyful moment for many families, but it also brought many challenges. Thousands of South Africans had left the country, many in the 1960s, many after 1976, and many in the 1980s. Some had lived in exile for thirty years, in countries such as Tanzania, Zambia, Angola, Britain, the Soviet Union and the United States. When they returned, many found that South Africa had changed greatly, and that they had changed too. Some had studied abroad and had qualifications that were not recognised in South Africa, while others had spent years as soldiers in Umkhonto we Sizwe or the PAC's Azanian People's Liberation Army and had few skills for civilian life. Many returned to areas with high unemployment and a shortage of housing, and some struggled to find work and support their families. Some exiles had married people from other countries and had children who did not speak a South African language. Some found that family members had died, or that relationships had been damaged by years of separation. There were also legal and political problems. Exiles needed indemnity, a guarantee that they would not be prosecuted for political offences, and this was negotiated in agreements such as the Pretoria Minute. Some exiles had been accused of being spies or had experienced abuses in the camps, which later became a subject of investigation. The United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees helped to organise the return of thousands of exiles, and organisations were set up to help with reintegration. The experience of exiles shows that the end of apartheid did not immediately solve the personal, social and economic problems that it had created.

### subtopic_4 — Prisoners, Exiles and the First Agreements, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why do some people say 1990 was a turning point and others say 1994 was?

**Tutor:** When historians discuss the end of apartheid, they sometimes argue about which date was the real turning point. Those who say that 1990 was the turning point argue that the unbanning of the liberation movements and the release of Mandela made the end of apartheid almost certain. Once the ANC, PAC and SACP were legal, and once Mandela was free, it would have been very difficult for the government to go back to the old system. The speech of 2 February 1990 was a clear break with the past, because it ended thirty years of bans and accepted the principle that all South Africans should take part in negotiating a new constitution. It also led to the repeal of the main apartheid laws in 1990 and 1991. Those who say that 1994 was the turning point argue that apartheid only really ended when all South Africans could vote and when a democratically elected government took office. Between 1990 and 1994, black South Africans still had no vote, and negotiations nearly broke down several times, amid violence in which thousands died. It was not certain in 1990 that a peaceful settlement would be reached. Both views have strong evidence. A useful way to think about it is that 1990 was the turning point that opened the door to change, while 1994 was the moment when the change was achieved. CAPS describes 1960, 1976 and 1990 as turning points, because each one changed the direction of South African history. In an essay, you should use evidence to support your argument and show that you understand that other interpretations are also possible.
