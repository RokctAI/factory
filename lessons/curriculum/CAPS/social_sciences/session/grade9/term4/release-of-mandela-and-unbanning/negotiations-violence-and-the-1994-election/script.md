# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the negotiations and violence between 1990 and 1994 and the democratic election of 1994. It covers the main stages of the negotiations, from CODESA to the Multi-Party Negotiating Process and the interim constitution; the political violence that threatened the negotiations, including the conflict between the ANC and Inkatha, the massacres at Boipatong and Bisho, and the assassination of Chris Hani; the crises of early 1994, including the threat of a boycott by Inkatha and right-wing groups; and the election of April 1994, its results and the inauguration of Nelson Mandela as President. The anchor idea is that on 27 April 1994 South Africans of all races voted together for the first time, and that the ANC won the election and Nelson Mandela became President. The governing idea is that a negotiated settlement was reached in spite of terrible violence, because leaders on different sides chose compromise rather than war.

## Subtopic: The Negotiations: From CODESA to the Interim Constitution

After the unbanning of the liberation movements in 1990, the government and the ANC first worked to remove obstacles to negotiations, through agreements such as the Groote Schuur Minute and the Pretoria Minute. In September 1991, the National Peace Accord was signed by the government, the ANC, the Inkatha Freedom Party and other organisations, in an attempt to reduce political violence by setting up peace committees and a code of conduct for the police and political parties.

Formal negotiations began on 20 December 1991, at the Convention for a Democratic South Africa, known as CODESA, held at the World Trade Centre in Kempton Park, near Johannesburg. Nineteen parties and organisations took part, including the government, the National Party, the ANC, the South African Communist Party, the Inkatha Freedom Party and representatives of the homelands. Some parties refused to attend: the Conservative Party on the right, which rejected a non-racial democracy, and the PAC and AZAPO on the left, which believed that the talks would favour the government. At CODESA 1, most parties signed a Declaration of Intent, committing themselves to an undivided South Africa with a multi-party democracy, universal adult suffrage and a Bill of Rights.

The government faced a challenge from the right. After the Conservative Party won a by-election in Potchefstroom in February 1992, De Klerk called a referendum of white voters on 17 March 1992, asking whether they supported the continuation of the reform process. About sixty-nine percent voted yes, which gave De Klerk a strong mandate to continue negotiating.

CODESA 2 met in May 1992, but it deadlocked. The main disagreement was about how a new constitution would be adopted and how power would be shared. The government wanted a high percentage of votes, such as seventy-five percent on some matters, to be needed to approve the constitution, and it wanted strong protections for minorities and powerful regions, which would effectively give it a veto. The ANC wanted a lower percentage, so that the majority could decide. The talks broke down, and soon afterwards violence brought them to a halt.

After the crises of 1992, which you will read about in the next section, the government and the ANC signed a Record of Understanding on 26 September 1992, in which they agreed on a democratically elected constitutional assembly and on measures to deal with violence, including the fencing of certain hostels. The SACP leader Joe Slovo proposed what became known as the sunset clauses: for a period of five years after the election, there would be a Government of National Unity in which parties with enough votes would share power, and existing civil servants would keep their jobs. This compromise helped to reassure the government and its supporters. In April 1993, a new round of talks, the Multi-Party Negotiating Process, began at Kempton Park with twenty-six parties, including some that had stayed away from CODESA. In November 1993, it agreed on an interim constitution, which included a Bill of Rights, a Constitutional Court, nine provinces and a set of constitutional principles that the final constitution would have to follow. A Transitional Executive Council was set up to oversee the government in the run-up to the election.

So the next question is: what violence threatened the negotiations?

## Subtopic: Violence: Boipatong, Bisho and the Assassination of Chris Hani

The years between 1990 and 1994 were among the most violent in South Africa's history. According to widely used estimates, about fourteen thousand people died in political violence during this period. Much of the violence took place in Natal and on the Witwatersrand, between supporters of the ANC and supporters of the Inkatha Freedom Party. In the townships of the Witwatersrand, many attacks were linked to hostels, where many Inkatha-supporting migrant workers lived, and there were also attacks on trains, in which gunmen killed commuters. The ANC accused a third force, made up of elements within the security forces, of fuelling the violence to weaken the ANC. In 1991, the government admitted that it had secretly funded Inkatha, in what was called the Inkathagate scandal, and later investigations, including by the Goldstone Commission and the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, found evidence that some members of the security forces had supported attacks. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission also found that ANC supporters had carried out killings, including of Inkatha supporters.

On the night of 17 June 1992, armed men, mainly from the KwaMadala hostel, which housed Inkatha supporters, attacked residents of Boipatong, a township in the Vaal Triangle, and killed about forty-five people, including women and children. Residents accused the police of helping the attackers or failing to stop them. When De Klerk visited Boipatong, angry crowds forced him to leave. The ANC withdrew from the negotiations and launched a campaign of rolling mass action, including strikes, marches and a general strike in August 1992.

On 7 September 1992, the ANC led a march of tens of thousands of people to Bisho, the capital of the Ciskei homeland, to demand the removal of the Ciskei military ruler, Brigadier Oupa Gqozo, and free political activity in the homeland. When some marchers tried to go through a gap in the fence around the stadium, soldiers of the Ciskei Defence Force opened fire, killing twenty-eight marchers and one soldier, and wounding about two hundred people. Boipatong and Bisho showed how close the country was to disaster, and they helped to push both the government and the ANC back to the negotiating table, leading to the Record of Understanding.

The greatest crisis came on 10 April 1993, when Chris Hani, the general secretary of the South African Communist Party and a former chief of staff of Umkhonto we Sizwe, was shot dead outside his home in Dawn Park, Boksburg. He was one of the most popular leaders in the country, especially among young people. The killer was Janusz Waluś, a Polish immigrant, who was linked to Clive Derby-Lewis, a senior member of the Conservative Party; they hoped to provoke a race war that would wreck the negotiations. An Afrikaner neighbour, who saw the killer's car and noted its registration number, helped the police to arrest him quickly. Anger swept the country, and there was a real danger of widespread violence. On the evening of the assassination, Nelson Mandela spoke on national television, calling for calm and pointing out that a white woman had helped to bring the killer to justice. Many people believe that his speech helped to prevent a disaster. In the weeks that followed, the negotiators agreed to set a date for the election: 27 April 1994.

Violence continued. In June 1993, members of the AWB, a right-wing group, drove an armoured vehicle through the glass front of the World Trade Centre during the negotiations. On 25 July 1993, members of the Azanian People's Liberation Army, the armed wing of the PAC, attacked the St James Church in Kenilworth, Cape Town, killing eleven worshippers. In August 1993, Amy Biehl, an American student working for voter education, was killed by a crowd in Gugulethu.

So the next question is: how was the election saved in early 1994?

## Subtopic: The Crises of Early 1994

In early 1994, it seemed possible that the election would be disrupted. The Inkatha Freedom Party, led by Mangosuthu Buthelezi, refused to take part, arguing that the interim constitution did not give enough power to the provinces and did not recognise the position of the Zulu king. The IFP joined the Freedom Alliance with the Conservative Party, some right-wing Afrikaner groups and the leaders of the Bophuthatswana and Ciskei homelands, which also wanted to stay out of the election.

In March 1994, a crisis broke out in Bophuthatswana. Civil servants and residents protested against the homeland leader, Lucas Mangope, who refused to allow his people to vote. Members of right-wing groups, including the AWB, entered Bophuthatswana in an attempt to support Mangope. Several of them were shot dead by members of the Bophuthatswana police, in scenes shown on television. The incident weakened the armed right wing. Mangope was removed from power, and Bophuthatswana was brought back under South African control. Soon afterwards, Ciskei's ruler also stepped down. General Constand Viljoen, a former head of the defence force, who had led the Afrikaner Volksfront, then decided to take part in the election through a new party, the Freedom Front, rather than resist it by force.

On 28 March 1994, thousands of IFP supporters marched through central Johannesburg, and violence broke out. At least nineteen people were killed that day, including several who were shot by ANC security guards outside the ANC headquarters at Shell House. Only one week before the election, on 19 April 1994, after mediation and an agreement that recognised the position of the Zulu monarchy, the IFP agreed to take part. Because the ballot papers had already been printed, a sticker with the IFP's name and logo was added to the bottom of millions of ballot papers.

In the days before the election, right-wing extremists set off bombs in Johannesburg and elsewhere, killing about twenty people. However, these attacks did not stop the election.

So the final question is: what happened in the election of 1994?

## Subtopic: The Election of 1994 and Its Results, and the Error Museum

The election was held from 26 to 29 April 1994. 26 April was set aside for special voters, such as the elderly, sick and disabled, and 27 April was the main voting day, with voting extended in some areas because of problems with ballot papers and voting stations. Nearly twenty million people voted, many of them for the first time in their lives. People of all races stood together in long queues that stretched for kilometres, sometimes waiting for many hours in the sun. Many people described the day as one of the most moving of their lives. The election was organised by the Independent Electoral Commission, chaired by Judge Johann Kriegler. There were many problems, including shortages of ballot papers and allegations of irregularities in some areas, especially in KwaZulu-Natal, but the commission declared that the election was substantially free and fair.

The results gave the ANC 62.65 percent of the vote and 252 of the 400 seats in the National Assembly. The National Party won 20.39 percent and 82 seats, and the Inkatha Freedom Party won 10.54 percent and 43 seats. Smaller parties included the Freedom Front, the Democratic Party, the PAC and the African Christian Democratic Party. The ANC won seven of the nine provinces; the National Party won the Western Cape, and the IFP won KwaZulu-Natal.

Under the interim constitution, a Government of National Unity was formed. Nelson Mandela was elected President by the National Assembly and inaugurated at the Union Buildings in Pretoria on 10 May 1994, in front of leaders from around the world. Thabo Mbeki of the ANC and F.W. de Klerk of the National Party became Deputy Presidents, and the IFP also had ministers in the cabinet. In his inauguration speech, Mandela said: "Never, never and never again shall it be that this beautiful land will again experience the oppression of one by another." Mandela and De Klerk had jointly received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1993 for their work in ending apartheid peacefully.

The elected Parliament then acted as a Constitutional Assembly and drew up the final Constitution, which was approved by the Constitutional Court and signed into law by Mandela at Sharpeville on 10 December 1996. Today, 27 April is a public holiday, Freedom Day.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "The transition to democracy was completely peaceful." The negotiations were peaceful, but about fourteen thousand people died in political violence between 1990 and 1994.

Exhibit two: "Only the ANC and the National Party took part in negotiations." Many parties took part, including the IFP, the SACP and homeland representatives.

Exhibit three: "The ANC won a two-thirds majority in 1994." It won 62.65 percent, below two-thirds, and shared power in a Government of National Unity.

Exhibit four: "The final Constitution was adopted in 1994." The interim constitution governed the 1994 election; the final Constitution was signed in December 1996.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a long table, a country holding its breath and a queue that stretched for kilometres.

## Subtopic: A Long Table

After 1990, leaders from many parties sat around a long table at the World Trade Centre in Kempton Park. They had to agree on rules for a new, democratic South Africa.

It was hard. The government wanted protections for minorities, and the ANC wanted the majority to decide. The talks broke down in 1992. Then the two sides found compromises, such as sharing power for five years. In 1993, they agreed on a temporary constitution with a Bill of Rights.

So why was it so dangerous?

## Subtopic: A Country Holding Its Breath

While leaders talked, violence raged. Supporters of the ANC and Inkatha fought each other, and some police secretly helped the attackers. In Boipatong in 1992, about forty-five people were killed in one night. At Bisho, soldiers shot dead twenty-eight marchers.

Then, on 10 April 1993, a man shot Chris Hani, one of the country's most popular leaders. The whole country held its breath. Nelson Mandela went on television and asked everyone to stay calm. The leaders then set a date for the election.

Right up to the end, there were threats and bombs. Just a week before the vote, Inkatha agreed to take part.

So what happened on election day?

## Subtopic: A Queue That Stretched for Kilometres

On 27 April 1994, people of all races stood together in long queues to vote. Many were voting for the first time in their lives. Some waited all day in the sun, and nobody wanted to go home.

The ANC won about sixty-three percent of the vote. On 10 May 1994, Nelson Mandela became President, with Thabo Mbeki and F.W. de Klerk as his deputies. Today, 27 April is Freedom Day.

Here is the line to remember: between 1990 and 1994, leaders negotiated a new democracy in spite of terrible violence, and on 27 April 1994 South Africans voted together for the first time, making Nelson Mandela President.
