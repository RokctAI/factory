### subtopic_1 — Botha's Reforms, the Tricameral Parliament and the United Democratic Front
**Assistant:** Why did the Tricameral Parliament cause so much resistance?

**Tutor:** The Tricameral Parliament, created by the constitution of 1983, was meant by the government to be a reform that would win the support of coloured and Indian South Africans, but it ended up causing some of the strongest resistance in South African history. There were several reasons. First, it completely excluded Africans, who made up about three-quarters of the population. The government's argument that Africans should exercise their political rights in the homelands or in Black Local Authorities was rejected by most African people, who saw themselves as South Africans with a right to vote in their own country. Second, many coloured and Indian people saw the new Parliament as an attempt to divide them from Africans and to make them junior partners in apartheid. The white House of Assembly was larger than the other two houses combined in the joint bodies, so it could not be outvoted, and the coloured and Indian houses had little real power. Many people therefore argued that taking part would give apartheid a false appearance of legitimacy. Third, the new constitution brought together many different groups in opposition. The campaign against it led to the formation of the United Democratic Front, which united hundreds of organisations across racial lines, and it helped to build a mass movement that went on to challenge apartheid in the townships. Fourth, the new constitution came into effect at the same time as rent increases and economic hardship in the townships, so political anger combined with economic grievances. The low turnout in the elections of 1984 showed how little support the new Parliament had. Ironically, a reform designed to strengthen apartheid became one of the triggers of the uprising that helped to bring it to an end.

### subtopic_2 — Internal Resistance and the Township Uprisings
**Assistant:** How should we understand the violence used by some activists, such as necklacing?

**Tutor:** This is a difficult and painful question, and historians try to answer it honestly, without either excusing or ignoring the violence. During the township uprisings of the mid-1980s, some activists, often young people, attacked those whom they regarded as collaborators with apartheid, such as councillors, police officers and suspected informers. Some were killed by necklacing, in which a tyre filled with petrol was placed around the victim's neck and set alight. People's courts in some townships punished people without fair trials, and some innocent people were accused and killed because of rumours or personal disputes. To understand this, historians look at the context. Many young people had grown up under extreme repression, had seen friends shot, detained and tortured, and had lost faith in legal ways of achieving change. Informers did put activists in danger, and the state used them widely. The breakdown of authority in the townships also created space for violence and for criminals who used political slogans. However, understanding the context does not make the violence acceptable. Many leaders of the UDF, the churches and other organisations, including Archbishop Desmond Tutu, who once intervened to save a man from a mob, condemned necklacing as wrong and harmful to the struggle. It caused deep fear and divisions in communities, and it was used by the government to portray the resistance as criminal and to justify repression. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission later found that such killings were gross violations of human rights, just as it found that the much larger-scale violence of the state was. A balanced understanding of history recognises both the courage of those who resisted and the harm that some actions caused.

### subtopic_3 — Repression: States of Emergency, Detentions and Covert Killings
**Assistant:** Did sanctions help to end apartheid?

**Tutor:** Historians and economists have debated how important sanctions were in ending apartheid, and there are arguments on both sides. Those who argue that sanctions were important point to several effects. When foreign banks refused to roll over South Africa's loans in 1985, the rand collapsed, and the government had to suspend debt repayments, which showed that the economy depended on foreign capital and confidence. Sanctions by the United States, the European Community and the Commonwealth, together with disinvestment by companies such as Barclays and General Motors, reduced investment, made it harder to obtain technology and increased unemployment. Sports and cultural boycotts made many white South Africans feel isolated and question whether apartheid was worth it. Business leaders began to pressure the government to negotiate, and some met the ANC in exile, such as at a meeting in Zambia in 1985. Those who argue that sanctions were less important point out that South Africa found ways around many sanctions, that some important trading partners, such as Britain under Margaret Thatcher, opposed broad sanctions, and that the poor were often the hardest hit by job losses. Some people, including Mangosuthu Buthelezi, opposed sanctions for this reason. They argue that internal resistance, the cost of repression, the end of the Cold War and changes in the region were more important. Most historians today conclude that sanctions were one of several factors that worked together. Internal resistance made the country hard to govern, repression was expensive and did not restore stability, and external pressure, including sanctions, made the costs of continuing apartheid higher. Together, these factors helped to persuade the government that negotiation was the best option.

### subtopic_4 — External Pressure and the Road to Negotiations, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why did the end of the Cold War matter for South Africa?

**Tutor:** The Cold War was the long period of rivalry between the United States and its allies on one side and the Soviet Union and its allies on the other, from the late 1940s until about 1990. It mattered for South Africa in several ways. The National Party government presented itself as a defender of the West against communism, and it argued that the ANC was controlled by communists because the ANC was allied with the South African Communist Party and received weapons and training from the Soviet Union and its allies. This argument helped the government to win some sympathy from Western governments, such as the United States under Ronald Reagan and Britain under Margaret Thatcher, who were reluctant to impose sanctions. Inside South Africa, P.W. Botha's idea of a total onslaught was built on the fear of communism. When the Cold War began to end in the late 1980s, these arguments lost much of their power. The Soviet Union, under Mikhail Gorbachev, was reforming and reducing its support for liberation movements abroad, and it encouraged negotiated solutions to conflicts. The fall of the Berlin Wall in November 1989 and the collapse of communist governments in Eastern Europe meant that the government could no longer claim convincingly that a communist takeover was a real threat. At the same time, the ANC could no longer rely on the same level of Soviet support for an armed struggle. Agreements in 1988 led to the withdrawal of Cuban forces from Angola and to the independence of Namibia. These changes made it easier for F.W. de Klerk to persuade his party and white voters that negotiation was possible, and for the ANC to accept a negotiated settlement.
