# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains, in broad outline, the events of the 1980s that led towards the end of apartheid and the election of 1994. It covers the government's reforms under P.W. Botha, including the Tricameral Parliament, and the response of the United Democratic Front and the trade unions; the internal resistance of the mid-1980s, including the township uprisings, boycotts and the violence that accompanied them; the repression used by the state, including states of emergency, detentions and covert killings; and the external pressure from sanctions, disinvestment and the changing international situation, which helped to bring the government to the negotiating table. The anchor idea is that by the late 1980s, South Africa was in a crisis that neither the government nor the liberation movements could resolve by force alone. The governing idea is that internal resistance, repression and external pressure worked together to convince leaders on both sides that negotiation was necessary.

## Subtopic: Botha's Reforms, the Tricameral Parliament and the United Democratic Front

P.W. Botha became Prime Minister in 1978. He believed that South Africa faced what he called a total onslaught from communists and the liberation movements, supported by hostile countries, and he responded with a total strategy. This strategy combined military power and security with reforms intended to win the support of some groups and to make apartheid more acceptable at home and abroad. As you learnt in the previous lesson, the government recognised black trade unions in 1979 and relaxed some apartheid laws, such as those that banned marriages across racial lines, which were repealed in 1985.

The centrepiece of Botha's reforms was a new constitution, approved by white voters in a referendum in November 1983. It created a Tricameral Parliament with three houses: the House of Assembly for white people, the House of Representatives for coloured people and the House of Delegates for Indian people. Each house dealt with its own affairs, such as education and housing, and general affairs were dealt with jointly, but the white house was the largest and could not be outvoted. The constitution also created the office of an executive State President with wide powers, and Botha became the first executive State President in 1984. Africans were excluded altogether. The government argued that Africans should exercise their political rights in the homelands, or in Black Local Authorities in the townships.

The Tricameral Parliament provoked massive opposition. Many coloured and Indian people rejected it as an attempt to divide them from Africans and to make them junior partners in apartheid. In the elections of August 1984, the turnout was very low: roughly thirty percent of registered coloured voters and about twenty percent of registered Indian voters took part, and far fewer of those eligible had registered at all.

To oppose the new constitution, about five hundred and sixty organisations, including civic associations, student groups, women's groups, churches, sports bodies and trade unions, came together to form the United Democratic Front, the UDF. It was launched on 20 August 1983 at Mitchells Plain, near Cape Town, at a rally attended by more than ten thousand people. The Reverend Allan Boesak gave a famous speech calling for all South Africans to work together for a democratic, non-racial country. The UDF supported the Freedom Charter, and its leaders included people such as Albertina Sisulu, Archie Gumede and Terror Lekota. Although it said it was not the ANC, it shared many of the ANC's ideas, and many of its members saw the ANC as the leading liberation movement. A smaller grouping, the National Forum, also formed in 1983 by Black Consciousness organisations such as AZAPO, opposed the constitution too.

Trade unions also became a powerful force. On 1 December 1985, the Congress of South African Trade Unions, COSATU, was launched in Durban, with about half a million members, and Elijah Barayi as its first president. COSATU combined demands for better wages and working conditions with demands for political rights, and it organised strikes and stay-aways that had a major impact on the economy.

So the next question is: how did resistance develop in the townships?

## Subtopic: Internal Resistance and the Township Uprisings

The township uprisings began in the Vaal Triangle, south of Johannesburg, on 3 September 1984, the day the new constitution came into effect. Residents of Sebokeng, Sharpeville and other townships protested against rent increases imposed by the Black Local Authorities, which many residents regarded as corrupt and illegitimate. Crowds attacked the homes of councillors, and several councillors were killed, including the deputy mayor of Lekoa, Sam Dlamini, who was killed at his home in Sharpeville. Police opened fire on crowds, and dozens of people died. In October 1984, the government sent thousands of soldiers into Sebokeng in a large operation to search houses, a sign that the army was being used inside the country's townships.

Over the next two years, the uprising spread across the country. Students boycotted schools, and many townships refused to pay rent or service charges. In the Eastern Cape, consumer boycotts of white-owned shops put pressure on local businesses, which in turn put pressure on the government. Street committees and civic organisations took over some functions of local government in townships where councillors had resigned. In November 1984, a stay-away in the Transvaal, organised by students and unions, was supported by hundreds of thousands of workers. In 1985, the ANC called on people through its Radio Freedom broadcasts to make the townships ungovernable and apartheid unworkable.

The resistance also involved violence, and it is important to understand this honestly. Some activists attacked people they regarded as collaborators, including councillors, police officers and suspected informers. Some were killed by necklacing, in which a tyre filled with petrol was placed around a person's neck and set alight. People's courts in some townships punished people without fair trials. Many leaders of the UDF and the churches condemned necklacing, and it caused deep divisions in communities. It was also used by the government to argue that the resistance was violent and criminal.

There was also violent conflict between supporters of different organisations. In Natal and later on the Witwatersrand, a bitter conflict developed between supporters of the UDF and the ANC on one side and Inkatha, led by Mangosuthu Buthelezi, on the other. Inkatha was a Zulu cultural and political organisation that opposed apartheid but also opposed sanctions and the armed struggle, and Buthelezi led the KwaZulu homeland government. Thousands of people were killed in this conflict in the late 1980s and early 1990s. Later investigations, including by the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, found evidence that some members of the security forces had secretly supported and trained Inkatha members, and that the UDF and ANC side also carried out killings.

Women played a central role in this resistance. They organised rent boycotts, ran street committees, supported detainees' families and led campaigns against the forced removal of communities. Organisations such as the Federation of Transvaal Women and the United Women's Congress were affiliated to the UDF. Churches also became important: many church leaders, such as Archbishop Desmond Tutu, who won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1984, and the Reverend Frank Chikane, spoke out against apartheid and protected activists, and church buildings were often used for meetings and funerals when other gatherings were banned.

So the next question is: how did the state respond to this resistance?

## Subtopic: Repression: States of Emergency, Detentions and Covert Killings

The government responded to the uprisings with severe repression. On 21 July 1985, Botha declared a state of emergency in thirty-six magisterial districts, mainly in the Eastern Cape and the Witwatersrand. On 12 June 1986, just before the tenth anniversary of the Soweto uprising, a national state of emergency was declared, and it was renewed every year until 1990. Under the states of emergency, the police and army had wide powers to arrest and detain people without trial, to ban meetings and to restrict the media. Television cameras and journalists were often banned from areas of unrest. Tens of thousands of people were detained, including many children, and many were tortured. In 1988, the UDF and many other organisations were restricted from carrying out any activities.

Thousands of people were killed in political violence during these years, many by the police and the army. On 21 March 1985, twenty-fifth anniversary of Sharpeville, police opened fire on a funeral procession at Langa near Uitenhage, killing about twenty people. The army was deployed in townships, and soldiers in armoured vehicles patrolled the streets. Many young white men were conscripted into the army, and some opposed this; the End Conscription Campaign, formed in 1983, campaigned against compulsory military service.

The state also used covert methods. Security police units, such as the unit based at Vlakplaas near Pretoria, and a secret military unit called the Civil Cooperation Bureau, killed and kidnapped activists. In June 1985, four activists from Cradock in the Eastern Cape, including Matthew Goniwe, were abducted and murdered by security police; they became known as the Cradock Four. The government also supported vigilante groups, such as the witdoeke in the Crossroads settlement near Cape Town, who attacked UDF supporters in 1986. Outside the country, South African forces raided neighbouring countries to attack ANC members, as in Maseru in Lesotho in 1982 and Gaborone in Botswana in 1985, killing ANC members and also civilians. Much of this was only proved in the 1990s, through investigations and the Truth and Reconciliation Commission.

Repression weakened the organisations of resistance for a time, but it could not end the uprising. By 1989, resistance was rising again through the Mass Democratic Movement, an alliance of the UDF, COSATU and other groups, which launched a defiance campaign in which people deliberately used whites-only facilities such as hospitals and beaches.

So the final question is: what external pressures helped to bring change?

## Subtopic: External Pressure and the Road to Negotiations, and the Error Museum

International pressure on South Africa grew sharply in the mid-1980s. On 15 August 1985, P.W. Botha gave a speech in Durban that many had expected to announce major reforms, a speech that came to be known as the Rubicon speech. Instead, he refused to make significant concessions and warned the world not to push South Africa too far. The speech had a disastrous effect on confidence. Foreign banks, led by Chase Manhattan Bank of the United States, refused to roll over South Africa's short-term loans, the value of the rand fell sharply, and in September 1985 the government had to suspend repayments on part of its foreign debt.

Many governments imposed sanctions. In October 1986, the United States Congress passed the Comprehensive Anti-Apartheid Act, which banned new investment and many imports from South Africa, overriding a veto by President Ronald Reagan. The European Community and the Commonwealth also imposed measures in 1986, although some governments, such as the British government under Margaret Thatcher, opposed broad sanctions. Many foreign companies, including Barclays Bank, General Motors and IBM, withdrew from South Africa in a process known as disinvestment. Sports and cultural boycotts isolated South Africa further. Campaigns to free Nelson Mandela grew, and a concert at Wembley Stadium in London in June 1988 for his seventieth birthday was broadcast to many countries.

Other changes in the region and the world also mattered. South African forces had been fighting in Angola against Angolan government troops supported by Cuba. After fierce fighting around Cuito Cuanavale in 1987 and 1988, which both sides claimed as a victory, agreements were signed in December 1988 that led to the withdrawal of Cuban troops from Angola and to Namibia's independence in March 1990. The fall of the Berlin Wall in November 1989 and the weakening of the Soviet Union reduced the government's fear of communism and reduced the Soviet support available to the ANC.

Behind the scenes, talks had already begun. From 1985, Nelson Mandela, still in prison, held secret talks with government ministers, and he met P.W. Botha on 5 July 1989. In 1987, a group of mainly Afrikaans-speaking South Africans met ANC leaders in Dakar, Senegal. In January 1989, Botha suffered a stroke, and in August 1989 he resigned and was replaced by F.W. de Klerk, who became State President in September 1989. In October 1989, De Klerk released Walter Sisulu and other senior ANC prisoners. The stage was set for the dramatic announcements of February 1990.

Inside the National Party and white society, opinions were shifting too. Some white South Africans continued to support Botha's hard line, and in 1982 the Conservative Party had been formed by those who believed that he was already giving away too much. Others, including many business leaders, academics, students and church members, increasingly argued that apartheid could not survive and that the government should negotiate with the ANC. By the end of the decade, both the government and the ANC had come to accept that neither side could win outright by force.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "The Tricameral Parliament gave all South Africans the vote." It gave separate houses to white, coloured and Indian voters, but Africans were excluded.

Exhibit two: "Sanctions alone ended apartheid." Sanctions were important, but internal resistance, the costs of repression and changes in the region and world also mattered.

Exhibit three: "All violence in the 1980s came from one side." Most deaths were caused by the security forces, but there was also violence by activists, vigilantes and between organisations.

Exhibit four: "Talks only began after Mandela was released." Mandela held secret talks with the government from 1985 while still in prison.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a parliament with three doors, a township on fire and a bank that said no.

## Subtopic: A Parliament With Three Doors

In 1983, the government built a new Parliament with three doors: one for white people, one for coloured people and one for Indian people. There was no door for the African majority, the biggest group of all.

Most coloured and Indian people refused to walk through their doors, and few voted. Instead, hundreds of groups joined hands and formed the United Democratic Front, the UDF. Workers formed a giant union federation called COSATU.

So what happened in the townships?

## Subtopic: A Township on Fire

In 1984, in the Vaal, people protested against higher rents. The protest spread. Students stayed out of school. People refused to pay rent. Some councillors were attacked, and some suspected informers were killed in terrible ways, which many leaders condemned.

The government sent in soldiers. It declared a state of emergency and locked up tens of thousands of people, including children. Secret police squads killed activists, such as the Cradock Four.

So what did the rest of the world do?

## Subtopic: A Bank That Said No

In 1985, President Botha gave a speech, and everyone hoped he would announce big changes. He did not. So an American bank said no more loans. Other banks followed, and the rand crashed.

Countries stopped trading. Companies packed up and left. Sports teams would not play against South Africa. Millions of people around the world sang "Free Nelson Mandela".

Secretly, Mandela was already talking to the government from prison. In 1989, a new president, F.W. de Klerk, took over.

Here is the line to remember: in the 1980s, mass resistance inside the country, harsh repression and growing pressure from the world pushed South Africa into a crisis that only negotiation could solve.
