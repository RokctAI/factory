# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the unbanning of political organisations and the release of Nelson Mandela and other political prisoners in 1990. It covers the reasons why F.W. de Klerk decided to unban the liberation movements; what he announced in his speech of 2 February 1990; the release of Mandela on 11 February 1990 and the reactions to it; and the release of other political prisoners, the return of exiles and the first agreements between the government and the ANC. The anchor idea is that on 2 February 1990 De Klerk unbanned the ANC, PAC and SACP, and that on 11 February 1990 Mandela was released after twenty-seven years in prison. The governing idea is that these events were a turning point because they ended thirty years of bans and opened the way to negotiations for a democratic South Africa, although they did not by themselves end apartheid.

## Subtopic: Why De Klerk Unbanned the Liberation Movements

F.W. de Klerk became leader of the National Party in February 1989, after P.W. Botha suffered a stroke, and he became State President in September 1989. Before then, De Klerk had been seen as a conservative within the party, and few people expected him to make dramatic changes. Yet within five months of becoming President, he had unbanned the liberation movements and announced the release of Mandela. Historians have identified several reasons for this decision.

The first reason was the crisis inside South Africa. As you learnt in the previous lesson, the uprisings of the 1980s, the boycotts, the strikes and the defiance campaign of the Mass Democratic Movement had made many parts of the country very difficult to govern. The states of emergency had suppressed resistance for a time, but they had not destroyed it, and they were very expensive. Many leaders in government and the security forces had come to believe that the conflict could not be won by force.

The second reason was the economy. Sanctions, disinvestment, the refusal of foreign banks to lend money, and the cost of the war in Angola and of repression at home had weakened the economy. Growth was slow, inflation was high, and unemployment was rising. Business leaders were pressing the government to negotiate, because they believed that only a political settlement would bring back investment.

The third reason was international change. The end of the Cold War, symbolised by the fall of the Berlin Wall in November 1989, meant that the government could no longer convincingly argue that the ANC was part of a communist threat. The Soviet Union was reducing its support for armed struggles. The agreements over Angola and Namibia, which led to Namibia's independence in March 1990, showed that negotiated settlements were possible in the region, and the peaceful transition in Namibia reassured some white South Africans.

The fourth reason was that talks had already begun. Mandela had been holding secret talks with government representatives since the mid-1980s, and he had met P.W. Botha in July 1989 and De Klerk in December 1989. These talks had built some trust and convinced government leaders that Mandela was someone they could negotiate with. In October 1989, De Klerk had released Walter Sisulu and other senior leaders, and their release had not led to the violence that some had feared.

Historians debate De Klerk's motives. Some argue that he was a pragmatist who realised that apartheid could not survive and who hoped that by acting early, while the government was still strong, he could negotiate a settlement that protected the interests of white South Africans, perhaps through power-sharing. Others give him more credit for courage and a genuine change of heart. Most agree that his decision was taken under great pressure, but that he could have chosen to resist for longer, and that his decision made a peaceful transition possible.

There were also limits to what De Klerk intended at this stage. In 1990, the National Party did not accept simple majority rule. It proposed systems of power-sharing and protection for minority groups, such as a rotating presidency or a second house of Parliament in which minorities would have a veto. The ANC, by contrast, demanded one person, one vote in a single, undivided South Africa. These different goals would shape the difficult negotiations of the next four years.

So the next question is: what did De Klerk announce on 2 February 1990?

## Subtopic: The Speech of 2 February 1990

On 2 February 1990, at the opening of Parliament in Cape Town, De Klerk made the announcements that changed South Africa. He announced that the bans on the African National Congress, the Pan Africanist Congress, the South African Communist Party and many other organisations would be lifted immediately. Restrictions on organisations such as the United Democratic Front and COSATU were also lifted. These organisations could now operate legally inside South Africa, open offices, hold meetings and recruit members, for the first time in thirty years in the case of the ANC and PAC.

De Klerk also announced that political prisoners who had not been convicted of violent crimes would be released; that restrictions on the media under the state of emergency would be lifted; that the death penalty would be suspended while it was being reviewed; and that the government intended to release Nelson Mandela unconditionally, soon. He called on all political leaders to join negotiations for a new constitution in which all South Africans would have equal rights. He said that the time for talking had arrived.

The speech caused astonishment in South Africa and around the world. Some members of the National Party had been told only shortly before. The Conservative Party and right-wing groups, such as the Afrikaner Weerstandsbeweging, the AWB, led by Eugène Terre'Blanche, accused De Klerk of betraying white South Africans. Many black South Africans celebrated in the streets, although many also remained cautious, because apartheid laws, such as the Group Areas Act and the Population Registration Act, were still in force, and the state of emergency had not yet been fully lifted. The ANC welcomed the announcements but said that it would continue to press for the complete end of apartheid.

The speech also changed the situation for the liberation movements. For thirty years, the ANC and PAC had been organised mainly as underground and exile movements, with secret structures, military wings and offices abroad. Now they had to become legal political organisations inside the country, with branches, membership cards, newspapers and public leaders. This was a huge task. The ANC held its first legal national conference inside South Africa for more than thirty years in Durban in July 1991, where Mandela was elected president of the organisation and Cyril Ramaphosa, a former leader of the National Union of Mineworkers, became secretary-general.

So the next question is: how did Mandela's release unfold?

## Subtopic: The Release of Nelson Mandela on 11 February 1990

Nelson Mandela had been arrested in August 1962 and sentenced first to five years in prison for leaving the country without a passport and inciting workers to strike, and then, in 1964, at the end of the Rivonia Trial, to life imprisonment for sabotage. He spent eighteen years on Robben Island, then six years at Pollsmoor Prison in Cape Town, from 1982, and then fourteen months in a house at Victor Verster Prison near Paarl, from December 1988. During these years, he became the most famous political prisoner in the world, a symbol of the struggle against apartheid. In 1985, he had rejected an offer from P.W. Botha to release him if he renounced violence, saying that only free men can negotiate.

On 9 February 1990, De Klerk told Mandela that he would be released the next day. Mandela asked for a week's delay so that his family and the ANC could prepare, but De Klerk insisted that the release could not be postponed for long, and they agreed on Sunday 11 February. That afternoon, Mandela walked out of the gate of Victor Verster Prison, holding the hand of his wife, Winnie Madikizela-Mandela, and raising his fist in salute. The moment was broadcast live on television around the world. Thousands of people had gathered outside the prison and along the road.

That evening, Mandela spoke from the balcony of Cape Town City Hall to a huge crowd on the Grand Parade. He said: "I stand here before you not as a prophet but as a humble servant of you, the people." He thanked the people who had struggled for his release, paid tribute to the ANC and its allies, and said that the armed struggle would continue until the reasons for it had been removed, while expressing hope that a climate for a negotiated settlement would soon be created. He also called on white South Africans to join in shaping a new South Africa. Two days later, on 13 February, he addressed a rally of about a hundred thousand people at the FNB Stadium near Soweto.

Reactions to the release were varied. Millions of South Africans celebrated in the streets, and many people wept with joy. Governments around the world welcomed it. Some white South Africans were frightened about the future, while others were relieved that a peaceful solution might be possible. Some critics on both sides were suspicious: right-wing groups feared a black government, while some in the liberation movements, including the PAC, worried that the ANC would make too many compromises in negotiations.

In the weeks after his release, Mandela travelled to Lusaka in Zambia to meet the ANC's leadership in exile, and then visited many countries in Africa, Europe and North America, where he was welcomed by huge crowds and by heads of state. He thanked the countries and people who had supported the struggle, but he also urged them to keep sanctions in place until apartheid had been dismantled completely, a position that caused tension with the South African government.

So the final question is: what happened to other political prisoners and exiles, and what came next?

## Subtopic: Prisoners, Exiles and the First Agreements, and the Error Museum

Other political prisoners were released in stages. Govan Mbeki had been released in November 1987, and Walter Sisulu, Ahmed Kathrada, Raymond Mhlaba, Andrew Mlangeni, Elias Motsoaledi and Wilton Mkwayi, together with the PAC's Jafta Masemola and others, had been released in October 1989. After February 1990, hundreds more political prisoners were released over the following months and years, although there were disagreements about which prisoners counted as political, especially those who had been convicted of violent acts.

Thousands of exiles began to return home. Some had lived abroad for thirty years. Oliver Tambo, the president of the ANC, who had left South Africa in 1960 and who had suffered a stroke in 1989, returned in December 1990. Other leaders, such as Thabo Mbeki and Chris Hani, also returned. Many exiles, including former soldiers of Umkhonto we Sizwe and students who had studied abroad, returned with the help of the United Nations refugee agency, but many found it difficult to find work and housing, and some had been away so long that they hardly knew their own country.

The first formal talks between the government and the ANC took place at Groote Schuur, the official residence in Cape Town, in May 1990. In the Groote Schuur Minute of 4 May 1990, both sides committed themselves to a peaceful process of negotiations and agreed to work towards the release of political prisoners and the return of exiles. In the Pretoria Minute of 6 August 1990, the ANC agreed to suspend the armed struggle, a major concession, and the government agreed to speed up the release of political prisoners and to review security laws. The state of emergency was lifted in most of the country in June 1990, and in Natal in October 1990.

Apartheid laws were also repealed. The Separate Amenities Act was repealed in 1990, which meant that facilities such as beaches, parks and libraries were open to all. In June 1991, the Group Areas Act, the Land Acts and the Population Registration Act were repealed. However, black South Africans still did not have the vote, and the hardest part of the process, negotiating a new constitution, still lay ahead.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Apartheid ended on 2 February 1990." The speech unbanned organisations and announced Mandela's release, but many apartheid laws remained until 1991, and black South Africans could not vote until 1994.

Exhibit two: "Mandela was released without any talks beforehand." He had held secret talks with the government since the mid-1980s and met both Botha and De Klerk.

Exhibit three: "Only the ANC was unbanned." The PAC, the SACP and many other organisations were unbanned at the same time.

Exhibit four: "Everyone welcomed the unbanning." Most people celebrated, but right-wing groups such as the Conservative Party and the AWB strongly opposed it, and some in the liberation movements were suspicious.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a speech that surprised everyone, a walk through a prison gate and a long road home.

## Subtopic: A Speech That Surprised Everyone

By 1989, South Africa was stuck. The townships were in revolt, the economy was weak, other countries had turned away, and the Cold War was ending. A new president, F.W. de Klerk, decided that things had to change.

On 2 February 1990, he stood up in Parliament and said: the ANC, the PAC and the Communist Party are no longer banned. Many political prisoners will be freed. And Nelson Mandela will be released.

People could hardly believe their ears. Some danced in the streets. Some right-wing groups were furious.

So what happened to Mandela?

## Subtopic: A Walk Through a Prison Gate

Nelson Mandela had been in prison for twenty-seven years: on Robben Island, at Pollsmoor and finally at Victor Verster Prison near Paarl.

On Sunday 11 February 1990, he walked out of the prison gate, holding Winnie's hand, with his fist raised high. People around the world watched on television.

That evening, he spoke to a huge crowd from the balcony of Cape Town City Hall. He said he stood before them not as a prophet, but as a humble servant of the people.

So who else came home?

## Subtopic: A Long Road Home

Other leaders were freed too, such as Walter Sisulu. Thousands of exiles came home after many years abroad, including Oliver Tambo, the ANC's president.

The government and the ANC began to talk. The ANC agreed to stop its armed struggle. Laws like the Group Areas Act were scrapped in 1991.

But black South Africans still could not vote. The hardest part, agreeing on a new constitution, was still to come.

Here is the line to remember: in February 1990, De Klerk unbanned the liberation movements and Mandela walked free after twenty-seven years, opening the road to negotiations, but apartheid had not yet ended.
