### subtopic_1 — What Natural Resources Are
**Assistant:** Why does South Africa rely so heavily on coal for electricity?

**Tutor:** South Africa's heavy reliance on coal has historical, geological and economic causes. First, South Africa has very large coal reserves, mainly in Mpumalanga, Limpopo and parts of KwaZulu-Natal and the Free State, and much of this coal lies relatively close to the surface, where it can be mined cheaply by open-cast methods. Second, South Africa has very little oil and, until recently, little known natural gas, and its rivers are too small and irregular for large-scale hydroelectric power, so coal was the obvious local energy source. Third, during the twentieth century, the state electricity utility, Eskom, built a series of very large coal-fired power stations near the coalfields, such as Kendal, Matimba and later Medupi and Kusile, which produced large amounts of relatively cheap electricity for mines, industry and cities. Fourth, during apartheid, South Africa faced international oil sanctions, so the government invested heavily in making fuel from coal through Sasol, to reduce its dependence on imported oil. All this created an economy built around coal, with tens of thousands of jobs in coal mining and power stations, especially in Mpumalanga. However, this reliance now causes serious problems. Coal-fired power stations make South Africa one of the world's larger emitters of greenhouse gases, and the air pollution they produce harms people's health. Many of the older power stations have become unreliable, contributing to load shedding. South Africa is therefore trying to diversify its energy supply by adding solar, wind and gas power, while planning a just transition that protects workers and communities that depend on coal. The challenge is to provide enough reliable, affordable electricity while reducing pollution and emissions.

### subtopic_2 — Non-Renewable Resources
**Assistant:** How can a renewable resource become non-renewable?

**Tutor:** A renewable resource can become effectively non-renewable when people use it faster than natural processes can replace it, so that the stock of the resource falls until it can no longer recover. This applies to conditionally renewable resources, which depend on natural growth or replenishment. Consider groundwater. Rainwater soaks into the ground and slowly refills underground stores of water called aquifers. If people pump water out of an aquifer more slowly than the rain refills it, the resource is renewable. But if they pump faster, especially in dry areas where recharge is slow, the water table falls, boreholes dry up and, in some cases, the aquifer may take thousands of years to refill. Some very old groundwater, sometimes called fossil water, receives almost no recharge at all today. Forests are another example. Trees regrow after logging, but if a forest is cleared completely, the soil may be eroded, the climate may become drier and the forest may never return, as has happened in parts of the world. Fish populations can also collapse if too many adults are caught, because there are too few left to reproduce, and other species may take over their place in the ecosystem, preventing recovery even after fishing stops. Soil, as you learnt in Term 3, forms so slowly that it is effectively non-renewable when it is eroded quickly. In each case, the key idea is the sustainable yield: the amount that can be used each year without reducing the resource for the future. When use stays within the sustainable yield, the resource remains renewable; when use exceeds it for a long time, the resource can be lost. This is why careful management is essential.

### subtopic_3 — Renewable Resources
**Assistant:** If metals can be recycled, why are minerals still called non-renewable?

**Tutor:** Minerals are called non-renewable because the natural deposits from which they are mined, called ore bodies, formed over millions of years through geological processes, and they are not being replaced at any rate that matters to people. Once an ore body has been mined, it is gone. Recycling is very valuable, but it works differently. When a metal such as aluminium, copper, iron or gold is recycled, the metal that was already mined is collected, melted down and reused. This means that the same metal can be used many times, which reduces the need to mine new ore, saves energy and reduces waste and pollution. For example, recycling aluminium uses only a small fraction of the energy needed to make new aluminium from bauxite ore. However, recycling has limits. Not all metal is recovered; some is lost through wear, corrosion, waste that is thrown away, or because it is mixed into products in small amounts that are hard to separate, such as the metals in electronic devices. Some materials lose quality when recycled. The demand for metals is also growing, especially for metals used in batteries, electric vehicles and renewable energy technology, such as lithium, cobalt, copper and platinum group metals, so recycling alone cannot yet meet all the demand. So recycling makes mineral resources last much longer, which is why it is an important part of sustainable resource use, but it does not make the original deposits renewable. South Africa, with its large reserves of platinum group metals used in catalytic converters and potentially in hydrogen fuel cells, is likely to remain an important mining country even as recycling grows.

### subtopic_4 — Using Resources Wisely, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What is a just transition, and why does it matter in Mpumalanga?

**Tutor:** A just transition is the idea that when a country moves away from polluting industries, such as coal mining and coal-fired power stations, towards cleaner energy, the change should be managed in a way that is fair to the workers and communities who depend on those industries. The word just means fair. Mpumalanga is at the centre of this issue in South Africa. Most of the country's coal-fired power stations and many of its coal mines are located on the Mpumalanga Highveld, in towns such as Witbank, now called eMalahleni, which means place of coal, Middelburg, Secunda and Ermelo. Tens of thousands of people work directly in coal mining and power generation, and many more depend on these jobs indirectly, through businesses such as shops, transport and services. Local municipalities also depend on these industries for income. At the same time, the area suffers from some of the worst air pollution in the country, which harms people's health, and coal mining has damaged land and water through acid mine drainage. As older power stations reach the end of their lives and the country adds more renewable energy, some coal mines and power stations will close. Without careful planning, this could leave thousands of people without jobs and towns in decline. A just transition aims to prevent this by retraining workers for new jobs, for example in renewable energy, building, maintenance or rehabilitating mined land; by attracting new industries to the region; by protecting pensions and social support; and by involving workers and communities in decisions. It also means cleaning up pollution left by mining. Balancing the needs of workers, communities, the economy and the environment is difficult, which is why this remains an important debate.
