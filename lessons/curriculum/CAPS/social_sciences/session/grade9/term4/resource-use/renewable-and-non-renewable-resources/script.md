# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains natural resources and the difference between renewable and non-renewable resources. It covers what natural resources are and how people use them; non-renewable resources, especially fossil fuels and minerals, with examples from South Africa; renewable resources, including resources that are always available and resources that can be renewed only if they are used carefully; and the reasons why the way people use resources matters for the future, including South Africa's reliance on coal and the move towards renewable energy. The anchor idea is that a non-renewable resource exists in a fixed amount and is used up when it is used, while a renewable resource can be replaced by natural processes within a human lifetime. The governing idea is that even renewable resources can be destroyed if people use them faster than nature can replace them, so all resources must be used wisely.

## Subtopic: What Natural Resources Are

A natural resource is anything that occurs in nature and that people can use to meet their needs and wants. Natural resources include air, water, soil, plants, animals, minerals, fossil fuels, sunlight and wind. People use them for food, water, shelter, clothing, energy, transport, medicine and the raw materials for manufacturing. Almost everything you use in a day, from the food on your plate to the phone in your pocket, began as natural resources.

Something becomes a resource when people have the knowledge, technology and need to use it. Uranium, for example, was of little value until people learnt how to use it to generate nuclear power. Sunlight has always been available, but large-scale solar power became practical only when solar panels became efficient and cheap enough. In this way, what counts as a resource changes over time.

Natural resources are not evenly spread across the world. Some countries are rich in minerals, others in fertile soil, forests or fish. South Africa is very rich in mineral resources. It has some of the world's largest reserves of platinum group metals, manganese and chromium, as well as large deposits of gold, coal, iron ore, diamonds and other minerals. Mining has been central to South Africa's economy since diamonds were discovered near Kimberley in 1867 and gold on the Witwatersrand in 1886. On the other hand, South Africa is a dry country, with an average rainfall of about 450 millimetres a year, well below the world average, so water is one of its scarcest resources.

Geographers usually divide natural resources into two main groups, according to whether they can be replaced: non-renewable resources and renewable resources. This difference is very important for planning how resources should be used.

Resources can also be grouped in other ways. Biotic resources come from living things, such as forests, crops, livestock and fish, while abiotic resources come from non-living things, such as water, air, minerals and sunlight. Some resources are owned by individuals or companies, such as a farmer's land, while others are shared by everyone, such as the air, the oceans and the atmosphere. Shared resources are especially easy to overuse, because no single person is responsible for looking after them, a problem you will meet again when you study fishing.

So the next question is: what are non-renewable resources?

## Subtopic: Non-Renewable Resources

A non-renewable resource is a resource that exists in a fixed amount, or that forms so slowly that it cannot be replaced within a human lifetime. Once it has been used up, it is gone. The two main groups of non-renewable resources are fossil fuels and minerals.

Fossil fuels are coal, oil and natural gas. They formed from the remains of plants and tiny sea creatures that lived millions of years ago, which were buried under layers of sediment and slowly changed by heat and pressure. South Africa's coal, mined mainly in Mpumalanga and Limpopo, formed from plants that grew in swamps about 250 to 300 million years ago. When fossil fuels are burnt, they release energy, but they cannot be used again, and nature will take millions of years to form more.

South Africa depends heavily on coal. Coal-fired power stations generate around 80 percent of the country's electricity, and the company Sasol makes liquid fuels and chemicals from coal at Secunda. Coal has given South Africa relatively cheap electricity and many jobs, but burning it releases large amounts of carbon dioxide, which contributes to climate change, as well as other pollutants that harm people's health, especially in the Highveld. South Africa has very little oil of its own and imports most of its oil.

Minerals are naturally occurring substances found in rocks, such as gold, platinum, iron ore, copper, diamonds and limestone. Metals can sometimes be recycled, which means the same material can be used again, but the ore deposits themselves are non-renewable. Once a gold mine has extracted all the gold that can be mined profitably, the mine closes. South Africa's gold production has fallen greatly since its peak in 1970, when the country produced about 1 000 tonnes of gold in a single year, because the richest and most accessible deposits have been used up and the remaining gold lies very deep. Some gold mines in South Africa are among the deepest in the world, reaching more than three kilometres below the surface.

Because non-renewable resources will eventually run out, or become too expensive to extract, they must be used carefully. Using them efficiently, recycling metals and developing alternatives are all ways to make them last longer.

Geographers also distinguish between the total amount of a resource that exists and the reserves, which are the part that has been discovered and can be extracted profitably with current technology and prices. Reserves can change: new discoveries or better technology can increase them, while falling prices can make some deposits unprofitable. This is why it is hard to say exactly when a non-renewable resource will run out, but it is certain that, at some point, the reserves will be exhausted or too costly to use.

So the next question is: what are renewable resources?

## Subtopic: Renewable Resources

A renewable resource is a resource that can be replaced by natural processes within a human lifetime, or that is always available. Renewable resources can be divided into two kinds.

The first kind are resources that are always available, sometimes called flow resources or inexhaustible resources, because using them does not reduce the amount available. Sunlight, wind and the energy of tides and waves are examples. No matter how much solar energy people use today, the sun will shine again tomorrow. South Africa has excellent sunshine, with some of the highest levels of solar radiation in the world, especially in the Northern Cape, and strong winds along the coast, especially in the Eastern and Western Cape. Since 2011, the government's Renewable Energy Independent Power Producer Procurement Programme has led to the building of many solar and wind farms, and many households and businesses have installed solar panels on their roofs, especially during the years of load shedding.

The second kind are resources that can renew themselves, but only if they are used carefully. These are sometimes called conditionally renewable resources. They include water, soil, forests, grasslands, fish and other wildlife. Water is renewed by the water cycle; forests regrow after trees are cut; fish reproduce. However, if people use these resources faster than they can be replaced, they can be damaged or even destroyed. If a forest is cut down faster than it can regrow, it disappears. If too many fish are caught, there are not enough adults left to breed, and the population collapses. If soil is eroded faster than it forms, as you learnt in Term 3, it is effectively lost. If groundwater is pumped out faster than rain can refill it, the water table falls, and boreholes dry up. In this sense, conditionally renewable resources can behave like non-renewable resources when they are misused.

The rate at which a renewable resource can be used without reducing the amount available for the future is called its sustainable yield. For example, the sustainable yield of a fishery is the number of fish that can be caught each year while still leaving enough fish to breed and maintain the population. Using resources within their sustainable yield is the key to making them last.

Water is the most important conditionally renewable resource in South Africa. Because rainfall is low and unreliable and many rivers flow only part of the year, the country has built hundreds of dams and large schemes that move water between river basins, such as the Lesotho Highlands Water Project, which sends water from the mountains of Lesotho to Gauteng. In 2018, after three years of severe drought, Cape Town came close to Day Zero, the day when municipal taps would have been turned off. Residents cut their water use dramatically, and the rains eventually returned, but the crisis showed how quickly a renewable resource can become scarce when demand is high and supply falls.

So the final question is: why does the way people use resources matter?

## Subtopic: Using Resources Wisely, and the Error Museum

The demand for natural resources has grown enormously. The world's population has grown from about 2.5 billion in 1950 to more than 8 billion today, and people in wealthier countries and growing cities use far more resources per person than in the past. This puts pressure on both non-renewable and renewable resources.

Using resources has both benefits and costs. Mining coal and minerals creates jobs, income and exports, and provides the materials needed for electricity, buildings, vehicles and technology. In South Africa, mining has built cities such as Johannesburg and Kimberley and still employs hundreds of thousands of people. But resource use can also cause environmental damage, such as air pollution, water pollution from acid mine drainage, the destruction of land and habitats, and climate change. Communities near mines and power stations often bear these costs, while the benefits may go elsewhere. When non-renewable resources run out, towns that depend on them may decline, as has happened in some former gold-mining towns.

For renewable resources, the main danger is overuse. In the next lesson, you will look at over-fishing of the oceans, one of the clearest examples of a renewable resource being used faster than it can recover.

South Africa faces important choices. It needs electricity for its economy and its people, and coal is still the main source. At the same time, the world is trying to reduce carbon emissions to slow climate change, and South Africa has committed to reducing its emissions over time. This shift from fossil fuels to renewable energy is sometimes called the energy transition. It brings opportunities, such as new jobs in solar and wind energy, cleaner air and a more reliable electricity supply, but also challenges, such as the loss of jobs in coal mining towns in Mpumalanga, which must be managed fairly. The idea that the transition should protect workers and communities is called a just transition.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Renewable resources can never run out." Conditionally renewable resources such as fish, forests, soil and groundwater can be destroyed if used faster than they are replaced.

Exhibit two: "Coal is renewable because plants keep growing." Coal takes millions of years to form, so it is non-renewable on a human timescale.

Exhibit three: "Recycling metals makes ore deposits renewable." Recycling reuses metal already mined, but the ore deposits themselves are still finite.

Exhibit four: "South Africa has plenty of every resource." It is rich in minerals and sunshine, but water is scarce and most oil is imported.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a box of matches, a sunny roof, and a fruit tree.

## Subtopic: A Box of Matches

Natural resources are things from nature that people use: water, soil, plants, animals, rocks, metals, coal, oil, sunlight and wind. Your food, your clothes, your house and your phone all started as natural resources, taken from the land, the sea or deep underground.

Some resources are like a box of matches. There are only so many in the box. Each time you strike one, it is gone, and you cannot get it back. When the box is empty, it is empty.

These are non-renewable resources. Coal, oil and gas are fossil fuels made from plants and tiny creatures that lived millions of years ago. Gold, platinum, iron and diamonds are minerals found in rocks. Nature took millions of years to make them, so once we use them up, they are gone for good.

South Africa makes most of its electricity by burning coal. Coal has given the country power and jobs, but it pollutes the air and adds to climate change, and one day the best coal will run out. South Africa's gold mines already have to dig more than three kilometres underground to find gold.

So what about resources that come back?

## Subtopic: A Sunny Roof

Now picture a roof covered in solar panels on a sunny day. The panels turn sunlight into electricity. Tomorrow the sun will shine again, and the day after that. Using sunlight today does not leave less for tomorrow.

Sunlight and wind are resources that never run out, no matter how much we use. They are renewable.

South Africa is one of the sunniest countries in the world, and it has strong winds along its coasts. In recent years, many solar and wind farms have been built, and lots of families, schools and businesses have put solar panels on their roofs.

So are all renewable resources safe forever?

## Subtopic: A Fruit Tree

Picture a fruit tree. Every year it gives you fruit. If you pick the fruit and look after the tree, it will feed you for many years. But if you cut the tree down for firewood, there will be no more fruit.

Fish, forests, soil and water are like the fruit tree. They can renew themselves, but only if we do not take too much. Catch too many fish, and there are not enough left to have babies. Cut down forests too fast, and they disappear. Wash away soil, and it takes hundreds of years to come back.

Water is like this too. In 2018, after years of drought, Cape Town nearly ran out of tap water, and everyone had to save every drop.

So every resource needs care. We should use coal and metals carefully, recycle what we can, use more sunlight and wind, and never take more fish, trees or water than nature can replace.

Here is the line to remember: non-renewable resources like coal and gold are used up for good, renewable resources like sunlight never run out, and resources like fish, forests and soil stay renewable only if we use them wisely.
