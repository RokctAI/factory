# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains over-fishing of the oceans as an example of the unwise use of a renewable resource. It covers the importance of the oceans and fish as a resource for food, jobs and trade, including South Africa's fishing industry; what over-fishing is and why it happens, including modern fishing technology, rising demand and the problem of shared resources; the effects of over-fishing on fish populations, marine ecosystems and people, including the collapse of the Newfoundland cod fishery; and South African examples of over-fishing, such as West Coast rock lobster, abalone and line fish. The anchor idea is that fish are a renewable resource only as long as enough adult fish are left in the sea to breed and replace those that are caught. The governing idea is that when fishing removes fish faster than they can reproduce, populations collapse, ecosystems are damaged and the people who depend on fishing lose their livelihoods.

## Subtopic: The Oceans and Fish as a Resource

Oceans cover about 71 percent of the earth's surface. They provide food, jobs, transport routes, minerals, energy and recreation, and they help to regulate the world's climate. Fish and other seafood are among the most important resources the oceans provide. Billions of people rely on fish as an important source of protein, and in many coastal communities in developing countries, fish is the main source of animal protein. Millions of people around the world work in fishing, fish processing and related industries, and many of them are small-scale fishers using small boats and simple gear.

Fish are not spread evenly across the oceans. The richest fishing grounds are found over continental shelves, the shallow areas of sea floor around the continents, and in areas of upwelling, where cold, nutrient-rich water rises from the deep ocean to the surface. These nutrients feed tiny drifting plants called phytoplankton, which are eaten by tiny animals called zooplankton, which in turn feed small fish such as anchovies and sardines, which are eaten by larger fish, seabirds, seals and whales. This chain of feeding relationships is called a food chain, and many linked food chains form a food web.

South Africa has a long coastline of about 3 000 kilometres, washed by two very different ocean currents. Along the west coast flows the cold Benguela Current, which brings strong upwelling and makes the west coast one of the most productive fishing areas in the world. Along the east coast flows the warm Agulhas Current, which supports a greater variety of fish species but fewer fish in total. South Africa's commercial fishing industry is based mainly on the west and south coasts. Its most valuable catch is hake, caught mainly by trawlers, and it also catches large quantities of small pelagic fish, such as sardines, also called pilchards, and anchovies, which are used for canned fish, fish meal and fish oil. Other catches include snoek, tuna, squid and West Coast rock lobster. The industry employs tens of thousands of people, many of them in small coastal towns on the West Coast and in the Western and Eastern Cape, where few other jobs exist. Small-scale fishers in communities along the coast have fished for generations, and many depend on fishing for food and income.

Fish are a renewable resource, because fish reproduce and their populations can grow again. A fish population, called a fish stock, can provide a catch year after year, as long as enough adults are left to breed.

So the next question is: what is over-fishing, and why does it happen?

## Subtopic: What Over-Fishing Is and Why It Happens

Over-fishing happens when fish are caught faster than the population can replace them through reproduction and growth. If too many adult fish are caught, there are not enough left to breed, so each year fewer young fish join the population. If too many young fish are caught before they have had the chance to breed even once, the same problem occurs. The fish stock shrinks, and eventually it may collapse, meaning that it falls to such low levels that fishing is no longer worthwhile and the population may not recover.

According to the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, or FAO, the proportion of the world's assessed fish stocks that are over-fished has risen from about 10 percent in 1974 to more than a third today. Many of the remaining stocks are fished at the maximum sustainable level, with little room for any increase.

There are several reasons for over-fishing. The first is rising demand. The world's population has grown rapidly, and people are eating more fish per person than ever before, partly because fish is seen as a healthy food. Fish is also used to make fish meal to feed farmed fish, chickens and pigs.

The second is modern fishing technology. Large factory ships can stay at sea for months, catching, processing and freezing enormous quantities of fish on board. Sonar and satellite technology allow fishing fleets to find shoals of fish easily. Huge nets, such as trawl nets dragged along the sea floor and purse seine nets that encircle whole shoals, and longlines carrying thousands of baited hooks, can catch vast numbers of fish quickly. Fish have far fewer places to hide.

The third is that the oceans are a shared resource. Beyond about 370 kilometres, or 200 nautical miles, from a country's coast, the high seas belong to no country, and even within a country's waters, many fishers share the same stocks. Each fisher has an incentive to catch as much as possible before others do, because any fish they leave behind may simply be caught by someone else. When everyone acts this way, the shared resource is overused. This is sometimes called the tragedy of the commons.

The fourth is weak management and illegal fishing. Some governments set catch limits that are too high, often under pressure from fishing industries and communities that depend on jobs. Others lack the boats and money to patrol their waters. Illegal, unreported and unregulated fishing, often by foreign fleets, takes large catches, especially off the coasts of West Africa and other developing regions. Government subsidies, such as cheap fuel for fishing fleets, can also keep too many boats fishing even when stocks are falling.

So the next question is: what are the effects of over-fishing?

## Subtopic: Effects of Over-Fishing and the Newfoundland Cod

The first effect is on the fish themselves. Over-fished stocks shrink, and the largest, oldest fish, which produce the most eggs, are usually caught first, so the population becomes dominated by smaller, younger fish. Some species, such as certain sharks, tunas and groupers, which grow slowly and breed late, are especially vulnerable.

The second effect is on marine ecosystems. Because species are linked in food webs, removing large numbers of one species affects many others. When predators such as large fish and sharks are over-fished, the animals they eat may increase, upsetting the balance of the ecosystem. When small fish such as sardines and anchovies are over-fished, the seabirds, seals, penguins and larger fish that eat them go hungry. In South Africa, the decline of sardine and anchovy off the west coast is one of the reasons the African penguin population has fallen dramatically, so that it was classified as critically endangered in 2024. Fishing also causes bycatch, which is the accidental catching of species that are not being targeted, such as dolphins, turtles, seabirds and sharks, many of which die. Bottom trawling can damage the sea floor and its slow-growing corals and sponges. Lost or abandoned fishing nets, called ghost nets, continue to trap and kill marine life for years.

The third effect is on people. When fish stocks collapse, fishing communities lose their jobs, incomes and food, and processing factories and related businesses close. Coastal towns may decline as young people leave.

The most famous example is the collapse of the Atlantic cod fishery off Newfoundland in Canada. For centuries, the Grand Banks were one of the richest fishing grounds on earth. From the 1950s, large factory trawlers from many countries began fishing there, and catches rose to record levels in the 1960s. Even after Canada took control of the fishing grounds within 200 nautical miles of its coast in 1977, catch limits were set too high, and scientists' warnings were not acted on in time. By the early 1990s, the cod population had fallen to a tiny fraction of its former size. In July 1992, the Canadian government announced a moratorium, which is a complete ban on fishing for northern cod. About 30 000 to 40 000 people lost their jobs, in what was described as the largest single layoff in Canadian history. The ban was expected to last two years, but the cod took decades to show even partial recovery, partly because the ecosystem had changed.

So the final question is: has over-fishing affected South Africa?

## Subtopic: South African Examples, and the Error Museum

South Africa has experienced over-fishing of several important species.

The West Coast rock lobster, often called crayfish, is found in the cold waters of the west coast. It has been fished commercially for more than a century and is very valuable, especially for export. Over-fishing, poaching and changes in the environment, including low-oxygen events that cause lobsters to walk out of the water onto beaches, have reduced the population to a very small fraction of its original size. Scientists have repeatedly warned that the stock is severely depleted, and fishing limits have been cut, causing hardship for small-scale fishers who depend on it.

Abalone, known in South Africa as perlemoen, is a large sea snail that lives on rocks in shallow water along the south-western coast. It grows slowly and is highly valued in East Asian markets, especially in Hong Kong and mainland China, where it is regarded as a luxury food. From the 1990s, organised crime syndicates became involved in large-scale poaching of abalone, paying divers in poor coastal communities to collect it illegally and smuggling it out of the country, sometimes in exchange for drugs. The commercial abalone fishery was closed in 2008 because stocks had collapsed, although a smaller fishery was later reopened. Poaching has continued, and millions of abalone are estimated to be taken illegally every year.

Many line fish, which are caught with hooks and lines from boats or from the shore, have also declined. Species such as red steenbras, silver kob and seventy-four, a type of seabream, were heavily over-fished in the twentieth century; the seventy-four population collapsed, and catching it was banned. In 2000, the government declared an emergency in the line fishery and greatly reduced the number of commercial line-fishing boats.

Not all South African fisheries are in trouble. The hake trawl fishery, which was heavily fished by foreign fleets until South Africa took control of its waters, was later managed more carefully, and in 2004 it became the first hake fishery in the world to be certified as sustainable by the Marine Stewardship Council. You will learn more about sustainable fishing in the next lesson.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "The ocean is so big that it can never run out of fish." More than a third of the world's assessed fish stocks are over-fished, and some have collapsed.

Exhibit two: "If fishing stops, fish always recover quickly." The Newfoundland cod took decades to show even partial recovery after fishing stopped in 1992.

Exhibit three: "Over-fishing only affects the species being caught." It affects whole food webs, including seabirds, seals and penguins, and bycatch kills many other species.

Exhibit four: "Poaching abalone is a small problem." Large-scale organised poaching helped collapse abalone stocks and led to the closure of the fishery in 2008.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a bank account in the sea, a net with no holes, and an empty basket.

## Subtopic: A Bank Account in the Sea

Imagine you have money in a bank account that earns interest every year. If you spend only the interest, your money lasts forever. But if you start spending the money itself, the account shrinks each year, the interest gets smaller, and one day the account is empty.

Fish in the sea are like that bank account. The adult fish are the savings. The baby fish they produce each year are the interest. If fishers catch only about as many fish as are born each year, the fish will last forever. But if they catch too many adults, fewer babies are born, and the fish start to run out.

South Africa has rich seas, especially on the west coast, where a cold current brings up food from the deep ocean. Boats catch hake, sardines, snoek, squid and crayfish. Thousands of families in small coastal towns depend on fishing for food and jobs.

Fishers who take only what the sea can replace will have fish to catch next year, and their children will too.

So why do people take too many fish?

## Subtopic: A Net With No Holes

Today's fishing boats are like floating factories. They use sonar to find fish, huge nets to scoop up whole shoals, and freezers to keep the catch for months. Fish have almost nowhere left to hide.

More and more people want to eat fish, so fishing boats keep going further and catching more every year.

The sea also belongs to everyone, and that causes a problem. If you leave some fish behind to breed, another boat might just catch them. So everyone grabs as much as they can, and soon there is not enough for anybody.

The big nets also catch dolphins, turtles, sharks and seabirds by accident, and many of them die.

So what happens when the fish run out?

## Subtopic: An Empty Basket

Off the coast of Canada, there were once so many cod that sailors joked you could scoop them up in a basket. But big factory ships caught too many. In 1992, the cod were almost gone, the fishing was stopped, and tens of thousands of people lost their jobs. Thirty years later, the cod have still not fully come back.

South Africa has its own warnings. Crayfish on the west coast have become rare. Criminal gangs have stolen so much perlemoen, a sea snail sold for high prices overseas, that legal fishing for it was closed in 2008. Some fish that anglers used to catch easily, like the seventy-four, almost disappeared.

When small fish like sardines run short, penguins and seabirds go hungry too. South Africa's African penguins are now in serious danger.

Here is the line to remember: fish are a renewable resource only if we leave enough adults to breed, and over-fishing empties the sea, harms whole ecosystems and destroys the jobs of fishing communities.
