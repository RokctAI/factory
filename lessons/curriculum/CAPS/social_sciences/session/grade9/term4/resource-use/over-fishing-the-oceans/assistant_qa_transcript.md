### subtopic_1 — The Oceans and Fish as a Resource
**Assistant:** Why do upwelling areas like South Africa's west coast have so many fish?

**Tutor:** Upwelling areas are among the most productive parts of the ocean because of the way nutrients move through the water. In most of the open ocean, the sunlit surface layer, where tiny plants called phytoplankton can grow, is poor in nutrients such as nitrates and phosphates. These nutrients are found mainly in deeper, colder water, where dead organisms sink and decompose, but there is not enough light there for plants to grow. In an upwelling area, winds and the rotation of the earth push surface water away from the coast, and cold, nutrient-rich water rises from the deep to replace it. Off South Africa's west coast, the south-easterly winds that blow mainly in summer drive the surface water of the Benguela Current offshore, causing strong upwelling. When the nutrient-rich water reaches the sunlit surface, phytoplankton grow very rapidly, just as crops grow well when fertiliser is added. The phytoplankton are eaten by zooplankton, and these feed huge shoals of small fish such as sardines and anchovies, which in turn feed larger fish such as hake and snoek, as well as seabirds, Cape fur seals, African penguins, dolphins and whales. This is why the Benguela upwelling system supports some of the largest fish catches in Africa. Upwelling areas cover only a tiny percentage of the ocean surface, but they produce a large share of the world's fish catch. However, upwelling systems also vary from year to year. Changes in winds and ocean temperatures can cause the numbers of small fish to rise and fall sharply, and occasionally, when large amounts of plankton die and decompose, oxygen levels in the water fall, causing events in which lobsters and fish die or crawl out onto beaches. This natural variability makes careful management even more important.

### subtopic_2 — What Over-Fishing Is and Why It Happens
**Assistant:** What is the tragedy of the commons, and how does it apply to fishing?

**Tutor:** The tragedy of the commons is an idea that describes what can happen when a resource is shared by many people and no one owns or controls it. The term was popularised by the ecologist Garrett Hardin in 1968, using the example of a common pasture shared by many herders. Each herder gains the full benefit of adding one more animal to the pasture, while the cost of overgrazing is shared among all the herders. So each herder has an incentive to add more animals, and if everyone does this, the pasture is destroyed and everyone loses. The oceans, and especially the high seas beyond national boundaries, are a classic example of a commons. If one fishing company decides to catch fewer fish to allow the stock to recover, it loses income, while other companies may simply catch the fish it left behind. So, without rules, each fisher has an incentive to catch as many fish as possible, as quickly as possible, which leads to over-fishing even though everyone would be better off in the long run if the stock were protected. Many fisheries have suffered from this problem. However, later researchers, especially the economist Elinor Ostrom, who won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2009, showed that the tragedy is not inevitable. Communities can manage shared resources successfully when they create clear rules about who may use the resource and how much, monitor each other, punish rule-breakers and take part in decision-making. Governments can also help by setting catch limits, issuing fishing rights or quotas, and creating protected areas. In the next lesson, you will see how these ideas are used in sustainable fishing.

### subtopic_3 — Effects of Over-Fishing and the Newfoundland Cod
**Assistant:** Why did the Newfoundland cod not recover quickly after fishing stopped?

**Tutor:** When the Canadian government banned cod fishing off Newfoundland in 1992, many people expected the cod to recover within a few years. Instead, recovery was extremely slow, and the population remained very low for many years, only beginning to show signs of partial recovery in the 2000s and 2010s. Scientists have suggested several reasons. First, the population had been reduced to a tiny fraction of its former size, so there were very few adult fish left to breed. Cod do not reach maturity until they are several years old, so even under ideal conditions it takes years for a population to rebuild. Second, the largest, oldest fish, which produce the most eggs and the healthiest young, had been caught first, so the remaining population was made up of smaller, younger fish. Third, the ecosystem had changed. With cod gone, other species that cod used to eat, such as shrimp and crab, increased, and some species that compete with young cod or eat cod eggs and young fish became more common. In effect, the cod had lost its place at the top of the food web, and the new balance made it harder for cod to return. Fourth, changes in ocean temperature and in the availability of food, especially a small fish called capelin, which cod eat, may also have slowed recovery. Finally, some cod continued to be caught as bycatch in other fisheries. The Newfoundland story is now used around the world as a warning that a fish stock can be pushed past a tipping point, after which it may not recover quickly even if all fishing stops. That is why scientists stress the importance of acting early, before a collapse happens.

### subtopic_4 — South African Examples, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why do poor coastal communities sometimes become involved in abalone poaching?

**Tutor:** Abalone poaching in South Africa is a complex problem that cannot be understood without considering both crime and poverty. Many coastal communities along the south-western coast, such as Hangberg in Hout Bay, Gansbaai, Hawston and others, have high levels of unemployment and poverty. Many residents have fished for generations, but under apartheid, and in the early years after it, many small-scale fishers felt that they were excluded from legal fishing rights, which were often given to large companies or a limited number of rights holders. Some believed that the system was unfair and that they had a historical right to harvest marine resources. At the same time, abalone is extremely valuable in East Asian markets, where dried abalone is a luxury food and a symbol of wealth, so international crime syndicates can pay divers more for a single night's poaching than they might earn in weeks of legal work. These syndicates organise the poaching, transport, drying and smuggling, and they have sometimes paid with drugs, which has contributed to drug addiction and gang violence in coastal communities. For some young people, poaching appears to be the only way to earn a living. The consequences are serious. Abalone stocks have been severely depleted, legal fishers have lost their livelihoods, and communities have suffered from crime and violence. Solving the problem requires more than policing. It also requires fairer allocation of fishing rights, which the Small-Scale Fisheries Policy of 2012 aimed to address, creating other job opportunities in coastal towns, combating international smuggling networks and reducing demand in importing countries. Abalone farming, which has grown in South Africa, also supplies the market legally without taking wild abalone.
