### subtopic_1 — The Uprising Spreads Across the Witwatersrand and the Universities
**Assistant:** Why did the uprising spread so quickly to other parts of the country?

**Tutor:** The uprising spread quickly for several connected reasons. The first and most important reason is that the grievances that caused the Soweto protest were shared by young people throughout the country. Every black school in South Africa was part of the Bantu Education system, with its low spending, overcrowded classrooms and under-qualified teachers, and every township was subject to the pass laws, influx control and a lack of political rights. When students in Soweto stood up, students elsewhere recognised their own situation in the story. The second reason is the shock of the shootings. News of children being shot, and especially the photograph of Hector Pieterson, created a wave of anger and grief that moved many young people to act, often in solidarity with the victims. Third, there were networks that helped to spread the protest. The South African Students' Movement had branches in schools in different areas, and the South African Students' Organisation had members at universities. Black Consciousness ideas had created a shared language of pride and resistance among young people of different backgrounds. Fourth, the way the government responded also helped the protest to spread. Each time the police shot protesters, funerals became political gatherings, and new clashes followed. This created a cycle in which repression increased anger rather than ending it. Finally, the media, including newspapers such as The World and international radio and television, spread news of events quickly. It is important to notice that the spread was uneven: in some places protests were intense and long-lasting, while in others they were brief or did not happen at all, depending on local conditions, organisation and the strength of the police presence.

### subtopic_2 — Cape Town and the Rest of the Country
**Assistant:** Why was the participation of coloured students in Cape Town significant?

**Tutor:** The participation of coloured students in the uprising in Cape Town was significant for several reasons. Apartheid classified South Africans into separate race groups and treated them differently. Coloured people had more rights than Africans in some ways: for example, they were not subject to the pass laws, and under the Coloured Labour Preference Policy they were given preference for jobs in the Western Cape. The government hoped that these differences would make coloured people see themselves as separate from Africans, and perhaps closer to white South Africans. However, coloured people were also oppressed: they had lost their vote on the common voters' roll in the 1950s, they had been forcibly removed from areas such as District Six under the Group Areas Act, and their children attended segregated, under-resourced schools. In 1976, many coloured students rejected the idea that they were separate from Africans. Influenced by Black Consciousness, which defined black as including all people oppressed by apartheid, they saw the struggle as a common one. Students from schools in Bonteheuwel, Athlone, Manenberg and elsewhere, and from the University of the Western Cape, boycotted classes and marched in solidarity. Some were shot and killed by the police. Their participation showed that the government's strategy of dividing people according to race was not working, and it laid the foundation for the non-racial movements of the 1980s, such as the United Democratic Front, which was launched in Mitchells Plain, a coloured area of Cape Town, in 1983. It is also important to say that not all coloured people supported the uprising, just as not all Africans did, and that communities were divided by class, age and political views.

### subtopic_3 — Stay-Aways and Community Campaigns
**Assistant:** What happened at the Mzimhlophe hostel, and what does it teach us?

**Tutor:** In August 1976, during the second stay-away called by the Soweto Students' Representative Council, violent clashes broke out in Soweto between students and some residents of the Mzimhlophe hostel. Hostels were large buildings where migrant workers, mainly men from rural areas and homelands, lived without their families while they worked in the cities. Many hostel dwellers had little contact with township residents, and their lives were very different from those of township families. Some hostel dwellers resented the stay-away, because they could not afford to lose their wages, and they felt that students who were too young to work were telling them what to do. Some students had used force to stop people from going to work, which increased the anger. There is evidence, including statements by witnesses, that the police encouraged hostel dwellers to attack students and residents, and that the police did little to stop the attacks. Many people were killed and injured. This event teaches several lessons. First, it shows that communities were not united, and that there were real differences of interest and experience between groups, such as students and migrant workers, and between township residents and hostel dwellers. Second, it shows that the government was willing to exploit these divisions to weaken resistance, a pattern that would be repeated in the 1980s and early 1990s. Third, it shows that student leaders learnt from their mistakes: the SSRC met with hostel dwellers to explain its aims, and the third stay-away in September was much more successful. Historians use such events to show that history is rarely a simple story of two sides.

### subtopic_4 — Repression, the Death Toll and Divided Responses, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why is it difficult to know exactly how many people died in the uprising?

**Tutor:** Historians face several problems when they try to establish how many people died in the uprising of 1976 and 1977. The most important official source is the report of the Cillié Commission, which the government set up to investigate the unrest. It reported that 575 people had died between June 1976 and February 1977, and that about 3 900 people had been injured, with most of the dead killed by the police. However, many people have questioned whether this figure is complete. The commission was appointed by the government, and some people did not trust it or were afraid to give evidence to it. Some families did not report deaths, because they feared that they would be targeted by the police, and some people who were injured avoided hospitals for the same reason. Some bodies may have been buried without official records. On the other hand, some unofficial estimates may have been exaggerated, because reports were based on rumours in a time of great fear. Historians try to deal with this uncertainty by comparing several kinds of sources, such as official records, hospital and mortuary records, newspaper reports, funeral records and the testimony of survivors and families. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission, which collected evidence in the 1990s, also heard testimony about deaths during the uprising. Because of these problems, historians usually say that at least 575 people died according to the official figure, and that the real total may have been higher. It is important to remember that each number represents a person with a name, a family and a future that was taken away.
