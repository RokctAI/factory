### subtopic_1 — Long-Term Causes: Bantu Education and Life Under Apartheid
**Assistant:** If Afrikaans was only the spark, why did it matter so much?

**Tutor:** It is true that the deeper causes of the Soweto uprising were Bantu Education and the whole system of apartheid, but the Afrikaans Medium Decree mattered because it turned a general, long-standing anger into a specific, urgent grievance that every pupil could feel in the classroom every day. Think about what it meant in practice. A pupil in Standard 5 who spoke isiZulu or Sesotho at home, and who was just starting to understand mathematics in English, was now told to learn mathematics and social studies in Afrikaans, a language that many of the teachers themselves could not speak well. This threatened the pupil's chances of passing examinations, getting into high school and eventually finding a job. So the decree was an educational disaster before it was anything else. The decree also carried a strong political message. Afrikaans was the home language of many South Africans of all backgrounds, including many coloured people, and it is today one of South Africa's official languages, but in the 1970s, to many black pupils, it was associated with the government, the police, pass officials and the people who enforced apartheid. Being forced to learn in it felt like being forced to accept the authority of the system that oppressed them. The government's refusal to listen to school boards, principals and parents who objected also showed that black communities had no say over their own children's education. That is why a language policy could become a turning point: it was a concrete example of everything that was wrong with apartheid, and it affected thousands of young people at once, in the same place, at the same time.

### subtopic_2 — The Immediate Cause: The Afrikaans Medium Decree
**Assistant:** What was Black Consciousness, and how did it influence the students?

**Tutor:** Black Consciousness was a movement and a set of ideas that developed in South Africa in the late 1960s and 1970s, mainly among black university students. Its best-known leader was Steve Biko, a medical student who helped to found the South African Students' Organisation, SASO, which was launched in 1969. Black Consciousness argued that the first step towards liberation was a change in the minds of black people themselves. Centuries of colonialism and racism, it said, had taught many black people to feel inferior and to depend on others. Black people therefore needed to take pride in their identity, culture and history, to reject the idea that white was better, and to lead their own struggle. In Black Consciousness, the word black included Africans, coloured people and Indians, all of whom were oppressed by apartheid. The movement was critical of white liberals, not because it hated white people, but because it believed that black people had to speak and act for themselves. These ideas spread to high school students through the South African Students' Movement, through young teachers who had been at university, and through publications, speeches and cultural events. They gave the generation of 1976 a strong sense of confidence and a belief that they could challenge the authorities directly, without waiting for their parents or for older organisations. Many students who marched on 16 June had never belonged to the ANC or PAC, which had been banned since 1960. However, Black Consciousness gave them a language of pride and resistance. Historians generally agree that the movement did not plan every detail of the uprising, but its ideas helped to create the mood that made the uprising possible.

### subtopic_3 — Black Consciousness and the Student Leaders
**Assistant:** Why do accounts of what happened at the start of the shooting differ?

**Tutor:** When historians study the events of 16 June 1976, they find that different sources give different accounts of exactly how the shooting began, and this is common in history when events are fast, frightening and politically sensitive. The police version, given to the Cillié Commission of inquiry that the government set up, was that the students were hostile, that they threw stones at the police before any shots were fired, and that the police fired in self-defence when they were threatened by a large crowd. Many students and other witnesses gave a different account. They said that the march was peaceful, that the students were singing, that a police dog was released or tear gas was fired into the crowd, and that some stones were thrown only after this. Some witnesses said that the police fired without any warning. Historians weigh these accounts by asking several questions. Who was the witness, and where were they standing? When was the account given, and was the person under pressure? Is there physical evidence, such as photographs or medical reports, that supports one account? It is clear from the evidence that the police used live ammunition against schoolchildren, many of them unarmed and young, and that Hector Pieterson, aged twelve, was among the first killed. Even if some stones were thrown, most historians, and later the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, judged that firing on schoolchildren was an excessive and unjustified response. The fact that the details are disputed does not mean that we cannot know what happened; it means that we must use evidence carefully and be honest about what remains uncertain.

### subtopic_4 — The Events of 16 June 1976, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Who was Hector Pieterson, and why is he remembered?

**Tutor:** Hector Pieterson was a twelve-year-old schoolboy who was shot by the police in Orlando West, Soweto, on 16 June 1976. His family name was originally Pitso, but it had been changed to Pieterson. He was not one of the organisers of the march, and some accounts suggest that he was simply following the older students. He was carried by Mbuyisa Makhubo, an eighteen-year-old student, with his sister Antoinette running alongside, and he was pronounced dead at a nearby clinic. He is remembered for several reasons. First, the photograph taken by Sam Nzima captured a moment of grief and horror in a way that words could not. It was published in newspapers around the world and became a symbol of the brutality of apartheid and of the courage of young South Africans. Second, Hector's youth made his death especially shocking, because it showed that the state was prepared to shoot children. Third, his story has come to represent all the young people who died in the uprising, many of whose names are less well known, such as Hastings Ndlovu, who was also killed on 16 June. Today, the Hector Pieterson Memorial and Museum stands in Orlando West, close to where he was shot, and visitors from around the world go there to learn about the uprising. Antoinette Sithole has spoken about her brother at many events. The story of Mbuyisa Makhubo is also sad: he was harassed by the police after the photograph was published, fled the country, and was last heard of in the late 1970s. 16 June is now a public holiday, Youth Day, which honours the role of young people in the struggle for freedom and asks young South Africans to think about the challenges they face today.
