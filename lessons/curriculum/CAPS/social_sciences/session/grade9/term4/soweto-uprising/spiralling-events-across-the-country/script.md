# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains how the protests that began in Soweto on 16 June 1976 spiralled across South Africa in the months that followed. It covers the spread of the uprising to other townships on the Witwatersrand and to the universities; its spread to Cape Town and other parts of the country, including the role of coloured and Indian students; the stay-aways and community campaigns organised by the Soweto Students' Representative Council; and the government's response, the death toll and the divisions that appeared within communities. The anchor idea is that within weeks a local student protest had become a national uprising involving students, workers and communities across the country. The governing idea is that the uprising spread because the grievances behind it, Bantu Education and apartheid, were shared everywhere, and because the shootings in Soweto turned anger into action.

## Subtopic: The Uprising Spreads Across the Witwatersrand and the Universities

On 17 June 1976, the day after the first shootings, the violence in Soweto continued. Police patrolled the streets in armoured vehicles called Hippos, and students continued to attack government buildings, beer halls, bottle stores and vehicles. The same day, protests broke out in other townships on the Witwatersrand, including Alexandra, north of Johannesburg, Kagiso near Krugersdorp, and Tembisa near Kempton Park. In Alexandra, students and other young people burned administration offices and beer halls, and the police opened fire. Over the following days, protests spread to townships on the East Rand and West Rand, and to Pretoria's townships, such as Atteridgeville and Mamelodi.

The universities also responded. At the University of Zululand, near Empangeni in KwaZulu-Natal, students burned buildings in protest on 18 June. At the University of the North, known as Turfloop, near Polokwane, students held protest meetings and burned buildings, and the university was closed. Students at other universities for black students, including Fort Hare in the Eastern Cape, also protested. Many of these students had been influenced by the South African Students' Organisation and Black Consciousness, and they saw the uprising as part of the same struggle.

White students also responded. Some students at the University of the Witwatersrand marched in Johannesburg in solidarity with the Soweto students, and some were attacked by white onlookers. This showed that the uprising divided white South Africa too: a minority of white South Africans sympathised with the students and criticised the government, while most supported the government's demand for order.

The speed of the spread surprised the government. It showed that the conditions that had led to the Soweto protest, overcrowded schools, Bantu Education, poverty and the lack of political rights, existed in townships throughout the country, and that young people everywhere were ready to act. News of the shootings travelled quickly through newspapers, radio and word of mouth, and the photograph of Hector Pieterson became a symbol that moved people to protest.

The Soweto students also organised themselves more formally. The action committee that had planned the march became the Soweto Students' Representative Council, the SSRC, with representatives from each school. Tsietsi Mashinini was its first president. The SSRC continued to organise protests and meetings, and it became the main voice of the students during the uprising.

Teachers and parents on the Witwatersrand often found themselves torn. Some teachers quietly supported the boycotts and helped students to organise, while others tried to keep schools open and examinations running. Many schools were damaged or burned, and in some areas schooling stopped for months. The government blamed students for destroying their own schools, while students argued that the schools had never served them and that Bantu Education itself was the real destruction.

So the next question is: how did the uprising spread beyond the Witwatersrand?

## Subtopic: Cape Town and the Rest of the Country

In the Western Cape, the uprising began in earnest in August 1976. On 11 August, students in the African townships of Langa, Gugulethu and Nyanga marched in solidarity with Soweto, and the police opened fire, killing several people. Buildings were burned, and clashes continued for days.

What made the uprising in Cape Town especially significant was that coloured students joined it in large numbers. Students from schools in coloured areas such as Bonteheuwel, Athlone and Manenberg, and students at the University of the Western Cape, boycotted classes and marched in solidarity with African students. Many were influenced by Black Consciousness, which taught that coloured, Indian and African people were all black in the sense that they were all oppressed by apartheid. In early September 1976, hundreds of coloured students marched into the centre of Cape Town, and police used batons, tear gas and live ammunition to disperse them. Buildings at the University of the Western Cape were set on fire. The participation of coloured students alarmed the government, because it had hoped that the coloured community would side with white South Africans against the African majority. Indian students in some places, including in Durban, also joined boycotts.

The uprising also spread to the Eastern Cape, including Port Elizabeth and its townships, such as New Brighton, and to East London and smaller towns. There were protests in the Free State and in parts of what are now North West and Limpopo, including in some of the homelands. By the end of 1976, protests had taken place in more than a hundred towns and townships. The spread was uneven: in some places protests lasted for months, while in others they were brief, and in some rural areas there were few protests at all.

In many places, the protests followed a similar pattern. Students marched, boycotted schools and burned symbols of government authority, such as administration offices, beer halls, schools and police vehicles. The police responded with tear gas, dogs, batons and live ammunition. Funerals of people killed by the police became mass political events, which were often followed by more clashes and more deaths, so that the violence moved in a cycle.

The uprising also reached smaller places that rarely appeared in the news. In towns in the Karoo, the Free State and the northern Transvaal, groups of students boycotted classes, held meetings and sometimes clashed with local police. Because reporters seldom visited these towns, much of what we know about them comes from later oral histories, local newspapers and the records of trials, which is a reminder that historians must sometimes look beyond the big cities to understand a national event.

So the next question is: how did the students try to involve workers and the wider community?

## Subtopic: Stay-Aways and Community Campaigns

The students realised that school boycotts alone could not force the government to change, and that the support of workers would greatly increase their power. The SSRC therefore called on workers to stay away from work in a series of stay-aways, also called stay-at-homes.

The first major stay-away was held from 4 to 6 August 1976 in Johannesburg. Students marched to stations and asked workers not to travel, and many workers stayed at home. A second stay-away was called from 23 to 25 August. During this stay-away, there were violent clashes in Soweto between students and some migrant workers who lived in the Mzimhlophe hostel. Many hostel dwellers were migrants from rural areas, and some of them were angry that the stay-away cost them their wages and that students were trying to stop them from going to work. There is evidence that police encouraged hostel dwellers to attack students and residents. Dozens of people were killed in the fighting. This showed that communities were not united, and that the government was willing to exploit divisions between groups.

The SSRC then met with hostel dwellers and explained its aims, and the third stay-away, from 13 to 15 September 1976, was the most successful. Hundreds of thousands of workers in Johannesburg stayed away, and in Cape Town many African and coloured workers also stayed away. Businesses were badly affected. The stay-aways showed the potential power of workers in the struggle against apartheid, an idea that would become very important in the 1980s.

The students also organised community campaigns. They campaigned against shebeens and beer halls, arguing that alcohol was destroying the community and that people should not spend money on drinking while young people were being killed. They called on people not to celebrate Christmas in December 1976 but to mourn those who had died. In 1977, the SSRC led a successful campaign against an increase in rents in Soweto, and it forced the resignation of the Soweto Urban Bantu Council, a body that residents regarded as a puppet of the government. Students also boycotted examinations, and in 1977 many schools in Soweto were almost empty.

The SSRC's leaders were hunted by the police. In August 1976, Tsietsi Mashinini fled the country, and Khotso Seatlholo became president of the SSRC; he too went into exile in early 1977. Daniel Sechaba Montsisi then led the SSRC until he was arrested in June 1977. Many other student leaders were detained, and others went into exile, as you will learn in the next lesson.

So the final question is: how did the government respond, and what was the human cost?

## Subtopic: Repression, the Death Toll and Divided Responses, and the Error Museum

The government responded to the uprising mainly with force. The Prime Minister, B.J. Vorster, and the Minister of Justice and Police, Jimmy Kruger, argued that the unrest was caused by agitators and communists, rather than by real grievances, and they promised to restore order. The police were given wide powers. Thousands of people, including students, teachers, journalists, Black Consciousness leaders and community leaders, were detained without trial under security laws such as the Internal Security Act and the Terrorism Act. Some people died in detention; for example, Mapetla Mohapi, a Black Consciousness leader in the Eastern Cape, died in police custody in August 1976. Schools were closed in many areas, and meetings were banned.

The government appointed a commission of inquiry, led by Justice Petrus Cillié, to investigate the unrest. The Cillié Commission reported that 575 people had died in the unrest between June 1976 and February 1977, and that about 3 900 people had been injured. Most of those who died were killed by the police. Many people believed that the real death toll was higher, because not all deaths were reported. The commission blamed agitators and Black Consciousness organisations for the unrest, although it also acknowledged that the Afrikaans instruction and poor living conditions had contributed to it.

Responses to the uprising within black communities were not uniform. Many parents supported their children and formed organisations such as the Black Parents' Association, which included Winnie Mandela and the doctor Nthato Motlana, to help families of victims, pay for funerals and speak to the authorities. Some parents, however, feared for their children's safety and their education, and worried that years of schooling were being lost. Some workers supported the stay-aways, while others resented losing their wages or were intimidated into staying at home. Some homeland leaders criticised the students, while others criticised the government. These different responses show that the uprising was a complex event, in which people made difficult choices under great pressure.

There were also important responses outside South Africa. The uprising was reported around the world, and in many countries people protested against apartheid. Anti-apartheid movements in Britain, the United States and elsewhere gained support, and the United Nations Security Council condemned the shootings on 19 June 1976.

Finally, the uprising changed the mood of the country. For the first time since the early 1960s, there was open, widespread resistance to apartheid inside South Africa. The government had shown that it could still crush protest with force, but it had also been shown to be vulnerable. Many business leaders began to worry about the future of the economy, and some white South Africans started to question whether apartheid could last.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "The uprising stayed in Soweto." Within days, it spread across the Witwatersrand, and by the end of 1976 it had reached more than a hundred towns and townships.

Exhibit two: "Only African students took part." Coloured students in Cape Town and some Indian students also joined the protests.

Exhibit three: "All workers and parents supported every action." Many did, but some opposed the stay-aways, and there were violent clashes at the Mzimhlophe hostel.

Exhibit four: "The official death toll was 176 for the whole uprising." The Cillié Commission reported 575 deaths by February 1977, and many believe the real figure was higher.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a fire spreading in the wind, an empty factory and a list of names.

## Subtopic: A Fire Spreading in the Wind

After the police shot students in Soweto on 16 June 1976, the anger did not die down. It spread like a fire in the wind. The next day, students in Alexandra, Kagiso and Tembisa were marching too. Soon, students at universities such as Turfloop and the University of Zululand were protesting.

Why did it spread so fast? Because students everywhere faced the same problems: crowded schools, Bantu Education and no say in their own lives. The photograph of Hector Pieterson made them feel that what happened in Soweto could happen to them.

In August, the fire reached Cape Town. African students in Langa, Gugulethu and Nyanga marched. Then coloured students joined them, saying: we are in this together.

So how did the students get the grown-ups involved?

## Subtopic: An Empty Factory

The students knew that closing schools was not enough. If workers stayed home, factories would stand empty, and the government would have to listen.

So the students' council, the SSRC, called stay-aways. The first was in early August. The second, later in August, went badly: some hostel workers who wanted to go to work fought with the students, and many people died. The students then talked to the hostel workers and explained their aims. The third stay-away, in September, worked: hundreds of thousands of workers stayed home.

The students also asked people not to drink in beer halls and not to celebrate Christmas, but to mourn the dead.

So what did the government do?

## Subtopic: A List of Names

The government said that the trouble was caused by troublemakers and communists. Police arrested thousands of people and kept them in jail without trial. Some died in jail. The student leaders were hunted, and many fled the country.

A government inquiry later counted 575 people killed and about 3 900 injured. Many people believe the real number was even higher. Each number was a person with a name and a family.

Here is the line to remember: after 16 June, the uprising spread across South Africa, drawing in students of different backgrounds, workers and communities, and the government answered with force, detention and hundreds of deaths.
