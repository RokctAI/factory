# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the causes, leaders and events of 16 June 1976 in Soweto. It covers the long-term causes of the uprising, including Bantu Education and the conditions in which black South Africans lived in the 1970s; the immediate cause, the Afrikaans Medium Decree of 1974; the influence of the Black Consciousness Movement and the student organisations that planned the protest; and the events of 16 June itself. The anchor idea is that on 16 June 1976 thousands of Soweto students marched peacefully against being taught in Afrikaans, and that the police opened fire on them, killing Hector Pieterson and others. The governing idea is that the uprising grew out of deep anger at Bantu Education and apartheid as a whole, and that the Afrikaans decree was the spark rather than the only cause.

## Subtopic: Long-Term Causes: Bantu Education and Life Under Apartheid

The deepest cause of the uprising was Bantu Education. As you learnt in earlier grades, the Bantu Education Act of 1953 took control of black schooling away from the provinces and the mission churches and placed it under a central government department. Its architect, Hendrik Verwoerd, argued that black children should be educated for the roles that apartheid planned for them, and he said in Parliament that there was no place for the African in European society above the level of certain forms of labour. Many mission schools, which had provided some of the best education available to black South Africans, closed rather than accept the new system.

By the 1970s, the results were plain to see. The government spent far less on each black pupil than on each white pupil; in the mid-1970s, spending on a white child was more than ten times the spending on a black child. Classrooms in Soweto were badly overcrowded, often with sixty or more pupils in a class, and some schools ran double sessions, with one group in the morning and another in the afternoon. Many teachers were under-qualified, textbooks were scarce, and parents had to pay for books and uniforms that white pupils received free. Pupils and parents saw Bantu Education as a deliberate attempt to keep black people in an inferior position.

Conditions in the townships added to the anger. Soweto, short for South Western Townships, was a large group of townships south-west of Johannesburg, home to roughly a million people. Houses were small and crowded, many had no electricity, and there were few shops, parks or sports fields. The pass laws and influx control controlled where people could live and work, and residents had no say in how Soweto was run. In the early 1970s, the economy slowed, unemployment rose, and prices increased sharply, partly because of the world oil crisis of 1973. In 1973, a wave of strikes by black workers in Durban had also shown that ordinary people were willing to challenge the system, after more than a decade in which open political opposition had been crushed.

The government had also decided in 1972 to expand secondary schooling in Soweto, which meant that many more teenagers were in school than before. In 1976, a change to the school system meant that two groups of pupils, those finishing Standard 5 and those finishing Standard 6, moved into secondary school at the same time, making overcrowding even worse. Large numbers of young people, frustrated and crowded together, were therefore in a position to organise.

So the next question is: what was the immediate cause of the protest?

## Subtopic: The Immediate Cause: The Afrikaans Medium Decree

The immediate cause of the uprising was the Afrikaans Medium Decree. In 1974, the Department of Bantu Education issued an instruction that from Standard 5, now Grade 7, in black schools, half of the subjects had to be taught through Afrikaans and half through English. Mathematics and social studies, for example, were to be taught in Afrikaans. The policy was strongly supported by the Deputy Minister of Bantu Education, Andries Treurnicht, and the Minister, M.C. Botha.

There were several reasons why pupils, parents and teachers objected. Many teachers could not speak Afrikaans well enough to teach in it, and many pupils were already struggling to learn in English, which was their second or third language. Learning difficult subjects such as mathematics in a third language would make it even harder to pass. Many black South Africans also saw Afrikaans as the language of the oppressor, because it was the language of the National Party government, the police and the officials who enforced the pass laws. English, by contrast, was seen as the language of international communication, business and opportunity. The decree was therefore seen not only as an educational problem but as a political insult.

Resistance grew through 1975 and early 1976. School boards and some principals objected, and some parents' organisations protested to the department, but the government refused to change the policy. Treurnicht argued that the government paid for black education and therefore had the right to decide the language of instruction. In early 1976, pupils at Orlando West Junior Secondary School and other Soweto schools began to refuse to attend classes taught in Afrikaans, and some boycotted examinations. By May and June 1976, boycotts had spread to several schools, and in some cases pupils clashed with police.

Teachers were caught in the middle. Many were employed by the department and could lose their jobs if they openly opposed its instructions, yet they knew that the policy would damage their pupils' learning. Some school principals and members of school boards who resisted the decree were dismissed or pressured to resign. The Tswana School Board in Meadowlands, for example, refused to implement the instruction and members were removed from their posts. For pupils, this showed that peaceful complaints through official channels were being ignored, and it encouraged them to take action themselves.

So the next question is: who led the protest, and what ideas inspired them?

## Subtopic: Black Consciousness and the Student Leaders

The students were inspired by the ideas of the Black Consciousness Movement. Black Consciousness developed in the late 1960s, when Steve Biko and other black students broke away from the multiracial National Union of South African Students and formed the South African Students' Organisation, SASO, in 1968, launched formally in 1969. Black Consciousness taught that black people had to free themselves psychologically from feelings of inferiority created by racism, and that they should lead their own struggle for liberation instead of depending on white liberals. Biko wrote that the most potent weapon in the hands of the oppressor is the mind of the oppressed. Black Consciousness encouraged pride in being black and in African culture, history and identity. In 1972, the Black People's Convention was formed to spread these ideas more widely.

Black Consciousness ideas reached high school students through organisations such as the South African Students' Movement, SASM, which had branches in Soweto schools, and through young teachers who had been influenced by SASO. Many students who had grown up after the banning of the ANC and PAC in 1960 knew little about these organisations, but they were proud, confident and willing to confront authority.

On 13 June 1976, at a meeting of SASM in Soweto, students formed an action committee to organise a mass protest march against Afrikaans. The leading figure was Tsietsi Mashinini, a student at Morris Isaacson High School, who was a gifted speaker. Other leaders included Seth Mazibuko of Orlando West Junior Secondary School and Murphy Morobe. The action committee later became the Soweto Students' Representative Council, the SSRC. The plan was kept secret from parents and teachers, partly to protect them and partly so that the police would not hear of it. The students agreed that the march would be peaceful, and they made placards with slogans such as "Down with Afrikaans" and "If we must do Afrikaans, Vorster must do Zulu". The route was planned so that columns of students from different schools would meet and walk together to Orlando Stadium, where a mass meeting would be held.

It is worth noting that historians still discuss how much the protest was planned by Black Consciousness organisations and how much it was a spontaneous student response. The evidence suggests both: Black Consciousness provided ideas and organisational networks, while the students themselves made the decisions and took the risks.

So the final question is: what happened on 16 June 1976?

## Subtopic: The Events of 16 June 1976, and the Error Museum

Early on the morning of Wednesday 16 June 1976, students gathered at their schools and set out in columns, carrying placards, singing and chanting. Estimates of the number of students who marched range from about ten thousand to twenty thousand. The atmosphere was mostly peaceful and even cheerful, and the students had been told not to provoke the police.

As the columns converged near Orlando West High School and Vilakazi Street, a group of police arrived and confronted the marchers. Accounts of exactly what happened differ. Students sang the anthem Nkosi Sikelel' iAfrika, and some students threw stones at the police after a police dog was released, or after tear gas was fired; the police later said that the stones came first. The police then opened fire with live ammunition. Among the first to be killed were Hastings Ndlovu, aged fifteen, and Hector Pieterson, aged twelve.

A photograph taken by Sam Nzima of The World newspaper became one of the most famous images of the twentieth century. It shows an eighteen-year-old student, Mbuyisa Makhubo, carrying the dying Hector Pieterson, with Hector's sister, Antoinette Sithole, running beside them. Hector was taken to a nearby clinic but was pronounced dead. The photograph was published around the world, and it turned the events in Soweto into international news.

The shootings turned the peaceful march into an uprising. Angry students attacked symbols of apartheid and government authority, burning West Rand Administration Board offices, beer halls and bottle stores, which they saw as places that took money from the community and encouraged drunkenness. Vehicles were set alight. Two white officials were killed by crowds, among them Dr Melville Edelstein, a sociologist who worked for the West Rand Administration Board and who had warned that young people were deeply frustrated. The government sent in heavily armed police reinforcements, and by the evening Soweto was in turmoil. Violence continued over the following days, and the official figure for deaths on 16 and 17 June alone was in the dozens. Over the following months, as you will learn in the next lesson, the uprising spread across the country.

The response of the government on the first day was to treat the uprising as a law-and-order problem. The Minister of Justice and Police, Jimmy Kruger, blamed agitators, and the Prime Minister, B.J. Vorster, said that order would be maintained at all costs. On 6 July 1976, the government withdrew the instruction that Afrikaans had to be used as a medium of instruction, but by then the protest had grown far beyond the language issue.

Historians remind us that the people of Soweto did not experience 16 June only as victims. Parents, nurses, doctors, taxi drivers and ordinary residents helped wounded students, hid young people from the police and carried messages between families. Journalists, many of them black reporters working for newspapers such as The World and the Rand Daily Mail, risked their safety to record what happened, and their reports became important historical sources. The courage of the students also changed how many adults saw them: a generation that some had thought of as unpolitical had stood up to the most powerful police force on the continent.

It is also important to remember that people outside Soweto experienced the day in different ways. Some white South Africans were shocked by the shootings and called for reforms, while others accepted the government's view that the protest was the work of agitators. Understanding these different perspectives helps us to see why the uprising divided the country.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "The students marched to start a violent riot." The march was planned as a peaceful protest; violence spread after the police opened fire.

Exhibit two: "The uprising was only about Afrikaans." Afrikaans was the spark, but the deeper causes were Bantu Education and apartheid as a whole.

Exhibit three: "The ANC planned and led the march from exile." The march was organised by students, through SASM and its action committee, inspired by Black Consciousness.

Exhibit four: "Hector Pieterson was the only person killed on 16 June." Others were killed that day, including Hastings Ndlovu, and many more died in the weeks that followed.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a crowded classroom, a homemade placard and a famous photograph.

## Subtopic: A Crowded Classroom

Imagine sixty children squeezed into one classroom, sharing a few old textbooks, with a teacher who has had little training. That was the reality for many black pupils in Soweto in the 1970s. Bantu Education gave black children far less money and far fewer chances than white children.

Then, in 1974, the government gave a new order. From Standard 5, half of the subjects would be taught in Afrikaans. Pupils who were already learning in English, their second or third language, now had to learn mathematics in yet another language. Many teachers could not even speak Afrikaans well.

For many people, Afrikaans was the language of the police and of the pass laws. The order felt like an insult.

So what did the students do?

## Subtopic: A Homemade Placard

Students began to refuse to go to classes taught in Afrikaans. They had grown up proud and confident, inspired by Steve Biko's Black Consciousness, which taught them to believe in themselves and lead their own struggle.

On 13 June 1976, students in the South African Students' Movement made a plan. Led by Tsietsi Mashinini, they would march peacefully to Orlando Stadium. They kept the plan secret, even from their parents. They made placards: "Down with Afrikaans!"

Their plan had a few simple rules. Walk together. Stay calm. Do not fight. They wanted the government and the world to hear them.

So what happened on the day of the march?

## Subtopic: A Famous Photograph

On the morning of 16 June 1976, thousands of students marched through Soweto, singing. Near Orlando West High School, the police blocked their way. Stones were thrown, and the police opened fire on the children.

Twelve-year-old Hector Pieterson was shot. An older student, Mbuyisa Makhubo, picked him up and ran, with Hector's sister Antoinette beside him. A photographer, Sam Nzima, took a picture. Hector died, and the photograph shocked the whole world.

Angry students then burned government offices and beer halls. The protest had become an uprising. Today, 16 June is Youth Day in South Africa.

Here is the line to remember: on 16 June 1976, Soweto students marched peacefully against Afrikaans and Bantu Education, and when police opened fire, killing Hector Pieterson and others, a protest became an uprising.
