### subtopic_1 — Exile and the Revival of the Armed Struggle
**Assistant:** Why did so many young people join the ANC rather than Black Consciousness groups in exile?

**Tutor:** This is an interesting question, because most of the young people who led the uprising of 1976 had been inspired by Black Consciousness, not by the ANC. Many of them had been small children when the ANC was banned in 1960, and they knew little about it. Yet when they went into exile, most of them joined the ANC. Historians give several reasons for this. The most important is that the ANC had a well-established organisation in exile. Since the early 1960s, Oliver Tambo and other leaders had built offices in many countries, relationships with governments in Africa and Europe, and training camps for Umkhonto we Sizwe. The young exiles wanted military training and weapons, and the ANC could provide them. The Black Consciousness Movement, by contrast, had developed mainly as a movement inside South Africa, and it had few structures, camps or international contacts abroad. The PAC also had an exile organisation, but in the 1970s it was weakened by internal divisions and leadership disputes, which made it less attractive to many young exiles. In addition, the ANC was able to explain its history and its ideas, such as the Freedom Charter, in ways that many young people found convincing, and it welcomed people of all races. Some young exiles found that Black Consciousness and the ANC shared important goals, such as ending apartheid and building a free South Africa. However, not all exiles joined the ANC. Some joined the PAC, and some Black Consciousness activists formed their own organisation in exile. After the end of apartheid, AZAPO continued to represent Black Consciousness ideas in politics.

### subtopic_2 — Repression: The Death of Steve Biko and the October 1977 Bannings
**Assistant:** Why did Steve Biko's death have such a big impact?

**Tutor:** Steve Biko's death in police custody on 12 September 1977 had a huge impact, both inside South Africa and around the world. Inside South Africa, Biko was one of the most respected and influential black leaders of his generation. He was the main thinker of the Black Consciousness Movement, which had inspired the students of 1976, and even though he had been banned since 1973, his ideas continued to spread. His death showed how dangerous detention without trial could be, and how the police could act without being held responsible. The government's response made the situation worse. Jimmy Kruger, the Minister of Justice and Police, said that Biko's death left him cold, and the police at first claimed that he had died after a hunger strike. Journalists such as Donald Woods and Helen Zille investigated and showed that Biko had died of brain injuries after being assaulted. The inquest found that no one was responsible, which convinced many people that the legal system protected the police. Internationally, Biko's death was reported widely, and many people who had not followed South African politics learnt about apartheid for the first time. Western diplomats attended his funeral, and his death contributed to the UN arms embargo of November 1977. Donald Woods later wrote a book about Biko, which became the basis of a film called Cry Freedom, and the musician Peter Gabriel wrote a song called Biko, which spread his name further. For many South Africans, Biko became a martyr whose ideas about pride, dignity and self-reliance continued to inspire people long after his death. Today, many places are named after him, and his ideas are still studied and debated.

### subtopic_3 — International Pressure and Government Reforms
**Assistant:** Did the government's reforms after 1976 make apartheid weaker or stronger?

**Tutor:** Historians have debated whether the reforms introduced by the government after 1976, especially under P.W. Botha from 1978, strengthened or weakened apartheid. The government's aim was to strengthen its position. It hoped that by improving conditions for some black South Africans, such as urban workers with permanent rights and a growing middle class, it could win their support or at least reduce their anger, while repression would deal with those who wanted full equality. Botha's slogan was that South Africa had to adapt or die. Some reforms did improve conditions. Black trade unions were legally recognised in 1979, spending on black education increased, some job reservation was ended, and urban residents gained more security. However, the reforms did not change the basic structure of apartheid. Black South Africans still had no vote in Parliament, the homelands policy continued, influx control remained for many, schools remained separate, and repression continued. In the longer term, many historians argue that the reforms weakened apartheid rather than strengthening it. The recognition of trade unions, for example, allowed black workers to form powerful unions, which later came together in the Congress of South African Trade Unions in 1985 and played a major role in resistance. Reforms also raised expectations: when people saw that change was possible, they demanded more. In addition, the reforms divided the National Party, and some members left in 1982 to form the Conservative Party, because they believed that Botha was giving away too much. This suggests that the government was trapped: reform raised demands, while repression increased anger, and neither could restore the stability that apartheid needed.

### subtopic_4 — A New Generation of Resistance and the Memory of 1976, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Was 1976 really a turning point?

**Tutor:** A turning point is an event after which things are significantly different from what they were before. To decide whether 1976 was a turning point, historians compare the situation before and after it. Before 1976, after the bannings of the ANC and PAC in 1960 and the imprisonment of leaders such as Nelson Mandela after the Rivonia Trial, there was relatively little open resistance inside South Africa. Historians sometimes call the period from about 1964 to the early 1970s the quiet years, although Black Consciousness, the Durban strikes of 1973 and other developments show that resistance had not disappeared. After 1976, the situation was very different. Thousands of young people joined the liberation movements in exile, and the ANC and MK grew much stronger. Inside the country, a new generation of activists formed student organisations, trade unions and community organisations, which later came together in the United Democratic Front and the Congress of South African Trade Unions. International pressure increased, with the mandatory arms embargo and growing campaigns for sanctions. The government itself began to change its approach, combining reform with repression. These are strong arguments that 1976 was a turning point. However, some historians point out that change was gradual, that many of the forces that grew after 1976 had roots before it, and that apartheid continued for another eighteen years. They argue that 1976 was one of several important turning points, alongside 1960 and 1990. When you answer a question like this in an essay, it is best to give evidence for both sides and reach a reasoned conclusion of your own.
