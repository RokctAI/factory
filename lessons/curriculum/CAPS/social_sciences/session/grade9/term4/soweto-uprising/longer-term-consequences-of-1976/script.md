# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the longer-term consequences of the 1976 uprising for resistance and repression in South Africa. It covers the exodus of young people into exile and the revival of the armed struggle; the harsher repression that followed, including the death of Steve Biko in detention and the bannings of October 1977; the international responses and the government's reforms of the late 1970s; and the rise of a new generation of organisations and activists inside the country, as well as how 1976 is remembered today. The anchor idea is that 1976 was a turning point because it revived resistance inside and outside South Africa after more than a decade of quiet. The governing idea is that the government responded with both repression and limited reform, and that neither succeeded in ending resistance; instead, the struggle grew stronger, leading to the mass resistance of the 1980s.

## Subtopic: Exile and the Revival of the Armed Struggle

One of the most important consequences of the uprising was that thousands of young people left South Africa. Some fled because they were wanted by the police, and others left because they had decided that peaceful protest could not defeat apartheid and that they needed military training. Many young people crossed the borders into Botswana, Swaziland and Lesotho, often on foot and at night, and from there they travelled to countries such as Tanzania, Zambia and Angola. Estimates of the number who left between 1976 and the end of the 1970s range from several thousand to more than ten thousand.

Most of the young exiles joined the African National Congress, although some joined the Pan Africanist Congress and some stayed with Black Consciousness groups. Many of them had grown up with Black Consciousness ideas and knew little about the ANC, which had been banned since 1960, but the ANC had an organisation in exile, led by Oliver Tambo, with offices, training camps and international support. The ANC was therefore able to absorb many of the young exiles. This greatly strengthened the ANC, which had been weakened by the arrests of its leaders in the 1960s, such as Nelson Mandela and Walter Sisulu, who were serving life sentences on Robben Island after the Rivonia Trial of 1963 and 1964.

Many young exiles joined Umkhonto we Sizwe, the ANC's armed wing, and were trained in camps in Angola and other countries, as well as in the Soviet Union and East Germany. From the late 1970s, MK carried out more attacks inside South Africa. Its main targets were police stations, government buildings, railway lines and economic targets such as fuel and power installations. In June 1980, MK attacked the Sasol oil-from-coal plants at Sasolburg and Secunda, causing huge fires. In 1982, MK bombed the Koeberg nuclear power station near Cape Town while it was still being built. Some attacks killed civilians: in May 1983, a car bomb outside the air force headquarters in Church Street, Pretoria, killed nineteen people, including civilians, and injured more than two hundred. The ANC said that its targets were the military and the state, but critics argued that such attacks also killed innocent people. The armed struggle remains a subject that people discuss and debate, and the Truth and Reconciliation Commission later found that both the state and the liberation movements had committed gross human rights violations, while noting that the scale of the state's violations was far greater.

The ANC also set up a school in exile for young South Africans whose education had been interrupted. The Solomon Mahlangu Freedom College, known as SOMAFCO, opened in 1978 at Mazimbu in Tanzania. It was named after Solomon Mahlangu, a young man from Mamelodi who had left South Africa after 1976 to join MK. He returned in 1977 with a comrade, and during an incident in Goch Street, Johannesburg, his comrade shot and killed two people. Mahlangu was convicted of murder on the basis that the two had acted together, and although he had not fired the shots, he was hanged on 6 April 1979, despite international appeals for clemency. Before his execution, he is reported to have said: "My blood will nourish the tree that will bear the fruits of freedom." He became a symbol of the generation of 1976.

So the next question is: how did the government increase repression after 1976?

## Subtopic: Repression: The Death of Steve Biko and the October 1977 Bannings

The government's response to the uprising and its aftermath was harsh. Thousands of people were detained without trial under security laws, and many were tortured. Detention without trial meant that the police could hold people for long periods without charging them in court, and without allowing them to see lawyers or family. Some detainees died in custody; in the 1970s, dozens of people died in police detention, and the police often claimed that they had committed suicide, fallen down stairs or slipped in the shower.

The most famous death in detention was that of Steve Biko, the founder of the Black Consciousness Movement. Biko had been banned since 1973, which meant that he was restricted to the area of King William's Town and was not allowed to speak in public or be quoted. On 18 August 1977, he was arrested at a roadblock near Grahamstown, now Makhanda, and taken to the security police headquarters in Port Elizabeth, now Gqeberha. There he was interrogated, chained, and assaulted, and he suffered serious head injuries. Instead of being taken to hospital nearby, he was driven, naked and in the back of a police vehicle, more than 1 000 kilometres to Pretoria, where he died on 12 September 1977. He was thirty years old.

The Minister of Justice and Police, Jimmy Kruger, said that Biko's death left him cold, and the police at first claimed that Biko had died after a hunger strike. Journalists, including Donald Woods, the editor of the Daily Dispatch in East London, and Helen Zille, then a journalist at the Rand Daily Mail, exposed the truth that Biko had suffered brain injuries. An inquest found that no one was to blame for his death. Biko's funeral in King William's Town was attended by about fifteen thousand people, including diplomats from many Western countries. His death caused international outrage. In the late 1990s, five former police officers applied to the Truth and Reconciliation Commission for amnesty for Biko's death, but amnesty was refused, because the commission found that they had not told the full truth and that the killing had no political objective. They were never prosecuted, because the time limit for prosecution had passed and there was not enough evidence.

On 19 October 1977, a day that became known as Black Wednesday, the government banned nineteen organisations, most of them Black Consciousness organisations, including the South African Students' Organisation, the Black People's Convention, the South African Students' Movement and the Soweto Students' Representative Council, as well as the Christian Institute led by Beyers Naudé. It also banned The World and Weekend World, the newspapers with the largest black readership, and detained their editor, Percy Qoboza. Donald Woods and Beyers Naudé were banned, and Woods later fled the country. The government hoped that these measures would crush resistance completely.

So the next question is: how did the world respond, and did the government try to reform?

## Subtopic: International Pressure and Government Reforms

The uprising and the death of Steve Biko led to growing international pressure on South Africa. On 4 November 1977, the United Nations Security Council passed Resolution 418, which imposed a mandatory arms embargo on South Africa. This meant that all member states were required to stop selling weapons and military equipment to South Africa. It was the first time that the United Nations had imposed mandatory sanctions of this kind against a member state. South Africa responded by expanding its own arms industry through Armscor, so the embargo did not stop it from arming itself, but it was an important sign of international isolation.

Other forms of pressure also grew. In 1977, Commonwealth leaders signed the Gleneagles Agreement, in which they agreed to discourage sporting contact with South Africa. The anti-apartheid movement grew in many countries, and campaigns called on companies and banks to stop investing in South Africa. In the United States, the Sullivan Principles, introduced in 1977, called on American companies operating in South Africa to treat their black employees fairly. Some critics argued that these principles allowed companies to stay in South Africa, while others saw them as a first step towards disinvestment.

Inside South Africa, the government began to introduce some reforms. In 1978, B.J. Vorster resigned as Prime Minister after a financial scandal known as the Information Scandal, and P.W. Botha replaced him. Botha argued that South Africa had to adapt or die, and his government introduced some changes, while keeping the basic structure of apartheid in place. The Wiehahn Commission led to the legal recognition of black trade unions in 1979, which allowed black workers to join registered unions and bargain with employers. The Riekert Commission recommended that black people with the right to live in urban areas should be treated as permanent residents, although influx control for others became stricter. In education, the Department of Bantu Education was renamed the Department of Education and Training in 1979, and spending on black education increased, but schools remained separate and unequal. In 1977, community councils, and from 1982 Black Local Authorities, were introduced to run townships, but many residents saw them as illegitimate puppets of the government.

Historians often describe the government's strategy after 1976 as a mixture of reform and repression. It hoped that limited reforms would win the support of some black South Africans, particularly urban workers and the middle class, while repression would crush those who wanted more. Instead, the reforms, such as the recognition of trade unions, gave black South Africans new ways to organise, and they created stronger organisations that would challenge apartheid in the 1980s.

So the final question is: how did 1976 shape the resistance that followed?

## Subtopic: A New Generation of Resistance and the Memory of 1976, and the Error Museum

The uprising produced a new generation of activists who were confident, organised and determined. Even after the bannings of 1977, new organisations were formed inside the country. The Azanian People's Organisation, AZAPO, was formed in 1978 to continue Black Consciousness ideas. The Congress of South African Students, COSAS, was formed in 1979, and it organised school boycotts in 1980 in the Western Cape and elsewhere. Black trade unions grew rapidly after 1979. Community organisations, called civics, were formed to deal with issues such as rents, transport and services. Many of these organisations later joined the United Democratic Front, launched in 1983, which you will learn about in the next lesson.

Many of the young people of 1976 became important leaders. Some, such as Murphy Morobe, became leaders of the United Democratic Front in the 1980s. Others returned from exile after 1990 and became politicians, soldiers, teachers and business people. Tsietsi Mashinini, the first leader of the SSRC, lived in exile for the rest of his life and died in Guinea in 1990, before he could see the end of apartheid.

The uprising also had costs that people still discuss. Many young people lost years of schooling because of boycotts, detention and exile. In the 1980s, some activists used the slogan "liberation before education", while others, including many parents and teachers, argued that education was itself a weapon for liberation. In 1986, the National Education Crisis Committee called on students to return to school under the slogan "people's education for people's power".

Today, 1976 is remembered in many ways. 16 June is a public holiday, Youth Day, which honours the courage of young people in the struggle and asks young South Africans to reflect on their role in society today. The Hector Pieterson Memorial and Museum in Orlando West opened in 2002. Many schools, streets and buildings are named after people of 1976, including Tsietsi Mashinini and Solomon Mahlangu. Historians see 1976 as a turning point because it ended the quiet years after 1960 and began a period of resistance that continued until apartheid ended.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "After 1976, the struggle ended because the uprising was crushed." Resistance grew, both in exile through the ANC and MK, and inside the country through new organisations and unions.

Exhibit two: "Steve Biko died of a hunger strike." He died of brain injuries after being assaulted in police custody.

Exhibit three: "The government made no changes after 1976." It introduced limited reforms, such as recognising black trade unions in 1979, while keeping apartheid's basic structure.

Exhibit four: "The 1977 arms embargo stopped South Africa from getting weapons." It increased isolation, but South Africa expanded its own arms industry through Armscor.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a bag packed at night, a cell in Pretoria and a door that opened a crack.

## Subtopic: A Bag Packed at Night

After 1976, many young people knew the police were looking for them. Others decided that marching was not enough. So they packed a small bag at night, left their families, and walked across the border into Botswana, Swaziland or Lesotho.

Thousands left. Most joined the ANC in exile, led by Oliver Tambo. Many trained as soldiers in Umkhonto we Sizwe and later came back to attack government targets. Some attacks also killed ordinary people, which people still debate today.

One of them, Solomon Mahlangu, was hanged in 1979. The ANC named a school in Tanzania after him.

So what happened to people who stayed?

## Subtopic: A Cell in Pretoria

Inside South Africa, the police arrested thousands of people and held them without trial. Some died in jail.

The most famous was Steve Biko, the leader of Black Consciousness. In 1977, he was arrested and badly beaten by the police. He was driven more than a thousand kilometres to Pretoria in the back of a police van, and he died there. The minister said it left him cold. The world was furious.

A month later, the government banned nineteen organisations and two newspapers.

So did anything change?

## Subtopic: A Door That Opened a Crack

The United Nations banned all countries from selling weapons to South Africa. Other countries began cutting sports and business ties.

The government made a few changes. In 1979, black workers were allowed to join trade unions. Bantu Education got a new name. But apartheid stayed.

Still, the door had opened a crack. New groups formed: student groups, unions and community groups. In the 1980s, they would push that door wide open.

Here is the line to remember: after 1976, young people went into exile and revived the armed struggle, the government answered with more repression and small reforms, and a new generation of resistance grew that would shape the 1980s.
