# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains factory farming and the genetic modification of crops, two modern technologies in food production. It covers what factory farming, also called intensive livestock farming, is and how it works, with South African examples such as broiler chickens, egg production and cattle feedlots; the benefits and costs of factory farming for food supply, animal welfare, human health and the environment; what genetically modified crops are, how they are made and how they are used in South Africa; and the arguments for and against genetically modified crops, including scientific evidence and social and economic concerns. The anchor idea is that both technologies aim to produce more food at lower cost by controlling and changing how animals and plants are raised. The governing idea is that each brings real benefits and real risks, so a well-informed person weighs the evidence on both sides rather than accepting simple claims that a technology is entirely good or entirely bad.

## Subtopic: What Factory Farming Is

Factory farming, also called intensive livestock farming or industrial animal farming, is a system of raising large numbers of animals in confined spaces, such as sheds, cages or pens, with the aim of producing as much meat, milk or eggs as possible at the lowest cost. It is called factory farming because it applies the methods of a factory, such as specialisation, standardisation, mechanisation and large scale, to raising animals.

In contrast, in extensive or free-range farming, animals spend much of their time outdoors, grazing or foraging over larger areas, at lower densities.

Factory farming developed mainly after World War II, especially in the United States and Europe, as demand for cheap meat grew and as new technologies, such as automated feeding and watering systems, vaccines, antibiotics and specially bred animals, made it possible to keep large numbers of animals indoors. It has since spread around the world, including to South Africa.

In South Africa, the poultry industry is the largest part of agriculture by value. Chicken is the most commonly eaten meat in the country, because it is relatively cheap. Most broiler chickens, which are chickens raised for meat, are kept in large, climate-controlled sheds, each holding tens of thousands of birds. They are bred to grow very quickly and reach market weight in about five to six weeks, eating carefully formulated feed made mainly from maize and soybeans. Many egg-laying hens are kept in battery cages, which are small wire cages stacked in rows in large sheds, although some producers have moved to cage-free or free-range systems in response to consumer demand.

Pigs are also often raised intensively in indoor pens. Many South African beef cattle spend most of their lives grazing on farms, but in the final months before slaughter, the majority are sent to feedlots, where thousands of cattle are kept in open pens and fed a high-energy diet of grain and other feed to fatten them quickly. Large feedlots are found in areas such as the Free State, North West, Gauteng and the Northern Cape.

So the next question is: what are the benefits and costs of factory farming?

## Subtopic: Benefits and Costs of Factory Farming

Supporters of factory farming point to several benefits. It produces large quantities of meat, milk and eggs efficiently, using less land per kilogram than extensive farming. This has made animal protein much cheaper and more widely available, which is important for food security and nutrition, especially for poorer households; chicken and eggs are among the most affordable sources of high-quality protein in South Africa. Factory farms can control conditions such as temperature, feed and disease, producing consistent products all year round. The industry provides jobs on farms, in feed mills, abattoirs, processing plants and shops, and it supports grain farmers who grow maize and soybeans for animal feed.

Critics point to several serious costs.

Animal welfare is one of the main concerns. In many intensive systems, animals are kept at high densities with little space to move, cannot carry out natural behaviours such as foraging, dust-bathing or nesting, and may suffer from stress, injuries and health problems. Broiler chickens bred for very rapid growth can suffer from leg problems and heart failure. Battery cages give each hen very little space. Organisations such as the National Council of SPCAs in South Africa campaign for better welfare standards, and some countries, including the countries of the European Union, have banned conventional battery cages.

Human health is another concern. Many factory farms use antibiotics, not only to treat sick animals but sometimes to prevent disease in crowded conditions or to promote growth. Overuse of antibiotics contributes to antibiotic resistance, in which bacteria evolve so that antibiotics no longer work against them, which is a serious threat to human medicine. Crowded conditions can also allow diseases to spread rapidly; outbreaks of avian influenza, or bird flu, have led to the culling of millions of chickens in South Africa, most recently in 2023, causing egg and chicken shortages and price increases.

There are environmental costs. Large numbers of animals produce huge amounts of manure, which can pollute water with nutrients and bacteria if it is not managed properly, contributing to eutrophication. Feedlots and sheds can cause unpleasant smells and attract flies. Growing grain to feed animals uses large amounts of land, water, fertiliser and energy; it takes several kilograms of feed to produce one kilogram of beef, although chickens convert feed to meat much more efficiently than cattle. Livestock farming as a whole, especially cattle, produces significant greenhouse gas emissions, particularly methane.

Finally, there are economic concerns. Large companies can dominate the industry, making it hard for small farmers to compete. South Africa's poultry industry also faces competition from cheap imported chicken, which affects local jobs.

There are alternatives and improvements. Some farmers in South Africa raise free-range chickens or pasture-fed cattle, which give animals more space and natural behaviour, although these products usually cost more. Retailers and consumers can encourage higher welfare standards, and stricter rules on antibiotic use, manure management and stocking densities can reduce the harms of intensive systems. Eating smaller amounts of meat, especially beef, also reduces pressure on land, water and the climate.

So the next question is: what are genetically modified crops?

## Subtopic: Genetically Modified Crops

Every living thing contains genes, which are sections of DNA that carry instructions for its characteristics, such as size, colour, resistance to disease or ability to survive drought. For thousands of years, farmers have changed crops and animals by selective breeding: choosing plants or animals with useful traits and breeding from them, so that over many generations the traits become more common. Maize, for example, was developed from a wild grass called teosinte by farmers in Mexico thousands of years ago.

Genetic modification, also called genetic engineering, is a modern technique in which scientists change an organism's DNA directly in a laboratory, often by inserting a gene from another organism. The result is a genetically modified organism, or GMO. Genetic modification can introduce traits much faster than traditional breeding, and it can use genes from unrelated species, which is not possible through normal breeding.

The two most common traits in genetically modified crops are insect resistance and herbicide tolerance. Insect-resistant crops, often called Bt crops, contain a gene from a soil bacterium, Bacillus thuringiensis, which produces a protein that is toxic to certain insect pests, such as the maize stalk borer, but not to people or most other animals. This reduces crop losses and the need to spray insecticides. Herbicide-tolerant crops can survive being sprayed with a particular weedkiller, allowing farmers to control weeds without damaging the crop. Other genetically modified crops have been developed to tolerate drought or to contain extra nutrients; for example, Golden Rice was developed to contain beta-carotene, which the body uses to make vitamin A, to help prevent vitamin A deficiency, which causes blindness in children in parts of Asia.

South Africa was one of the first countries in Africa to grow genetically modified crops commercially, starting with Bt cotton in the late 1990s and Bt maize in 1998. It regulates genetically modified organisms under the Genetically Modified Organisms Act of 1997, which requires every new crop to be assessed for risks to human health and the environment before it is approved. Today, South Africa is the largest grower of genetically modified crops in Africa, and the great majority of its maize, soybeans and cotton are genetically modified varieties. This means that many South Africans eat food made from genetically modified maize every day, in the form of maize meal and other products.

So the final question is: what are the arguments for and against genetically modified crops?

## Subtopic: The Genetic Modification Debate, and the Error Museum

Supporters of genetically modified crops make several arguments. They can increase yields by reducing losses to pests and weeds, which helps food security. Bt crops can reduce the amount of insecticide sprayed, which benefits farm workers and the environment. Herbicide-tolerant crops make it easier to use conservation agriculture, in which farmers do not plough, because weeds can be controlled by spraying instead, reducing soil erosion. Drought-tolerant and nutrient-enriched varieties could help farmers cope with climate change and improve nutrition. Major scientific organisations, including the World Health Organization and national academies of science, have concluded that the genetically modified foods currently approved on the market are as safe to eat as their conventional equivalents.

Critics raise several concerns. Some worry about long-term environmental effects, such as genes spreading from genetically modified crops to wild relatives or to non-genetically modified crops through pollen, and effects on biodiversity. Pests and weeds can evolve resistance: in South Africa, the maize stalk borer developed resistance to the first type of Bt maize within about a decade of its introduction, and herbicide-resistant weeds have appeared in several countries where herbicide-tolerant crops are widely grown. There are economic and social concerns too. Genetically modified seeds are patented and sold by a small number of large multinational companies, and farmers usually have to buy new seed every season rather than saving their own, which can increase costs and dependence, especially for small-scale farmers. Some people argue that the main causes of hunger are poverty and inequality, not low yields, so genetically modified crops do not address the root problem. Others are concerned about consumers' right to know what they are eating; South Africa's consumer protection regulations require labelling of some genetically modified products, but many people feel labelling is not clear or complete. Some people also object to genetic modification on ethical or religious grounds.

Overall, the evidence suggests that genetically modified crops are neither a miracle cure nor a disaster. Their effects depend on the particular crop and trait, how they are managed, and who controls and benefits from them. Strong regulation, independent research, measures to manage resistance, fair access for small farmers and clear information for consumers are all important.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Factory farming has no benefits." It produces large amounts of affordable protein, such as chicken and eggs, which is important for food security.

Exhibit two: "Changing plants' genes is completely new." Farmers have changed crops by selective breeding for thousands of years; genetic modification is a faster, more direct method.

Exhibit three: "Approved GM foods are known to be poisonous." Major scientific bodies have concluded that approved GM foods are as safe to eat as conventional foods.

Exhibit four: "GM crops solve every farming problem." Pests can become resistant, seed costs and corporate control are concerns, and hunger is mainly caused by poverty.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a chicken city, a recipe with a new ingredient, and a scale with two pans.

## Subtopic: A Chicken City

Imagine a huge shed, as long as a football field, with tens of thousands of chickens inside. Lights, food and water are controlled by machines. The chickens grow so fast that they are ready in about six weeks. That is a chicken city, and it is an example of factory farming.

Factory farming means raising lots of animals in small spaces to make meat, eggs and milk as cheaply as possible. Most chicken and eggs in South African shops come from farms like this. Many cattle also spend their last months in feedlots, where they are fed grain to fatten them quickly.

The good side: chicken and eggs are cheaper, so more families can afford protein. Factory farms create many jobs, from the farms to the shops.

The worrying side: animals may be crowded and unable to move freely. Diseases like bird flu can spread fast, and millions of chickens have had to be killed. Too many antibiotics can make germs stronger, so medicines stop working for people too. Lots of manure can pollute rivers and dams nearby.

So what about the plants we grow?

## Subtopic: A Recipe With a New Ingredient

Every living thing has a recipe inside it, called its genes. Genes decide things like how tall a plant grows or whether insects can eat it.

For thousands of years, farmers changed plants slowly by saving seeds from the best ones. Genetic modification is a faster way. Scientists add a new ingredient to the plant's recipe, often a gene from another living thing.

For example, scientists took a gene from a soil bacterium and put it into maize. Now the maize makes its own protection against stalk borers, caterpillars that eat maize stems, so farmers need to spray less poison.

South Africa grows more GM crops than any other country in Africa. Most of our maize is GM, so many of us eat it every day in our pap.

So is GM food good or bad?

## Subtopic: A Scale With Two Pans

Picture an old-fashioned scale with two pans. On one side, put the good points. On the other, put the worries. Then see how they balance.

Good points for GM crops: bigger harvests, less insect spray, and crops that might survive drought or carry extra vitamins. Top scientific bodies say approved GM foods are safe to eat.

Worries: insects can become resistant, as stalk borers did in South Africa. A few big companies own the seeds, and farmers must buy new seed every year. Some people want clearer labels so they know what they are eating.

The same scale works for factory farming: cheap protein and jobs on one side, animal welfare, disease and pollution on the other. Different people may weigh these things differently, and that is why it is important to know the facts before you decide.

Here is the line to remember: factory farming and GM crops help produce more affordable food, but they also raise real concerns, so we must weigh the evidence on both sides and manage them carefully.
