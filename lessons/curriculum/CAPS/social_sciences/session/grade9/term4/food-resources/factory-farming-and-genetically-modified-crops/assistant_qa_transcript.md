### subtopic_1 — What Factory Farming Is
**Assistant:** Why is chicken so much cheaper than beef in South Africa?

**Tutor:** Chicken is generally cheaper than beef for several reasons, most of which are linked to biology and the efficiency of intensive production. First, chickens grow extremely quickly. Modern broiler chickens have been bred to reach market weight in about five to six weeks, while beef cattle usually take one and a half to two years or more before slaughter. This means that a poultry farmer can produce many batches of chickens in a year in the same shed, while a cattle farmer must feed and care for each animal for much longer. Second, chickens convert feed into meat much more efficiently than cattle. A broiler chicken needs roughly two kilograms of feed to gain one kilogram of body weight, while cattle need several times more feed per kilogram of weight gain, partly because they are larger and use more energy just to maintain their bodies. Third, chickens can be raised at high densities in climate-controlled sheds, using relatively little land, and many tasks, such as feeding, watering and ventilation, are automated, which reduces labour costs. Fourth, the poultry industry in South Africa is highly organised and integrated, with large companies controlling breeding, feed production, farming, slaughter and processing, which creates economies of scale. Finally, South Africa also imports large quantities of cheap chicken, which keeps prices low, although this has been controversial because it competes with local producers. The low price of chicken is important for food security, because it gives many poorer households access to affordable protein. However, critics argue that the low price does not include hidden costs, such as the welfare of the animals, the environmental impacts of growing feed and managing manure, and the risks of antibiotic resistance, which society may pay for in other ways.

### subtopic_2 — Benefits and Costs of Factory Farming
**Assistant:** How does antibiotic use in farm animals affect human health?

**Tutor:** Antibiotics are medicines that kill bacteria or stop them from growing. They have saved countless human lives since they came into wide use in the 1940s. In farming, antibiotics are used to treat sick animals, which is important for animal welfare and food safety. In some intensive farming systems, however, antibiotics have also been given routinely to healthy animals in their feed or water, either to prevent diseases that spread easily in crowded conditions or, in some countries, because small doses can make animals grow faster. The problem is that whenever antibiotics are used, bacteria that happen to be resistant to them survive and multiply, while the others die. Over time, this produces populations of resistant bacteria. When antibiotics are used widely and often, especially at low doses over long periods, resistance develops faster. Resistant bacteria can then spread from animals to people in several ways: through contact with animals, through meat that is not handled or cooked properly, and through manure that contaminates soil and water. Genes for resistance can also pass between different types of bacteria. As a result, some infections in people become much harder or even impossible to treat, which the World Health Organization describes as one of the greatest threats to global health. It is important to note that human overuse of antibiotics, for example taking them for viral infections such as colds, against which they do not work, is also a major cause of resistance. Many countries, including the countries of the European Union, have banned the use of antibiotics to make animals grow faster, and South Africa has a national strategy to reduce the misuse of antibiotics in both people and animals. Better hygiene, vaccination and lower stocking densities can reduce the need for antibiotics on farms.

### subtopic_3 — Genetically Modified Crops
**Assistant:** How did stalk borers become resistant to Bt maize in South Africa?

**Tutor:** Bt maize contains a gene from the bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis that makes the plant produce a protein that is toxic to certain caterpillars, including the maize stalk borer, Busseola fusca, an important pest in South Africa. When Bt maize was introduced in 1998, it was very effective, and farmers could reduce their use of insecticide sprays. However, insects can evolve resistance in the same way that bacteria evolve resistance to antibiotics. In any large population of stalk borers, a few individuals may, by chance, carry genes that allow them to survive the Bt protein. When Bt maize is planted widely, the susceptible insects die, but the rare resistant ones survive and breed, passing on their resistance. Over many generations, the proportion of resistant insects increases. To slow this process, farmers who plant Bt maize are required to plant refuges, which are areas of non-Bt maize near the Bt crop. Susceptible insects survive in the refuges and mate with any resistant insects from the Bt fields, so that resistance genes are diluted and spread more slowly. In South Africa, however, many farmers did not plant refuges properly, partly because the rules were not well understood or enforced. In addition, the first Bt maize varieties produced a relatively low dose of the toxin against this particular pest, which made it easier for partly resistant insects to survive. By the mid-2000s, about eight years after Bt maize was introduced, farmers and scientists reported stalk borers surviving on Bt maize, and resistance was confirmed by research. Seed companies responded by introducing new varieties with two different Bt proteins, which are harder for insects to overcome. The case is now used worldwide as a lesson in managing resistance carefully.

### subtopic_4 — The Genetic Modification Debate, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Should consumers have the right to know if their food is genetically modified?

**Tutor:** Many people believe that consumers have a right to know what is in their food, including whether it contains genetically modified ingredients, and South Africa's Consumer Protection Act and its regulations include requirements for labelling some genetically modified foods. Supporters of labelling argue that it respects consumer choice: people may wish to avoid genetically modified foods for personal, ethical, religious, environmental or economic reasons, even if scientific bodies consider approved products safe to eat, just as people may choose halal, kosher, organic or vegetarian food. They also argue that labelling promotes transparency and trust in the food system and allows long-term monitoring. Opponents of compulsory labelling argue that it can be misleading, because a label may suggest that genetically modified foods are dangerous when the scientific evidence does not show that approved products are less safe than conventional ones. They point out that labelling and testing add costs, which may increase food prices, an important concern in a country where many households struggle to afford food. In South Africa, where most maize is genetically modified, it can also be difficult and costly to separate genetically modified and non-genetically modified grain throughout the supply chain, so many products would need labels saying they may contain genetically modified ingredients, which some say gives little useful information. There are also debates about what level of genetically modified content should trigger a label. A balanced view recognises that both sides raise reasonable points. Clear, accurate labelling that informs without frightening consumers, combined with good public education about how genetically modified crops are made and regulated, may be the best way to respect people's choices while keeping food affordable.
