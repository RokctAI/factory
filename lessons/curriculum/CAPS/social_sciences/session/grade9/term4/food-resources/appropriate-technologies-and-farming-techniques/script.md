# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains appropriate technologies and farming techniques that help people grow food sustainably. It covers the meaning of appropriate technology and why it matters for small-scale farmers; appropriate technologies for managing water, such as rainwater harvesting and drip irrigation; farming techniques that protect and improve soil and control pests naturally, such as composting, mulching, crop rotation, intercropping and push-pull; and the use of these methods by small-scale farmers and food gardens in South Africa, including indigenous crops and ways of reducing losses after harvest. The anchor idea is that appropriate technology is technology that fits the people and place where it is used: it is affordable, simple to use and repair with local skills and materials, and kind to the environment. The governing idea is that combining appropriate technology with good farming techniques and local knowledge can increase food production and food security without depending on expensive inputs.

## Subtopic: What Appropriate Technology Means

Not every technology suits every situation. A large combine harvester may be ideal for a commercial grain farm of thousands of hectares on the Highveld, but it is of little use to a farmer with one hectare on a steep slope in the Eastern Cape, who cannot afford to buy it, run it or repair it. Expensive inputs such as hybrid seed, chemical fertilisers and pesticides, which you studied in the previous lessons, can raise yields, but many small-scale farmers cannot afford them every season, and they may cause debt and environmental harm.

Appropriate technology is technology that is suited to the social, economic and environmental conditions of the place and people where it is used. The idea was made popular by the British economist E.F. Schumacher in his book Small is Beautiful, published in 1973, in which he argued for intermediate technology: tools that are more productive than traditional methods but much cheaper and simpler than the high technology of rich countries.

Appropriate technologies usually share several features. They are affordable for the people who will use them. They are simple to use, maintain and repair, using local skills, tools and materials, so users do not depend on outside experts or imported spare parts. They use local resources, including local knowledge. They are often small in scale and labour-intensive, creating work rather than replacing it, which matters in places with high unemployment. And they are environmentally sustainable, using little energy, conserving water and soil and producing little pollution.

Appropriate technology does not mean old-fashioned or primitive. It can include modern inventions, such as solar panels, mobile phones and drip irrigation kits, as long as they suit local needs. For example, the Hippo Water Roller, invented in South Africa in the early 1990s, is a large plastic drum that can be rolled along the ground, allowing people to carry about ninety litres of water at a time instead of carrying a heavy bucket on their heads.

In farming, appropriate technologies and techniques are especially important for small-scale and subsistence farmers, who produce a large share of the food eaten in Africa, and for households and communities growing food in gardens.

A useful test is to ask four questions about any technology. Is it affordable for the people who will use it? Can it be made, used and repaired with local skills and materials? Does it suit the local climate, soil and culture? And does it protect the environment rather than harm it? If the answer to all four is yes, the technology is appropriate for that place. The same machine can be appropriate in one place and inappropriate in another: a large tractor may suit a commercial farm of thousands of hectares, but it is not appropriate for a family with one hectare, no money for fuel and no mechanic nearby.

So the next question is: how can farmers manage water better?

## Subtopic: Appropriate Technologies for Water

Water is often the biggest limit on farming in South Africa, where rainfall is low and unreliable in most areas. Several appropriate technologies help farmers make the most of every drop.

Rainwater harvesting means collecting and storing rain where it falls. Gutters on roofs can channel rainwater into tanks, such as the plastic tanks now common in South African towns and villages, or into underground storage tanks built of brick or concrete. The stored water can then be used to irrigate gardens in dry periods. In fields, farmers can build small earth banks, ridges or furrows along the contours to stop runoff and let water soak into the soil.

Planting pits are an important example. In the Sahel region of West Africa, farmers such as Yacouba Sawadogo in Burkina Faso revived a traditional method called zaï: digging small pits, about twenty to forty centimetres wide, in hard, crusted soil, and filling them with manure or compost before planting. The pits catch rainwater and concentrate nutrients around the plant's roots, and termites attracted by the manure dig tunnels that help water soak in. On land where nothing grew, zaï pits have produced crops and trees.

Drip irrigation delivers water slowly and directly to the roots of plants through pipes or tubes with small holes, called emitters. Because water is not sprayed into the air or spread over the whole field, much less is lost to evaporation and runoff. Drip irrigation can use up to about half as much water as flood irrigation for the same crop. Simple, low-cost drip kits, fed by gravity from a raised tank or drum, are suitable for small farms and food gardens, and even a plastic bottle with small holes buried next to a plant can work as a simple drip system.

Grey water, which is water that has already been used for washing, can be reused to water gardens, as long as it does not contain strong chemicals. Treadle pumps, which are operated by stepping up and down on pedals, and small solar-powered pumps allow farmers to lift water from rivers or wells without expensive fuel.

Mulching, covering the soil around plants with a layer of dry grass, leaves, straw or crop remains, also saves water, because it keeps the soil cool, reduces evaporation and stops weeds that compete for water.

Rainwater harvesting is another simple method. Water running off a roof can be led by gutters into a tank, and water running off a field can be guided into small dams or pits. In a dry country like South Africa, where much of the land receives less than 500 millimetres of rain a year, every litre saved in a wet month can keep a garden alive in a dry month. Grey water from washing can also be reused on plants, as long as it does not contain strong chemicals.

So the next question is: which techniques protect soil and control pests?

## Subtopic: Techniques for Soil and Pest Control

Healthy soil is the foundation of good farming. Several techniques improve soil fertility without expensive chemical fertilisers.

Composting turns organic waste, such as vegetable peelings, garden cuttings, leaves and manure, into compost, a dark, crumbly material rich in nutrients and humus. Added to soil, compost feeds plants, improves the soil's structure and helps it hold water. Animal manure can be used in a similar way. Trench beds, which are deep trenches filled with layers of organic material, such as cardboard, tins, leaves, grass and manure, and topped with soil, are widely used in South African food gardens; they hold water and release nutrients for several years.

Crop rotation means growing different crops in the same field in a planned sequence over different seasons or years, rather than growing the same crop every time. Different crops use different nutrients and are attacked by different pests and diseases, so rotation helps prevent the soil from being exhausted and breaks the life cycles of pests. Legumes, such as beans, peas, cowpeas and groundnuts, are especially useful in rotations because bacteria in nodules on their roots take nitrogen from the air and add it to the soil, acting as a natural fertiliser for the next crop.

Intercropping means growing two or more crops together in the same field at the same time. A traditional example in southern Africa is growing maize with beans and pumpkins. The maize provides a stalk for the beans to climb, the beans add nitrogen, and the pumpkins' large leaves cover the ground, keeping it moist and shading out weeds. Intercropping also spreads risk: if one crop fails, the others may still produce food.

Conservation agriculture, which you met in Term 3, combines minimum soil disturbance, permanent soil cover with mulch or cover crops, and crop rotation. It reduces erosion, builds organic matter and saves labour and fuel, and it is promoted for small-scale farmers as well as commercial farms.

Pests can also be controlled naturally. Integrated pest management combines several methods, such as encouraging natural predators like ladybirds and birds, planting pest-repelling plants such as marigolds and garlic, removing infected plants, and using chemical pesticides only as a last resort. A successful example from East Africa is push-pull technology, developed by scientists in Kenya. Farmers plant a legume called desmodium between rows of maize, which pushes away stem borer moths with its smell and suppresses the parasitic weed striga, and plant Napier grass around the field, which pulls the moths away from the maize and traps their larvae. Push-pull has increased maize yields for thousands of small farmers while also providing fodder for livestock.

Agroforestry means growing trees together with crops or livestock. Trees provide shade, fruit, firewood and fodder, protect soil from wind and water erosion, and some, such as certain acacias, add nitrogen to the soil.

So the final question is: how are these methods used in South Africa?

## Subtopic: Small-Scale Farming and Food Gardens in South Africa, and the Error Museum

South Africa has a dual farming system. A relatively small number of large commercial farms produce most of the country's marketed food, while millions of households practise small-scale farming or keep food gardens, mainly in rural areas and former homelands but also in towns and cities. For many of these households, growing some of their own food is an important way of improving food security, saving money and getting fresh, nutritious vegetables.

Food gardens are found at homes, schools, clinics, churches and community centres. Many schools run gardens with the support of government and non-governmental organisations, linked to the National School Nutrition Programme, so that learners can eat fresh vegetables and learn gardening skills. Urban agriculture projects, such as community gardens on unused land in the Cape Flats and in Soweto, provide food and income in cities.

Indigenous and traditional crops are an important part of appropriate farming. Crops such as sorghum, millet, cowpeas, bambara groundnuts and amaranth, known in many South African languages as morogo or imifino, are adapted to local conditions, often tolerate drought and heat better than maize, and are highly nutritious. Many communities have grown them for centuries, and there is renewed interest in them as a way to cope with climate change. Community seed banks allow farmers to save and share seeds of local varieties, preserving biodiversity and reducing dependence on bought seed.

Reducing losses after harvest is just as important as growing more. Much food is lost to pests, mould and spoilage during storage. Appropriate technologies such as airtight storage bags and containers, which stop insects from breathing and multiplying in stored grain, and solar dryers, which use the sun's heat to dry fruit and vegetables so they can be stored, help farmers keep more of what they grow.

Information technology also helps. Mobile phones allow farmers to receive weather forecasts, advice on pests and planting, and market prices, and to sell their produce.

Appropriate technology has limits. It works best when farmers also have secure access to land and water, training and extension support, credit, and roads and markets to sell their surplus. It cannot by itself solve problems such as unemployment and land inequality. But it gives people practical tools to improve their own food security in ways they can afford and control.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Appropriate technology means old, primitive tools." It means technology suited to local needs and resources, which can include modern inventions such as drip kits and solar pumps.

Exhibit two: "Only large, expensive machines can increase yields." Methods such as zaï pits, push-pull and composting have greatly increased small farmers' yields.

Exhibit three: "Planting the same crop every year keeps soil healthy." Crop rotation, especially with legumes, restores nutrients and breaks pest cycles.

Exhibit four: "Indigenous crops are not worth growing." Crops such as sorghum, cowpeas and morogo are nutritious and often more drought-tolerant than maize.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: the right key for the lock, a bottle that waters a plant, and three sisters in a field.

## Subtopic: The Right Key for the Lock

A key only works if it fits the lock. A shiny, expensive key that does not fit is useless.

Farming tools are the same. A giant tractor is perfect for a huge farm, but a family with a small field on a hillside cannot afford it, cannot fix it and cannot even turn it around.

Appropriate technology means tools and methods that fit the people using them. They are cheap, simple to use and fix with local materials, and kind to the environment. They can be very old ideas or brand-new inventions.

In Burkina Faso, a farmer named Yacouba Sawadogo dug small pits in dry, hard ground and filled them with manure. The pits caught the rain, and crops and trees grew where there had been desert. His tools were a hoe, manure and know-how. That is appropriate technology.

So how can farmers save water?

## Subtopic: A Bottle That Waters a Plant

Take a plastic bottle, poke a few small holes in it, bury it next to a plant and fill it with water. The water drips slowly to the roots, and almost none is wasted. That is the idea behind drip irrigation, which can use far less water than flooding a field.

Farmers and gardeners can catch rain from their roofs in tanks. They can reuse washing water on their gardens. They can cover the soil with dry grass, called mulch, so the sun does not dry it out.

In a dry country like South Africa, every drop counts.

So how can farmers feed the soil and fight pests without expensive chemicals?

## Subtopic: Three Sisters in a Field

Picture three sisters who help each other: maize, beans and pumpkins planted together. The maize stands tall so the beans can climb it. The beans add natural fertiliser to the soil. The pumpkin's big leaves cover the ground, keeping it damp and blocking weeds.

Farmers also change crops each season, so the soil does not get tired and pests do not settle in. They turn kitchen scraps and manure into rich compost. They plant flowers and smelly plants that keep insects away.

Many South African families and schools grow food gardens like this. Old local crops such as sorghum, cowpeas and morogo grow well even when it is dry, and they are very healthy.

Here is the line to remember: appropriate technologies and smart farming techniques, like catching rain, drip watering, compost and growing crops together, help people grow more food with what they have, while protecting the land.
