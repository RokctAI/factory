### subtopic_1 — What Food Security Means
**Assistant:** How can South Africa be food secure nationally but have hungry households?

**Tutor:** This apparent contradiction is explained by the difference between national food availability and household access. At the national level, South Africa's farms, together with imports, provide enough food for everyone in the country. The country produces most of its staple maize in a normal year, is a major exporter of fruit and wine, and has well-developed food processing, storage and retail systems. So the first pillar of food security, availability, is largely met. However, food security also requires access, which means that each household must be able to obtain enough nutritious food. In South Africa, access is the main problem. Unemployment is very high, and many households have little or no income from work. Many depend on social grants, which help but are small compared with the cost of a healthy diet. Most food must be bought, because most South Africans, especially in cities and in former homelands, do not have land or resources to grow their own food, a legacy partly of apartheid land policies. When food prices rise, for example because of drought, rising fuel costs or global price changes, poor households must cut back, often by buying cheaper, less nutritious food or skipping meals. Utilisation is also a problem in some areas, because households may lack clean water, sanitation or affordable fuel for cooking, and illness may reduce the body's ability to use nutrients. Stability is affected by droughts, job losses and crises such as the COVID-19 pandemic, when hunger rose sharply. So, while South Africa as a whole is food secure, many individual households are not, which is why policies focus on income, jobs, grants, school meals and support for small farmers, not only on increasing production.

### subtopic_2 — Food Security in the World and in South Africa
**Assistant:** Why is stunting such a serious problem?

**Tutor:** Stunting means that a child is significantly shorter than expected for their age, according to international growth standards. It is caused mainly by long-term undernutrition, especially during the first one thousand days of life, from pregnancy until a child's second birthday, when the body and brain are developing most rapidly. Stunting is linked to a lack of nutritious food, including protein, vitamins and minerals, as well as to poor health of mothers during pregnancy, repeated infections such as diarrhoea, which prevent the body from absorbing nutrients, and a lack of clean water and sanitation. Stunting is serious because its effects are mostly permanent. Stunted children often have weaker immune systems, so they become ill more easily. Their brain development may be affected, which can lead to lower performance at school and fewer opportunities later in life. As adults, people who were stunted as children may earn less and are at higher risk of chronic diseases such as diabetes and heart disease. Stunting can also pass between generations, because women who were stunted as children are more likely to have smaller babies. In South Africa, about 27 percent of children under five were found to be stunted in the 2016 Demographic and Health Survey, which is high for a middle-income country that produces enough food. This shows that the problem is not just a shortage of calories, but a lack of nutritious, varied diets and of healthy living conditions. Addressing stunting requires support for mothers and young children, such as good antenatal care, breastfeeding support, nutritious foods, clean water and sanitation, child support grants and early childhood development programmes, because investing in the first years of life has lifelong benefits.

### subtopic_3 — Science, Technology and the Green Revolution
**Assistant:** Why was the Green Revolution less successful in Africa than in Asia?

**Tutor:** The Green Revolution transformed food production in much of Asia and Latin America in the 1960s and 1970s, but its impact in sub-Saharan Africa was much smaller, for several reasons. First, the crops were different. The Green Revolution focused on improving wheat and rice, which were the main staple foods in India, Pakistan, the Philippines and Mexico. In much of Africa, people relied more on maize, sorghum, millet, cassava, yams and other crops, which received much less research attention at the time. Second, the environmental conditions were different. The new varieties performed best with plenty of water and fertiliser. In Asia, large irrigated river plains, such as those of the Indus and Ganges, allowed this. In much of Africa, farming depended on unreliable rainfall, and irrigation was limited, while many African soils are old and weathered, with different nutrient problems. Third, infrastructure and markets were weaker. To use Green Revolution technology, farmers needed roads to receive fertiliser and seeds and to sell their surplus, credit to pay for inputs, storage facilities and agricultural extension officers to advise them. In many African countries, especially in rural areas, these were lacking. Fourth, governments in parts of Asia invested heavily in agriculture and supported farmers with subsidies and guaranteed prices, while many African governments in the decades after independence gave farming less support, and some faced conflict and economic crises. Fifth, many African farmers are smallholders farming a variety of crops on small plots, often with women doing much of the work, and technologies designed for large, single-crop fields did not always suit them. Since the 2000s, there have been renewed efforts to develop improved, drought-tolerant varieties of African crops and support small farmers, with some success.

### subtopic_4 — Benefits and Costs of Technology, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What is eutrophication, and why does it affect South African dams?

**Tutor:** Eutrophication is the process by which a body of water, such as a dam, lake or river, becomes over-enriched with nutrients, especially nitrogen and phosphorus. These nutrients act as fertiliser for algae and water plants, which then grow excessively. In the short term, this can turn the water green and cover it with thick mats of algae or floating plants such as water hyacinth. When the algae and plants die, they are decomposed by bacteria, which use up large amounts of oxygen dissolved in the water. Fish and other animals can then die from lack of oxygen. Some types of algae, called cyanobacteria or blue-green algae, can also produce toxins that are dangerous to people, livestock and pets, and the water becomes expensive to treat for drinking. The nutrients come from several sources. Agriculture contributes when fertilisers applied to fields are washed off by rain into streams, or when manure from livestock and feedlots enters water. Untreated or poorly treated sewage from towns is also a major source, especially where wastewater treatment works are overloaded or broken, as is the case in many South African municipalities. Detergents containing phosphates add to the problem. South Africa is particularly vulnerable because it relies heavily on dams for water storage, and many dams are downstream of large cities and farming areas. Hartbeespoort Dam, west of Pretoria, is one of the best-known examples, with a long history of algal blooms and water hyacinth infestations, linked to nutrients from Gauteng's wastewater and other sources. Solutions include improving sewage treatment, using fertilisers more carefully, for example through precision agriculture, keeping strips of vegetation along rivers to trap nutrients, and removing excess plants.
