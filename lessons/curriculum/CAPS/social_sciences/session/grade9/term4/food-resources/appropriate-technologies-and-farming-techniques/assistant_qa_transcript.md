### subtopic_1 — What Appropriate Technology Means
**Assistant:** Why might a high-tech solution fail in a poor rural community?

**Tutor:** A high-tech solution can fail in a poor rural community even if it works well elsewhere, because technology only succeeds when it fits the conditions in which it is used. First, there is cost. Expensive machines, imported equipment and inputs such as hybrid seed, chemical fertilisers and pesticides may be unaffordable for small-scale farmers, or may force them into debt that they cannot repay if a harvest fails. Second, there is maintenance. If a machine breaks down and spare parts must be imported or a specialist must be called from far away, it may stand idle for months or be abandoned. Many development projects have left behind broken pumps, tractors and irrigation systems for exactly this reason. Third, there is energy. Equipment that needs diesel or a reliable electricity supply may not work where fuel is expensive and the grid is unreliable or absent. Fourth, there are skills and knowledge. If the people using the technology were not involved in choosing it and were not trained to use and repair it, they may not be able to operate it properly. Fifth, the technology may not suit the land or the farming system. Large machines are difficult to use on small, steep or irregular fields, and methods designed for single-crop fields may not suit farmers who grow several crops together. Sixth, some technologies replace labour, which may increase unemployment in places where jobs are scarce. Finally, a technology may not match local needs or culture. Appropriate technology tries to avoid these problems by involving local people in choosing and designing solutions, using local materials and skills, keeping costs low and protecting the environment. This does not mean that advanced technology is never useful, but that it must fit the people and place.

### subtopic_2 — Appropriate Technologies for Water
**Assistant:** How do legumes act as a natural fertiliser?

**Tutor:** Legumes are a family of plants that includes beans, peas, cowpeas, lentils, groundnuts, soybeans, lucerne and many trees, such as acacias. They have a special relationship with certain soil bacteria, called rhizobia. When a legume grows, these bacteria enter its roots and form small swellings called nodules. Inside the nodules, the bacteria do something that plants cannot do on their own: they take nitrogen gas from the air in the soil and convert it into forms of nitrogen that plants can use. This process is called nitrogen fixation. Nitrogen is one of the most important nutrients for plant growth, needed to make proteins and the green pigment chlorophyll, and it is often the nutrient that limits crop yields. The air contains huge amounts of nitrogen, but most plants cannot use it directly, so farmers usually add nitrogen fertiliser, which is expensive and requires a lot of energy to manufacture. In return for the nitrogen, the legume supplies the bacteria with sugars made by photosynthesis. Legumes therefore enrich the soil with nitrogen. When their roots, leaves and stems decompose after harvest, or when they are ploughed into the soil as green manure, much of this nitrogen becomes available to the next crop. This is why farmers rotate legumes with crops such as maize, which need a lot of nitrogen, or grow them together through intercropping. Using legumes can reduce the amount of fertiliser needed, saving money and reducing the risk of nutrient pollution in rivers. Legumes also provide valuable food, because their seeds are rich in protein, which is especially important for households that cannot afford much meat, and their leaves and stems can feed livestock.

### subtopic_3 — Techniques for Soil and Pest Control
**Assistant:** How does push-pull farming work?

**Tutor:** Push-pull is a natural pest control method developed by scientists at the International Centre of Insect Physiology and Ecology in Kenya, working with farmers and researchers in East Africa. It was designed to deal with two major problems facing maize farmers: stem borers, which are moth larvae that tunnel into maize stems and destroy the crop, and striga, also called witchweed, a parasitic weed that attaches to maize roots and steals water and nutrients. Both can cause farmers to lose much of their harvest. In the push-pull system, farmers plant rows of a legume called desmodium between the rows of maize. Desmodium gives off a smell that repels stem borer moths, pushing them away from the maize. At the same time, a border of Napier grass, or another suitable grass, is planted around the edge of the field. Napier grass attracts the moths more strongly than maize does, pulling them away from the crop, so they lay their eggs on the grass instead. The grass also produces a sticky substance that traps many of the young larvae, so few survive. Desmodium has another important benefit: chemicals released by its roots stop striga seeds from developing properly, so the weed is greatly reduced. As a legume, desmodium also fixes nitrogen, improving the soil, and covers the ground, reducing erosion and keeping the soil moist. Both desmodium and Napier grass can be cut and fed to livestock, providing extra income from milk or meat. Studies have shown that push-pull can greatly increase maize yields for small farmers without chemical pesticides. Newer versions use drought-tolerant plants suited to drier areas. It is a good example of appropriate technology based on scientific research and farmers' knowledge working together.

### subtopic_4 — Small-Scale Farming and Food Gardens in South Africa, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why are indigenous crops getting new attention in South Africa?

**Tutor:** Indigenous and traditional crops, such as sorghum, pearl millet, cowpeas, bambara groundnuts, and leafy vegetables like amaranth, spider plant and pumpkin leaves, often called morogo or imifino, were once widely grown and eaten in South Africa. During the twentieth century, they were increasingly replaced by maize and by vegetables such as cabbage, and they were sometimes seen as food for the poor. Several developments have brought new attention to them. The first is climate change. Scientists predict that parts of South Africa will become hotter and drier, with more frequent droughts. Many indigenous crops are better adapted to heat, drought and poor soils than maize, which needs more water, so they could help farmers cope with a changing climate. The second is nutrition. Many indigenous crops are rich in protein, vitamins and minerals. Cowpeas and bambara groundnuts are good sources of protein, and leafy vegetables like amaranth are rich in iron, calcium and vitamins, which helps address hidden hunger, a lack of essential nutrients even when people eat enough calories. The third is cost. Many indigenous crops can be grown from saved seed, with little or no fertiliser or pesticide, so they suit small-scale farmers and food gardens. The fourth is biodiversity and heritage. Growing a wider range of crops reduces the risks of relying on a single crop and preserves knowledge passed down through generations, often by women farmers. South African researchers, the Agricultural Research Council and universities are now studying and promoting these crops, and community seed banks help preserve local varieties. Challenges remain, including limited markets, a lack of improved varieties and the perception that they are old-fashioned, but interest is clearly growing.
