# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the concept of food security and the role of science and technology in food production. It covers the meaning of food security and its four pillars of availability, access, utilisation and stability; food security and food insecurity in the world and in South Africa, including the causes of hunger; how science and technology have increased food production, including the Green Revolution, improved seeds, fertilisers, pesticides, irrigation and mechanisation; and the benefits and the environmental and social costs of these technologies, together with newer technologies that aim to produce food more sustainably. The anchor idea is that food security exists when all people, at all times, can get enough safe and nutritious food for an active and healthy life. The governing idea is that science and technology have greatly increased the amount of food produced, but hunger is mainly a problem of access and inequality, so producing more food is necessary but not enough on its own.

## Subtopic: What Food Security Means

The most widely used definition of food security comes from the World Food Summit, organised by the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations in 1996. It states that food security exists when all people, at all times, have physical and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food that meets their dietary needs and food preferences for an active and healthy life.

This definition has four main parts, often called the four pillars of food security.

The first pillar is availability. There must be enough food produced locally or imported to feed the population. Availability depends on farming, fishing, food processing, storage and trade.

The second pillar is access. People must be able to obtain the food that is available, either by growing it, buying it or receiving it through social support. Access depends mainly on income and food prices, but also on whether shops and markets are nearby and whether people can travel to them. A family may live next to a supermarket full of food, but if they have no money, they are not food secure.

The third pillar is utilisation. People must be able to use food properly to stay healthy. This depends on eating a varied and nutritious diet, not just enough calories; on having clean water, sanitation and fuel to cook; on knowing how to prepare food safely; and on good health, because illnesses such as diarrhoea prevent the body from absorbing nutrients.

The fourth pillar is stability. People must have reliable access to food at all times, not just in good years. Droughts, floods, sudden price increases, unemployment, conflict and pandemics can disrupt food security, even for people who are usually able to feed themselves.

Food insecurity is the opposite: a situation in which people lack secure access to enough safe and nutritious food. It ranges from worrying about having enough food, to reducing the quality or quantity of meals, to going whole days without eating. Long-term undernutrition in young children causes stunting, which means being too short for one's age, and can permanently affect their physical and mental development. Food insecurity can also take the form of eating enough calories but not enough nutritious food, which can contribute to obesity and diseases such as diabetes, sometimes in the same communities where others are undernourished.

So the next question is: how food secure are the world and South Africa?

## Subtopic: Food Security in the World and in South Africa

The world produces enough food to feed everyone. Yet, according to the United Nations, roughly 700 to 800 million people, about one in eleven people on earth, faced hunger in recent years, and many more could not afford a healthy diet. Hunger is most common in parts of sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, and in countries affected by conflict, such as Sudan, Yemen and Somalia. At the same time, large amounts of food are wasted, especially in wealthy countries.

South Africa is food secure at the national level. It produces most of the food it needs, including maize, which is the staple food for most South Africans, as well as wheat, sugar, fruit, vegetables, meat, milk and eggs, and it exports large quantities of citrus, grapes, wine, maize and other products. However, many South African households are food insecure. Surveys by Statistics South Africa have found that about one in five households report inadequate or severely inadequate access to food, and the South African Demographic and Health Survey of 2016 found that about 27 percent of children under five were stunted. Food insecurity is highest in poor rural areas, especially in provinces such as the Eastern Cape, KwaZulu-Natal and Limpopo, and in informal settlements in the cities.

The main cause of food insecurity in South Africa is lack of access, not lack of food. High unemployment means that many households have little income, and food prices have risen sharply at times, for example during droughts and after global price shocks. Many poor households spend a large share of their income on food, so price increases hit them hardest. Apartheid land policies left most black South Africans without land to farm, and many rural households today produce little of their own food. Other causes include drought and climate change, which affect harvests; poor nutrition knowledge and the high cost of nutritious foods such as vegetables, fruit, eggs and meat compared with cheap, energy-dense foods; and diseases such as HIV and tuberculosis, which affect households' ability to work and absorb nutrients.

The South African government addresses food insecurity in several ways. The Constitution states that everyone has the right to have access to sufficient food and water. Social grants, especially the Child Support Grant, which reaches millions of children, help households buy food. The National School Nutrition Programme provides a cooked meal every school day to millions of learners in poorer schools. Government and non-governmental programmes also support food gardens and small-scale farmers. During the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, the government introduced a temporary Social Relief of Distress grant, partly because hunger rose sharply during lockdown.

So the next question is: how have science and technology increased food production?

## Subtopic: Science, Technology and the Green Revolution

For most of history, farmers relied on human and animal labour, simple tools, natural rainfall and seeds saved from their own crops. Food production increased mainly by farming more land. Over the past two centuries, and especially since the mid-twentieth century, science and technology have transformed farming, allowing far more food to be produced on the same area of land.

Mechanisation means using machines, such as tractors, combine harvesters, planters and irrigation pumps, to do farm work. Machines allow a small number of workers to farm very large areas quickly. In South Africa, large commercial grain farms on the Highveld are highly mechanised.

Fertilisers add nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium to the soil. In the early twentieth century, scientists developed a way to make nitrogen fertiliser from the air on an industrial scale, which allowed farmers to increase their yields greatly. Pesticides, which kill insects that damage crops, herbicides, which kill weeds, and fungicides, which kill plant diseases, also reduce crop losses.

Irrigation supplies water to crops artificially, through canals, sprinklers, centre pivots or drip systems, allowing farming in dry areas and in dry seasons. In South Africa, irrigated farming along rivers such as the Orange, Vaal and Olifants produces a large share of the country's fruit, vegetables and wine, and irrigation uses more than half of the country's water.

Plant and animal breeding has produced varieties that give higher yields, resist diseases and pests, or tolerate drought. Hybrid seeds, which are produced by crossing two different parent plants, give strong, high-yielding crops, though farmers usually need to buy new seed each year.

The most famous example of science increasing food production is the Green Revolution. From the 1940s to the 1970s, scientists, led by the American plant breeder Norman Borlaug, developed new high-yielding varieties of wheat and rice. These varieties had shorter, stronger stems, which allowed them to carry heavier heads of grain without falling over, and they responded well to fertiliser and irrigation. When they were introduced in Mexico, India, Pakistan and other parts of Asia in the 1960s, together with fertilisers, pesticides and irrigation, yields rose dramatically. India, which had faced the threat of famine in the mid-1960s, became self-sufficient in wheat within a few years. Borlaug received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970, and the Green Revolution is often said to have saved hundreds of millions of people from hunger.

Modern technology continues to change farming. Biotechnology includes genetic modification of crops, which you will study in the next lesson. Precision agriculture uses satellite navigation, drones, soil sensors and computer data to apply water, fertiliser and pesticides only where they are needed. Weather forecasting and mobile phone apps give farmers information about weather, prices and pests. Improved storage, refrigeration and transport reduce food losses after harvest.

So the final question is: what are the costs of these technologies?

## Subtopic: Benefits and Costs of Technology, and the Error Museum

The benefits of science and technology in food production are enormous. World food production has grown faster than the world's population over the past sixty years, so that the amount of food available per person has increased, even as the population more than doubled. Food prices fell in real terms for much of this period, and the share of the world's population that is undernourished has fallen. Higher yields also mean that less natural land has had to be cleared for farming than would otherwise have been needed.

However, these technologies have also had costs. Environmental costs include the overuse of fertilisers, which washes into rivers and dams, causing eutrophication, the excessive growth of algae and water plants that use up oxygen and kill fish; South Africa's Hartbeespoort Dam has long suffered from this problem. Pesticides can harm pollinating insects such as bees, birds, fish and farm workers, and pests can become resistant to them. Over-irrigation can cause waterlogging and salinisation, which is the build-up of salt in soils, and can drain rivers and aquifers. Monocultures, where huge areas are planted with a single crop variety, reduce biodiversity and make crops vulnerable to new diseases. Mechanised farming uses large amounts of fossil fuel, and farming as a whole is a major source of greenhouse gases.

Social costs have also been debated. The Green Revolution's seeds worked best with fertilisers, pesticides and irrigation, which richer farmers could afford more easily than poor ones, so in some areas inequality grew and small farmers fell into debt. Mechanisation reduced the number of jobs on farms, contributing to rural unemployment, including in South Africa, where farm employment fell sharply over the last decades of the twentieth century. Farmers who buy hybrid or patented seeds every year become dependent on seed companies. The Green Revolution also had less success in sub-Saharan Africa, where crops, soils, rainfall and infrastructure were different, and where many farmers grow crops such as sorghum, millet and cassava that received less research attention.

These lessons have shaped newer approaches. Many scientists and organisations now aim for sustainable intensification, which means producing more food on existing farmland while reducing environmental harm, through methods such as conservation agriculture, efficient drip irrigation, integrated pest management, which combines natural predators with limited pesticide use, and improved, drought-tolerant varieties suited to small farmers. The next two lessons look at factory farming and genetic modification, and at appropriate technologies.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "If a country produces enough food, everyone is food secure." South Africa produces enough food, yet about one in five households has inadequate access to food.

Exhibit two: "Food security just means having enough calories." It also requires safe, nutritious food, clean water and stable access at all times.

Exhibit three: "The Green Revolution had only benefits." It raised yields enormously but also caused pollution, pesticide problems, water overuse and greater inequality in some areas.

Exhibit four: "Hunger is caused mainly by a shortage of food in the world." The world produces enough food; hunger is caused mainly by poverty, inequality, conflict and price shocks.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a full shop and an empty pocket, a four-legged table, and a magic seed with a price tag.

## Subtopic: A Full Shop and an Empty Pocket

Imagine a supermarket packed with bread, maize meal, vegetables and meat. Now imagine standing outside with no money in your pocket. The food is there, but you cannot get it.

That is the story of hunger in South Africa. Our farms grow enough food for everyone, and we even sell lots of fruit and wine to other countries. But many families do not have enough money, because so many people have no jobs. When food prices go up, poor families are hit the hardest.

About one in five South African households sometimes does not have enough food. Many young children do not grow as tall as they should, because they have not had enough nutritious food.

Food security means that everyone, all the time, can get enough safe, healthy food. It is not only about how much food a country grows. It is about whether every person can actually get it and eat well.

So what does food security need?

## Subtopic: A Four-Legged Table

Think of food security as a table with four legs. If one leg breaks, the table falls.

Leg one is having enough food: farms must grow it, or it must be brought in. Leg two is being able to get the food: having money, or land to grow it, or help such as grants and school meals. Leg three is using the food well: eating a mix of healthy foods, with clean water and safe cooking. Leg four is keeping it steady: having food every day, even during a drought or when prices jump.

South Africa helps keep the table standing with child support grants, school meals for millions of learners, and food gardens. The Constitution even says everyone has the right to enough food and water.

So how has science helped produce more food?

## Subtopic: A Magic Seed With a Price Tag

About sixty years ago, a scientist named Norman Borlaug bred new kinds of wheat with short, strong stems that could carry lots of grain. With fertiliser and water, these seeds produced far more food. India, which had feared famine, soon grew enough wheat for itself. This was called the Green Revolution, and it saved millions of people from hunger.

Science also gave farmers tractors, irrigation, fertilisers and sprays to kill pests. Today, farmers even use drones and satellites.

But the magic seed came with a price tag. Too much fertiliser pollutes rivers and dams. Pest sprays can kill bees and harm farm workers. Irrigation can drain rivers. Machines replaced many farm jobs. Poor farmers often could not afford the seeds, fertiliser and water the new crops needed.

Here is the line to remember: science and technology help farmers grow far more food, but food security also needs every person to be able to get and use healthy food, every day, without harming the land.
