# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbour on 7 December 1941 and its significance in World War II. It covers the causes of the conflict between Japan and the United States, especially Japanese expansion in Asia and the struggle over oil; the planning and execution of the attack; the American and global response; and the consequences in the Pacific during 1942, including the Battle of Midway. The anchor date is 7 December 1941, which President Franklin D. Roosevelt called "a date which will live in infamy". The governing idea is that Pearl Harbour was a tactical success for Japan but a strategic mistake, because it united the United States behind a war that its industrial strength made Japan very unlikely to win.

## Subtopic: Japanese Expansion and the Road to War

To understand Pearl Harbour, start with Japan's situation in the 1930s. Japan was an industrialised island nation with a growing population but very few natural resources of its own: little oil, iron ore, rubber or tin. Its military leaders, who gained increasing control over the government during the 1930s, believed Japan needed an empire in Asia to secure raw materials and markets, just as European powers had built empires. Many Japanese also resented Western racism, such as the rejection of a racial equality clause at the Paris Peace Conference in 1919 and American laws that excluded Japanese immigrants in 1924. The Great Depression, which hit Japanese silk exports hard, strengthened the argument for expansion.

In 1931 the Japanese army seized Manchuria in north-eastern China and turned it into a puppet state called Manchukuo. When the League of Nations condemned this, Japan simply left the League in 1933, another sign of the weakness of collective security that you saw with Germany. In July 1937 Japan launched a full-scale war against China. Japanese forces captured the capital Nanjing in December 1937 and committed the Nanjing Massacre, in which tens of thousands, and by many estimates hundreds of thousands, of Chinese civilians and prisoners were murdered and many women raped. China fought on, and the war became a costly stalemate.

In September 1940 Japan signed the Tripartite Pact with Germany and Italy, forming the Axis alliance. With France defeated by Germany and the Netherlands occupied, their colonies in Asia seemed open. Japan moved into northern French Indochina in 1940 and southern Indochina in July 1941, placing its forces within reach of the oil of the Dutch East Indies, today Indonesia, and the rubber and tin of British Malaya.

The United States, which opposed Japanese expansion and supported China, responded with economic pressure. In July 1941 it froze Japanese assets in America, and from August 1941 it cut off oil exports to Japan. This was decisive, because Japan imported about 80 per cent of its oil from the United States, and its navy had reserves for only about eighteen months to two years. Britain and the Dutch government in exile joined the embargo. In the negotiations that followed, the United States demanded that Japan withdraw from China and Indochina; the Hull Note of 26 November 1941 restated these demands. Japan's leaders, now headed by Prime Minister General Hideki Tojo, saw two choices: give up their empire in China, or seize the resources of South-East Asia by force. They chose war.

So the question becomes: why attack Pearl Harbour, thousands of kilometres from the oil Japan wanted?

## Subtopic: The Attack of 7 December 1941

The answer is the United States Pacific Fleet, which had been based at Pearl Harbour on the island of Oahu in Hawaii since 1940. Japan's planners knew that if they invaded the Dutch East Indies and Malaya, the American fleet could attack their long supply lines. Admiral Isoroku Yamamoto, commander of Japan's Combined Fleet, who had studied in the United States and knew its industrial power, argued that Japan's only chance was to destroy the American fleet at the start, gaining six months to a year to conquer South-East Asia and build a defensive perimeter. He hoped the Americans would then accept a negotiated peace. He reportedly warned that he could run wild for six months but had no confidence after that.

A strike force of six aircraft carriers under Vice-Admiral Chuichi Nagumo sailed in secret from northern Japan on 26 November 1941, keeping radio silence and taking a northern route across the empty North Pacific. On the morning of Sunday 7 December 1941, from about 370 kilometres north of Oahu, the carriers launched 353 aircraft in two waves: torpedo bombers, dive bombers, high-level bombers and fighters. The first wave struck at 7:55 a.m. local time. An American radar station had detected the incoming planes, but an officer assumed they were a flight of American bombers expected from California. A coded Japanese message breaking off negotiations had also been decoded in Washington, but the warning reached Hawaii too late.

The attack lasted about two hours. Japanese pilots used specially modified torpedoes that could run in the shallow water of the harbour. All eight American battleships in port were damaged and four were sunk; the USS Arizona exploded when a bomb ignited its forward magazine, killing 1 177 of its crew, almost half of all American deaths that day. About 188 American aircraft were destroyed, mostly on the ground at nearby airfields, where they had been parked close together to guard against sabotage. In total 2 403 Americans were killed, including 68 civilians, and 1 178 were wounded. Japan lost 29 aircraft, five midget submarines and 64 men.

Yet the attack failed in important ways. The three American aircraft carriers of the Pacific Fleet were at sea and escaped. Nagumo decided against a third wave, so the fuel storage tanks, holding millions of litres of oil, the repair shops and the submarine base were left largely intact. All but three of the damaged ships were eventually repaired and returned to service. Within hours of Pearl Harbour, Japanese forces also attacked British Malaya, Hong Kong, the American-controlled Philippines, Guam and Wake Island: Pearl Harbour was one part of a coordinated offensive across a vast area.

So what did the attack do to the United States and the wider war?

## Subtopic: The American Response and a Global War

Before December 1941, most Americans wanted to stay out of the war. A powerful movement called America First opposed involvement, although Roosevelt had already supported Britain with weapons through the Lend-Lease Act of March 1941. Pearl Harbour ended this debate overnight. On 8 December 1941, Roosevelt addressed Congress, describing 7 December as "a date which will live in infamy" and declaring that the attack had been "unprovoked and dastardly". Congress declared war on Japan within an hour; the Senate voted 82 to 0, and the House of Representatives 388 to 1, the single vote against coming from Jeannette Rankin, a pacifist who had also opposed entering World War I. Britain declared war on Japan on the same day.

On 11 December 1941, Hitler declared war on the United States, and Italy followed. Germany was not required to do so by the Tripartite Pact, which only obliged members to help if one was attacked, and historians still debate why Hitler made this choice. He underestimated American power and believed the United States was already effectively at war with Germany in the Atlantic. Whatever the reason, the European war and the Asian war were now one global conflict, with the United States, Britain and the Soviet Union in alliance against Germany, and the United States and Britain, joined by China, against Japan. The Allies agreed on a "Germany first" strategy, because Germany was considered the more dangerous enemy.

The American economy was turned to war production on a scale never seen before. By 1944 the United States was producing about 40 per cent of the world's weapons, including tens of thousands of aircraft a year and dozens of aircraft carriers, far beyond Japan's capacity. Millions of men enlisted, and millions of women entered factories, symbolised by the poster figure Rosie the Riveter. African American workers moved north and west for war jobs, and African American soldiers served in segregated units, fighting for freedom abroad while facing discrimination at home, a contradiction that fed the later civil rights movement.

Fear and racism after Pearl Harbour also led to one of the most serious injustices of the American home front: the forced removal and imprisonment of about 120 000 people of Japanese ancestry, most of them American citizens, which is the subject of the next lesson.

So what happened in the Pacific in the months after the attack?

## Subtopic: Japan's Advance, the Battle of Midway and the Error Museum

For six months Japan's forces advanced with astonishing speed. Hong Kong fell on Christmas Day 1941. Japanese troops pushed down the Malay Peninsula, and on 15 February 1942 the British fortress of Singapore surrendered, with about 80 000 British, Australian and Indian troops taken prisoner, the largest surrender in British military history. The Dutch East Indies, with their oil, were conquered by March 1942. In the Philippines, American and Filipino forces surrendered at Bataan in April 1942, and thousands of prisoners died during the Bataan Death March. Burma fell, and Japanese aircraft bombed Darwin in northern Australia. Japan called its empire the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere and claimed to be liberating Asians from Western colonialism, but its rule was often brutal, with forced labour and the exploitation of resources for Japan's war.

The turning point came at the Battle of Midway on 4 to 7 June 1942. Yamamoto planned to lure the remaining American carriers into a trap near Midway Atoll in the central Pacific. But American code-breakers had partly broken the Japanese naval code and knew the target and timing. American dive bombers caught the Japanese carriers while they were refuelling and rearming their aircraft and sank four of them, together with many experienced pilots, for the loss of one American carrier. Japan could not replace these losses, while American shipyards produced new carriers continually. Midway, together with the long struggle for Guadalcanal from August 1942 to February 1943, ended Japan's advance. The Allies then began an island-hopping campaign across the Pacific towards Japan, leading to the events studied in Term 2.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Pearl Harbour destroyed the American Pacific Fleet." It did not. The carriers were absent, the fuel tanks and repair yards survived, and most damaged ships were repaired.

Exhibit two: "The United States entered the war against Germany because Germany attacked Pearl Harbour." Japan attacked Pearl Harbour; Germany declared war on the United States four days later.

Exhibit three: "The attack was random and unprovoked by any dispute." Roosevelt called it unprovoked because Japan attacked without a declaration of war during negotiations, but it followed years of conflict over Japanese expansion in China and the American oil embargo. Explaining causes is not the same as justifying the attack.

Exhibit four: "Pearl Harbour was Japan's only target on 7 and 8 December." Japan attacked Malaya, Hong Kong, the Philippines, Guam and Wake Island in the same coordinated offensive.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: an empty fuel tank, a Sunday morning and a sleeping giant waking up.

## Subtopic: A Country That Needed Oil

Imagine a busy factory that runs on fuel, but the factory owner owns no petrol station. Every drop has to be bought from a neighbour. That was Japan in the 1930s: a modern industrial country, with ships, factories and a large army, but almost no oil, iron or rubber of its own.

Japan's army leaders decided the answer was to take land and resources from their neighbours. In 1931 they grabbed Manchuria in China, and in 1937 they started a full war against China. In the Chinese city of Nanjing, Japanese soldiers murdered huge numbers of ordinary people. Then Japan joined Germany and Italy as an ally, and moved its troops into French Indochina, closer to the oil fields of what is now Indonesia.

The United States did not like this at all. It decided to stop selling Japan oil. That was serious, because about 80 per cent of Japan's oil came from America. Without oil, Japan's navy would run dry in about two years. America said Japan could have oil again if it left China. Japan's leaders refused. They decided to take the oil they needed by force, from the Dutch and British colonies in South-East Asia.

But there was a problem. The big American navy in the Pacific could stop them. So what did Japan do about that navy?

## Subtopic: A Sunday Morning in Hawaii

Japan's admiral Yamamoto had a bold plan: knock out the American navy with one surprise blow, then grab South-East Asia before America could recover.

Six Japanese aircraft carriers, giant ships that act like floating airports, sailed secretly across the ocean towards Hawaii, keeping their radios silent. Early on Sunday 7 December 1941, they sent off 353 aircraft. Many American sailors at Pearl Harbour were asleep or getting ready for church. A radar operator did see the planes coming, but his officer thought they were American planes and told him not to worry.

At 7:55 in the morning the bombs and torpedoes began to fall. In about two hours, all eight battleships in the harbour were damaged and four sank. The battleship Arizona blew up, and 1 177 sailors on it died. Altogether 2 403 Americans were killed. Japan lost only 29 planes.

But here is the twist. America's aircraft carriers were out at sea that morning, so they were safe. And Japan did not bomb the big fuel tanks or the repair yards. So the American navy was badly hurt, but not finished.

So how did America react?

## Subtopic: A Sleeping Giant Wakes Up

Before Pearl Harbour, most Americans wanted to stay out of the war. After Pearl Harbour, almost everyone wanted to fight. The next day President Roosevelt said 7 December was "a date which will live in infamy", and America declared war on Japan. A few days later, on 11 December, Hitler declared war on America too. Now it was a world war in every sense.

America's factories switched to making planes, ships and tanks, far more than Japan could ever make. Women went to work in the factories in their millions.

For six months Japan kept winning. It captured Hong Kong, Singapore, the Philippines and Indonesia, and even bombed Darwin in Australia. But in June 1942, at a tiny island called Midway, American code-breakers knew Japan's plan in advance. American planes sank four Japanese aircraft carriers. Japan could never replace them, and from then on it was mostly retreating.

Here is the line to remember: Pearl Harbour was a brilliant surprise attack but a terrible mistake, because it woke up the strongest factory in the world and pulled it into the war.
