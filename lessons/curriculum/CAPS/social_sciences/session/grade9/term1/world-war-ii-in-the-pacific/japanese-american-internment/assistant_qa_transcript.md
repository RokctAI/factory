### subtopic_1 — Background: Racism and Fear After Pearl Harbour
**Assistant:** Why were Japanese Americans treated differently from German and Italian Americans?

**Tutor:** The difference is best explained by race, and that is the conclusion the official commission reached in 1983. The United States was also at war with Germany and Italy, and the government did arrest and intern several thousand German and Italian nationals it considered dangerous, after individual hearings. But there was no mass removal of German Americans or Italian Americans, who numbered in the millions and included many who were not citizens. Several reasons explain the contrast. Germans and Italians were Europeans and were seen by most white Americans as part of the national community, while Japanese Americans had faced decades of racist laws and propaganda, such as the 1924 ban on Japanese immigration and laws preventing Issei from becoming citizens or owning land. Japanese Americans were a small, visible minority concentrated on the West Coast, so removing them was politically easy and economically attractive to some competitors. Pearl Harbour created anger directed specifically at Japan, and newspapers used racist language that described Japanese people as treacherous by nature. General DeWitt openly said that ethnicity itself determined loyalty. In contrast, removing millions of German and Italian Americans would have disrupted the economy and angered powerful voters. Historians use this comparison as evidence that the policy was not based on actual threat, since no individual assessment was made, and that it was based on racial assumptions about who could be a loyal American.

### subtopic_2 — Executive Order 9066 and Forced Removal
**Assistant:** What did families lose when they were forced to leave their homes?

**Tutor:** Families lost property, income, freedom, health and, in a deeper sense, their sense of belonging. With only days to prepare and permission to take only what they could carry, many sold homes, cars, farm equipment and furniture to buyers who knew they had to accept almost any price. Farmers had to leave crops in the fields just before harvest. Businesses built over decades were closed or taken over. Some left possessions with friends or in government storage, and many returned to find them stolen or vandalised. Because Issei were not allowed to own land in California, some had placed property in their American-born children's names or relied on white friends, and some of those arrangements failed. The Federal Reserve estimated losses at the time at about 400 million dollars, a huge sum, while a later study suggested losses worth billions in today's money; the 1988 payments of 20 000 dollars per survivor were symbolic rather than full compensation. Beyond money, families lost years of education and careers, elderly people died in the camps, and parents lost authority as children grew up in mess halls and barracks. Many survivors described shame and silence, feeling they had been treated as criminals by their own country. For South African learners this has a familiar echo: in Term 3 you will study forced removals under the Group Areas Act, where families also lost homes, businesses and communities because of race.

### subtopic_3 — Life in the Camps and the Loyalty Questionnaire
**Assistant:** Why did so many young Nisei men choose to serve in the army?

**Tutor:** Their reasons were personal and varied, and they show how difficult the choices were. Many Nisei were American citizens who had grown up pledging allegiance to the flag and saw themselves as fully American, so they wanted to fight for their country despite its injustice. Many believed that military service was the best way to prove the loyalty of the whole community and to win back rights for their families; they hoped that a record of courage would make discrimination after the war harder to justify. Some were already in the army before Pearl Harbour, especially in Hawaii, where the 100th Infantry Battalion was formed. In early 1943 the army allowed Nisei to volunteer and formed the 442nd Regimental Combat Team. In Italy and France it suffered very high casualties, including the rescue of a trapped Texas battalion in the Vosges mountains in October 1944, and its members won thousands of awards, including Purple Hearts for wounds and, eventually, 21 Medals of Honor, most awarded after a review in 2000. Thousands of others served as linguists in the Pacific, translating captured documents and questioning prisoners. Not everyone agreed with volunteering. Draft resisters at Heart Mountain argued that a government should restore rights before demanding military service, and many of them went to prison. Historians today recognise both groups as responding, in different ways, to the same injustice.

### subtopic_4 — Resistance, the Courts, Redress and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How did the Supreme Court rule on the removal of Japanese Americans?

**Tutor:** The Supreme Court largely accepted the government's arguments during the war, which is why these cases are now seen as some of the worst decisions in its history. In Hirabayashi v. United States in 1943, the Court upheld a curfew applied only to people of Japanese ancestry, accepting that military necessity justified it. In Korematsu v. United States, decided in December 1944, a majority of six judges upheld Fred Korematsu's conviction for refusing to leave the exclusion zone. Justice Hugo Black wrote that Korematsu was not excluded because of hostility to his race but because of military danger. Three judges dissented strongly: Justice Murphy said the exclusion fell into the ugly abyss of racism, and Justice Robert Jackson warned that the decision lay about like a loaded weapon ready for any authority with a plausible claim of urgent need. On the same day, in Ex parte Endo, the Court ruled that a loyal citizen could not be detained, which led to the lifting of exclusion in January 1945. In the 1980s, the historian Peter Irons and the researcher Aiko Herzig-Yoshinaga discovered that government lawyers had hidden intelligence reports showing there was no military necessity. Korematsu's conviction was overturned in 1983, and Hirabayashi's in 1987. The case shows that courts can fail to protect rights in times of fear, which is why constitutional protections matter most when they are tested.
