# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the treatment of prisoners of war held by Japan during World War II. It covers who the prisoners were and why Japanese military culture and policy led to such harsh treatment; conditions in camps and on forced marches and transport ships; forced labour on the Burma-Thailand Railway; and liberation, war crimes trials and memory. The anchor figure is the death rate: roughly one in four Western Allied prisoners held by Japan died in captivity, compared with roughly one in twenty-five of those held by Germany and Italy. The governing idea is that the brutality was not caused by any national character but by specific ideas, policies, military pressures and the breakdown of the laws of war, and that remembering it accurately, while also pursuing reconciliation, is the historian's responsibility.

## Subtopic: Who Were the Prisoners and Why Were They Treated So Harshly?

Japan's victories between December 1941 and May 1942 left it in control of an enormous number of prisoners. The surrender of Singapore in February 1942 alone delivered about 80 000 British, Australian and Indian soldiers. Further surrenders came in the Dutch East Indies, Hong Kong and the Philippines. In total about 140 000 Western Allied military prisoners were held by Japan: British, Australian, Dutch, American, Canadian, New Zealander and others, including a small number of South Africans, mostly airmen and sailors. Japan also held tens of thousands of Indian soldiers and huge numbers of Asian civilians and forced labourers. Western civilians living in the colonies, including women and children, were interned in separate camps.

Several factors explain the treatment of these prisoners. The first is military ideology. Japanese soldiers were trained in an interpretation of the samurai code, known as Bushido, which was promoted by the militarist state in the 1930s and taught that surrender was shameful and that a soldier should fight to the death. The Field Service Code of 1941 told soldiers never to live to experience the shame of being taken prisoner. Many Japanese guards therefore regarded prisoners as men who had lost their honour and did not deserve respect.

The second is law. Japan had signed but not ratified the Geneva Convention of 1929 on prisoners of war. Early in 1942 its government said it would apply the convention's principles where possible, but in practice this promise was not kept. Prisoners were forced to work on military projects, which the convention forbade, and were denied adequate food, medicine and contact with the Red Cross.

The third is a harsh internal culture. Discipline within the Japanese army itself was brutal, with officers routinely beating soldiers, and this violence was passed down to prisoners. Many camp guards were Korean or Taiwanese conscripts from Japan's colonies, at the bottom of the hierarchy and themselves mistreated. Fourth, racism ran in both directions: Japanese propaganda portrayed Western prisoners as symbols of colonial arrogance, while Allied propaganda depicted the Japanese in dehumanising racial terms. Finally, Japan was itself short of food, shipping and medicine as the war turned against it, and prisoners were at the end of every supply line.

It is worth noting how historians know all this. The main primary sources are prisoners' secret diaries, many written on scraps of paper and hidden in bamboo or buried; drawings by prisoner artists such as Jack Chalker and Ronald Searle; Red Cross and Allied intelligence reports; Japanese military records, many of which were deliberately burned in August 1945; the evidence given at post-war trials; and oral testimony recorded from survivors decades later. Each type of source has strengths and limitations: a diary is immediate but personal and fragmentary, trial evidence was collected to prove guilt, and memories recorded fifty years later may have been shaped by films and books. A good historian compares several kinds of source before reaching a conclusion, and asks whose voices are missing, such as those of the Asian labourers and the Korean guards.

So the next question is: what did these conditions mean for the prisoners themselves?

## Subtopic: Conditions in Captivity: Bataan, Changi and the Hell Ships

The suffering began at the moment of surrender. On the Bataan Peninsula in the Philippines, about 76 000 American and Filipino troops surrendered in April 1942, already weak from hunger and malaria. They were forced to march about 100 kilometres in tropical heat to a camp, with little food or water. Those who fell behind were often beaten, bayoneted or shot. This became known as the Bataan Death March. Estimates of deaths vary, but several thousand Filipinos and several hundred Americans died on the march, and many more died in the camp in the following months.

In Singapore, most prisoners were first held at Changi, a former British military area at the east of the island. Changi became a symbol of captivity for Australians and Britons. Conditions there were initially better than elsewhere because the prisoners organised themselves: they ran gardens, a hospital, lectures, concerts and even a camp university. But rations of poor rice were far too small, and diseases caused by malnutrition, such as beriberi and pellagra, spread. From Changi, work parties were sent all over the Japanese empire.

Discipline in the camps was arbitrary and violent. Prisoners were beaten for failing to bow to guards, slapped for minor offences and made to stand for hours in the sun. Escape attempts were punished by execution. Medicine was scarce, and doctors among the prisoners performed operations with improvised instruments; the Australian surgeon Edward "Weary" Dunlop became famous for his care of sick men and his courage in standing up to guards on their behalf. Prisoners secretly built radios to hear news of the war, at the risk of death if discovered.

Prisoners were moved by sea in unmarked cargo ships, crammed into airless holds with little water and no sanitation. These became known as hell ships. Because they were not marked as carrying prisoners, Allied submarines and aircraft sank several of them unknowingly. When the Montevideo Maru was torpedoed by an American submarine on 1 July 1942, about 1 000 Australian prisoners and civilians died, Australia's worst maritime disaster. Thousands of prisoners died in such sinkings. Near the end of the war, the Sandakan death marches in Borneo in 1945 killed almost all of about 2 400 Australian and British prisoners; only six, all of whom escaped, survived.

So what was the largest single project prisoners were forced to work on?

## Subtopic: The Burma-Thailand Railway

After Japan conquered Burma in 1942, it needed a supply route to its army there that avoided the dangerous sea journey around Singapore, where Allied submarines were active. It decided to build a railway about 415 kilometres long from Ban Pong in Thailand to Thanbyuzayat in Burma, through thick jungle, mountains and river valleys. Before the war, British engineers had surveyed the route and judged it would take five years. The Japanese army ordered it completed in about a year, and it was finished in October 1943, roughly sixteen months after work began.

The labour came from about 60 000 Allied prisoners of war and an estimated 200 000 or more Asian labourers, called romusha, recruited or forced from Malaya, Burma, Thailand and the Dutch East Indies. The work was done largely by hand, with picks, shovels, hammers and baskets of earth, cutting through rock and building wooden bridges and embankments. The most notorious section was Hellfire Pass in Thailand, a deep rock cutting where prisoners worked in shifts up to eighteen hours long during the speed-up period of 1943, known as the speedo, often by torchlight at night.

Conditions were appalling. Rations were mainly small quantities of rice, sometimes with a little vegetable or dried fish. The monsoon rains of 1943 turned camps into mud. Cholera spread through the camps, killing men within hours; dysentery, malaria, beriberi and tropical ulcers that ate into flesh were common. Sick men were forced to work to meet quotas. About 12 000 to 13 000 Allied prisoners died, roughly one in five. The death toll among Asian labourers was far higher, estimated at between 80 000 and 100 000, but they are often left out of Western accounts, and their deaths were less well recorded because they had no officers or organisation to keep records.

The railway is often called the Death Railway. The bridge over the Khwae Yai River near Kanchanaburi in Thailand became famous through a novel and a 1957 film, The Bridge on the River Kwai. The film is a powerful story, but it is a fictional account and historians point out that it gives a misleading picture of the real prisoners, their officers and the conditions they faced, a useful reminder to check popular media against historical sources.

Today the railway is remembered at several sites. The Hellfire Pass Memorial Museum, built with Australian support, preserves the cutting and a walking trail along the old line. The war cemeteries at Kanchanaburi and Chungkai in Thailand and Thanbyuzayat in Myanmar hold the graves of thousands of Allied prisoners, cared for by the Commonwealth War Graves Commission. A short section of the railway still carries trains in Thailand, including over the rebuilt bridge at Kanchanaburi, and tourists travel it every day, sometimes without knowing how it was built.

So what happened when the war ended, and how has this history been remembered?

## Subtopic: Liberation, Justice, Memory and the Error Museum

When Japan surrendered in August 1945, the surviving prisoners were freed by Allied forces in the weeks that followed. Many weighed only thirty or forty kilograms, and many suffered lifelong physical and psychological damage. Some prisoners had been ordered to be killed if Allied forces invaded, and the sudden surrender probably saved many lives.

Justice was pursued through war crimes trials. At the International Military Tribunal for the Far East in Tokyo, from 1946 to 1948, twenty-eight Japanese leaders were charged; seven, including the wartime Prime Minister Hideki Tojo, were sentenced to death and executed. Thousands of lower-ranking officers and guards were tried in separate courts across Asia, and about 900 were executed. Some Korean and Taiwanese guards were among them, which raised questions about the responsibility of colonial conscripts. Critics also noted that some crimes were not prosecuted, including the human experiments of Unit 731, whose leaders were given immunity by the United States in exchange for their research data, and the system of sexual slavery imposed on tens of thousands of so-called comfort women, mostly Korean, which was fully acknowledged only decades later.

The South African writer and soldier Laurens van der Post, who was a prisoner of the Japanese in Java from 1942 to 1945, later wrote that he and his fellow prisoners had chosen not to hate their captors, and that the cycle of revenge had to be broken. Survivors' responses varied widely. Some never forgave. Others, like the British officer Eric Lomax, who was tortured on the railway, eventually met their former tormentors and reconciled; his story is told in the book The Railway Man. These responses connect with debates you will study about truth, justice and reconciliation in South Africa after apartheid.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Japanese people are naturally cruel." This is a racist generalisation. The brutality came from specific ideas, training, policies and wartime pressures, and many individual Japanese also behaved humanely. Historians explain causes; they do not blame entire peoples.

Exhibit two: "Only Western soldiers died on the railway." Far more Asian labourers died than Allied prisoners, but they are often forgotten.

Exhibit three: "The Bridge on the River Kwai is an accurate record." It is a fictional film and should be checked against primary sources such as prisoners' diaries and drawings.

Exhibit four: "Japan followed the Geneva Convention." Japan had signed but not ratified it, and in practice ignored its rules on forced labour, food and medical care.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three things: a rule about surrender, a long hungry march and a railway through the jungle.

## Subtopic: A Rule About Surrender

Imagine being taught all your life that giving up is the most shameful thing you could ever do. That is what Japanese soldiers in World War II were taught. Their rule said: never be taken prisoner. Fight to the end.

Now imagine you are a guard trained like that, and suddenly you are in charge of tens of thousands of enemy soldiers who did surrender. Many guards looked down on these prisoners as people who had lost their honour.

In early 1942 Japan won battle after battle and captured about 140 000 soldiers from Britain, Australia, the Netherlands, America and other countries, including a few South Africans. Japan had promised the world it would follow the rules for treating prisoners, but it did not keep that promise.

Many guards were themselves beaten by their own officers, and that violence was passed on down the line. Food and medicine were short everywhere, and prisoners came last.

So what happened to the prisoners?

## Subtopic: A Long Hungry March

In the Philippines in April 1942, about 76 000 American and Filipino soldiers surrendered. They were already sick and hungry. They were made to walk about 100 kilometres in burning heat, with almost no water. Anyone who fell down might be beaten or killed. Thousands died. It is called the Bataan Death March.

In Singapore, prisoners were kept at a camp called Changi. They grew vegetables, gave lessons and even put on concerts to keep their spirits up, but they were always hungry. Prisoner doctors did amazing work with almost no medicine.

Prisoners who were moved by sea were packed into the dark holds of cargo ships, called hell ships. Because the ships had no markings, Allied submarines sometimes sank them by mistake, and thousands of prisoners drowned.

Overall, about one in four Western prisoners held by Japan died. For prisoners held by Germany and Italy, it was about one in twenty-five.

So what was the hardest job they were forced to do?

## Subtopic: A Railway Through the Jungle

Japan wanted a railway from Thailand to Burma, about 415 kilometres through jungle and mountains. Engineers once said it would take five years. The Japanese army said: do it in about a year.

About 60 000 prisoners of war and more than 200 000 workers from Asian countries were forced to build it, mostly by hand with picks, shovels and baskets. At a place called Hellfire Pass they cut through solid rock, sometimes working through the night by firelight. Rain turned the camps to mud, and diseases like cholera spread fast.

About 12 000 to 13 000 Allied prisoners died, and somewhere between 80 000 and 100 000 Asian workers died, though they are often forgotten. That is why it is called the Death Railway.

When Japan surrendered in August 1945, the survivors were freed, many of them as thin as skeletons. Japanese leaders were put on trial in Tokyo, and seven were executed. A South African prisoner named Laurens van der Post later said that he chose not to hate his captors, because hatred only keeps the cruelty going.

Here is the line to remember: the cruelty came from ideas, rules and choices, not from a whole people, and the best response to it is truth, justice and remembering everyone who suffered.
