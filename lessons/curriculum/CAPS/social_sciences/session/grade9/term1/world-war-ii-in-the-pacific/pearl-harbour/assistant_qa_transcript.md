### subtopic_1 — Japanese Expansion and the Road to War
**Assistant:** Why did Japan's leaders believe they had to go to war with the United States?

**Tutor:** Japan's leaders saw themselves as trapped by the choice between giving up their empire and going to war, and they chose war. Since 1937 Japan had been fighting a full-scale war in China that it could not win quickly, and that war consumed enormous amounts of oil, steel and other materials, most of which Japan had to import. When Japan moved into southern French Indochina in July 1941, the United States froze Japanese assets and stopped oil exports, and Britain and the Dutch government in exile followed. Japan depended on the United States for about 80 per cent of its oil, and its navy calculated that its reserves would last only about eighteen months to two years. The American position in negotiations was that Japan should withdraw from China and Indochina, which the army leaders regarded as unacceptable, since it would mean admitting that years of war and many thousands of deaths had been for nothing. The oil of the Dutch East Indies offered an alternative, but seizing it meant war with Britain and the Netherlands, and Japanese planners assumed it would also bring in the United States, whose Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbour and bases in the Philippines threatened their route. So they decided to strike first. In an essay, it is important to show both the pressure Japan faced and the choices its leaders made: the embargo was a cause, but the decision for war was their own.

### subtopic_2 — The Attack of 7 December 1941
**Assistant:** Was the United States completely surprised by the attack on Pearl Harbour?

**Tutor:** The United States expected war with Japan but was surprised by where it came. American code-breakers had broken Japan's diplomatic code, a programme called Magic, so Washington knew negotiations were failing and that Japan was preparing to act. On 27 November 1941, commanders in Hawaii and the Philippines received a war warning. However, American leaders expected Japan to strike in South-East Asia, perhaps the Philippines or Malaya, not Hawaii, which was about 6 000 kilometres from Japan and seemed too far for a major attack. In Hawaii, commanders feared sabotage by local people of Japanese descent more than an air attack, which is why aircraft were parked close together where they could be guarded, making them easy targets. On the morning itself, a radar station detected the approaching planes almost an hour before the attack, but the report was dismissed. A final Japanese message breaking off negotiations was decoded in Washington that morning, but the warning was sent by commercial telegram and arrived after the bombs had fallen. Several official inquiries later blamed the commanders in Hawaii, Admiral Kimmel and General Short, while others pointed to failures in Washington. Some people have claimed that Roosevelt knew of the attack in advance and allowed it to happen, but historians have found no reliable evidence for this theory. The better explanation is a combination of misjudgement, poor communication and Japanese skill in keeping the operation secret.

### subtopic_3 — The American Response and a Global War
**Assistant:** Why did Hitler declare war on the United States after Pearl Harbour?

**Tutor:** This is one of the most debated decisions of the war, because Hitler was not obliged to do it. The Tripartite Pact of 1940 committed Germany, Italy and Japan to help each other if one of them was attacked by a power not yet at war, but Japan had attacked the United States, not the other way round. Historians suggest several reasons. First, Hitler believed the United States was already fighting Germany unofficially. Through Lend-Lease, America was supplying Britain and the Soviet Union, and American warships were escorting convoys in the Atlantic and had exchanged fire with German submarines. Declaring war would allow German submarines to attack American shipping freely along the American coast, which they did with devastating effect in early 1942. Second, Hitler underestimated the United States, which in his racist worldview he saw as weak, divided and dominated by Jews, and he did not grasp how quickly American industry could produce weapons. Third, he hoped Japan would keep the Americans busy in the Pacific and might eventually attack the Soviet Union, which Japan never did. Fourth, he wanted to keep the initiative and show solidarity with his ally. The decision was a grave strategic error. It removed any chance that the United States would concentrate only on Japan, and it allowed Roosevelt to pursue the Germany first strategy with full public support, bringing American power to Europe in North Africa, Italy and finally Normandy.

### subtopic_4 — Japan's Advance, the Battle of Midway and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why was Midway the turning point of the Pacific war?

**Tutor:** Midway was the turning point because it destroyed the striking power of the Japanese navy in a single day and shifted the initiative to the United States for the rest of the war. Before the battle, Japan had more aircraft carriers in the Pacific and a corps of highly trained, experienced pilots. Yamamoto wanted to lure the American carriers that had escaped Pearl Harbour into a decisive battle and destroy them. But American code-breakers in Hawaii, led by Commander Joseph Rochefort, had worked out that Japan's target, called AF in Japanese messages, was Midway. They confirmed it with a clever trick: Midway sent a false message that its water plant had broken, and Japanese messages then reported that AF was short of water. Admiral Chester Nimitz positioned his three carriers to ambush the Japanese fleet. On 4 June 1942, after several failed American attacks, dive bombers found the Japanese carriers with their decks crowded with aircraft, fuel and bombs, and set three on fire within minutes; a fourth was sunk later that day. Japan lost four carriers and about 3 000 men, including many skilled aircrew and mechanics, while the Americans lost one carrier. The deeper reason Midway was decisive is economic: during the war the United States built dozens of new carriers, while Japan could build only a few. After Midway, Japan was on the defensive, and the Americans and their allies began the long campaign towards Japan.
