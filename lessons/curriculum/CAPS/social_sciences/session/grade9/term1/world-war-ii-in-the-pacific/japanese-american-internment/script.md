# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the forced removal and internment of Japanese Americans during World War II. It covers the background of anti-Asian racism in the United States and the fear that followed Pearl Harbour; Executive Order 9066 and the removal process; life in the camps and the loyalty questionnaire; and resistance, military service, the Supreme Court cases and the eventual apology and redress. The anchor date is 19 February 1942, when President Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066, and the anchor figure is about 120 000 people removed, roughly two thirds of them American-born citizens. The governing idea is that a democracy at war violated the rights of its own citizens on the basis of race, and that the official investigation decades later concluded that the causes were race prejudice, war hysteria and a failure of political leadership.

## Subtopic: Background: Racism and Fear After Pearl Harbour

Japanese immigrants began arriving in Hawaii and on the West Coast of the United States in the late nineteenth century, working on plantations, railways and farms. They became especially successful as farmers, growing a large share of California's vegetables on small plots, often land others had considered poor. This success provoked resentment from white farmers and politicians, who joined a long tradition of anti-Asian racism that had already produced the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882.

Laws discriminated against Japanese immigrants directly. Under American naturalisation law, immigrants of Asian origin could not become citizens; the Supreme Court confirmed this for Japanese immigrants in the Ozawa case of 1922. California's Alien Land Laws of 1913 and 1920 prevented non-citizens from owning farmland, and the Immigration Act of 1924 stopped further Japanese immigration altogether. As a result, the community had two generations with different legal positions: the Issei, the immigrant first generation, who were permanently non-citizens, and the Nisei, the second generation, born in America and therefore American citizens by birth. By 1941 about 127 000 people of Japanese ancestry lived in the mainland United States, most of them in California, Oregon and Washington, and about 150 000 lived in Hawaii, where they made up more than a third of the population.

The attack on Pearl Harbour on 7 December 1941 unleashed fear and anger. Within days the FBI arrested about 2 000 Issei community leaders, such as Buddhist priests, Japanese language teachers and newspaper editors, whom it had been watching for years. Rumours spread that Japanese Americans had helped the attack by signalling to aircraft, or were preparing sabotage on the West Coast. None of these rumours was true. Newspapers, patriotic organisations and farming groups that stood to gain from removing competitors demanded action. The general in charge of West Coast defence, John DeWitt, argued that ethnic origin itself made people dangerous, famously saying "a Jap's a Jap", and even claimed that the absence of sabotage proved that sabotage was being planned.

Government intelligence reports, including one by Curtis Munson for the President in late 1941 and assessments by naval intelligence, concluded that the great majority of Japanese Americans were loyal and posed little threat, and that the few suspects were already known. These findings were ignored.

A related comparison helps here. In South Africa during the same war, the Smuts government interned several thousand people it regarded as security risks, including German nationals and members of the pro-German Ossewabrandwag movement, among them the future Prime Minister John Vorster, who was held at Koffiefontein. Those detentions were based on suspected political activity by individuals, whereas the American policy removed an entire ethnic group, including children, without any individual assessment. That difference, between punishing what a person has done and punishing who a person is, is at the heart of this lesson.

So the next question is: how did the government turn this fear into policy?

## Subtopic: Executive Order 9066 and Forced Removal

On 19 February 1942, President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066. It did not mention Japanese Americans by name; instead it authorised the military to declare military areas from which any persons could be excluded. In practice, General DeWitt used it to declare the western half of California, Oregon and Washington, and southern Arizona, an exclusion zone for everyone of Japanese ancestry, citizens and non-citizens alike, including children, the elderly and orphans with as little as one-sixteenth Japanese ancestry. Congress backed the order with a law in March 1942 making it a crime to disobey.

Notices were posted on telephone poles and buildings, giving families often less than a week to leave. They could take only what they could carry: bedding, clothes, cutlery and essentials. Homes, farms, businesses, cars and possessions had to be sold quickly at a fraction of their value, left with neighbours or simply abandoned. Losses have been estimated at hundreds of millions of dollars at the time. Families were given numbered tags to wear, as if they were luggage.

People were first taken to temporary assembly centres, often racecourses and fairgrounds. At the Santa Anita racetrack in California, families lived in horse stables that still smelled of manure. From mid-1942 they were moved by train, with blinds drawn, to ten permanent camps run by a civilian agency, the War Relocation Authority. The camps were built in remote, harsh places: Manzanar and Tule Lake in California, Poston and Gila River in Arizona, Topaz in Utah, Heart Mountain in Wyoming, Minidoka in Idaho, Granada in Colorado, and Rohwer and Jerome in Arkansas. The government called them relocation centres; many historians and survivors prefer terms such as incarceration camps or concentration camps in the original sense of places where civilians are concentrated, while being careful to distinguish them clearly from Nazi death camps. Because the word internment strictly refers to holding enemy citizens, many prefer incarceration for the imprisonment of American citizens.

Hawaii offers an important comparison. Although it was closer to Japan and had just been attacked, only about 1 200 to 1 800 of its roughly 150 000 people of Japanese ancestry were detained, because they were essential to the economy and military leaders there resisted mass removal. This suggests that mass removal on the mainland was not a military necessity.

So what was life like inside the camps?

## Subtopic: Life in the Camps and the Loyalty Questionnaire

Each camp held between about 7 000 and 18 000 people behind barbed wire, watched by armed guards in watchtowers. People lived in rows of hastily built wooden barracks covered in tar paper, divided into small rooms in which a whole family, sometimes with strangers, shared one space with a single light bulb and army cots. There was little privacy: latrines and showers were shared and had no partitions at first, and meals were eaten in large mess halls, which weakened family life as children ate with friends rather than parents. The climate was harsh, with desert heat and dust storms in summer and freezing winters. At least seven people were shot by guards, including James Wakasa at Topaz in 1943, an elderly man killed near the fence.

People rebuilt community life as best they could. They established schools, newspapers, churches and temples, baseball teams, gardens and farms that grew much of their own food. Adults worked at camp jobs for very low wages of a few dollars a month. Artists and photographers recorded camp life; the photographer Ansel Adams and the Manzanar resident Toyo Miyatake left important visual sources, though some government photographs were censored to hide the barbed wire and guards.

In 1943 the government introduced a loyalty questionnaire for all adults, partly to recruit soldiers and to allow some people to leave for work or college in the interior. Two questions caused great anguish. Question 27 asked whether men were willing to serve in the armed forces wherever ordered, and question 28 asked people to swear unqualified allegiance to the United States and to renounce allegiance to the Japanese emperor. For the Issei, who were barred from American citizenship, renouncing Japanese nationality could leave them stateless; for the Nisei, the question insulted citizens who had never held another allegiance. Some answered no to both, either in protest at their imprisonment or out of confusion and fear; they were called the "no-no boys" and were segregated at Tule Lake, which became a high-security camp.

Many young Nisei men served in the army. The 442nd Regimental Combat Team, made up mainly of Japanese Americans from Hawaii and the camps, fought in Italy and France and became, for its size and length of service, the most decorated unit in American military history, with its motto "Go for broke". Others served as translators and interpreters against Japan in the Military Intelligence Service. They fought for freedom abroad while their families remained behind barbed wire.

So the final question is: who resisted, and how did the United States eventually face what it had done?

## Subtopic: Resistance, the Courts, Redress and the Error Museum

Some Japanese Americans challenged the orders in court. Fred Korematsu, a welder from California, refused to leave and was arrested; Gordon Hirabayashi, a university student, deliberately broke the curfew and refused to register; Minoru Yasui, a lawyer, tested the curfew. In Hirabayashi v. United States in 1943 and Korematsu v. United States in December 1944, the Supreme Court upheld the government's actions as military necessity. In Korematsu, three judges dissented; Justice Frank Murphy wrote that the exclusion fell into "the ugly abyss of racism". On the same day as Korematsu, in Ex parte Endo, the Court ruled that the government could not continue to detain a citizen whose loyalty it accepted, and the exclusion orders were lifted from January 1945. Others resisted the draft from inside the camps, such as the Heart Mountain Fair Play Committee, who said they would serve once their rights as citizens were restored; dozens were imprisoned for this.

The camps closed during 1945 and 1946. Many families returned to find homes vandalised, farms taken and businesses gone, and faced continued hostility. They rebuilt slowly, and many survivors did not speak about the camps even to their children for decades.

In the 1970s a redress movement grew, led by former internees and their children. In 1980 Congress created the Commission on Wartime Relocation and Internment of Civilians, which reported in 1983 that the policy was not justified by military necessity and was caused by "race prejudice, war hysteria and a failure of political leadership". In 1983 Korematsu's conviction was overturned after researchers found that the government had hidden intelligence evidence from the Supreme Court in 1944. In 1988 President Ronald Reagan signed the Civil Liberties Act, which formally apologised and paid 20 000 dollars to each surviving internee; about 82 000 people received payment. In 2018 the Supreme Court stated that the Korematsu decision had been wrong the day it was decided.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Only Japanese citizens living in America were interned." About two thirds of those removed were American citizens by birth; the rest were immigrants who had been legally prevented from becoming citizens.

Exhibit two: "Japanese Americans were removed because they had committed sabotage." No Japanese American was ever convicted of spying or sabotage for Japan during the war.

Exhibit three: "The American camps were the same as Nazi death camps." They were not. American camps were unjust imprisonment based on race, with deaths from poor conditions and some shootings, but they were not designed for murder. Comparing carefully, with accurate distinctions, is good historical practice.

Exhibit four: "Everyone accepted removal without protest." Many complied because they had little choice, but there were court challenges, draft resistance, protests in the camps and, decades later, the redress movement.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three things: a notice on a pole, a room in a barracks and a letter of apology.

## Subtopic: A Notice on a Telephone Pole

Imagine you are twelve years old, born in California, and you love baseball and your mother's cooking. Your grandparents came from Japan many years ago. Your family runs a small vegetable farm. Then, in December 1941, Japan bombs Pearl Harbour, and suddenly some of your neighbours look at you differently, as if you were the enemy, even though you have never been to Japan.

That is what happened to thousands of children. Even before the war, many white Americans were prejudiced against people from Asia. The law did not allow Japanese immigrants to become citizens or own farmland in California. But their children, born in America, were citizens.

After Pearl Harbour, rumours spread that Japanese Americans were spies. They were not. The government's own experts said almost all were loyal. But on 19 February 1942, President Roosevelt signed an order, Executive Order 9066, that let the army remove people from the West Coast.

Notices went up on telephone poles. Families had about a week to leave. They could take only what they could carry. Farms and shops were sold for almost nothing. Some families first lived in horse stables at a racetrack.

So where were they sent next?

## Subtopic: A Room in a Barracks

About 120 000 people, two out of three of them American citizens, were sent to ten camps in deserts and swamps far from the coast. Think of rows and rows of wooden huts, surrounded by barbed wire, with soldiers watching from towers.

A whole family lived in one small room with a single light bulb. Everyone ate together in big halls and used shared toilets. In summer the dust storms got into everything; in winter it was freezing.

People did their best to stay strong. They started schools, newspapers, baseball teams and vegetable gardens. Then the government gave adults a loyalty test. One question asked if they would fight for America, and another asked them to give up any loyalty to Japan's emperor. Some answered no as a protest, because how can you ask someone to prove loyalty while you keep them behind a fence?

And here is something remarkable: thousands of young Japanese American men joined the army. Their unit, the 442nd, became one of the most decorated in American history, while their families were still in the camps.

So did anyone stand up against all this?

## Subtopic: A Letter of Apology

Yes. A young welder named Fred Korematsu refused to leave and was arrested. His case went all the way to the Supreme Court, but in 1944 the court said the government was allowed to do it. Three judges disagreed, and one called it racism.

The camps closed in 1945 and 1946. Many families came home to find their houses broken into and their farms gone. Many never talked about the camps, even to their children.

Years later, those children asked questions and demanded justice. In 1983 an official commission said the real causes were race prejudice, war hysteria and a failure of political leadership. In 1988 President Reagan signed a law that apologised and paid each survivor 20 000 dollars. Fred Korematsu's conviction was cancelled, and later he received a top award for standing up for rights.

Here is the line to remember: fear and racism led a democracy to lock up its own citizens, and admitting the wrong took more than forty years.
