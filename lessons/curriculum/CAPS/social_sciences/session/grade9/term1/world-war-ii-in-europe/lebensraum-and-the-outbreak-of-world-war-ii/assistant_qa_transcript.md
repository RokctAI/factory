### subtopic_1 — Hitler's Aims and the First Steps, 1933 to 1936
**Assistant:** Could Britain and France have stopped Hitler in 1936?

**Tutor:** Many historians believe that the Rhineland in March 1936 was the best opportunity, because Germany was militarily weak and Hitler himself regarded the move as a gamble, but stopping him would have required a willingness to fight that neither government nor public had. The German force that entered the Rhineland was small, about 22 000 soldiers with some armed police, and the German army had only begun to rearm a year earlier. Hitler later said that the forty-eight hours after the march were the most nerve-racking of his life, and some German generals had orders to withdraw if the French army moved. France had a much larger army and the Maginot Line of fortresses. Yet France was in the middle of an election campaign, its generals overestimated German strength and advised against acting, and its government would not act without Britain. In Britain, public opinion largely saw the Rhineland as German territory and Versailles as unfair; the newspapers spoke of Germany walking into its own back garden. Neither country wanted to risk a war over a zone inside Germany. The consequences were serious: Germany fortified the Rhineland, making it harder for France to help its eastern allies, Hitler's prestige at home soared, and he concluded that the democracies would always back down. When you write an essay on appeasement, this is a good example of how a decision that seemed reasonable at the time looks different with hindsight, a distinction historians must handle carefully in Grade 10 and Grade 12 History.

### subtopic_2 — Appeasement: Austria, the Sudetenland and Munich
**Assistant:** Was appeasement simply cowardice?

**Tutor:** Most historians today reject that simple verdict, while still regarding appeasement as a policy that failed, because the people who made it faced real constraints and reasonable fears. The cowardice view was set out in 1940 in a book called Guilty Men, which blamed Chamberlain and his colleagues for leaving Britain unprepared, and Churchill's speeches reinforced it. Later historians, working with government records, emphasised the pressures: Britain's armed forces were not ready, and its military chiefs warned in 1938 that Britain could not defend Czechoslovakia or protect its cities from bombing; the public had been deeply marked by the losses of 1914 to 1918; many British people accepted that Versailles had been harsh; the Empire's Dominions, including South Africa, opposed war over Central Europe; the economy was still weak after the Depression; and some feared the Soviet Union more than Germany. Seen this way, Munich bought a year in which Britain built the fighter aircraft and radar stations that won the Battle of Britain in 1940. Critics answer that Germany also rearmed during that year, gained the Czech arms factories and army equipment, and that the Soviet Union, excluded at Munich, turned to Hitler in 1939. A strong answer weighs both, using evidence, and judges whether appeasement was understandable in 1938 even if it proved mistaken. That kind of balanced judgement is what source-based and essay questions reward from Grade 9 through Grade 12.

### subtopic_3 — From Prague to Poland, 1939
**Assistant:** Why would Stalin sign a pact with Hitler when they were enemies?

**Tutor:** Because each dictator calculated that the pact served his immediate interests, even though they remained ideological enemies who expected to fight each other eventually. For Hitler, the pact removed the danger of a war on two fronts, which he believed had cost Germany World War I. With the Soviet Union neutral, he could invade Poland and then turn west against France and Britain, and he would receive Soviet raw materials such as oil and grain despite the British blockade. For Stalin, there were several reasons. He distrusted Britain and France, who had excluded him from Munich and whose negotiators in Moscow in August 1939 were junior officials with no power to sign. Poland refused to let Soviet troops cross its territory, which made an alliance with the West militarily difficult. His army had been weakened by his own purges, in which thousands of officers had been executed, so he needed time to rebuild it. And the secret protocol gave him eastern Poland, the Baltic states of Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania, and part of Romania, pushing the Soviet border westwards. He may also have hoped that Germany and the Western democracies would exhaust each other in a long war. The gamble failed in June 1941, when Hitler broke the pact and invaded the Soviet Union in the largest military operation in history. The pact remains controversial in eastern Europe, where the secret protocol led to occupation, deportations and suffering in the countries it divided.

### subtopic_4 — Axis versus Allies, South Africa's Decision, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why was South Africa's decision to join the war so close?

**Tutor:** Because white South Africa was deeply divided between those who saw the country as part of the British Commonwealth and those Afrikaner nationalists who wanted full independence from Britain and saw no reason to fight its wars. Only Parliament decided, and Parliament was elected almost entirely by white voters, so the debate reflected divisions within the white minority. Prime Minister Hertzog, leader of the governing United Party, believed South Africa had no direct interest in a European war and that the country should remain neutral, while fulfilling treaty obligations such as allowing Britain to use the naval base at Simon's Town. Some of his supporters also had sympathy for Germany, partly because Britain had fought the Boer republics only forty years earlier, and some admired Nazi ideas. His deputy Jan Smuts argued that Nazi Germany threatened the whole free world, including South Africa, and that the country should stand with Britain and the Commonwealth. The United Party split, and on 4 September 1939 Smuts's motion won by 80 votes to 67. The governor-general refused Hertzog's request to dissolve Parliament for an election, Hertzog resigned, and Smuts formed a government. The division continued throughout the war: the Ossewabrandwag carried out acts of sabotage, and the bitterness helped the National Party, which opposed the war, to win the 1948 election. Black South Africans had no vote in the decision, and their service on unequal terms became a grievance that strengthened the struggle for rights you will study in Term 3.
