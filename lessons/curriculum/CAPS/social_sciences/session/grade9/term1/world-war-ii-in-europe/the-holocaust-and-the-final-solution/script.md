# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the Holocaust, the genocide of European Jews by Nazi Germany and its collaborators during World War II, and the Nazi plan known as the Final Solution. It covers the meaning of the terms Holocaust and genocide; the ghettos and the mass shootings that began the killing; the Wannsee Conference and the organisation of the Final Solution; the extermination camps; and questions of responsibility, rescue, liberation, justice and remembrance. The anchor fact is the number itself: about six million Jewish people were murdered, roughly two thirds of the Jews of Europe, among them about one and a half million children. The governing idea is that the Holocaust was not a sudden outburst but the end point of a process that began with words and laws, carried out by a modern state with the participation of many thousands of ordinary people, and that remembering it accurately is a defence against its repetition.

## Subtopic: From Persecution to Genocide: Ghettos and Mass Shootings

The word Holocaust comes from a Greek word meaning sacrifice by fire; many Jewish people prefer the Hebrew word Shoah, meaning catastrophe. Genocide is the deliberate killing of members of a national, ethnic, racial or religious group with the intent to destroy that group in whole or in part. The term was created in 1944 by the lawyer Raphael Lemkin, a Polish Jew who lost most of his family in the Holocaust, and genocide was made a crime under international law by the United Nations Genocide Convention of 1948.

The war brought millions more Jews under Nazi control. When Germany conquered Poland in 1939, about two million Polish Jews fell into its hands. The Nazis forced Jews into ghettos, closed areas of cities sealed off by walls or fences. The largest was the Warsaw ghetto, sealed in November 1940, where more than 400 000 people were crowded into about 3,4 square kilometres, often seven or more to a room. Jews were forced to wear a yellow Star of David on their clothing. In the ghettos, deliberate starvation, disease and forced labour killed hundreds of thousands: in Warsaw alone more than 80 000 people died of hunger and disease before deportations began. Jewish councils were made to administer the ghettos under German orders, placing them in impossible positions.

In June 1941 Germany invaded the Soviet Union. Behind the advancing army came the Einsatzgruppen, mobile killing units of the SS and police, assisted by other German units and by local collaborators. They rounded up Jewish communities town by town and shot them at the edges of pits and ravines. At Babi Yar, a ravine outside Kyiv, 33 771 Jews were shot in two days at the end of September 1941. By the end of the war, the shootings in the east had killed about one and a half to two million Jews, together with Roma, communist officials and others. This was the first stage of systematic mass murder, and it shows that the Holocaust was not carried out only in camps.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of Holocaust, Shoah and genocide, the ghettos, and the mass shootings by the Einsatzgruppen.

## Subtopic: The Final Solution and the Wannsee Conference

The Nazis used deliberately bland language to hide their crimes. The Final Solution of the Jewish Question was their term for the plan to murder all the Jews of Europe. Historians debate exactly when the decision was made: it emerged during the second half of 1941, as the mass shootings in the Soviet Union expanded and the first experiments with poison gas were made, and it was driven from the top, by Hitler, Himmler and Reinhard Heydrich, the head of the Reich Security Main Office.

On 20 January 1942 Heydrich chaired a meeting of fifteen senior officials from government ministries, the SS and the Nazi Party at a villa beside the Wannsee lake in Berlin. Its purpose was not to decide whether to murder the Jews of Europe, but to coordinate how the different departments would carry it out. The minutes, taken by Adolf Eichmann, listed the Jewish populations of every European country, including those not yet under German control such as Britain, adding up to about eleven million people. They spoke of evacuation to the east and of natural reduction through hard labour, code words for murder. A copy of these minutes survived the war and is among the most important documents proving the planning of the genocide.

The Final Solution depended on the machinery of a modern state. Railway officials scheduled trains to carry Jews from across Europe to the camps, charging the SS for each passenger. Police and local officials in occupied and allied countries rounded people up. Companies supplied gas, built crematoria and used slave labour. Clerks recorded property seized from victims. Collaborating governments and local police in countries such as France, Hungary, Slovakia and Romania, and in the occupied Soviet territories, took part in deportations and killings. The genocide therefore involved hundreds of thousands of people, from those who planned it to those who did their jobs and looked away.

This section's questions are ready: the meaning of the Final Solution, the Wannsee Conference and its purpose, the minutes, and the role of the state and its collaborators.

## Subtopic: The Extermination Camps

Two kinds of camp must be distinguished. Concentration camps, such as Dachau, Buchenwald and Sachsenhausen, held prisoners in brutal conditions of forced labour, starvation and punishment, and many thousands died in them. Extermination camps, also called death camps, were built for one purpose: to murder people on arrival. There were six, all in occupied Poland: Chełmno, Bełżec, Sobibór, Treblinka, Majdanek and Auschwitz-Birkenau. Majdanek and Auschwitz were both concentration and extermination camps.

Jews were deported to the camps from all over Europe, from Amsterdam, Paris, Prague, Athens, Budapest and the ghettos of Poland, packed into closed goods wagons for journeys of days without food, water or sanitation. Many died on the way. At Bełżec, Sobibór and Treblinka almost everyone was killed within hours of arrival in gas chambers using exhaust fumes from engines; about 870 000 people or more were murdered at Treblinka alone. At Auschwitz-Birkenau, SS doctors made a selection on the railway ramp: a minority judged fit for labour were sent into the camp, tattooed with numbers and worked, often to death; the elderly, the sick, mothers and young children were sent directly to gas chambers disguised as shower rooms, where the poison Zyklon B was used. The bodies were burned in crematoria. Victims' belongings, clothing, hair and gold teeth were taken and sent to Germany. About 1,1 million people were murdered at Auschwitz, about one million of them Jews; the others included Poles, Roma and Sinti, and Soviet prisoners of war.

The Nazis also murdered other groups in vast numbers. Between 200 000 and 500 000 Roma and Sinti were killed. About three million Soviet prisoners of war died of deliberate starvation, exposure and execution. Millions of Polish and other Slavic civilians were killed. Disabled people, gay men, Jehovah's Witnesses and political prisoners died in the camps. Historians use the term Holocaust for the genocide of the Jews, while recognising all victims of Nazi persecution.

As Soviet forces advanced in 1944 and 1945, the SS tried to destroy evidence and forced prisoners on death marches westward, during which tens of thousands died. Soviet soldiers liberated Auschwitz on 27 January 1945, now International Holocaust Remembrance Day; British troops entered Bergen-Belsen in April 1945 and found thousands of unburied bodies.

Take this section's questions now: concentration and extermination camps, deportation, selection at Auschwitz, the other victims, and liberation.

## Subtopic: Responsibility, Rescue, Remembrance and the Error Museum

Historians describe several groups of people in the Holocaust. Perpetrators planned and carried out the killing. Collaborators helped, from governments to individuals who betrayed their neighbours. Bystanders saw what was happening and did nothing, through fear, indifference or self-interest. Victims were persecuted and murdered. Resisters fought back, as you will see in the next lesson. Rescuers risked their lives to save others: the German industrialist Oskar Schindler protected about 1 200 Jewish workers; the Polish social worker Irena Sendler and her network smuggled about 2 500 Jewish children out of the Warsaw ghetto; the Swedish diplomat Raoul Wallenberg issued protective passports to tens of thousands of Jews in Budapest; and in Denmark ordinary people ferried about 7 000 Jews to safety in Sweden in October 1943. Israel honours more than 28 000 such people as Righteous Among the Nations.

After the war, the International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg in 1945 and 1946 tried leading Nazis for crimes against peace, war crimes and a new charge, crimes against humanity; twelve were sentenced to death. Eichmann was captured in Argentina and tried in Israel in 1961. These trials established that following orders is no defence for taking part in mass murder. The horror of the Holocaust shaped the Universal Declaration of Human Rights of 1948 and the Genocide Convention, which you will study in Term 3.

Survivors' testimony, the Nazis' own documents, photographs taken at liberation, the camps themselves and the records of the trials provide overwhelming evidence. Anne Frank's diary, written while her family hid in Amsterdam from 1942 to 1944, gives a single voice to the millions; she died in Bergen-Belsen in early 1945, aged fifteen. South Africa has Holocaust and Genocide Centres in Cape Town, Johannesburg and Durban, where survivors who settled in South Africa have told their stories.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: the Holocaust happened mainly in Germany; most killing took place in occupied eastern Europe, especially Poland and the Soviet Union. Two: the Wannsee Conference decided to begin killing Jews; mass killing had already begun, and Wannsee coordinated it. Three: all camps were extermination camps; concentration camps and extermination camps differed in purpose. Four: only a few Nazi leaders were responsible; hundreds of thousands of perpetrators and collaborators took part. Five: the evidence for the Holocaust is uncertain; it is among the most thoroughly documented events in history, and Holocaust denial is a form of antisemitism that contradicts the evidence.

The questions for this section are ready: perpetrators, bystanders and rescuers, the Nuremberg trials, Anne Frank, remembrance in South Africa and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same history again, told gently but truthfully, through walls built around a neighbourhood, a meeting at a house beside a lake, and one girl's diary — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Walls Around a Neighbourhood

Before we begin, take a breath. This is the hardest history you will study, and it is important to study it carefully, because the people it happened to were real: families like yours, with names, jobs, favourite foods and plans for the future.

When Germany conquered Poland in 1939, millions of Jewish people came under Nazi rule. The Nazis forced them out of their homes and into small, closed parts of cities called ghettos, surrounded by walls and guards. Imagine your whole town squeezed into a few streets, many families in one flat, not enough food, no medicine, and the gates locked. In the Warsaw ghetto, more than 400 000 people were crammed into an area of about three square kilometres. Tens of thousands died of hunger and disease. Jewish people had to wear a yellow star on their clothes so they could be picked out.

In 1941 Germany invaded the Soviet Union. Behind the army came special killing squads. They went from town to town, rounded up the Jewish families, men, women and children, and shot them beside pits. In one ravine called Babi Yar, near the city of Kyiv, more than 33 000 people were murdered in two days.

There is a word for trying to destroy a whole group of people because of who they are: genocide. The genocide of Europe's Jews is called the Holocaust, or in Hebrew, the Shoah, meaning catastrophe.

Keep the walled neighbourhood in mind, and try the questions for this part: the ghettos, the yellow star, the killing squads, and the meaning of genocide.

## Subtopic: A Meeting at a House Beside a Lake

On 20 January 1942, fifteen senior Nazi officials met for lunch and a meeting at a large villa beside a lake called Wannsee, in Berlin. They were not deciding whether to kill the Jews of Europe; the killing had already started. They were organising how every government department would work together to do it. They called it the Final Solution. In their notes they listed about eleven million Jewish people across Europe. They used polite words like evacuation to hide what they meant: murder. A copy of those notes survived the war, and it is one of the clearest pieces of proof we have.

After that, the Nazis built and used death camps, all of them in Poland. Trains from all over Europe carried Jewish families there in locked goods wagons, for days, with no food or water. At camps like Treblinka almost everyone was murdered within hours of arriving. At Auschwitz-Birkenau, the largest, Nazi doctors divided people on the railway platform: a few were sent to work as slaves; most, including old people, mothers and small children, were sent straight to gas chambers made to look like showers. About 1,1 million people were murdered at Auschwitz alone.

It took a huge number of people to do this: officials, police, railway workers, guards, and many others who did their jobs and did not ask questions. And the Nazis murdered other groups too: Roma and Sinti, Soviet prisoners of war, Polish civilians and disabled people.

In total, about six million Jewish people were murdered, about two out of every three Jews in Europe, among them about one and a half million children.

Hold the meeting at the lake in mind, and try this part's questions: what the Final Solution meant, the death camps, and the six million.

## Subtopic: Anne's Diary and Why We Remember

Anne Frank was a Jewish girl who lived in Amsterdam. When she was thirteen, her family went into hiding in a secret annexe behind a bookcase in her father's office building. For two years they stayed silent during the day, while brave helpers brought them food. Anne wrote in her diary about her hopes, her quarrels with her mother, her dreams of being a writer. In August 1944 someone betrayed them. Anne died in the camp at Bergen-Belsen in early 1945, aged fifteen, only weeks before British troops arrived. Her diary was saved, and millions of people have read it.

Not everyone looked away. Some people risked their lives to save others. In Denmark, ordinary fishermen carried about 7 000 Jewish people across the sea to safety in Sweden. A Polish woman named Irena Sendler smuggled about 2 500 children out of the Warsaw ghetto. A German factory owner, Oskar Schindler, saved about 1 200 of his Jewish workers.

On 27 January 1945, Soviet soldiers reached Auschwitz and found the survivors. That date is now International Holocaust Remembrance Day. After the war, Nazi leaders were put on trial at Nuremberg for crimes against humanity, and the world agreed that following orders is never an excuse for murder.

Why do we remember? Because it began with words, lies and small laws, the steps you studied in earlier lessons, and because people who stayed silent let it grow. South Africa has Holocaust and Genocide Centres in Cape Town, Johannesburg and Durban, where survivors who made their homes here told their stories.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Holocaust was the murder of about six million Jews by Nazi Germany and its helpers. It moved from ghettos, to mass shootings, to the Final Solution organised at Wannsee in 1942, to death camps like Auschwitz and Treblinka. Others were murdered too. Some people rescued, many looked away. We remember so that it never happens again. Grade 11 History and Term 3's human rights lesson build on this.

Hold Anne's diary and the rescuers in mind, and take the final questions of the lesson: rescue, liberation, the trials, and why we remember.
