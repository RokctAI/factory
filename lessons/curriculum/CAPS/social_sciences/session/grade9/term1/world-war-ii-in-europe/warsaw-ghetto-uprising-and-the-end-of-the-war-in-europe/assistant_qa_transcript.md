### subtopic_1 — The Warsaw Ghetto and the Great Deportation
**Assistant:** Why did the ghetto fighters rise up if they knew they could not win?

**Tutor:** The fighters left their own explanations, so historians can answer this question from primary sources. By the end of 1942 the young people of the ŻOB knew from escapees and from the Polish underground that the trains went to Treblinka and that resettlement meant death. They had watched about 265 000 of their families and neighbours taken away during the Great Deportation, often without resistance, because people had believed the German lies and hoped that obedience would save them. The fighters were determined that this would not happen again. Their reasons were several. They wanted to choose the manner of their own deaths rather than be murdered helplessly in gas chambers. They wanted to make the Germans pay a price for the killing and to save at least some lives by delaying deportations, which the January 1943 resistance had done. They wanted to defend Jewish honour and to answer the Nazi propaganda that portrayed Jews as weak and cowardly. And they wanted to leave a message for the future, which is why the uprising is so closely linked to the hidden archive of Ringelblum. Mordechaj Anielewicz wrote in his last letter that the dream of his life had been realised: Jewish self-defence in the ghetto had become a fact. In source-based questions you may be asked to explain the purpose of such a letter; it is both a personal farewell and a deliberate statement for history, so it shows the fighters' values as well as events.

### subtopic_2 — The Uprising of April and May 1943
**Assistant:** How did German forces finally destroy the ghetto during the uprising?

**Tutor:** When the German forces entered the ghetto on 19 April 1943, they expected the operation to take three days. Instead they were ambushed with gunfire, grenades and petrol bombs and forced to withdraw, losing some soldiers and vehicles. The SS general Jürgen Stroop took command with about 2 000 men, including SS units, police, army engineers and auxiliaries, supported by artillery and armoured vehicles. Because the fighters and thousands of civilians were hidden in bunkers, cellars and attics, Stroop decided to burn the ghetto systematically, block by block, with flamethrowers and explosives. People were forced out by fire and smoke; some jumped from burning buildings. Bunkers were located with the help of informers and listening devices, then smoked out with gas or blown up, and the sewers were flooded. The fighters kept up resistance from the ruins for almost four weeks. Stroop recorded the operation in daily reports and a leather-bound album of photographs for his superiors, titled The Jewish Quarter of Warsaw Is No More. It claimed about 56 000 Jews captured or killed, including about 7 000 shot in the ghetto. Ironically, this perpetrator's record became evidence: one photograph of a small boy with raised hands is among the most famous images of the Holocaust, and the report was used at the Nuremberg trials. Stroop was later tried and executed in Poland in 1952.

### subtopic_3 — The Tide Turns: Stalingrad, North Africa, Italy and D-Day
**Assistant:** What role did South Africans play in the Allied victory in Europe?

**Tutor:** South Africa entered the war on 6 September 1939 after Parliament voted narrowly, by 80 votes to 67, to support Britain; Prime Minister Hertzog resigned and Jan Smuts took over. The decision divided white South Africa, and some Afrikaner nationalists opposed the war, a division that influenced politics after 1945. Because there was no conscription, all South Africans who served were volunteers, about 334 000 full-time, of whom about 211 000 were white, about 77 000 African and about 46 000 coloured and Indian. South African forces first fought in East Africa against Italy in 1940 and 1941, helping to free Ethiopia. In North Africa they suffered heavy losses at Sidi Rezegh in November 1941 and the surrender of Tobruk in June 1942, then took part in the decisive victory at El Alamein in late 1942. The South African Air Force flew in North Africa and Italy, and supply flights to the Warsaw Uprising of 1944. The 6th South African Armoured Division fought through Italy from 1944 until the German surrender there in May 1945. About 11 000 South Africans died. Black servicemen were denied weapons and equal pay, and after the war they received lesser rewards than white veterans, while the troopship Mendi tragedy of the First World War remained a bitter memory of unequal recognition.

### subtopic_4 — The Fall of Berlin, VE Day, the Cost of War and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why is 8 May 1945 remembered as Victory in Europe Day?

**Tutor:** Because on that date Germany's unconditional surrender to the Allies came into effect, ending almost six years of war in Europe that began with the invasion of Poland on 1 September 1939. After Hitler's suicide on 30 April 1945, he was succeeded by Admiral Dönitz, who tried to surrender to the Western Allies alone while continuing to fight the Soviet Union, so that German soldiers and civilians could escape westward. The Allies insisted on unconditional surrender to all of them together. The German general Alfred Jodl signed the surrender at Eisenhower's headquarters in Reims, France, on 7 May, to take effect at 23:01 Central European Time on 8 May. Stalin insisted on a second, formal signing in Berlin before Soviet commanders, completed late on 8 May, when it was already 9 May in Moscow. That is why Western countries remember VE Day on 8 May and Russia celebrates Victory Day on 9 May. Crowds celebrated in London, Paris, New York, Cape Town and Johannesburg. Yet victory was incomplete: the war against Japan continued until August, millions of displaced people and Holocaust survivors had nowhere to go, and the Allies who had won together were already distrustful of each other. Within a few years Europe was divided between a Soviet-controlled east and a Western-aligned west, which is why the end of World War II is also the beginning of the Cold War studied in Term 2.
