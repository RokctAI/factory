# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising of 1943 and the defeat of Nazi Germany in 1945. It covers the Warsaw Ghetto and the Great Deportation of 1942; the uprising itself, its leaders and its meaning; the turning points that shifted the war in favour of the Allies, including South Africa's part in North Africa and Italy; and the final collapse of the Third Reich, Victory in Europe Day and the cost of the war. The anchor dates are 19 April 1943, when the uprising began, and 8 May 1945, when Germany's unconditional surrender took effect. The governing idea is that resistance took many forms, armed and unarmed, and that while the uprising could not defeat the German army, it was a deliberate choice to die with dignity and to leave a record for the world, while the wider war was decided by the combined weight of the Allied armies, industry and peoples.

## Subtopic: The Warsaw Ghetto and the Great Deportation

Before the war, Warsaw had the largest Jewish community in Europe, about 375 000 people, roughly thirty per cent of the city's population. They were teachers, tailors, doctors, writers, rabbis, labourers and students, with schools, newspapers, theatres and political movements of every kind. After Germany conquered Poland in September 1939, the occupiers issued a stream of anti-Jewish decrees: Jews had to wear a white armband with a blue Star of David, their property was seized, their schools were closed and they were forced into labour.

In October 1940 the German authorities ordered the creation of a ghetto, and in November 1940 it was sealed behind a wall about three metres high topped with barbed wire and broken glass. More than 400 000 people, including Jews expelled from surrounding towns, were crammed into an area of about 3,4 square kilometres, so that on average seven or more people shared each room. The official food ration for Jews was deliberately set at a starvation level, often quoted at under 200 calories a day, far less than a person needs. Typhus spread through the crowded streets. Between 1940 and mid-1942, more than 80 000 people died of hunger and disease inside the ghetto.

Life went on in defiance of these conditions. Smugglers, many of them children slipping through holes in the wall, brought in food at the risk of being shot. Secret schools taught children, and soup kitchens fed the hungry. The historian Emanuel Ringelblum organised a secret archive called Oyneg Shabes, which collected diaries, photographs, posters, reports and testimonies to record what was happening. Before the end, its members buried the documents in milk cans and metal boxes. Parts of the archive were dug up from the ruins after the war in 1946 and 1950 and are among the most important primary sources of the Holocaust.

On 22 July 1942 the Germans began what is called the Great Deportation. Jews were told they were being resettled in the east for work. Each day thousands were marched to the Umschlagplatz, the loading square beside the railway, and packed into goods wagons. By September 1942 about 265 000 people had been deported to Treblinka, an extermination camp about 80 kilometres north-east of Warsaw, where almost all were murdered on arrival. Thousands more were killed in the ghetto itself. Among those deported was the educator Janusz Korczak, who refused offers to save himself and walked with the children of his orphanage to the train. When the deportations paused, only about 55 000 to 60 000 people remained in the ghetto, many of them hiding illegally.

So the starting question for the uprising is: once people understood that resettlement meant death, what choices remained to them?

## Subtopic: The Uprising of April and May 1943

As news of Treblinka reached the ghetto through escapees and the Polish underground, young people from Jewish youth and political movements concluded that they would not go quietly. The Jewish Combat Organisation, known by its Polish initials ŻOB, was formed in July 1942 and led from late 1942 by Mordechaj Anielewicz, a 23-year-old from a youth movement. A second group, the Jewish Military Union, known as ŻZW, was formed by members of a different political movement. Together they had perhaps 700 to 1 000 fighters. Their weapons were few: pistols, a small number of rifles, a machine gun or two, hand grenades and home-made petrol bombs, some bought at great cost or supplied by the Polish underground Home Army.

The first armed clash came on 18 January 1943, when the Germans began a new deportation and fighters attacked them in the streets. The Germans withdrew after several days, having taken about 5 000 people instead of the many more they intended. To the people of the ghetto this was a sign that resistance was possible, and many built hidden bunkers under their homes.

On 19 April 1943, the eve of the Jewish festival of Passover, German forces entered the ghetto to carry out its final destruction. They were met by gunfire and petrol bombs and were driven back. The SS commander Jürgen Stroop was put in charge, with about 2 000 troops supported by artillery and tanks. Unable to defeat the fighters in open combat, he ordered the ghetto to be burned building by building and the bunkers to be flooded or smoked out with gas. The fighters and civilians held out for almost four weeks, longer than some European countries had resisted the German army in 1940.

On 8 May 1943, German forces surrounded the ŻOB headquarters bunker at 18 Miła Street; Anielewicz and many of his comrades died there, many by their own hand rather than be captured. A small number of fighters, including Marek Edelman, escaped through the sewers. On 16 May Stroop declared that the Jewish residential district no longer existed and blew up the Great Synagogue of Warsaw to mark the end. His own report, a bound album with photographs titled The Jewish Quarter of Warsaw Is No More, claimed about 56 000 Jews captured or killed. Most survivors were deported to Treblinka, Majdanek or labour camps, and the ghetto was reduced to rubble.

The uprising had no hope of military victory, and the fighters knew it. Its significance was moral and historical. It was the largest Jewish revolt of the war and the first major urban uprising in occupied Europe. It inspired revolts at Treblinka in August 1943 and Sobibór in October 1943. It disproved the Nazi claim that Jews would not fight, and it showed that resistance included not only fighting but also smuggling food, teaching children, keeping records and maintaining faith and dignity. Anielewicz wrote in his last letter that the dream of his life had come true: Jewish self-defence in the ghetto had become a fact. In the Grade 11 study of the Holocaust, this broad meaning of resistance is a key concept.

So the next question is: how did the wider war turn from German victory to German defeat?

## Subtopic: The Tide Turns: Stalingrad, North Africa, Italy and D-Day

In 1941 and 1942, Germany controlled most of continental Europe. Three developments reversed this. First, Hitler's invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941 opened an enormous Eastern Front. Second, after Japan attacked Pearl Harbor in December 1941, Germany declared war on the United States, bringing the world's largest industrial economy into the war against it. Third, the Allies, especially the Soviet Union, the United States and Britain with its Commonwealth, learned to coordinate their production and strategy.

The Battle of Stalingrad, from August 1942 to February 1943, was the decisive turning point in the east. The German Sixth Army fought its way into the city on the Volga River but was encircled by a Soviet counter-attack in November 1942. Hitler forbade a breakout, and on 2 February 1943 the remnants surrendered; about 91 000 German soldiers were taken prisoner. Casualties on both sides were enormous, with total military and civilian deaths estimated at well over a million. After Stalingrad and the great tank battle at Kursk in July 1943, the Soviet Red Army advanced westward almost continuously.

In North Africa, South African forces played a major role. South Africa had entered the war in September 1939 after a narrow parliamentary vote, led by Prime Minister Jan Smuts, and all its soldiers were volunteers; about 334 000 South Africans served full-time, including men and women of all races. Black, coloured and Indian South Africans served in the Native Military Corps and the Cape Corps, but under segregationist policy they were generally not allowed to carry weapons and served as drivers, stretcher-bearers, guards and labourers. Lance-Corporal Job Maseko, captured at Tobruk in June 1942 when about 10 700 South Africans became prisoners, sank a German supply ship in the harbour with a home-made explosive and was awarded the Military Medal. South African divisions fought in the Second Battle of El Alamein in Egypt in October and November 1942, when British Commonwealth forces under Montgomery defeated Rommel's army; by May 1943 the German and Italian forces in North Africa had surrendered. The 6th South African Armoured Division then fought through Italy in 1944 and 1945 after the Allied landings in Sicily and mainland Italy, which led to Mussolini's fall in 1943.

On 6 June 1944, D-Day, the Western Allies under General Eisenhower landed on the beaches of Normandy in northern France in Operation Overlord, the largest seaborne invasion in history, with about 156 000 troops landing on the first day. After hard fighting, Paris was liberated in August 1944. Germany's last major offensive in the west, the Battle of the Bulge in the Ardennes in December 1944, was defeated by January 1945. Meanwhile Allied bombing destroyed German factories, transport and cities, killing hundreds of thousands of civilians, a policy still debated today.

So the final question is: how did the war in Europe end, and what did it cost?

## Subtopic: The Fall of Berlin, VE Day, the Cost of War and the Error Museum

By early 1945 Germany was squeezed between the Western Allies crossing the Rhine and the Red Army advancing from the east. As Allied soldiers reached the camps, the full horror of the Holocaust was revealed: Soviet troops liberated Auschwitz on 27 January 1945, and British troops entered Bergen-Belsen on 15 April 1945, filming the thousands of unburied dead. On 25 April American and Soviet troops met at the Elbe River. In Italy, Mussolini was captured and shot by Italian partisans on 28 April.

The Soviet assault on Berlin began in mid-April 1945. Hitler remained in his underground bunker, refusing to accept defeat and blaming the German people for failing him. On 30 April 1945, with Soviet troops a few hundred metres away, he killed himself. Berlin surrendered on 2 May. Germany's unconditional surrender was signed at Reims on 7 May and confirmed in Berlin; it took effect on 8 May 1945, celebrated as Victory in Europe or VE Day. Because of the time difference and a second signing, the Soviet Union celebrated on 9 May. The war against Japan continued until August 1945, which a later lesson covers.

The cost of the war is almost impossible to grasp. Total deaths are estimated at between 70 and 85 million people worldwide, the majority civilians. The Soviet Union lost about 27 million people, Poland about one in six of its pre-war population, and about six million Jews were murdered in the Holocaust. About 11 000 South Africans died in the war. Cities such as Warsaw, Berlin, Dresden and Stalingrad lay in ruins, and millions of displaced people were homeless across Europe. After the war, leading Nazis were tried at Nuremberg in 1945 and 1946, and Germany was divided into four occupation zones, a division that became the front line of the Cold War studied in Term 2 and in Grade 12.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "The Warsaw Ghetto Uprising and the Warsaw Uprising are the same event." They are different. The Warsaw Ghetto Uprising was the Jewish revolt of April and May 1943. The Warsaw Uprising was the revolt of the Polish Home Army against the Germans from August to October 1944, when the ghetto had already been destroyed.

Exhibit two: "The ghetto fighters expected to win." They did not. They fought knowing they would almost certainly die, to choose how they died, to kill those who were killing their people, and to leave a record of resistance.

Exhibit three: "D-Day alone defeated Germany." D-Day was crucial, but the Eastern Front, where most German soldiers fought and died, was the largest theatre of the war. Victory came from combined efforts on many fronts.

Exhibit four: "Black South Africans did not take part in the war." They served in large numbers in the Native Military Corps, although segregation denied them weapons, equal pay and, after the war, the recognition white veterans received.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of it as three scenes: a wall, a turning tide and a final day.

## Subtopic: Choosing to Fight Behind the Wall

Imagine a whole neighbourhood, bigger than your town's centre, suddenly surrounded by a high wall with broken glass on top. That is what happened to the Jews of Warsaw in Poland in November 1940. More than 400 000 people were squeezed into a tiny area, seven or more to a room. The Germans gave them so little food that tens of thousands starved or died of disease. Children risked their lives sneaking through holes in the wall to bring back bread.

People still did everything they could to stay human. Teachers ran secret classes. A historian named Emanuel Ringelblum and his friends collected diaries, photos and notes, then buried them in milk cans so the world would one day know the truth. Some of those cans were dug up after the war.

In the summer of 1942 the Germans said the Jews would be resettled to work in the east. It was a lie. About 265 000 people were taken by train to Treblinka and murdered there. A teacher named Janusz Korczak walked with the orphans he cared for all the way to the train, refusing to leave them.

When the young people still in the ghetto understood the truth, they made a decision: if we are going to die, we will die fighting. A 23-year-old named Mordechaj Anielewicz led them. On 19 April 1943 the Germans marched in to empty the ghetto, and the fighters attacked them with pistols and petrol bombs. The German army, with tanks and thousands of soldiers, could not beat them in the streets, so it burned the whole ghetto down, building by building. The fighters held out for almost four weeks. Anielewicz died on 8 May in his bunker at 18 Miła Street. On 16 May the Germans blew up the Great Synagogue and said the ghetto was gone.

They did not win, and they knew they would not. But they showed the world that people could resist, and others were inspired to rise up in the death camps of Treblinka and Sobibór.

So what was happening in the rest of the war at the same time?

## Subtopic: The Tide Turns

Think of the war like the tide at the beach. In 1942 the German tide had reached almost everywhere in Europe. Then it began to go out.

The first big turn was at a city called Stalingrad in the Soviet Union. The German army got trapped there in a freezing winter, and in February 1943 about 91 000 German soldiers surrendered. After that, the Soviet army kept pushing west.

At the same time, in the deserts of North Africa, soldiers from Britain and the Commonwealth, including many South Africans, beat the German general Rommel at El Alamein in Egypt in late 1942. South Africans then fought all the way up through Italy. About 334 000 South Africans volunteered. Black South Africans served too, but the government would not let them carry guns. One of them, Job Maseko, became a prisoner and still managed to blow up a German ship. He was given a medal for bravery.

Then on 6 June 1944, called D-Day, a huge Allied army crossed the sea from Britain and landed on the beaches of Normandy in France. About 156 000 soldiers landed on that first day. By August, Paris was free. Now Germany was being squeezed from both sides, like a tube of toothpaste.

So how did it finally end?

## Subtopic: The Last Days and the Cost

As Allied soldiers marched into Germany and Poland, they found the camps. On 27 January 1945 Soviet soldiers reached Auschwitz. In April British soldiers entered Bergen-Belsen and filmed what they saw so that nobody could ever deny it.

The Soviet army fought its way into Berlin in April 1945. Hitler hid in an underground bunker. On 30 April 1945 he killed himself. A week later Germany surrendered completely, and on 8 May 1945 people across Europe and beyond danced in the streets. That day is called VE Day, Victory in Europe Day. The war against Japan carried on until August.

But the cost was enormous. Somewhere between 70 and 85 million people died in the whole war, most of them ordinary people, not soldiers. The Soviet Union alone lost about 27 million. About six million Jews were murdered. About 11 000 South Africans died. Whole cities lay in rubble. Afterwards the Nazi leaders were put on trial at Nuremberg, and Germany was split into four zones run by the winners, which led straight into the Cold War you will study next term.

Here is the line to remember: the ghetto fighters could not win, but they chose dignity; the Allies won together, on many fronts; and peace came at a price the world must never forget.
