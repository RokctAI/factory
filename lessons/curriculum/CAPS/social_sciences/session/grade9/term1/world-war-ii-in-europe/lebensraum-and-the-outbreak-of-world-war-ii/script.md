# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains Nazi Germany's expansionist foreign policy from 1933 to 1939 and the outbreak of World War II. It covers Hitler's foreign policy aims, including lebensraum; the first breaches of the Treaty of Versailles from 1933 to 1936; the annexation of Austria and the Munich crisis of 1938 and the policy of appeasement; the occupation of Czechoslovakia, the Nazi-Soviet Pact and the invasion of Poland in 1939; and the two alliances of the war, the Axis and the Allies, with South Africa's decision to join. The anchor example is the Munich Agreement of 30 September 1938, which gave Germany the Sudetenland without Czechoslovakia being present and which Neville Chamberlain claimed had secured peace for our time. The governing idea is that each successful step encouraged the next, and that the failure to resist early made a larger war more likely.

## Subtopic: Hitler's Aims and the First Steps, 1933 to 1936

Hitler's foreign policy had three linked aims, set out in Mein Kampf. First, to destroy the Treaty of Versailles: to rearm, to recover lost territory and to end the restrictions on Germany. Second, to unite all German-speaking people in one Greater Germany, including those in Austria, Czechoslovakia and Poland. Third, to win lebensraum, living space, in eastern Europe, by conquering land from Poland and the Soviet Union whose Slavic peoples Nazi ideology regarded as inferior, and settling it with Germans. The third aim made a major war in the east almost certain.

Hitler moved cautiously at first, because Germany's army was still small. In October 1933 Germany left both the Disarmament Conference and the League of Nations. In January 1935 the people of the Saar, governed by the League since 1920, voted by about 90 percent to rejoin Germany, as the treaty allowed. In March 1935 Hitler openly broke the treaty by announcing conscription and an army of 550 000 men and revealing the existence of the Luftwaffe, the German air force. Britain, rather than protesting, signed the Anglo-German Naval Agreement in June 1935, allowing Germany a navy of 35 percent of the size of Britain's, which showed that the victors themselves no longer defended the treaty.

On 7 March 1936 German troops marched into the Rhineland, the zone along the French border that Versailles had demilitarised. It was a gamble: Hitler later admitted that if the French had resisted, his troops would have had to withdraw. France, in the middle of an election and unwilling to act without Britain, did nothing; many in Britain felt that Germany was only moving into its own back garden. The success strengthened Hitler at home and convinced him that the democracies would not fight.

In 1936 Germany drew closer to two other expansionist powers. Mussolini's Italy, criticised by the League for invading Ethiopia in 1935, formed the Rome-Berlin Axis with Germany in October 1936, and in November Germany and Japan signed the Anti-Comintern Pact against communism, which Italy joined in 1937. In the Spanish Civil War, from 1936 to 1939, German and Italian forces helped the nationalist general Franco; German aircraft bombed the town of Guernica in 1937, a rehearsal for the bombing of cities in the coming war.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three aims, leaving the League, conscription and the Luftwaffe, the Rhineland, and the origins of the Axis.

## Subtopic: Appeasement: Austria, the Sudetenland and Munich

In March 1938 Hitler achieved the Anschluss, the union of Germany and Austria, forbidden by Versailles. After pressure on the Austrian government and the threat of invasion, German troops crossed the border on 12 March and were welcomed by many Austrians; a plebiscite held under Nazi control in April recorded 99 percent approval. Britain and France protested but did nothing.

Hitler next turned to Czechoslovakia, a democracy with a strong army, fortifications and alliances with France and the Soviet Union. About three million German speakers lived in the Sudetenland, its border regions. Encouraged by Hitler, the Sudeten German party demanded autonomy, and in September 1938 Hitler threatened war unless the Sudetenland was handed over. British Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain flew to Germany three times to negotiate. At Munich on 29 to 30 September 1938, Chamberlain, French Prime Minister Édouard Daladier, Mussolini and Hitler agreed that Germany would occupy the Sudetenland. Czechoslovakia was not invited, and the Soviet Union was ignored. Abandoned by its allies, Czechoslovakia gave way, losing its border defences. Chamberlain returned to London waving a paper signed by Hitler and declared that it meant peace for our time.

This policy of making concessions to avoid war is called appeasement. Its supporters had reasons. The memory of World War I made another war seem unthinkable to most of the British and French public. Many believed that Versailles had been unfair to Germany and that its grievances were partly justified. Britain and France were not ready for war: British rearmament had only begun in earnest in 1936. Some saw Nazi Germany as a barrier against Soviet communism. The Depression had left little money for armies. And the Dominions, including South Africa under Prime Minister J. B. M. Hertzog, made it clear that they would not support a war over Czechoslovakia.

Critics, led by Winston Churchill, argued that appeasement only fed Hitler's appetite, that each concession made Germany stronger and the democracies weaker, and that the Czech army and fortresses given away at Munich were a serious military loss. Historians continue to debate whether appeasement was cowardly, or a realistic way to buy time to rearm.

This section's questions are ready: the Anschluss, the Sudetenland, the Munich Agreement and who was absent, and the arguments for and against appeasement.

## Subtopic: From Prague to Poland, 1939

Munich did not satisfy Hitler. On 15 March 1939 German troops occupied the rest of the Czech lands, Bohemia and Moravia, and entered Prague; Slovakia became a German puppet state. This was the turning point for appeasement: for the first time Hitler had seized territory with no German-speaking majority, which proved that his aims went beyond uniting Germans. On 31 March Britain and France gave a guarantee to Poland, promising to defend it if attacked. In April Italy invaded Albania, and in May Germany and Italy signed a military alliance, the Pact of Steel.

Hitler now demanded the return of Danzig and a route across the Polish Corridor. Britain and France held talks with the Soviet Union about an alliance, but they were slow and distrustful, and Poland refused to allow Soviet troops onto its soil. Stalin, who had seen the democracies give way at Munich without consulting him, doubted they would fight. On 23 August 1939 the world was stunned by the Nazi-Soviet Pact, signed by the foreign ministers Joachim von Ribbentrop and Vyacheslav Molotov. The two enemies agreed not to attack each other, and a secret protocol divided Poland and eastern Europe between them. The pact meant Germany could invade Poland without fearing a war on two fronts, and gave Stalin time and territory.

On 1 September 1939 German forces invaded Poland using blitzkrieg, lightning war: fast tank columns supported by dive-bombers and motorised infantry, punching through defences and encircling the enemy. On 3 September Britain and France, honouring their guarantee, declared war on Germany. On 17 September the Soviet Union invaded eastern Poland, as the secret protocol had arranged. Poland fell within about five weeks and was divided. World War II in Europe had begun.

Take this section's questions now: the occupation of Prague, the guarantee to Poland, the Nazi-Soviet Pact and its secret protocol, blitzkrieg, and 1 and 3 September 1939.

## Subtopic: Axis versus Allies, South Africa's Decision, and the Error Museum

The war was fought between two alliances. The Axis powers were Germany, Italy, which entered the war in June 1940, and Japan, which joined them in the Tripartite Pact of September 1940, together with smaller allies such as Hungary, Romania and Bulgaria. The Allies were at first Britain and France, with the members of the British Commonwealth, including Canada, Australia, New Zealand, India and South Africa. The Soviet Union joined the Allies when Germany invaded it in June 1941, and the United States when Japan attacked Pearl Harbor in December 1941. China had been fighting Japan since 1937. By 1942 the war had become truly global.

South Africa's entry was decided by one vote in parliament. When Britain declared war, Prime Minister Hertzog proposed that South Africa remain neutral; his deputy Jan Smuts proposed that it join Britain. On 4 September 1939 the House of Assembly voted for Smuts's motion by 80 votes to 67. Hertzog resigned, Smuts became prime minister, and South Africa declared war on Germany on 6 September. Many Afrikaner nationalists opposed the war, and an organisation called the Ossewabrandwag, some of whose members admired Nazi Germany, carried out sabotage; its members, including the future prime minister B. J. Vorster, were interned. All South African troops were volunteers, and about 334 000 served; Black South Africans again were officially barred from carrying arms in combat, though they served as drivers, stretcher-bearers and guards. South African forces fought in East Africa, North Africa and Italy.

By June 1940 Germany had conquered Denmark, Norway, the Netherlands, Belgium and France. Britain, under its new prime minister Winston Churchill, fought on alone, and in the Battle of Britain from July to October 1940 the Royal Air Force prevented a German invasion.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: Britain and France tried to stop the Rhineland occupation; they did nothing. Two: Czechoslovakia agreed at Munich; it was not even invited. Three: the Soviet Union was always an Ally; until June 1941 it had a pact with Germany and invaded Poland too. Four: the war began when Germany attacked Britain; it began with the invasion of Poland on 1 September 1939. Five: all South Africans supported joining the war; parliament was divided 80 to 67, and the prime minister resigned.

The questions for this section are ready: the Axis and the Allies, South Africa's vote, the fall of France, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same road to war again, through a bully testing the fence, feeding a crocodile, and two teams lining up — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Testing the Fence

Imagine a bully in the school yard who has been told he must stay behind a fence. He pushes on the fence a little, and watches the teachers. Nobody comes. He pushes a bit harder. Still nothing. Next he climbs over one rail. The teachers frown, but do nothing. Every time nobody stops him, he gets bolder.

That is what Hitler did with the Treaty of Versailles. His plan, written in Mein Kampf, was to tear up the treaty, bring all German speakers into one Germany, and grab living space, lebensraum, in the east, in Poland and the Soviet Union. That last part meant war.

First push, 1933: Germany walks out of the League of Nations. Second push, 1935: Hitler announces a big new army and an air force, both banned by the treaty. Britain does not protest; it even signs a deal allowing Germany a bigger navy. Third push, March 1936: German soldiers march into the Rhineland, the strip next to France where they were not allowed to be. Hitler later admitted that if France had fought back, his soldiers would have had to retreat. France did nothing.

Meanwhile Hitler made friends with other bullies. Mussolini's Italy, which had just invaded Ethiopia, joined Germany in the Rome-Berlin Axis in 1936, and Japan signed a pact against communism with them. The Axis was forming.

Keep the bully at the fence in mind and try the questions for this part: the three aims, the new army, and the Rhineland nobody defended.

## Subtopic: Feeding the Crocodile

In 1938 Hitler grabbed Austria. German soldiers crossed the border in March and Austria simply became part of Germany. Nobody stopped him.

Then he demanded the Sudetenland, the border area of Czechoslovakia where about three million German speakers lived. He threatened war. The British prime minister, Neville Chamberlain, flew to Germany to talk. At Munich in September 1938, the leaders of Britain, France, Italy and Germany agreed: Hitler could have the Sudetenland. Czechoslovakia, whose land it was, was not even invited to the meeting. Chamberlain came home waving a piece of paper and said it meant peace for our time.

Giving a bully what he wants so that he leaves you alone is called appeasement. Why did Britain and France do it? People remembered the horror of World War I and dreaded another. Many felt the Treaty of Versailles had been too harsh. Their armies were not ready. Some thought Hitler would stop communism. And countries like South Africa said they would not fight over Czechoslovakia.

But Winston Churchill warned that feeding Hitler would only make him hungrier. He described an appeaser as someone who feeds a crocodile, hoping it will eat him last. Six months after Munich, in March 1939, Hitler swallowed the rest of Czechoslovakia, a country full of people who were not German at all. Now everyone could see it: this was never about uniting Germans. Britain and France promised to defend Poland.

Hold the crocodile in mind and try this part's questions: Austria, Munich without the Czechs, and why appeasement failed.

## Subtopic: Two Teams Line Up

In August 1939 came a shock. Hitler and Stalin, sworn enemies, signed a deal not to attack each other, the Nazi-Soviet Pact. In secret, they agreed to split Poland between them. Now Hitler could attack Poland without worrying about a war on two sides.

On 1 September 1939 Germany invaded Poland with blitzkrieg, lightning war: tanks racing forward, dive-bombers screaming overhead. On 3 September Britain and France declared war on Germany. Two weeks later the Soviet Union invaded Poland from the east. World War II had begun.

Two teams lined up. The Axis: Germany, Italy and Japan. The Allies: Britain, France and the Commonwealth, including South Africa, joined later by the Soviet Union when Hitler attacked it in 1941, and the United States in December 1941.

In South Africa, parliament argued. Prime Minister Hertzog wanted to stay out. Jan Smuts wanted to join Britain. On 4 September 1939 the vote was 80 to 67 for war. Hertzog resigned, Smuts took over, and about 334 000 South African volunteers served, fighting in East Africa, North Africa and Italy.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Hitler wanted to destroy Versailles, unite all Germans and take living space in the east. He tested the fence: the army, the Rhineland, Austria. At Munich, Britain and France fed the crocodile the Sudetenland. In March 1939 he took the rest of Czechoslovakia. The Nazi-Soviet Pact cleared the way, and on 1 September 1939 Germany invaded Poland. South Africa joined by 80 votes to 67. In Grade 12 History, the Cold War begins where this war ends.

Hold the two teams in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: the Nazi-Soviet Pact, 1 September 1939, the Axis and the Allies, and South Africa's vote.
