### subtopic_1 — From Persecution to Genocide: Ghettos and Mass Shootings
**Assistant:** How could ordinary people take part in mass murder?

**Tutor:** Historians have studied this question closely, and their answer is that most perpetrators were not monsters or madmen but ordinary people shaped by ideology, pressure, routine and opportunity. The historian Christopher Browning studied a unit of German reserve policemen, middle-aged men from Hamburg, most of them not Nazi Party members, who in 1942 were ordered to shoot Jewish civilians in Poland. Their commander offered to excuse anyone who did not feel up to it, and only about a dozen of roughly 500 stepped out at first. Most took part, and over the following months the unit shot or deported tens of thousands of people. Browning found that conformity, the wish not to appear weak in front of comrades or leave the dirty work to others, mattered more than fear of punishment, since those who refused were not seriously punished. Years of propaganda had taught them to see Jews as enemies and outsiders, and the war made violence seem normal. Other perpetrators were committed Nazis and antisemites who believed in what they did, and others were careerists who wanted promotion. Many more people contributed at a distance: clerks, railway officials and engineers who never saw a victim but made the killing possible by doing their jobs. The lesson historians draw is uncomfortable: under certain conditions, many people can be drawn into atrocity, which is why individual conscience, the rule of law and the protection of human rights matter so much.

### subtopic_2 — The Final Solution and the Wannsee Conference
**Assistant:** Why was the Wannsee Conference so important if the killing had already started?

**Tutor:** Because it turned a set of killing operations into a coordinated, continent-wide programme involving the whole German state, and because its minutes provide written evidence of that plan. By January 1942 the Einsatzgruppen had already shot hundreds of thousands of Jews in the Soviet Union, the first gassings had begun at Chełmno in December 1941, and German Jews were being deported eastward. But the SS needed the cooperation of other parts of government: the foreign ministry to negotiate deportations from allied and occupied countries, the interior ministry for laws and definitions, the transport authorities for trains, the occupation administrations in Poland and the east, and the economic offices that controlled forced labour. Heydrich called the meeting to assert the SS's authority over the whole operation and to make sure every department understood its role. The officials discussed how to treat people of mixed descent and Jews married to non-Jews, and how Jews fit for work would be used for labour until they died, while the rest would be dealt with. No one present objected to the aim. The minutes, written by Eichmann in carefully disguised language, list about eleven million Jews across Europe as the target. The surviving copy was found by American prosecutors in 1947 and used in post-war trials. For a source-based question, it is a powerful example of how a document's language can conceal its meaning, and how historians interpret it alongside other evidence to understand what it really describes.

### subtopic_3 — The Extermination Camps
**Assistant:** Why were the extermination camps built in Poland and not in Germany?

**Tutor:** Mainly because most of Europe's Jews lived in eastern Europe, because occupied Poland was outside the Reich's normal legal and public life, and because the Nazis wanted the killing hidden from the German public and the world. Before the war about three million Jews lived in Poland, the largest Jewish community in Europe, and millions more lived in the western Soviet Union, the Baltic states, Hungary and Romania. Building the camps near these populations and near the main railway lines reduced transport problems. Occupied Poland was ruled directly by the SS and the German occupation government with no pretence of law; Poles themselves were treated as an inferior people, and the population had no means to protest. Remote sites such as Bełżec, Sobibór and Treblinka, in forests near small railway stations, made secrecy easier, and the SS tried to keep the killing hidden even from many Germans: euphemisms were used in documents, guards were sworn to secrecy, and deportees were told they were being resettled for labour. Secrecy was never complete: rumours spread, soldiers wrote home about shootings, the Polish underground sent reports to London, and in December 1942 the Allied governments publicly condemned the extermination of the Jews. Auschwitz was built in an area of Polish territory annexed to Germany, near a railway junction, and grew into the largest camp. The location of the camps sometimes leads to the false idea that Poland was responsible; the camps were built and run by Nazi Germany in occupied territory.

### subtopic_4 — Responsibility, Rescue, Remembrance and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do historians know six million Jewish people were murdered?

**Tutor:** By combining several independent kinds of evidence, which all point to the same order of magnitude: about six million, with careful estimates ranging between about 5,6 and 6 million. First, population records: historians compare the Jewish population of each country before the war, from censuses and community registers, with the number who survived, emigrated or were found after it. Poland's Jewish community of about three million was almost entirely destroyed; about 270 000 survived in Germany out of the Jews who lived in the Reich before persecution, partly through emigration. Second, the perpetrators' own records: SS and police reports counted victims, for example the Einsatzgruppen reports that listed the numbers shot in each place, deportation lists and railway timetables recorded the trains and the people on them, and SS statisticians produced reports for Himmler on the progress of the Final Solution. Third, the camps: transport records, camp registers, the physical remains of the sites and the testimony of guards and survivors allow estimates for each camp. Fourth, testimony: tens of thousands of survivors gave accounts, and perpetrators confessed at trials such as Nuremberg, where the Auschwitz commandant Rudolf Höss described the killing process. Because the Nazis destroyed some records, exact numbers for each place are still refined by research, which is normal in history. But the overall figure is not in serious doubt among historians, and people who deny the Holocaust do so in defiance of overwhelming evidence, usually out of antisemitism.
