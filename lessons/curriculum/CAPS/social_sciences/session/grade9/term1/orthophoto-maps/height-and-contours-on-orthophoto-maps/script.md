# Part 1 — Expert

This session teaches how height is shown on 1:10 000 orthophoto maps and how contour lines are used together with the photographic image to identify features. It covers the height information printed on an orthophoto map and the 5 metre contour interval; reading heights and slopes from orthophoto contours; recognising landforms from contour patterns over the image; identifying features by combining contours with tone, texture and shape; and calculating gradient at 1:10 000 and comparing with the 1:50 000 map. The anchor example is a small valley on an orthophoto in which a river, a farm dam, a road cutting and an eroded slope can all be confirmed by the contour pattern. The governing idea is that the photograph shows what is there and the contours show the shape of the ground it stands on, and the two together give an answer neither gives alone.

## Subtopic: Height Information on the Orthophoto Map

A vertical photograph shows the land in plan, so it cannot show height directly: a hilltop and a valley floor are flattened onto the same sheet of paper. The orthophoto map overcomes this by printing height information over the image. Contour lines are printed at a vertical interval of 5 metres, four times as many contours as on the 1:50 000 topographic map with its 20 metre interval. Every contour that is a multiple of 20 metres matches a contour on the topographic sheet, and between each pair of those there are three extra contours on the orthophoto.

Contour heights are printed at intervals along the lines, written so that they read facing uphill, and the heavier or labelled contours help you keep count where many lines are close together. Spot heights, with a dot and a figure, mark individual points such as road junctions and hilltops; trigonometrical stations mark surveyed beacons with a triangle and a height. All heights are in metres above mean sea level, the same datum as the topographic map.

The finer contour interval makes the orthophoto more sensitive to small features. On the topographic map, a low rise of 15 metres might not appear at all, because it does not cross a 20 metre contour, or might appear as a single small loop. On the orthophoto the same rise crosses three contours and shows its full shape. Small gullies, terraces, embankments and the shape of a dam basin become visible in the contours.

Because the image is a photograph, contours are printed in a colour or tone that stands out from it, and in black and white extracts they appear as thin lines that must be carefully distinguished from paths, fences and field boundaries. The key test is that contours never stop, never cross each other and run smoothly across the landscape, while fences and paths change direction sharply and connect to buildings and gates.

The questions for this section are with you now: the 5 metre interval, how orthophoto contours relate to topographic contours, spot heights and trig stations, and telling contours from fences.

## Subtopic: Reading Height and Slope on the Orthophoto

Heights are read on the orthophoto exactly as on the topographic map, but with finer steps. A point on the 1 535 metre contour is at 1 535 metres. A point between the 1 535 and 1 540 metre contours is between those heights, about 1 537 or 1 538 if it lies halfway. To find the height of an unlabelled contour, count from the nearest labelled one in steps of 5 metres, checking which way the land rises by the labels, by spot heights and by the river, which always flows downhill.

Slope is judged by contour spacing. Because the scale is 1:10 000, the same steepness of ground produces contours that are more widely spaced on the paper than on the topographic map, five times further apart for the same ground distance, but with four times as many lines. Close contours still mean steep ground, wide spacing gentle ground, and none flat ground. On the orthophoto the image confirms the reading: steep slopes are often wooded or bare rock with no fields and no buildings, while gentle slopes carry fields, houses and roads.

Slope shape is read in the same way as on the topographic map: contours close together at the bottom and wide at the top show a convex slope; wide at the bottom and close at the top, a concave slope; evenly spaced, a uniform slope.

Gradient is calculated with the same formula, vertical interval over horizontal equivalent, but with the 1:10 000 conversion: 1 centimetre is 100 metres. In the anchor valley, a road runs from a spot height of 1 520 metres at the river bridge to a point at 1 555 metres on the crest of the spur, 2,5 centimetres away on the map. The vertical interval is 35 metres. The horizontal equivalent is 2,5 times 10 000, which is 25 000 centimetres or 250 metres. The gradient is 35 over 250, which is 1:7,1. A farm track crossing gentle ground rises 15 metres over 3 centimetres: 300 metres, a gradient of 1:20.

This section's questions are ready: reading heights in 5 metre steps, contour spacing at 1:10 000, and gradient on the orthophoto.

## Subtopic: Landforms from Contours over the Image

Contour patterns on the orthophoto are the same as on the topographic map, but drawn over real ground, so each pattern can be checked against the image. A valley appears as contours forming V's pointing upstream, with the river, often marked by a dark, rough line of riverine trees and bush, running through the points of the V's. A spur appears as V's pointing downhill, often with a road or path following its gentle crest, and fields on its lower end. A hill shows closed loops, often crowned with a trig station and with natural vegetation or rock on its steep upper slopes. A ridge shows elongated closed loops, sometimes with a line of pylons or a fence along its crest.

The image adds evidence. Rivers are lined by vegetation that shows as a dark, rough band; fields stop where contours crowd together, because the slope becomes too steep to plough; dams lie where the contours of a valley narrow, since a short wall can then hold back a large basin of water. Houses on a farm sit on the gentle, well-drained slope just above the floodplain.

Contours also reveal features that are hard to see on a photograph alone. A cutting is a passage dug through high ground for a road or railway: on the orthophoto the contours on either side of the road bend sharply and crowd together, because the ground has been cut away. An embankment is a raised bank built across low ground: the contours are pushed outward along the road. A terrace is a level step cut into a slope for farming: the contours run in parallel, closely spaced steps with flat strips between. A donga is an erosion gully: the contours form a sharp, narrow V in a place where there is no permanent river, and the image shows a pale, raw scar with no vegetation.

Take this section's questions now: valleys, spurs, hills and ridges over the image, dam sites, cuttings and embankments, and dongas.

## Subtopic: Combining Photograph and Contours, and the Error Museum

The skill that orthophoto questions test most is identifying a feature from a combination of image and contours. Return to the anchor valley. A smooth, dark, roughly triangular area lies across the valley with a straight edge at its narrow, downstream end. The contours show the valley narrowing at the straight edge and widening upstream, and the 1 525 metre contour runs around the edge of the dark area. Combined evidence: a farm dam, with its wall at the narrow point and its water surface level along a contour, because still water lies flat, at one height.

Further along, a pale straight line crosses a spur, and the contours on either side of it bend inward and crowd together: a road in a cutting. On the opposite slope, closely spaced contours, pale bare ground and several narrow branching scars mark a steep, overgrazed slope with dongas, evidence of soil erosion, a Term 3 topic.

The orthophoto must also be linked to the topographic map. Find the outlined block on the 1:50 000 sheet that corresponds to the orthophoto. Contours of 1 520 and 1 540 metres appear on both maps; the orthophoto adds 1 525, 1 530 and 1 535 between them. Features shown by symbol on the topographic map, the dam, the road, the trig station, appear as images on the orthophoto. A common question asks which map is better for a task: the orthophoto for detail and small features, the topographic map for overall relief and long distances.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: orthophoto contours are 20 metres apart; they are 5 metres apart. Two: the edge of a dam's water can cross contour lines; still water lies at one height, so its edge follows a contour. Three: a fence and a contour look alike so either will do; contours run smoothly, never cross and never stop at a gate. Four: on the orthophoto 1 centimetre is 500 metres; it is 100 metres. Five: a V in the contours always means a permanent river; it may be a donga, a dry gully, so check the image.

The questions for this section are ready: identifying a dam and a cutting, linking the orthophoto to the topographic map, choosing the better map, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same height clues again, through a photo of a flat-looking hill, a dam filling up like a bath, and a road cut through a hill like a slice through cake — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Photo That Flattens the Hill

Take a photo of a hill from an aeroplane, straight down. Look at it. The hill looks flat. You can see the trees and the path on it, but not that it is a hill. A straight-down photo squashes all the ups and downs into a flat picture. So how does the orthophoto tell you the height?

It cheats, in a good way. Over the photo, it draws contour lines: lines joining places at the same height, just like on the topographic map. But here they are drawn every 5 metres of height instead of every 20. So where the topographic map shows one line, the orthophoto shows four. That means even a small bump in a field, or a little gully, shows up.

The heights are written on some of the lines, and there are spot heights, a dot and a number, and trig stations, a triangle on a hilltop, just like on the topographic map. All heights are measured from the sea.

One warning. On a black and white photo, a contour line can look like a fence or a footpath. Here is how you tell: a contour runs smoothly across the land, never stops, never crosses another one, and never turns sharply at a gate. A fence goes in straight lines and corners, and joins up with buildings.

To read a height, find the nearest labelled line and count in fives. If the line marked 1 520 is below you and you are two lines up, you are at 1 530.

Keep the flat-looking hill in mind and try the questions for this part: 5 metre steps, counting in fives, and fence or contour.

## Subtopic: The Dam That Fills Like a Bath

Think about water in a bath. However the bath is shaped, the top of the water is always perfectly flat and level. A farm dam is the same: the water's edge is all at one height. So on the orthophoto, the edge of a dam follows a contour line. That is a lovely clue: if you see a dark, smooth patch with its edge running along a contour, and a straight wall at the narrow end of a valley, you have found a dam.

Why at the narrow end? Because the farmer wants to build the shortest wall possible to hold back the most water. Where the valley's contours squeeze together, the valley is narrow; upstream, where they spread out, the valley is wide and holds a big pool.

The valley itself shows as V's in the contours, pointing upstream, with the river running through their points. On the photo the river is usually a dark, rough line, because trees and bushes grow thick along the water. The spur next to it shows V's pointing downhill, often with a path along its top. Fields stop where the contour lines bunch up, because a tractor cannot plough a slope that steep.

And measuring steepness works the same way, just with the orthophoto scale: 1 centimetre is 100 metres. A road climbs from 1 520 metres at the bridge to 1 555 metres on the spur, 2,5 centimetres away. Up 35, along 250, gradient 35 over 250, about 1:7.

Hold the bath in mind and try this part's questions: the dam edge on a contour, the narrow end of the valley, and gradient with 100 metres a centimetre.

## Subtopic: The Road Cut Through the Hill

Imagine a road that has to cross a small hill. Instead of going up and over, the builders slice straight through it, like a knife through a cake, leaving steep walls on either side. That is a cutting. On the orthophoto, the contour lines on either side of the road suddenly bunch up and bend inward, because the ground has been dug away. Where the road crosses a dip, the builders pile up earth to make a raised bank: an embankment, and the contours push outward.

Now look at a bare, pale slope with closely packed contours and narrow scars branching across it, each with its own little sharp V in the contours but no river. Those scars are dongas, gullies torn out by rainwater where the grass has gone. You will study them properly in Term 3.

Last skill: linking the two maps. On the topographic map, a little box shows where the orthophoto fits. Lines at 1 520 and 1 540 are on both maps; the orthophoto adds 1 525, 1 530 and 1 535 in between. Use the orthophoto for close-up detail, the topographic map for the big picture.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A straight-down photo hides height, so the orthophoto prints contours every 5 metres over it, with spot heights and trig stations. Close lines steep, far lines gentle, V's for valleys and spurs. Dam edges follow a contour. Cuttings make contours bunch around a road. Count in fives, and 1 centimetre is 100 metres. In Grade 10 Geography you will use exactly these skills on full map sets.

Hold the cutting through the hill in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: cuttings and embankments, dongas, and which map to use.
