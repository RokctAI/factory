# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises vertical aerial photographs from Grade 8 and develops the 1:10 000 orthophoto map made from them. It covers how aerial photographs are taken and the difference between vertical and oblique photographs; the distortions in a vertical photograph and how orthorectification removes them; the structure of an orthophoto map; and the interpretation of the photographic image using tone, texture, shape, size, pattern, shadow and association. The anchor example is an orthophoto of the edge of a country town, where a business area, formal and informal housing, an industrial strip along a railway and irrigated farmland with centre-pivot circles can all be identified from their appearance. The governing idea is that an orthophoto is read like a photograph but measured like a map.

## Subtopic: Aerial Photographs: Vertical and Oblique

An aerial photograph is a photograph of the earth's surface taken from the air, from an aircraft, a helicopter or, increasingly, a drone. For mapping, photographs are taken from an aircraft flying straight lines at a constant height, with a camera fixed in the floor pointing straight down and triggered at regular intervals. Each photograph overlaps the next along the flight line by about 60 percent, and neighbouring flight lines overlap sideways by about 20 to 30 percent, so that no ground is missed and every point appears on at least two photographs. Viewed in pairs through a stereoscope, overlapping photographs give a three-dimensional impression of the land, which was how heights and contours were first mapped from the air.

Photographs are classified by the angle of the camera. A vertical aerial photograph is taken with the camera pointing straight down, so it shows the ground in plan view, as a map does: you see roofs, not walls, and the tops of trees. It covers a roughly square area, its scale is nearly constant over flat land, and it is the type used for mapping. An oblique aerial photograph is taken with the camera tilted. A low oblique photograph does not show the horizon; a high oblique photograph does. Obliques show the sides of buildings and hills, which makes them easy to recognise and attractive in a brochure, but their scale decreases steadily from the foreground to the background, so distances cannot be measured on them.

Vertical photographs have practical strengths. They show everything visible from above, including features that are too small or too recent to appear on a map; they are cheaper to update than redrawing a map; and they show the true appearance of land use. Their weaknesses are that they show no names, no heights and no invisible features such as boundaries, and that a raw photograph is not exactly to scale.

In South Africa, the national mapping office flies aerial photography of the whole country in a regular cycle, and those photographs are the source both for updating the 1:50 000 topographic maps and for producing the 1:10 000 orthophoto series.

The questions for this section are with you now: how mapping photographs are taken, the overlap, vertical against low and high oblique, and the strengths and weaknesses of the vertical photograph.

## Subtopic: From Photograph to Orthophoto

A vertical aerial photograph is not yet a map, because its scale varies across the image. Three causes produce this. First, relief displacement: high ground is closer to the camera than low ground, so it appears at a larger scale, and tall objects such as towers and trees near the edges of the photograph appear to lean outward from the centre. Second, tilt: even a fixed camera tilts slightly as the aircraft rocks, so one side of the photograph is closer to the ground than the other. Third, lens distortion: the camera lens bends light slightly more near its edges. On flat land with a perfectly level aircraft the errors are small; in hilly terrain they can displace features by many metres.

Orthorectification is the process that removes these errors. Using a digital model of the height of the ground, built from earlier surveys or from overlapping photographs, and precise ground control points whose co-ordinates are known, a computer shifts every point on the image to the position it would occupy if viewed from directly above everywhere at once. The corrected images are joined into a seamless mosaic and cut into map sheets. The result, the orthophoto, has a uniform scale: every centimetre on it represents the same ground distance, so it can be measured exactly like a map.

The orthophoto map is the orthophoto with map information printed over it. In the South African series it is drawn at 1:10 000, so 1 centimetre represents 100 metres. Contour lines are printed at a 5 metre interval, together with spot heights, trigonometrical stations, place names, road and river names, a frame of latitude and longitude, and a margin with the scale, line scale and a reference to the topographic sheet that contains it. Twenty-five orthophoto sheets cover the area of one 1:50 000 topographic sheet.

The image itself on many school extracts is in black and white, so all interpretation depends on shades of grey, while digital versions are in colour.

This section's questions are ready: relief displacement and tilt, orthorectification, and the information printed on an orthophoto map.

## Subtopic: Reading the Image: Tone, Texture, Shape and More

Because the orthophoto has no symbols for most features, they are identified by seven clues. Tone is the shade of grey or colour. Water usually appears dark because it absorbs light, although a sunlit or silty surface may be pale; tarred roads are dark grey; dry grass, bare soil, sand and gravel roads are light; a freshly ploughed field is darker than a harvested one. Texture is the smoothness or roughness of an area: a dam or a lawn is smooth, a plantation is evenly rough, natural bush is unevenly rough and speckled.

Shape is often decisive, because people build in straight lines and regular geometry. Rectangular fields, straight roads, the oval of an athletics track, the cross of an airport runway and the perfect circles of centre-pivot irrigation, in which a long sprinkler arm rotates around a central point, are all human. Natural features curve irregularly: rivers meander and woodlands have ragged edges. Size separates similar shapes: using the scale, a 1 centimetre rectangle is a 100 metre building, a factory or a warehouse, while a 1 millimetre one is a house.

Pattern is the arrangement of repeated features. Houses in neat rows on a regular street grid indicate a planned formal residential area; small, tightly packed roofs with no regular layout and narrow irregular paths indicate an informal settlement; parallel rows of dots across a field indicate an orchard or vineyard. Shadow shows height and shape: tall objects such as pylons, water towers and trees cast long shadows that reveal their outline. In the southern hemisphere the sun at midday is in the northern part of the sky, so shadows fall towards the south, which gives an approximate check on the orientation of the image. Association is identifying a feature from what is around it: long sheds beside a railway siding suggest a factory or warehouse; a large building beside sports fields suggests a school.

Using several clues together gives a confident answer: a large, pale, rectangular, flat-roofed building beside a railway siding with trucks parked outside is a warehouse or factory.

Take this section's questions now: the seven clues, centre-pivot circles, formal and informal housing, and southern hemisphere shadows.

## Subtopic: Land Use on the Orthophoto and the Error Museum

Return to the anchor orthophoto of the edge of a country town. In its north-east corner, closely packed large buildings with almost no open space, a dense street grid and parking areas mark the central business district. To the south, regular rows of small, evenly spaced roofs on a curved street layout with gardens mark a formal residential suburb. On the western edge, many very small roofs, tightly packed with irregular paths and almost no vegetation, mark an informal settlement. Along the railway in the centre, long rectangular sheds with large roofs, sidings and open yards mark an industrial strip. Beyond the town, on the floodplain of the river, perfect circles of different tones, some dark green with growing crops and some pale with bare soil, mark centre-pivot irrigation, and between them rectangular fields and a farm dam.

Questions on land use ask you to identify a zone and give evidence from the image. A full answer names the land use, states its location on the orthophoto, and gives two clues: the industrial area along the railway, identified by its large rectangular buildings and the railway sidings. A second skill is to compare the orthophoto with the topographic map: find the same area in the block outlined on the topographic map, check the symbols there, and note any differences, such as a new suburb that appears on the orthophoto but not on an older map.

Orthophotos are also used to measure. Because the scale is uniform, you can measure the diameter of a centre-pivot circle, say 4 centimetres, which is 400 metres, and calculate its area, or measure the length of a new road to the nearest 10 metres.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: an oblique photograph is good for measuring; its scale changes across the image, so only vertical photographs and orthophotos are measured. Two: an orthophoto is the same as a raw aerial photograph; it has been corrected to a uniform scale. Three: water is always dark; silty or sunlit water can appear pale, so use shape and association too. Four: circles on farmland are rings of trees; they are centre-pivot irrigation fields. Five: shadows in South Africa fall to the north at midday; the sun is in the northern sky, so they fall towards the south.

The questions for this section are ready: identifying land-use zones with evidence, comparing the orthophoto with the topographic map, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same photographs again, through a drone flying over your neighbourhood, a wobbly photo straightened out, and a game of spot the difference — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Drone Over Your Neighbourhood

Imagine a drone flying over your street, its camera pointing straight down. Click. In the photo you see the tops of the roofs, the tops of the trees, the cars in the road, the soccer field, the river. You see everything the way a bird sees it, from directly above. That is a vertical aerial photograph. To photograph a whole town, an aeroplane flies back and forth in straight lines like a lawnmower, snapping pictures that overlap, so nothing is left out.

Now imagine the drone tips its camera sideways and takes another photo. Now you see the fronts of the houses, the sides of the hills, maybe even the sky at the top. That is an oblique photograph, oblique meaning slanted. If the sky shows, it is high oblique; if it does not, it is low oblique. Obliques are great for postcards because you recognise everything. But things in front look big and things at the back look tiny, so you cannot measure distances on them.

For maps, we use the straight-down photo. It shows everything, even the brand-new house built last month that is not on any map yet. But it has no names, no heights, and no lines for borders. It is just a picture.

Keep the drone in mind and try the questions for this part: straight down is vertical, slanted is oblique, and why we measure only on the straight-down one.

## Subtopic: Straightening the Wobbly Photo

Even the straight-down photo has a problem. The top of a hill is closer to the camera than the valley, so the hill looks a bit bigger than it should. Tall things near the edge of the photo, like a water tower, seem to lean outward. And the aeroplane wobbles a little, so one side of the photo is closer than the other. So you cannot quite measure on it.

Experts fix it with a computer. They feed in the height of every bit of ground and some points whose exact positions are known, and the computer stretches and nudges every part of the photo until it is all at exactly the same scale, as if you were looking straight down at every spot at once. The straightened photo is called an orthophoto. Ortho means straight.

Then they print map things on top: names of places and roads, contour lines every 5 metres, spot heights, a frame with latitude and longitude, and a scale. The scale is 1:10 000, so 1 centimetre on it is 100 metres on the ground. Ten centimetres is a kilometre. That makes it five times more zoomed-in than the topographic map, and 25 of them fit into one topographic sheet.

So you get the best of both: the real picture of the land, and a map you can measure on.

Hold the straightened photo in mind and try this part's questions: why the raw photo is wobbly, what an orthophoto is, and 1 centimetre for 100 metres.

## Subtopic: Spot the Difference

There are no symbols on the photo, so you play detective with your eyes. Ask seven questions. How dark or light is it? Water and tar are usually dark; dry grass, sand and gravel roads are light. Is it smooth or rough? A dam is smooth, a forest is rough. What shape is it? People build straight lines and neat shapes; nature makes wiggles. How big is it? A tiny square is a house; a long rectangle is a factory. Is there a pattern? Neat rows of houses mean a planned suburb; tiny roofs squeezed together with winding paths mean an informal settlement; rows of dots in a field mean an orchard. What does its shadow look like? A tall thin shadow means a pylon or a tower. What is next to it? A big shed beside a railway is probably a factory.

One shape you will see a lot on South African farmland is the perfect circle. Those are not crop circles from aliens; they are centre-pivot irrigation, a long sprinkler arm turning slowly around a point in the middle, watering a round field. Green circles have crops growing; pale circles are bare.

And a bonus clue. In South Africa the midday sun is in the north, so shadows point south. That tells you which way the photo is facing.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A vertical photo is taken straight down; an oblique one is slanted. A raw vertical photo is a bit wobbly, so it is straightened into an orthophoto, and map information is printed on top at 1:10 000. Read it with seven clues: dark or light, smooth or rough, shape, size, pattern, shadow and what is next to it. In Grade 10 Geography you will use orthophotos with every topographic map.

Hold the seven questions in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: land use on the photo, the circles on the farm, and shadows to the south.
