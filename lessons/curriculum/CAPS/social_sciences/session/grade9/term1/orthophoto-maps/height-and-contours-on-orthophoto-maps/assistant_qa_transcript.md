### subtopic_1 — Height Information on the Orthophoto Map
**Assistant:** Why does the orthophoto need a smaller contour interval than the topographic map?

**Tutor:** Because at 1:10 000 the map shows a small area in great detail, and a 20 metre interval would leave most of that detail without any height information. Over the 5 kilometre width of a typical orthophoto sheet, the land may rise and fall by only 40 or 50 metres. With a 20 metre interval, that whole sheet might carry only two or three contours, and the shapes of small valleys, gullies, embankments and the basin of a farm dam would be invisible. With a 5 metre interval the same sheet carries eight to ten contours, enough to show every spur and hollow that the photograph shows. There is also a balance to keep. At 1:50 000 a 5 metre interval would crowd the map with so many lines on steep ground that they would merge into a solid brown mass and hide the symbols beneath them, so the smaller-scale map uses the wider interval. In general, the larger the scale of a map, the smaller the contour interval it can carry, which is why a building plan at 1:500 may use contours at 1 metre or less. A practical consequence for you is that orthophoto contours must be counted in fives, and that every fourth orthophoto contour, the multiples of 20, matches a topographic contour, which is the quickest way to link the two maps. Grade 10 Geography uses both intervals in the same question, so check the margin of each map before any height or gradient calculation.

### subtopic_2 — Reading Height and Slope on the Orthophoto
**Assistant:** How do I calculate gradient on the orthophoto without mixing up the two scales?

**Tutor:** Use the same formula as always, but convert the map distance with the orthophoto's own scale, 1:10 000, so that 1 centimetre equals 100 metres. Gradient equals vertical interval over horizontal equivalent. The vertical interval comes from the heights: if a road rises from a spot height of 1 520 metres at a bridge to the 1 555 metre contour on a spur, the vertical interval is 35 metres. Count orthophoto contours in fives to get heights where no spot height is given. The horizontal equivalent comes from the measured map distance: 2,5 centimetres on the orthophoto times 10 000 is 25 000 centimetres, which divided by 100 is 250 metres. Then gradient equals 35 over 250, and dividing both by 35 gives 1 to 7,1. The mistake to avoid is using the topographic map's conversion, multiplying by 50 000 or by 500 metres per centimetre, which would make the horizontal equivalent 1 250 metres and the gradient 1:35,7, a slope five times gentler than the real one. A quick sense check helps: on the orthophoto, distances are short, a few hundred metres, and gradients over spurs and valley sides are often steep, 1:5 to 1:15. If your answer suggests a nearly flat slope where the contours are packed closely, recheck the scale. Write the scale you used in your working; it shows the examiner your method and earns the conversion mark.

### subtopic_3 — Landforms from Contours over the Image
**Assistant:** How can I be sure a V in the contours is a donga and not a stream?

**Tutor:** By combining the contour pattern with the photographic image and with the map's water symbols, because a donga and a stream valley make the same V shape in the contours. Both are channels cut into a slope by running water, so contours bend upslope where they cross them. The differences lie elsewhere. A stream that flows all or part of the year is shown with a blue line on the topographic map and usually has a line of riverine vegetation along it on the orthophoto, dark and rough in texture, because trees and reeds grow where water is available. A donga is an erosion gully, cut by storm runoff on a slope where the protective vegetation has been removed by overgrazing, trampling, ploughing or fire. On the orthophoto it shows as a pale, raw, sharp-edged scar, often with smaller branching gullies like the fingers of a hand, on bare or thinly vegetated ground, with no water and little vegetation in it. Its contours form a very sharp, narrow V, because a young gully has steep sides, and it often starts abruptly on a slope rather than running continuously to a river. Several dongas together on steep, closely contoured, bare slopes are strong evidence of soil erosion, which is one of the main Term 3 Geography topics and a key part of the Grade 11 Geography topic on soil and soil erosion. In an answer, give the contour evidence and the image evidence together.

### subtopic_4 — Combining Photograph and Contours, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why does the edge of a dam always follow a contour line?

**Tutor:** Because the surface of still water is level, so every point along the edge of a dam, where the water meets the land, is at the same height, and a line joining points of the same height is by definition a contour. Gravity pulls water to the lowest possible level, so in a dam with no wind and no flow, the surface settles flat and horizontal. Where that flat surface meets the sloping banks, it traces out a line at one height around the whole basin. On an orthophoto the water's edge therefore runs parallel to the contours on the banks, and often a contour, say 1 525 metres, coincides almost exactly with the shoreline, though the level changes with the seasons as the dam fills and empties. This gives a reliable test for distinguishing a dam from other dark, smooth areas, such as a tarred parking area or a dark ploughed field, whose edges cut across contours. It also shows where a dam can be built: at the narrow point of a valley, where the contours squeeze together and a short wall can hold back water that spreads upstream into the wider valley. The height of the wall determines which contour becomes the shoreline, and a planner can calculate the size of the future lake by tracing that contour around the valley on the orthophoto. Water management in South Africa is a Grade 10 Geography topic, and this skill is its map foundation.
