### subtopic_1 — Aerial Photographs: Vertical and Oblique
**Assistant:** Why do aerial photographs overlap so much along the flight line?

**Tutor:** So that every point on the ground is photographed at least twice from slightly different positions, which guarantees complete coverage and makes it possible to see and measure height. Along each flight line the camera fires so that each new photograph covers about 60 percent of the previous one, and the flight lines themselves are spaced so that neighbouring strips overlap sideways by roughly a quarter. Without overlap, small changes in the aircraft's speed, height or direction would leave gaps, and a single missed strip of ground would mean an expensive repeat flight. The deeper reason is stereoscopy. Your two eyes see the world from positions a few centimetres apart, and your brain uses the difference between the two views to judge depth. A pair of overlapping aerial photographs is the same thing on a huge scale: the two camera positions are hundreds of metres apart, so when the pair is viewed through a stereoscope, one photograph to each eye, the landscape appears in exaggerated three dimensions, hills standing up and valleys sinking down. Before computers, mapmakers traced contours by viewing such pairs in precise instruments, and today software measures the same differences automatically to build the digital model of ground height that is used to correct orthophotos. Grade 8 learners are often given a pocket stereoscope and a photo pair to try it, and in Grade 10 the idea returns when you interpret relief from photographs alongside contours.

### subtopic_2 — From Photograph to Orthophoto
**Assistant:** If high ground looks bigger on a raw photograph, how much error does that really cause?

**Tutor:** In flat country very little, but in mountainous country the displacement can be tens or even hundreds of metres, which is far too much for an accurate map. The camera sees the world in perspective from a point high above, like a lamp shining down. A point on a hilltop 300 metres above the valley floor is 300 metres closer to that point than the valley is, so it is drawn at a slightly larger scale, and its position on the photograph is pushed outward from the centre of the image. The size of the shift depends on three things: the height of the feature above the reference level, its distance from the centre of the photograph, and the flying height. As an illustration, with a flying height of about 5 000 metres, a hilltop 300 metres high and 8 centimetres from the centre of a photograph would be displaced by about half a centimetre on the image, which at a typical photograph scale is more than a hundred metres on the ground. A tall building near the edge leans outward so that you see one of its walls. Orthorectification uses a digital model of ground height to calculate and reverse the displacement for every point, so that hilltops and valleys are shown at the same scale and in their true positions. That correction is the entire difference between an aerial photograph and an orthophoto, and the reason only the corrected image carries a ratio scale that can be trusted for distance and gradient calculations in Grade 9 and Grade 10 mapwork.

### subtopic_3 — Reading the Image: Tone, Texture, Shape and More
**Assistant:** How can I tell an informal settlement from a formal township on a black and white orthophoto?

**Tutor:** By pattern, size, spacing and the street layout, because the two are built in different ways even when the houses are small in both. A formal residential area is planned before it is built: surveyors lay out streets and plots, services such as water, sewerage and electricity are installed, and houses are built on the plots. On the orthophoto this shows as a regular pattern, straight or gently curved streets of even width, roofs of similar size at similar distances from the road, each on its own plot, often with small yards, and open spaces such as schools, clinics and sports fields set aside at intervals. Many government-subsidised housing developments in South Africa show exactly this pattern of small, evenly spaced houses on a grid. An informal settlement grows without a plan, as people build their own shelters on available land, often close to work opportunities. On the orthophoto it appears as a dense cluster of very small roofs, packed closely with little or no space between them, with narrow, irregular paths instead of streets, few or no trees, and often on marginal land such as a floodplain, a steep slope or a strip beside a railway or a main road. Its tone is often uneven, because roofs are made of different materials. The distinction matters in Grade 10 and Grade 12 Geography, where settlement and urban issues are studied, and an exam answer should give at least two of these clues, for example the irregular layout and the small closely packed dwellings.

### subtopic_4 — Land Use on the Orthophoto and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why do shadows on South African photographs point south, and how can that help me?

**Tutor:** Because South Africa lies south of the Tropic of Capricorn, so the midday sun is always in the northern half of the sky, and shadows fall away from the sun, towards the south. The sun is directly overhead only between the tropics. On the summer solstice, around 21 December, it is overhead at the Tropic of Capricorn, about 23,5 degrees south; most of South Africa is further south than that, so even at its highest point in midsummer the sun is slightly to the north. In winter it is much lower in the northern sky, so shadows are long. On an aerial photograph taken around the middle of the day, every pylon, tree and building casts its shadow roughly towards the south, slightly south-west in the afternoon and south-east in the morning. This helps in three ways. First, it gives an approximate check of orientation if an extract has been printed without a north arrow or turned. Second, shadow length shows relative height: a water tower casts a much longer shadow than a house, and the shape of the shadow shows the outline of the object, which makes pylons, towers and tall trees easy to recognise. Third, very long shadows suggest a winter photograph or an early or late time of day. In the northern hemisphere, outside the tropics, the rule reverses. This is a good example of the southern hemisphere correctness that Geography exams reward, and Grade 10 Geography builds on it in the topic on the heating of the atmosphere.
