# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the origins and growth of the Nazi Party in the 1920s and the effects of the Great Depression of 1929 on Germany. It covers Hitler's background and his takeover of the party; Nazi ideas as set out in the party programme and in Mein Kampf; the Beer Hall Putsch of 1923 and the change of strategy that followed; the causes of the Great Depression; and its economic, social and political effects on Germany up to 1932. The anchor example is the contrast between two election results: 2,6 percent of the vote for the Nazis in May 1928 and 18,3 percent in September 1930, a sevenfold rise in two years separated by the Wall Street Crash. The governing idea is that the Nazis were a small fringe party until economic catastrophe gave their message an audience, and that historians explain their rise by combining long-term grievances, Nazi methods and the shock of the Depression.

## Subtopic: Hitler and the Birth of the Nazi Party

Adolf Hitler was born on 20 April 1889 in Braunau am Inn, in Austria, close to the German border. As a young man in Vienna he failed twice to gain entry to the Academy of Fine Arts and lived in poverty, absorbing the extreme German nationalism and antisemitism, hatred of Jewish people, that were common in the city's politics. In 1914 he volunteered for the Bavarian army, served through the war as a message runner on the Western Front, was wounded and was awarded the Iron Cross. He was in hospital, temporarily blinded by gas, when he heard of the armistice, and he later described it as a betrayal, embracing the stab-in-the-back myth.

After the war, while still working for the army in Munich, Hitler was sent to observe a small group called the German Workers' Party. He joined it in 1919, discovered a talent for powerful public speaking, and quickly became its main attraction. In 1920 the party was renamed the National Socialist German Workers' Party, NSDAP for short, from which the word Nazi comes, and it adopted a 25-point programme and the swastika flag. In 1921 Hitler became its leader with total control, the beginning of the Führer principle, in which the leader's word was law.

The party's programme combined several appeals. It demanded the cancellation of the Treaty of Versailles and the union of all German speakers in one state. It claimed that Jewish people could not be German citizens, a demand rooted in racist ideology that treated Jews as a separate and dangerous race. It promised to help small businesses and farmers and attacked big department stores and banks, while presenting itself as both nationalist and socialist in order to attract workers away from the Social Democrats and Communists. The same year, the party formed the Sturmabteilung, the SA, a uniformed paramilitary force known as the brownshirts, which protected Nazi meetings and violently attacked opponents.

In 1923 the party was still a regional force in Bavaria with a few tens of thousands of members, noisy but small.

The questions for this section are with you now: Hitler's background, the founding and naming of the NSDAP, the party programme, and the SA.

## Subtopic: The Beer Hall Putsch, Mein Kampf and a New Strategy

In November 1923, at the height of hyperinflation and the Ruhr crisis, Hitler believed the republic was ready to fall. On the evening of 8 November he and armed SA men burst into a Munich beer hall where the leaders of the Bavarian government were holding a meeting, and declared a national revolution. The next day, 9 November, Hitler and the wartime general Erich Ludendorff led about two thousand followers through Munich towards the city centre, planning a march on Berlin in imitation of Mussolini's march on Rome the year before. The Bavarian police opened fire: sixteen Nazis and four policemen were killed, and the Beer Hall Putsch collapsed.

Hitler was arrested and tried for high treason in 1924. He used the trial as a platform, his speeches reported in newspapers across Germany, and the sympathetic judges gave him the minimum sentence of five years, of which he served only about nine months in comfortable conditions in Landsberg prison. There he dictated Mein Kampf, meaning My Struggle, published in 1925. In it he set out his ideas: that humanity was divided into superior and inferior races, with so-called Aryans, including Germans, at the top and Jews portrayed as a deadly enemy; that Germany needed lebensraum, living space, to be conquered in eastern Europe at the expense of Slavic peoples; that the Treaty of Versailles must be destroyed; that communism must be crushed; and that Germany must be led by a single all-powerful leader. These ideas were false and racist, and they would guide Nazi policy.

The failure of the putsch taught Hitler that the state could not be overthrown by force while the army and police supported it. He decided instead to win power legally, through elections, and then destroy democracy from within. On his release he rebuilt the party: branches across Germany, a youth movement, the Hitler Youth, founded in 1926, an elite guard, the SS, and a skilled propaganda operation, from 1930 directed by Joseph Goebbels.

During the prosperous years of 1924 to 1929, however, few Germans were listening. In the Reichstag election of May 1928 the Nazis won just 2,6 percent of the vote and 12 seats out of 491.

This section's questions are ready: the putsch, the trial and Mein Kampf, the ideas in it, and the strategy of legal power.

## Subtopic: The Great Depression of 1929

During the 1920s the American economy boomed. Factories produced more cars, radios and household goods than ever, and millions of Americans bought shares on the New York Stock Exchange on Wall Street, many with borrowed money, expecting prices to keep rising. By 1929 share prices were far higher than the companies' real earnings justified, factories were producing more goods than could be sold, and farmers had been in difficulty for years.

In October 1929 confidence broke. Shareholders rushed to sell, and prices collapsed: on Black Tuesday, 29 October 1929, about 16 million shares were sold in a single day. This was the Wall Street Crash. Investors were ruined, banks that had lent money to buy shares failed, and people who had their savings in those banks lost them. Businesses cut production and laid off workers, who then bought less, so more businesses cut back in a downward spiral. American unemployment rose to about a quarter of the workforce by 1933.

The crisis spread across the world because economies were linked by trade and loans. America cut its imports and raised tariffs, taxes on imported goods, to protect its industries, and other countries replied with tariffs of their own, so world trade shrank by about two thirds between 1929 and 1934. American banks needing money recalled the loans they had made abroad. The result was the Great Depression, the most severe economic crisis of the twentieth century, lasting through most of the 1930s.

South Africa was hit too. Prices for wool, maize and diamonds collapsed, many farmers lost their land, and the movement of impoverished rural people to the towns accelerated. The Carnegie Commission reported in 1932 that about 300 000 white South Africans lived in severe poverty; Black South Africans, who already earned far less and had no vote, suffered even more, with little state help. South Africa's economy recovered after it left the gold standard at the end of 1932 and the gold price rose.

Take this section's questions now: the boom, the Wall Street Crash, how the Depression spread, and its effect on South Africa.

## Subtopic: The Depression in Germany and the Error Museum

Germany was hit harder than almost any other country, because its recovery had been built on American loans. When those loans were recalled, German banks and businesses lost their funding. Factories closed, exports fell, and in 1931 major banks collapsed. Industrial production fell by about 40 percent. Unemployment rose from about 1,3 million in September 1929 to about 6 million by early 1932, roughly a third of the workforce, and millions more worked short hours. Families depended on shrinking welfare payments, soup kitchens and charity; homelessness and malnutrition spread; young people left school with no prospect of work.

The political effects were immediate. In March 1930 the coalition government collapsed over the rising cost of unemployment insurance. President Hindenburg appointed Heinrich Brüning as chancellor, and when the Reichstag would not pass his budget, Brüning governed by presidential decree under Article 48. His policy of cutting spending, wages and benefits to balance the budget deepened the suffering and earned him the name the Hunger Chancellor. From 1930 Germany was effectively governed without parliamentary majorities, which weakened democracy before the Nazis took power.

Voters turned to the extremes, which promised radical solutions. In the election of September 1930 the Nazi vote rose to 18,3 percent and 107 seats, making them the second-largest party; the Communists also grew, to 13,1 percent and 77 seats. The Nazis offered something to everyone: work and bread to the unemployed, protection from communism to business owners and the middle class, a restored Germany free of Versailles to nationalists, and a scapegoat, Jews and communists, for everyone's suffering. Street battles between the SA and the Communists made many Germans long for order, which the Nazis also promised.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: Hitler was born in Germany; he was born in Austria. Two: the Beer Hall Putsch brought Hitler to power; it failed, and he turned to elections. Three: the Nazis were popular throughout the 1920s; they won only 2,6 percent in 1928. Four: the Depression began in Germany; it began with the Wall Street Crash in the United States in 1929. Five: only the Nazis gained from the Depression; the Communists also grew, as voters abandoned the centre for both extremes.

The questions for this section are ready: unemployment in Germany, Brüning and Article 48, the 1930 election, the Nazi appeal, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same rise again, through an angry soldier who finds a microphone, a failed attack that taught a dangerous lesson, and a sneeze in America that gave Germany pneumonia — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: An Angry Soldier Finds a Microphone

Start with one man. Adolf Hitler was born in 1889 in Austria, not Germany. As a young man he wanted to be a painter, was turned down by the art school twice, and drifted around Vienna, poor and bitter, soaking up the ugly ideas in the air there: extreme pride in being German, and hatred of Jewish people. In World War I he fought for Germany as a messenger and won a medal for bravery. When Germany lost, he refused to believe it, and he swallowed the stab-in-the-back lie whole.

After the war, in Munich, he joined a tiny political party with a few dozen members. He stood up to speak at a meeting, and the room went silent. He was a gifted, furious speaker who could make a crowd feel its anger and its pride at the same time. Within two years he was running the party. It got a new name, the National Socialist German Workers' Party, Nazi for short, a flag with a swastika, and a list of demands: tear up the Treaty of Versailles, bring all Germans into one country, and, from the very start, take away Jewish people's citizenship. Racism was in the party's plan from its first page.

It also got a private army of thugs in brown shirts, the SA, who guarded Nazi meetings and beat up opponents in the street.

But in the early 1920s it was a small, noisy party in one corner of Germany, and most people ignored it.

Keep the angry soldier in mind and try the questions for this part: Austria not Germany, the Nazi name and demands, and the brownshirts.

## Subtopic: The Failed Attack That Taught a Lesson

In November 1923, when money was worthless and Germans were desperate, Hitler thought his moment had come. He and his armed brownshirts stormed into a beer hall in Munich where government leaders were meeting and announced a revolution. The next day they marched through the city. The police opened fire. Sixteen Nazis and four policemen died, and the whole thing fell apart in minutes. That was the Beer Hall Putsch, putsch meaning a sudden attempt to grab power.

Hitler was arrested and put on trial for treason. But the judges liked him, the newspapers printed his speeches, and he was sentenced to only five years, of which he served about nine months, in a comfortable prison. There he wrote a book, Mein Kampf, My Struggle. In it he wrote that people belong to higher and lower races, with Germans at the top and Jewish people as the enemy; that Germany needed living space, lebensraum, to be taken from the countries to the east; and that Germany needed one leader with total power. All of it was false and hateful, and all of it later became policy.

And he learned a dangerous lesson: you cannot beat the police and army by force, so win votes instead, get into power legally, and then destroy democracy from the inside. He built the party up across the country, with a youth group and clever propaganda. But in 1928, while Germany was doing well, the Nazis got just 2,6 percent of the vote. Nobody was buying.

Hold the failed putsch in mind and try this part's questions: the beer hall, the short prison stay, Mein Kampf, and the new plan to win votes.

## Subtopic: When America Sneezed, Germany Caught Pneumonia

Remember that Germany's good years were built on money borrowed from America. In October 1929 the American stock market crashed. People who had borrowed to buy shares were ruined, banks failed, factories closed. There is an old saying: when America sneezes, the world catches a cold. This time Germany caught pneumonia. American banks wanted their money back, now. German factories lost their loans and shut down. By early 1932 about 6 million Germans had no job, about one worker in three. Families queued at soup kitchens. Young people finished school with nowhere to go.

The government argued about how to pay the unemployed, fell apart, and from 1930 the chancellor, Heinrich Brüning, ruled using the president's emergency powers, Article 48, cutting wages and benefits. People called him the Hunger Chancellor.

When people are hungry and frightened, the quiet middle parties start to sound useless, and the loud extreme parties start to sound like hope. In 1930 the Nazi vote jumped from 2,6 percent to 18,3 percent. The Communists grew too. The Nazis promised jobs, bread, order, a strong Germany, and someone to blame: Jews and communists.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Hitler, Austrian-born, took over a tiny party in Munich and built it on anger, nationalism and racism. His 1923 putsch failed, he wrote Mein Kampf in prison, and he switched to winning votes. Nobody listened until the Wall Street Crash of 1929 brought the Depression, recalled loans and about 6 million unemployed. Then the Nazi vote soared. Grade 11 History studies this rise in depth.

Hold the sneeze and the soup kitchen in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: the crash, unemployment, and the jump in the Nazi vote.
