### subtopic_1 — Hitler and the Birth of the Nazi Party
**Assistant:** Why did the judges give Hitler such a light sentence after the Beer Hall Putsch?

**Tutor:** Because many judges in the Weimar Republic shared the nationalist views of the defendants and were openly hostile to the republic they served. The judiciary had been appointed under the Kaiser, and most judges were conservative men who regarded the 1918 revolution as illegitimate and the republic's leaders as the November Criminals. Hitler's trial in Munich in early 1924 was held in Bavaria, where right-wing sympathies were especially strong, and the presiding judge allowed Hitler to make long political speeches from the dock, attacking the republic and presenting himself as a patriot who had acted for Germany. Newspapers across the country reported them, so the trial made Hitler a national figure for the first time. He was convicted but received the minimum sentence for treason, five years, with the possibility of early release; he was freed after about nine months. The law also required that a foreign national convicted of treason be deported, and Hitler was still an Austrian citizen, but the court ruled that this did not apply to a man who thought and felt as a German. Ludendorff, the war hero, was acquitted altogether. The contrast with the treatment of left-wing rebels, who were often given long sentences or executed, is striking evidence of the bias of the courts. For a source-based question this is a useful example of how institutions that are supposed to be neutral can help undermine a democracy, a theme that recurs in Grade 11 and Grade 12 History.

### subtopic_2 — The Beer Hall Putsch, Mein Kampf and a New Strategy
**Assistant:** How did one stock market crash in New York cause a depression all over the world?

**Tutor:** Because the crash destroyed wealth and confidence in the world's largest economy, and the links of trade and loans carried that damage to every other country. The crash itself wiped out the savings of investors, but its larger effect was on banks and spending. American banks had lent money for share buying and lost it; thousands of banks failed between 1930 and 1933, taking ordinary depositors' savings with them. People and businesses stopped spending and investing, factories cut production and laid off workers, and unemployed workers bought even less. The United States was the world's biggest buyer of raw materials and manufactured goods, so when it bought less, countries that sold to it, including South Africa with its wool and diamonds, lost income. In 1930 the United States passed the Smoot-Hawley tariff, sharply raising taxes on imports to protect American jobs; other countries retaliated with their own tariffs, and world trade collapsed. The second link was finance. American banks had lent heavily abroad in the 1920s, especially to Germany, and now they recalled those loans. Germany had used American loans to pay reparations to Britain and France, which in turn paid war debts to America, so the whole circle of payments broke. Governments made matters worse by cutting spending to balance their budgets. Grade 10 and Grade 11 History return to the Depression in the topic on capitalism in the USA from 1900 to 1940.

### subtopic_3 — The Great Depression of 1929
**Assistant:** Why did voters choose the Nazis instead of the democratic parties when things got bad?

**Tutor:** Because the democratic parties seemed unable to solve the crisis, while the Nazis offered confident answers, someone to blame and a promise of national rebirth, tailored to almost every group of voters. From 1930 the republic's governments could not even command a majority in the Reichstag, and Brüning's decree government cut wages and benefits, which looked like doing nothing for the suffering. The Nazis, by contrast, promised work and bread to the unemployed, protection against a communist revolution to business owners, farmers and the middle class, the restoration of German pride and the end of Versailles to nationalists and former soldiers, and a strong leader to those tired of quarrelling politicians. Their propaganda, under Goebbels, used mass rallies, posters, films, radio and aircraft tours to present Hitler as a saviour. They also offered scapegoats: Jews, communists and the November Criminals, blamed falsely for Germany's suffering, which turned fear into hatred. Middle-class and Protestant rural voters, young first-time voters and people who had not voted before moved strongly to the Nazis; Catholic voters and organised workers were more loyal to their own parties, the Centre Party, the Social Democrats or the Communists. Historians weigh these factors differently, and some stress the long-term weakness of democracy, others the Depression, others Nazi methods. A strong essay considers them together and reaches a reasoned judgement, which is exactly the essay skill developed in Grade 10 History.

### subtopic_4 — The Depression in Germany and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** If the Communists also grew, why didn't they and the Social Democrats unite against the Nazis?

**Tutor:** Because the two parties of the left were bitter enemies, and the Communist Party, following instructions from the Soviet Union, treated the Social Democrats as its main enemy rather than the Nazis. The split went back to 1919, when the Social Democrat-led government used the army and the Freikorps to crush the Spartacist uprising, and Communist leaders Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht were murdered. Communists never forgave this. From 1928 the Communist International in Moscow, led by Stalin, ordered communist parties to attack social democrats as social fascists, claiming they were as bad as the fascists because they supported the capitalist republic. The Communists believed that the Nazis would fail quickly and that their failure would open the way to a communist revolution. The Social Democrats, for their part, saw the Communists as a threat to democracy directed from Moscow. As a result, the combined vote of the two parties, which in November 1932 was larger than the Nazi vote, never acted together. Middle-class voters, meanwhile, were frightened by Communist growth and street violence, and many turned to the Nazis as the strongest barrier against revolution. Historians see the divided left as one of the reasons the Nazis came to power, though not the only one. Grade 11 History studies the Soviet Union and communism in Russia from 1900 to 1940, which explains the Moscow side of this story.
