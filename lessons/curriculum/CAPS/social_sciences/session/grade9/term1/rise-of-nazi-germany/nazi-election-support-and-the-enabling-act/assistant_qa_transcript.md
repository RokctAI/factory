### subtopic_1 — The Elections of 1932
**Assistant:** If most Germans never voted Nazi, why didn't the majority stop Hitler?

**Tutor:** Because the majority was divided into parties that would not work together, the constitution gave the president and a small circle around him the power to appoint a chancellor regardless, and once Hitler was in office he used state power and terror faster than opposition could organise. The anti-Nazi vote in 1932 was split between Communists, Social Democrats, the Catholic Centre Party and smaller liberal and conservative parties, which disagreed deeply about almost everything. The Communists and Social Democrats, the two largest anti-Nazi parties, were bitter enemies. The conservative nationalists, who might have blocked Hitler, instead brought him into government because they hated democracy and the left more than they feared him. After 30 January 1933, Hitler controlled the interior ministry and the Prussian police, and within a month the Reichstag Fire Decree allowed opponents to be arrested without charge. Communist leaders were in camps before they could act, Social Democrats faced intimidation, and trade unions, which had defeated the Kapp Putsch in 1920 with a general strike, were abolished in May 1933 before they called one. Many Germans who had not voted Nazi also accepted the new government because they welcomed the promise of order, jobs and national pride, or because they were afraid. Historians use the idea of consent and coercion: the Nazis ruled by a mixture of genuine support and fear. A good answer in Grade 11 History weighs both, using sources that show enthusiasm alongside sources that show repression.

### subtopic_2 — Hitler Becomes Chancellor, 30 January 1933
**Assistant:** Why did the conservatives think they could control Hitler?

**Tutor:** Because they underestimated him, despised him as a vulgar upstart, and believed that traditional power, the presidency, the army, the civil service and big business, would always outweigh a mass party. Papen, Hindenburg's son Oskar, the press baron Alfred Hugenberg and others came from the old aristocracy and wealthy elites. They wanted an authoritarian government without a parliament, a strong army freed from Versailles and the destruction of the left, goals they shared in part with Hitler. Their problem was that they had no popular support: Papen's party had a tiny share of the vote. Hitler had the largest party and the street power of the SA. By putting him in a cabinet where conservatives held most posts, with Papen as vice-chancellor and with Hindenburg's power to dismiss him, they believed they would gain his voters while keeping real control. They misjudged three things. First, Hitler's ruthlessness and his determination to hold total power. Second, the importance of the ministries the Nazis did control: the interior and the police, which gave them the means of repression. Third, Hindenburg's age: he was 85 and died eighteen months later. Within weeks the conservatives were sidelined; by 1934 Papen's own speechwriter was murdered in the Night of the Long Knives, and Schleicher, his rival, was shot dead. The episode is studied as an example of how democracies can be undermined not only by extremists but by established elites who think they can use them.

### subtopic_3 — The Reichstag Fire and the Enabling Act
**Assistant:** Was the Enabling Act legal?

**Tutor:** It was passed using the procedure for changing the constitution, so the Nazis presented it as legal, but the conditions under which it was passed make that claim deeply questionable. The Weimar constitution allowed amendments by a two-thirds majority of the Reichstag with at least two-thirds of its members present. On 23 March 1933 the vote of 444 to 94 exceeded two-thirds, so on paper the procedure was followed. But the 81 elected Communist deputies had been arrested, had fled or were in hiding under the Reichstag Fire Decree, and the rules were changed so that absent members were counted as present, which helped secure the quorum. Some Social Democrat deputies had also been arrested. The session took place with armed SA and SS men inside and outside the building, which is intimidation of a parliament. The Centre Party's votes were won by promises that Hitler later broke. A law passed by excluding elected opponents and under threat of violence lacks the free consent that democratic legitimacy requires, even if it follows the outward form. That is why historians describe the Nazi takeover as a legal revolution: it used the appearance of legality to destroy the rule of law. The lesson has been absorbed into later constitutions: Germany's post-war Basic Law and South Africa's 1996 Constitution protect the core of their bills of rights and their democratic order from being abolished by a simple vote of the legislature.

### subtopic_4 — Building the Dictatorship and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What was the Night of the Long Knives, and why did the army support it?

**Tutor:** It was the murder, between 30 June and 2 July 1934, of the leadership of the SA and of other political opponents by the SS on Hitler's orders, and the army supported it because it removed a rival that threatened the army's own position. By 1934 the SA had grown to more than three million men, far larger than the army's 100 000 allowed under Versailles. Its leader, Ernst Röhm, one of Hitler's oldest comrades, wanted the SA to absorb the army and to carry out a second revolution against the conservative elites and big business. The army's generals, conservative professionals, saw the SA as an undisciplined mob and a threat. Hitler needed the army for his plans to rearm and expand, and he needed the support of the generals and of President Hindenburg, who was close to death. So he chose the army. On 30 June the SS, under Heinrich Himmler, together with the Gestapo, arrested and shot Röhm and dozens of SA leaders, and used the opportunity to murder others: former chancellor Schleicher and his wife, conservatives around Papen, and old enemies of Hitler. Hitler then told the Reichstag that he had saved Germany from a plot and that he had been the supreme judge of the German people. The army thanked him, and a month later, when Hindenburg died, its soldiers swore personal loyalty to Hitler. The SS, which had carried out the killings, rose to become the most powerful organisation in Nazi Germany, the one that would later run the concentration and extermination camps.
