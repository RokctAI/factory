# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines Nazi Germany as an example of a fascist state and compares fascism with democracy. It covers the definition and origins of fascism; its main features and how each operated in Nazi Germany between 1933 and 1939; the experience of different groups, young people, women, workers and the churches, under Nazi rule; and the principles of democracy, illustrated by South Africa's Constitution, set against those of fascism. The anchor example is the Nazi slogan one people, one empire, one leader, which captures the fascist idea that the individual exists only to serve the nation and its leader. The governing idea is that fascism and democracy differ on the most basic question in politics: whether power belongs to a leader and a party or to citizens who hold that power accountable.

## Subtopic: What Fascism Is

Fascism is a form of extreme right-wing, authoritarian nationalism that arose in Europe after World War I. The word comes from Italy, where Benito Mussolini founded the Fascist movement in 1919 and became prime minister in 1922 after the March on Rome. Its name comes from the fasces, a bundle of rods tied around an axe that was carried before officials in ancient Rome as a symbol of authority: individual rods are weak, but bound together they are strong. Fascism took different forms in different countries, and Nazism, National Socialism, was the German form, with racism and antisemitism at its centre.

Historians identify a set of features that fascist states share. Extreme nationalism: the nation is placed above everything, including the rights of individuals, and is presented as superior to others. A single leader: power is concentrated in one person, who is presented as the embodiment of the nation and cannot be questioned; in Germany this was the Führer principle. A one-party state: all other parties are banned, and the ruling party merges with the state. Suppression of opposition: political opponents are imprisoned or killed, and secret police and informers watch the population. Propaganda and censorship: the state controls newspapers, radio, film, art and education. Militarism and expansion: war is glorified and the state seeks to conquer territory. Anti-communism and anti-democracy: fascists reject both liberal democracy and communism. Control of the economy: private ownership continues, but business and labour are directed to serve the state's goals.

Fascism appealed to people in the crises after 1918 because it promised order, national pride, strong leadership and protection against communism at a time when democracies seemed weak and divided. It also offered belonging: mass rallies, uniforms and marches made people feel part of something larger than themselves.

In Nazi Germany these features were combined with a racial ideology that divided humanity into superior and inferior races, which made persecution and, eventually, genocide central aims of the state rather than side effects.

The questions for this section are with you now: the origin of the word fascism, Mussolini and Italy, the main features of a fascist state, and why fascism appealed.

## Subtopic: How the Nazi State Controlled Germany

Nazi Germany used two main tools to control its people: terror and propaganda. Terror was carried out by the SS, the Schutzstaffel, originally Hitler's bodyguard, which under Heinrich Himmler grew into a vast organisation controlling the police and the concentration camps, and by the Gestapo, the secret state police, which could arrest anyone without trial and send them to a camp in what was called protective custody. The Gestapo was relatively small and relied heavily on denunciations by ordinary citizens: neighbours, colleagues and even family members reporting each other. By 1939 the camps held thousands of political prisoners, and many more had passed through them. The courts were also coordinated: judges were expected to judge in the spirit of the Führer, and special People's Courts tried political cases.

Propaganda was directed by Joseph Goebbels, who became minister of popular enlightenment and propaganda in March 1933. The ministry controlled newspapers, radio, cinema, theatre, music, literature and art. Cheap radio sets, the People's Receiver, were produced so that Hitler's speeches could reach every home. Films such as Leni Riefenstahl's record of the 1934 Nuremberg rally, Triumph of the Will, presented Hitler as a godlike leader. Huge rallies, parades and the 1936 Berlin Olympics displayed Nazi power. Censorship removed any contrary view: Jewish and opposition writers were banned, books were burned, and so-called degenerate modern art was removed from museums.

The economy was directed towards rearmament and self-sufficiency. Public works such as the building of the autobahns, motorways, together with conscription, reintroduced in 1935, and rearmament reduced unemployment from about 6 million in 1932 to below 1 million by 1937. Statistics were also improved by removing women and Jews from the figures. Trade unions had been abolished, and workers in the German Labour Front lost the right to strike, while the Strength Through Joy organisation offered cheap holidays and leisure to win their loyalty. In 1936 Hermann Göring's Four-Year Plan set out to prepare the economy for war within four years.

This section's questions are ready: the SS and Gestapo, denunciation, Goebbels's ministry, the People's Receiver, and the economy of rearmament.

## Subtopic: Life in the Nazi State

Young people were the Nazis' highest priority, because they would build the future Reich. School curricula were rewritten: history glorified Germany, biology taught false racial science, and physical education took up more time. Teachers had to join the Nazi Teachers' League, and Jewish teachers were dismissed. Outside school, boys joined the Hitler Youth, where they marched, camped, played sports, learned to shoot and were trained in loyalty to Hitler; girls joined the League of German Girls, which prepared them to be mothers of the nation. Membership became effectively compulsory in 1936 and legally required in 1939, and by then nearly all young Germans belonged. A minority resisted: groups such as the Edelweiss Pirates rejected the Hitler Youth, and members of the student group the White Rose distributed anti-Nazi leaflets in 1942 and 1943 and were executed.

Women were assigned a narrow role summed up in the slogan children, kitchen, church. Women were encouraged to leave paid work, especially in the professions, and to have many children; loans were given to newly married couples and reduced with each child, and mothers of large families received the Mother's Cross. Women were barred from serving as judges and lawyers. From the late 1930s, as rearmament created labour shortages, women were drawn back into factories.

The churches were handled carefully because most Germans were Christian. In 1933 Hitler signed a concordat, an agreement, with the Pope, promising freedom for the Catholic Church if it stayed out of politics, but he broke it, closing Catholic schools and youth groups. Among Protestants, the Nazis created a German Christian movement, and opponents formed the Confessing Church, many of whose pastors, including Martin Niemöller and Dietrich Bonhoeffer, were imprisoned; Bonhoeffer was executed in 1945.

For most Germans who were not targeted, the 1930s brought jobs and order, and many supported the regime; for those outside the national community, Jews, Roma, disabled people and political opponents, it brought exclusion, imprisonment and worse. Any account of life in Nazi Germany must hold both truths together.

Take this section's questions now: schools and the Hitler Youth, the role of women, the churches, and the different experiences of different groups.

## Subtopic: Fascism Compared with Democracy, and the Error Museum

Democracy means rule by the people, from the Greek demos, people, and kratos, power. Its core principles are the opposite of fascism's. In a democracy, government is chosen in regular free and fair elections, by secret ballot, in which every adult citizen has an equal vote. Several parties compete freely, and the losers can criticise the government and win the next election. Power is separated between the legislature that makes laws, the executive that carries them out and an independent judiciary that interprets them, so that no person or body holds all power. Everyone, including the president, is subject to the rule of law. Human rights, freedom of speech, of the press, of religion and of association, equality before the law and the right to a fair trial, are protected and cannot simply be removed by the government.

South Africa's Constitution of 1996 is an example. Chapter 2, the Bill of Rights, protects equality, human dignity, life, freedom and security of the person, and freedoms of expression, assembly, association and political choice. The Constitutional Court can strike down any law or action that violates the Constitution. Independent institutions created by Chapter 9, such as the Public Protector, the Human Rights Commission and the Electoral Commission, check the use of power. Elections are held every five years, and many parties compete.

Set side by side, the contrast is clear. In Nazi Germany one leader held all power; in a democracy power is shared and limited. One party was legal; in a democracy many parties compete. Opponents were imprisoned without trial; in a democracy opposition is legal and the accused have the right to a fair trial. The state controlled the media; in a democracy the press is free to criticise the government. Citizenship depended on race; in South Africa's democracy, equality before the law is guaranteed regardless of race, gender, religion or sexual orientation. The individual existed to serve the state; in a democracy the state exists to protect the rights of individuals.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: fascism began in Germany; it began in Italy under Mussolini. Two: a fascist state allows opposition parties if they are small; it bans all parties except its own. Three: the Gestapo arrested people only after fair trials; it held people without trial. Four: democracy means the majority can do anything; democracy protects the rights of minorities and individuals under a constitution. Five: Nazi Germany abolished private business; businesses remained privately owned but were directed by the state.

The questions for this section are ready: the principles of democracy, South Africa's Constitution, the comparison point by point, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same comparison again, through a team with a captain nobody may question, a loudspeaker on every street corner, and the rule book that protects every player — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Captain Nobody May Question

Imagine a soccer team where the captain makes every decision. Nobody votes for him. Nobody may question him. If a player disagrees, he is thrown off the team, or worse. There is only one team allowed in the whole league. Every player must wear the uniform, sing the team song and say the team is better than all others. That is fascism in a nutshell.

The word comes from Italy. A man called Benito Mussolini started the Fascist movement there in 1919 and took over the government in 1922. The name comes from an old Roman symbol: a bundle of sticks tied around an axe. One stick snaps easily; a bundle tied together is strong. The idea was that people must be tied tightly together under one leader.

Fascist states share a pattern. The nation is everything, more important than any person. One leader has all the power; in Germany he was called the Führer, the leader. Only one party is allowed. Anyone who opposes them is locked up. Newspapers, radio and films say only what the leader wants. War and conquest are celebrated. Communism and democracy are both hated.

Nazi Germany was Germany's version, with one terrible extra: racism at its centre. It claimed some people were superior and others inferior, and built its whole state around that lie.

Why did people go along? After the war and the Depression, many were desperate for order, jobs and pride, and fascism promised all three, with flags, marches and the feeling of belonging to something big.

## Subtopic: Fear and the Loudspeaker

The Nazis kept control with two things: fear and a loudspeaker.

Fear came from the SS, Hitler's black-uniformed force that ran the police and the camps, and the Gestapo, the secret police, who could arrest anyone and send them to a camp without a trial. The Gestapo was not huge. It did not need to be, because ordinary people reported each other: a neighbour who told a joke about Hitler, a colleague who complained. Nobody knew who might be listening.

The loudspeaker was propaganda, run by Joseph Goebbels. He controlled every newspaper, radio station and cinema. The government made cheap radios so every family could hear Hitler's speeches. Giant rallies with searchlights and thousands of marching men made Hitler look like a hero. Books the Nazis did not like were burned.

Young people got special attention. At school, lessons were rewritten to teach Nazi ideas. After school, boys joined the Hitler Youth to march, camp and train, and girls joined the League of German Girls. By 1939 it was the law to join. Women were told their place was children, kitchen and church.

And for many Germans, life in the 1930s felt better: new roads were built, the army grew, and unemployment fell from about 6 million to under 1 million by 1937. That made it easier to ignore what was happening to the people who were being shut out and locked up.

Hold fear and the loudspeaker in mind and try this part's questions: the Gestapo and informers, Goebbels's propaganda, and the Hitler Youth.

## Subtopic: The Rule Book That Protects Every Player

Now picture a different team. The players vote for their captain every season, and can vote him out. Anyone may say the captain made a bad call. There is a referee the captain cannot boss around. And there is a rule book that protects every single player, the fast and the slow, the new and the old, and even the captain must obey it. That is democracy, rule by the people.

South Africa's rule book is the Constitution of 1996. Its Bill of Rights protects everyone's equality and dignity, and freedom of speech, religion and the press, and the right to vote and to form or join any party. If the government breaks those rules, the Constitutional Court, the referee, can stop it. Many parties compete in elections every five years, and if voters are unhappy, they can change the government without a single shot fired.

Put the two teams side by side. One leader with all the power, or power shared and checked. One party, or many. Prison without trial, or a fair trial for everyone. The government controlling the news, or a free press that can criticise the government. Rights depending on your so-called race, or equal rights for all. People serving the state, or the state serving the people.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Fascism began with Mussolini in Italy. Its features: extreme nationalism, one leader, one party, terror, propaganda and war. Nazi Germany added racism at the core and controlled people with the SS, the Gestapo, Goebbels's propaganda and the Hitler Youth. Democracy is the opposite: elections, many parties, the rule of law, free media and protected rights, as in South Africa's Constitution. Grade 11 History studies Nazi Germany in depth.

Hold the rule book in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: democracy's principles, South Africa's Constitution and the side-by-side comparison.
