### subtopic_1 — From Boycott to Exclusion, 1933 to 1935
**Assistant:** How could ordinary Germans accept laws that took away their neighbours' rights?

**Tutor:** Through a combination of propaganda, fear, self-interest, gradual change and long-standing prejudice, and historians stress that acceptance ranged from enthusiastic support to silent discomfort. Propaganda was constant: newspapers, radio, films, posters and school lessons repeated that Jews were dangerous and alien, and many people who heard it daily came to see the exclusion of Jews as normal. Antisemitism was not invented by the Nazis; it had existed in Europe for centuries, and the Nazis built on existing prejudice. Change was gradual: each law was a small step from the last, presented as legal and orderly, so there was never a single moment that shocked most people into resistance. Fear mattered: by 1935 opponents had been sent to camps, the Gestapo relied on informers, and speaking up could cost a job or freedom. Self-interest mattered too: some Germans gained when Jewish colleagues were dismissed or Jewish businesses were sold cheaply. And many people were simply absorbed in their own lives, grateful for the jobs and order the regime seemed to bring. There were exceptions: some Germans helped Jewish neighbours, hid them or helped them escape, at great risk, and Kristallnacht's open violence disturbed many who had accepted the quieter legal persecution. The Grade 11 History case study on Nazi Germany asks exactly this question, and a strong answer distinguishes between perpetrators, bystanders, collaborators and rescuers, using sources that show each.

### subtopic_2 — The Nuremberg Laws of 1935
**Assistant:** Why did the Nazis need grandparents' religion to decide who was Jewish?

**Tutor:** Because their claim that Jews were a biological race was false, and when they tried to write it into law they found there was no physical or biological way to identify a Jewish person. Jewish identity is a matter of religion, culture, history and community, not of blood. People of Jewish descent look like their non-Jewish neighbours, share the same blood groups and cannot be identified by any test. The Nazi lawmakers therefore fell back on records that existed: church and synagogue registers of birth and marriage, which showed the religion of a person's grandparents. The November 1935 decree classified a person with three or four grandparents who had belonged to the Jewish religious community as a full Jew, even if that person had been baptised and raised as a Christian; a person with one or two such grandparents was a Mischling of the first or second degree, with different rules. So a supposedly racial law was enforced by checking religious records, which exposed the falseness of race science. Millions of Germans had to obtain an ancestry certificate proving their so-called Aryan descent to keep their jobs, and genealogy became an industry. South Africa's Population Registration Act of 1950, which you will study in Term 3, faced the same problem of defining race by law, and used appearance, acceptance and descent in ways that were equally arbitrary and caused great suffering. Both cases show that race classification is a social and political invention, not a scientific fact.

### subtopic_3 — Kristallnacht and the Refugee Crisis
**Assistant:** Why did so few Jewish people leave Germany before it was too late?

**Tutor:** In fact many did: about half of Germany's Jews emigrated between 1933 and 1939, but leaving was much harder than it sounds, and the doors of other countries were mostly closed. Emigration meant abandoning homes, businesses, careers, elderly relatives and a country many regarded as their own; in the early years many hoped the Nazis would not last or that the persecution would stop at discrimination. The Nazi state also made leaving expensive: emigrants had to pay a flight tax of 25 percent of their assets and could take almost no money out of the country, so they arrived abroad poor. Above all, emigrants needed somewhere to go, and receiving countries set strict limits during the Depression, fearing competition for jobs and, often, holding antisemitic views of their own. At the Évian Conference of July 1938, delegates from 32 countries expressed sympathy and almost all refused to increase their quotas; only the Dominican Republic offered substantial numbers. Britain restricted entry to Palestine, which it controlled, and the United States kept to its quota. South Africa's Aliens Act of 1937, passed after the arrival of refugees on the Stuttgart and protests by the pro-Nazi Greyshirts, sharply cut Jewish immigration. After Kristallnacht, Britain did accept about 10 000 unaccompanied Jewish children in the Kindertransport, but most of their parents could not follow. When war began in 1939, emigration became almost impossible, and in 1941 it was banned.

### subtopic_4 — Persecution of Other Groups and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why were people with disabilities targeted by the Nazis?

**Tutor:** Because Nazi ideology, drawing on the false science of eugenics, held that the nation was a biological body that had to be kept healthy by preventing so-called inferior people from having children, and later by killing them. Eugenics, the idea that a population could be improved by controlling who reproduced, was popular among scientists and politicians in many countries in the early twentieth century, including the United States, Britain and South Africa, and several American states had passed sterilisation laws. The Nazis took it to its most extreme conclusion. Their propaganda described disabled people as life unworthy of life and calculated their cost to the state, with school arithmetic problems asking how many houses could be built for the price of caring for patients. The Law for the Prevention of Hereditarily Diseased Offspring of July 1933 led to the forced sterilisation of about 400 000 people with conditions such as schizophrenia, epilepsy, hereditary blindness and deafness and alcoholism. In 1939 Hitler authorised a secret programme, named T4 after the address of its Berlin office, in which doctors selected disabled patients to be killed, first children and then adults, in six killing centres using gas chambers disguised as showers. About 70 000 had been killed when protests, notably a sermon by the Catholic bishop Clemens von Galen in August 1941, led to the official halt, but killing continued secretly, and the total reached about 200 000 or more. The staff and methods of T4 were transferred to the extermination camps, which links this programme directly to the Holocaust, the subject of a later lesson this term.
