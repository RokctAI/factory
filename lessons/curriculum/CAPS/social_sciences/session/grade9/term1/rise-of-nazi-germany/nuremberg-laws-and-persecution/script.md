# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains how the Nazi state stripped Jewish people in Germany of their rights between 1933 and 1938, with the Nuremberg Laws of 1935 at the centre, and how it persecuted other groups it defined as outsiders. It covers the first measures of 1933 and 1934; the content and meaning of the Nuremberg Laws; the escalation to Kristallnacht and the refugee crisis of 1938; and the persecution of Roma and Sinti, people with disabilities, gay men, Black Germans, Jehovah's Witnesses and political opponents. The anchor example is the Reich Citizenship Law of 15 September 1935, which turned Jewish Germans from citizens into subjects with no political rights. The governing idea is that the Holocaust grew out of a step-by-step process of exclusion, made possible by racist ideology, enforced by law and accepted by much of the population.

## Subtopic: From Boycott to Exclusion, 1933 to 1935

In 1933 about 525 000 Jewish people lived in Germany, less than one percent of the population. Most were fully part of German society: they were doctors, lawyers, teachers, business owners, scientists, artists and soldiers, about 100 000 of whom had served in World War I. Nazi ideology nonetheless portrayed them as a separate and dangerous race responsible for Germany's defeat, the Depression and communism. These claims were false. The idea that Jews were a biological race was itself pseudo-science, false science used to justify prejudice.

Persecution began within weeks of Hitler becoming chancellor. On 1 April 1933 the Nazis organised a national boycott of Jewish shops and businesses: SA men stood outside them with placards telling Germans not to buy from Jews, and Stars of David were painted on windows. On 7 April 1933 the Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service dismissed Jewish civil servants, including teachers and judges; at President Hindenburg's insistence, Jewish war veterans were at first exempted. Further laws limited the number of Jewish students in schools and universities, barred Jewish lawyers and doctors from many positions, and excluded Jews from journalism, the arts and the army. In May 1933 students and Nazi officials burned books by Jewish and opposition authors in public squares.

Each measure was presented as legal, and each was small enough that many Germans accepted it, looked away or even benefited, by taking a dismissed colleague's job or buying a business cheaply. Propaganda spread hatred daily: a newspaper called Der Stürmer printed vicious antisemitic cartoons, and schoolchildren were taught racial theory in biology lessons. Signs saying that Jews were not wanted appeared at the entrances to towns, swimming pools and parks.

By 1935, Jewish Germans had lost their jobs in public service and much of their place in public life, but they were still legally citizens. The next step took that away.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Jewish population of Germany in 1933, the boycott, the civil service law, and how exclusion was made to seem legal and normal.

## Subtopic: The Nuremberg Laws of 1935

The Nuremberg Laws were announced at the Nazi Party's annual rally in Nuremberg on 15 September 1935 and passed by the Reichstag, which met there for the purpose. There were two laws.

The Reich Citizenship Law declared that only a person of German or related blood could be a citizen of the Reich. Jewish Germans were reduced to the status of state subjects: they still had to obey the law and pay taxes, but they lost the right to vote, to hold public office and to the protection of citizenship. In a single law, people whose families had lived in Germany for centuries became outsiders in their own country.

The Law for the Protection of German Blood and German Honour banned marriages between Jews and citizens of German or related blood, and declared such marriages already made abroad to circumvent the law to be invalid. It made sexual relations between them a crime, punished by prison. It forbade Jews to employ German women under 45 as household servants and forbade Jews to fly the German flag.

The laws needed a definition of who was Jewish, and the decree that followed in November 1935 revealed their absurdity. Because there was no biological test for race, the Nazis defined Jewishness by the religion of a person's grandparents. A person with three or four Jewish grandparents was classified as a Jew, regardless of his or her own religion; a person with one or two Jewish grandparents was classed as a Mischling, of mixed blood, with partial rights. A Christian whose grandparents had been Jewish was therefore classified as Jewish, which shows that the so-called race laws rested on religion and ancestry, not science.

The Nuremberg Laws gave persecution a permanent legal framework. Hundreds of further regulations followed, and they were soon extended to Roma and Black Germans. Historians see them as a turning point, because they defined a group, separated it legally from the rest of society and prepared the way for everything that came later. You will meet laws with a disturbingly similar purpose in South Africa in Term 3, when the apartheid government passed the Population Registration Act and banned mixed marriages after 1948.

This section's questions are ready: the date and place, the Reich Citizenship Law, the Blood and Honour Law, and the definition by grandparents.

## Subtopic: Kristallnacht and the Refugee Crisis

The Berlin Olympic Games of 1936 brought a short pause, as signs were removed to impress foreign visitors, but persecution intensified in 1938. Jewish businesses were forcibly sold to non-Jews at low prices, a process called Aryanisation. Jewish passports were stamped with a red letter J, and Jews were required to add the name Israel or Sara to their own names. Jewish doctors lost their licences.

On 7 November 1938 a young Polish Jew, Herschel Grynszpan, whose parents had been expelled from Germany, shot a German diplomat in Paris. Goebbels used the death as an excuse for an organised attack across Germany and Austria, which Germany had annexed in March 1938. On the night of 9 to 10 November, SA men and Nazi supporters burned or wrecked hundreds of synagogues, smashed and looted about 7 500 Jewish shops, broke into homes and killed at least 91 Jewish people. About 30 000 Jewish men were arrested and sent to concentration camps such as Dachau, Buchenwald and Sachsenhausen. The night became known as Kristallnacht, the Night of Broken Glass, from the shattered windows that covered the streets. Afterwards the state fined the Jewish community one billion Reichsmarks for the damage done to it, and Jewish children were expelled from German schools.

Many Jewish families now tried desperately to leave. By 1939 about half of Germany's Jews had emigrated, but escape became harder as other countries closed their borders. At the Évian Conference in France in July 1938, 32 countries discussed the refugee crisis, and almost all refused to take in more refugees. South Africa was among the countries that restricted entry. In 1936 the ship Stuttgart brought about 537 German Jewish refugees to Cape Town, amid protests organised by the Greyshirts, a South African movement that copied Nazi ideas; in 1937 the government passed the Aliens Act, which in practice made it very difficult for German Jews to enter the country.

Take this section's questions now: Aryanisation, Kristallnacht and its consequences, emigration, and the closed doors of Évian and South Africa.

## Subtopic: Persecution of Other Groups and the Error Museum

Nazi racial ideology aimed to create a so-called pure national community and treated anyone who did not fit as an enemy or a burden. Roma and Sinti, peoples who had lived in Europe for centuries and were insultingly called Gypsies, were classified as racially inferior; from late 1935 the Nuremberg marriage ban was applied to them, many were forced into camps in the late 1930s, and during the war hundreds of thousands were murdered in a genocide that Roma call the Porajmos.

People with disabilities were targeted as a burden on the nation. A law of July 1933 allowed the forced sterilisation of people with conditions declared hereditary, such as some forms of blindness, deafness, epilepsy and mental illness, and about 400 000 people were sterilised. From 1939 a secret programme known as T4 murdered disabled children and adults in hospitals and gas chambers; about 70 000 had been killed by August 1941, and many more afterwards. The techniques and some of the staff of this programme were later used in the extermination camps.

Gay men were persecuted under a strengthened law of 1935; tens of thousands were convicted, and thousands were sent to concentration camps, where they were marked with a pink triangle. Black Germans, including children of German mothers and African soldiers of the French army that occupied the Rhineland, were forcibly sterilised in 1937. Jehovah's Witnesses, who refused to swear loyalty to Hitler or to serve in the army, were imprisoned in camps and marked with a purple triangle. Political opponents, communists, social democrats and trade unionists, were the first prisoners of the camps, marked with a red triangle. Pastor Martin Niemöller, imprisoned from 1937 to 1945, later reflected that when the Nazis came for the communists, the trade unionists and the Jews he had not spoken out, and when they came for him there was no one left to speak.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: Jewish people were a large part of the German population; they were less than one percent. Two: the Nuremberg Laws defined race scientifically; they defined it by the religion of grandparents. Three: persecution began with the war; it began in 1933 with boycotts and laws. Four: only Jews were persecuted; Roma and Sinti, disabled people, gay men, Black Germans, Jehovah's Witnesses and political opponents were also targeted. Five: other countries took in all Jewish refugees; most, including South Africa, restricted entry.

The questions for this section are ready: Roma and Sinti, disabled people, other targeted groups, Niemöller's reflection and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same story again, through a staircase of small steps, a law that decided who belonged, and the coloured triangles sewn onto prisoners' clothes — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Staircase of Small Steps

Imagine your family has lived in the same town for two hundred years. Your grandfather fought for the country in the war. Your mother is the town's doctor. You go to the local school and support the local football team. Now imagine that, one step at a time, all of that is taken away. That is what happened to Jewish Germans after 1933.

There were only about half a million Jewish people in Germany, less than one in every hundred people. The Nazis spread the lie that they were a dangerous separate race to blame for everything that had gone wrong. It was not true, but the government repeated it every day, in newspapers, posters, radio and school lessons.

Step one, April 1933: Nazi brownshirts stood outside Jewish-owned shops with signs telling people not to buy there. Step two, a week later: Jewish teachers, judges and other government workers were sacked. Step three: limits on Jewish students, Jewish lawyers, Jewish doctors, Jewish journalists and musicians. Books by Jewish writers were thrown on bonfires. Signs went up at swimming pools and parks: Jews not wanted.

Each step was small enough that many neighbours shrugged, looked away, or took the job or the shop that had been taken from a Jewish family. That is how a terrible thing can grow: one small step after another, while people stay quiet.

Keep the staircase in mind and try the questions for this part: less than one percent, the boycott, the sacking, and why small steps matter.

## Subtopic: A Law That Decided Who Belonged

In September 1935, at a huge Nazi rally in the city of Nuremberg, two new laws were announced. Together they are called the Nuremberg Laws.

The first law said that only people of so-called German blood could be citizens. Jewish Germans were no longer citizens. They could not vote, could not hold any public job, and had no protection as members of the nation. Overnight, people whose families had been German for centuries became outsiders in their own country.

The second law banned marriage between Jewish people and other Germans, made relationships between them a crime, and stopped Jewish families employing German women under 45 in their homes.

Here is the strangest part. To enforce a law about race, the Nazis had to decide who was Jewish. But there is no blood test for being Jewish, because Jewish people are not a separate race. So they checked your grandparents' religion. Three or four Jewish grandparents: you were classified as Jewish, even if you were a Christian who went to church every Sunday. That shows the whole thing was a lie dressed up as science.

Then it got worse. In November 1938, on Kristallnacht, the Night of Broken Glass, Nazi mobs burned hundreds of synagogues, smashed about 7 500 Jewish shops, killed at least 91 people and sent about 30 000 Jewish men to camps. Then the government fined the Jewish community for the damage. Families tried to flee, but many countries, South Africa among them, had closed their doors.

Hold the Nuremberg rally in mind and try this part's questions: no longer citizens, the marriage ban, grandparents' religion, and Kristallnacht.

## Subtopic: The Coloured Triangles

In the concentration camps, prisoners had coloured triangles sewn onto their clothes to show why they were there. The triangles tell us how many groups the Nazis targeted. Red for political prisoners: communists, socialists, trade unionists, the first people sent to the camps in 1933. Purple for Jehovah's Witnesses, who refused to swear loyalty to Hitler or to fight. Pink for gay men. Jewish prisoners wore a yellow triangle, often combined into a star.

Other groups suffered outside the camps too. Roma and Sinti families, who had lived in Europe for hundreds of years, were treated as racially inferior and later murdered in their hundreds of thousands. People with disabilities were sterilised against their will, about 400 000 of them, and from 1939 tens of thousands were murdered in a secret programme. Black Germans, many of them children, were forcibly sterilised in 1937.

A German pastor named Martin Niemöller, who was imprisoned from 1937 to 1945, said afterwards that when they came for the communists he said nothing, when they came for the trade unionists he said nothing, when they came for the Jews he said nothing, and when they came for him there was nobody left to speak.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Persecution started in 1933 with boycotts and sackings. The Nuremberg Laws of 1935 took away Jewish citizenship and banned mixed marriages, defining Jewishness by grandparents' religion. Kristallnacht in 1938 brought open violence. Roma and Sinti, disabled people, gay men, Black Germans, Jehovah's Witnesses and political opponents were persecuted too. Grade 11 History studies these ideas of race and their consequences in depth.

Hold the triangles and Niemöller's words in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: the other groups, the camps, and why speaking out matters.
