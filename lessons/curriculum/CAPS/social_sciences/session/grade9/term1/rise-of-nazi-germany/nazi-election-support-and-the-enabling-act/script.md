# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the public support for the Nazi Party in the elections of 1932 and 1933, Hitler's appointment as chancellor, and the creation of a dictatorship through the Enabling Act of 1933 and the measures that followed. It covers the election results of 1932 and who voted Nazi; the political intrigue that brought Hitler to power; the Reichstag fire, the decree that followed and the election of March 1933; the Enabling Act; and the coordination of German society into a one-party state by August 1934. The anchor example is the vote on the Enabling Act in the Kroll Opera House on 23 March 1933, carried by 444 votes to 94 while armed SA and SS men lined the hall. The governing idea is that the Nazis used the forms of law and the weaknesses of the constitution, together with violence and intimidation, to destroy democracy from within.

## Subtopic: The Elections of 1932

The year 1932 was the worst of the Depression in Germany, with about six million unemployed, and it brought five national votes. In the presidential election in March and April, the 84-year-old Paul von Hindenburg was re-elected with 53 percent in the second round, but Hitler came second with 36,8 percent, showing how far the Nazis had grown. In May, Hindenburg dismissed Chancellor Brüning and appointed the conservative aristocrat Franz von Papen, who had little support in the Reichstag and called an election.

In the Reichstag election of 31 July 1932 the Nazis won 37,3 percent of the vote and 230 of 608 seats. They were now by far the largest party, but they did not have a majority. Hitler demanded to be made chancellor, and Hindenburg, who distrusted him and called him the Bohemian corporal, refused. The Reichstag was dissolved again, and in the election of 6 November 1932 the Nazi vote fell to 33,1 percent and 196 seats, losing two million votes, while the Communists rose to 16,9 percent and 100 seats. The Nazi party was short of money, and some observers believed its peak had passed.

Historians have studied who voted Nazi. Support was strongest among Protestants rather than Catholics, in rural areas and small towns of the north and east, among farmers, shopkeepers, craftsmen, civil servants, white-collar workers and pensioners, and among young voters and people who had not voted before. It was weaker among Catholics, who largely stayed with the Centre Party, and among industrial workers organised in trade unions, who stayed with the Social Democrats or turned to the Communists, though many unemployed and unorganised workers did vote Nazi. Fear of communism was a powerful motive for middle-class voters, and Nazi propaganda presented the party as a movement of the whole nation rather than of one class.

It is important to state the limit of this support: at no free election did a majority of Germans vote for the Nazis. Their highest free result was 37,3 percent in July 1932.

The questions for this section are with you now: the presidential election, the results of July and November 1932, who voted Nazi, and the limit of Nazi support.

## Subtopic: Hitler Becomes Chancellor, 30 January 1933

Hitler came to power not through an election victory but through a political deal among conservative politicians around President Hindenburg. Papen was replaced as chancellor in December 1932 by General Kurt von Schleicher, who also failed to win Reichstag support. Papen, resentful, negotiated with Hitler in January 1933. He and other conservatives, together with some business and landowning interests, persuaded Hindenburg to appoint Hitler as chancellor at the head of a coalition, with Papen as vice-chancellor and conservatives holding most cabinet posts.

On 30 January 1933 Hindenburg appointed Hitler chancellor. Only three of the cabinet's members were Nazis: Hitler, Wilhelm Frick as minister of the interior, and Hermann Göring as minister without portfolio and Prussian interior minister, which gave him control of the police in Prussia, the largest German state. Papen is reported to have boasted that they had hired Hitler and that within two months they would have pushed him into a corner. The conservatives believed they could use Hitler's mass support to give their government a majority while controlling him from within. It was one of the most disastrous miscalculations in history.

That night the SA celebrated with torchlight parades through Berlin. Hitler immediately called a new Reichstag election for 5 March, hoping to win a majority, and used the power of government to campaign: Göring enrolled SA and SS men as auxiliary police in Prussia, giving Nazi thugs the authority of the state, and opposition meetings were broken up and newspapers banned.

In reading sources on this period, note who benefited from the claim that Hitler was swept to power by the people. Nazi propaganda called 30 January the seizure of power, but the evidence shows an appointment arranged by a small group of conservatives who did not represent a democratic majority.

This section's questions are ready: Papen, Schleicher and the deal, the three Nazis in the cabinet, the conservatives' miscalculation, and the seizure of power myth.

## Subtopic: The Reichstag Fire and the Enabling Act

On the night of 27 February 1933 the Reichstag building in Berlin was set on fire. A young Dutch communist, Marinus van der Lubbe, was arrested at the scene. Historians still debate whether he acted alone, as most now accept, or whether the Nazis were involved, but there is no doubt about how the Nazis used the fire. Hitler claimed it was the start of a communist uprising, and the next day Hindenburg signed the Reichstag Fire Decree, under Article 48, which suspended the constitution's guarantees of personal liberty, free speech, a free press, the right of assembly and the privacy of letters and telephone calls. Police could now arrest people without charge. Thousands of Communists and Social Democrats were seized, and the first concentration camp, at Dachau near Munich, opened in March 1933 for political prisoners.

The election of 5 March 1933 took place in this atmosphere of terror. The Nazis won 43,9 percent of the vote and 288 of 647 seats: still not a majority on their own, but with their allies in the German National People's Party, who won about 8 percent, they had a majority of seats.

Hitler wanted more: the power to make laws without the Reichstag at all. The Enabling Act, the Law to Remedy the Distress of People and Reich, allowed the government to pass laws, including laws that departed from the constitution, without the approval of the Reichstag or the president, for four years. Because it changed the constitution it needed a two-thirds majority. The 81 Communist deputies had been arrested or had fled and were simply not counted. The Catholic Centre Party was persuaded to vote in favour by promises to protect the Church. On 23 March 1933, in the Kroll Opera House, with SA and SS men lining the walls and crowds outside chanting threats, the act passed by 444 votes to 94. Only the Social Democrats voted against; their leader Otto Wels told the chamber that they could take their lives and their freedom but not their honour.

The Enabling Act gave the dictatorship a legal basis. From now on Hitler's cabinet could make any law it wished, and the Reichstag met only to hear his speeches.

Take this section's questions now: the Reichstag fire and its decree, the March 1933 election, the terms of the Enabling Act, and the vote of 23 March.

## Subtopic: Building the Dictatorship and the Error Museum

Using the Enabling Act, the Nazis brought every part of German life under their control, a process they called Gleichschaltung, coordination. The elected governments of the German states were replaced by Nazi governors. On 2 May 1933 the trade unions were abolished, their offices occupied and their leaders arrested, and workers were placed in the Nazi-controlled German Labour Front. The Communist and Social Democratic parties were banned, the other parties dissolved themselves under pressure, and on 14 July 1933 a law declared the Nazi Party the only legal party in Germany. Germany was now a one-party state. Jewish civil servants were dismissed from April 1933, books by Jewish and opposition authors were burned in public in May, and the Gestapo, the secret state police, was created to hunt opponents.

One threat to Hitler remained inside his own movement. The SA, now more than three million men under Ernst Röhm, wanted to replace the regular army and demanded a second revolution. The army leaders, whose support Hitler needed, feared the SA. On the night of 30 June 1934, the Night of the Long Knives, the SS murdered Röhm and other SA leaders, together with former chancellor Schleicher and other opponents, at least 85 people and probably more. Hitler claimed he had prevented a plot, and the army welcomed it.

On 2 August 1934 President Hindenburg died. Hitler combined the offices of chancellor and president into one, with the title Führer, leader. The same day, soldiers of the army swore an oath of personal loyalty not to the constitution but to Hitler himself. A referendum on 19 August, held without free debate, approved the change by about 90 percent. In eighteen months, a democracy had become a dictatorship.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: most Germans voted Nazi in free elections; the highest free result was 37,3 percent. Two: Hitler seized power by force in 1933; he was legally appointed chancellor by President Hindenburg after a deal with conservatives. Three: the Enabling Act was passed by a free and fair Reichstag; Communist deputies were excluded and the vote was taken under intimidation. Four: the Reichstag fire decree was passed after Hitler became Führer; it came in February 1933, days after he became chancellor. Five: the Night of the Long Knives targeted Jews; it targeted SA leaders and political rivals.

The questions for this section are ready: coordination, the one-party state, the Night of the Long Knives, Hitler as Führer, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same eighteen months again, through a scoreboard of votes, a back door into power, and a parliament that voted to switch itself off — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Scoreboard of Votes

Think of the elections as a scoreboard. In 1928 the Nazis had 2,6 percent. In 1930, with the Depression biting, 18,3 percent. In July 1932, with about six million Germans out of work, 37,3 percent: more than a third of all voters, and the biggest party in parliament. But not more than half. In November 1932 they actually dropped, to 33,1 percent. Some people thought the Nazis had peaked.

Who was voting for them? Farmers worried about losing their land. Shopkeepers squeezed by big stores and the Depression. Office workers and civil servants scared of falling into poverty. Lots of young people voting for the first time. And many middle-class people terrified that the Communists, who were growing too, would start a revolution like Russia's. Many Catholic voters stayed with their own Catholic party, and many workers in trade unions stayed with the Social Democrats or the Communists.

That same year, Hitler ran for president against the old war hero Paul von Hindenburg, who was 84. Hindenburg won with 53 percent, but Hitler got almost 37 percent.

Here is the number to remember: in a free election, the Nazis never won more than 37,3 percent. Most Germans never voted for them. So how did they end up running the country? Not through the front door. Through the back.

Keep the scoreboard in mind and try the questions for this part: 37,3 percent in July 1932, who voted Nazi, and why that was never a majority.

## Subtopic: The Back Door into Power

After the November 1932 election, a few powerful conservative men around President Hindenburg made a deal. Their own governments kept failing because nobody in parliament supported them. Hitler had millions of voters. So they thought: let us make Hitler chancellor, surround him with our own people, and use his voters while we control him. One of them, Franz von Papen, is reported to have bragged that within two months they would have Hitler squeezed into a corner.

On 30 January 1933 Hindenburg made Hitler chancellor. Only three Nazis were in the cabinet. The conservatives thought they had tamed him. They were completely wrong.

Within a month, on the night of 27 February, the parliament building, the Reichstag, burned. A young Dutch communist was caught there. Hitler shouted that the communists were starting a revolution, and the next day the president signed an emergency decree that cancelled people's basic rights: free speech, free newspapers, the right to meet, and protection from arrest without charge. Thousands of opponents were thrown into prison and into the first concentration camp, at Dachau.

Then came another election, in March 1933, with Nazi thugs now working as police. Even then, the Nazis got 43,9 percent. Still not half.

Hold the back door in mind and try this part's questions: the conservatives' deal, 30 January 1933, the fire and the decree.

## Subtopic: A Parliament That Switched Itself Off

Hitler wanted to make laws without parliament at all. To get that, he needed parliament to vote for it, by a two-thirds majority. So on 23 March 1933 the members met in an opera house, because the Reichstag building had burned. Brownshirts and SS men lined the walls, staring at them. The Communist members were already arrested or in hiding and were not counted. The Catholic party was promised protection for its church. The vote: 444 for, 94 against. Only the Social Democrats voted no, and their leader said: you can take our lives and our freedom, but not our honour. That law was the Enabling Act. Parliament had voted to switch itself off.

What came next happened fast. In May 1933 trade unions were shut down. By July, every party except the Nazis was gone. Jewish civil servants lost their jobs. Books were burned. In June 1934 Hitler had his own SA leaders murdered in the Night of the Long Knives, because the army feared them. In August 1934 Hindenburg died, and Hitler made himself Führer, leader, of everything, with soldiers swearing loyalty to him personally.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Nazis' best free result was 37,3 percent, never a majority. A deal among conservatives made Hitler chancellor on 30 January 1933. The Reichstag fire gave him an excuse to cancel people's rights. The Enabling Act let him make laws alone. By August 1934 Germany was a one-party dictatorship with Hitler as Führer. In Grade 11 History you will study how this dictatorship ruled.

Why does this matter to us? Because it shows that democracy is not only about voting once. It needs courts that protect rights, a free press, opposition parties that are allowed to exist, and citizens who notice when those things are being taken away step by step.

Hold the opera house vote in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: the Enabling Act, the one-party state and the Führer.
