# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the end of World War I for Germany, the founding of the Weimar Republic and the terms and consequences of the Treaty of Versailles of 1919. It covers the revolution of November 1918 and the Weimar constitution; the peace conference and the treaty's punishments of Germany under the headings of land, army, money and blame; the reaction of Germans and the weaknesses of the new republic; and the crisis of 1923 and the recovery that followed. The anchor example is the signing of the treaty in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles on 28 June 1919, five years to the day after the assassination at Sarajevo. The governing idea is that the peace was harsh enough to humiliate Germany but not strong enough to keep it weak, and that the new democracy was blamed for a defeat and a treaty it had not caused.

## Subtopic: Germany in Defeat and the Birth of the Weimar Republic

By the autumn of 1918 Germany's army was retreating, its allies had collapsed and its people were hungry because of the British naval blockade. Sailors at Kiel mutinied, workers' and soldiers' councils formed in many cities, and on 9 November 1918 Kaiser Wilhelm II abdicated and fled to the Netherlands. A republic was proclaimed in Berlin, and two days later its representatives signed the armistice. The army's leaders, who had told the Kaiser that the war was lost, were not the ones who signed, a fact that would matter later.

Elections for a National Assembly were held in January 1919, and because Berlin was in turmoil the Assembly met in the quiet town of Weimar, which gave the new state its name. The Weimar constitution, adopted in August 1919, made Germany one of the most democratic states in the world. All men and women over 20 could vote. The Reichstag, the parliament, was elected by proportional representation, so a party winning 10 percent of the votes won about 10 percent of the seats. The chancellor, the head of government, needed the support of the Reichstag. A president, elected every seven years, was head of state; the first was the Social Democrat Friedrich Ebert. The constitution guaranteed freedom of speech, of the press and of religion.

Two features later proved dangerous. Proportional representation produced many small parties, so no single party ever won a majority and every government was a coalition that could collapse when partners quarrelled; Germany had about twenty governments between 1919 and 1933. Article 48 allowed the president, in an emergency, to rule by decree without the Reichstag, a power intended for crises that could be used to bypass democracy.

The republic faced violence from both sides from the start. In January 1919 the communist Spartacist uprising in Berlin was crushed by government troops and the Freikorps, armed groups of former soldiers, and its leaders Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht were murdered. In March 1920 right-wing nationalists in the Kapp Putsch seized Berlin, and the government survived only because workers called a general strike.

The questions for this section are with you now: the abdication, the Weimar constitution, proportional representation and Article 48, and the uprisings of 1919 and 1920.

## Subtopic: The Treaty of Versailles: Land, Army, Money and Blame

The peace conference opened in Paris in January 1919. The decisions were dominated by the Big Three: President Woodrow Wilson of the United States, who wanted a fair peace based on his Fourteen Points and a League of Nations; Prime Minister David Lloyd George of Britain, who wanted Germany punished but not destroyed, as it was an important trading partner; and Prime Minister Georges Clemenceau of France, whose country had been invaded twice in fifty years and who wanted Germany too weak ever to attack again. Germany was not allowed to negotiate. It was handed the terms and told to sign or face renewed war, so Germans called the treaty a Diktat, a dictated peace. It was signed in the Hall of Mirrors at the Palace of Versailles on 28 June 1919.

The terms can be grouped under four headings. Land: Germany lost about 13 percent of its European territory and about 10 percent of its population. Alsace-Lorraine went to France; the coal-rich Saar was placed under the League of Nations for 15 years with its coal going to France; West Prussia and Posen went to the new Poland, creating the Polish Corridor that cut East Prussia off from the rest of Germany, and the port of Danzig became a free city. Smaller areas went to Belgium and Denmark, and the Baltic port of Memel was taken under Allied control. Union with Austria was forbidden. All Germany's colonies were taken and given to the victors as mandates under the League of Nations. German South West Africa became a mandate administered by the Union of South Africa, whose minister Jan Smuts had helped design the mandate system.

Army: the army was limited to 100 000 volunteers with no conscription, no tanks and no military aircraft; the navy was limited to six battleships and no submarines; and the Rhineland, the German territory bordering France, was demilitarised, with Allied troops occupying it for up to 15 years. Money: Germany had to pay reparations, compensation for war damage, fixed in 1921 at 132 billion gold marks. Blame: Article 231, the War Guilt Clause, made Germany accept responsibility for causing all the loss and damage of the war, which was the legal basis for reparations.

This section's questions are ready: the Big Three and their aims, the Diktat, the four headings of punishment and South West Africa as a mandate.

## Subtopic: German Reactions and the Weaknesses of the Republic

Germans of almost every political view regarded the treaty as unjust. Many had been told throughout the war that Germany was winning, no foreign army stood on German soil when the fighting stopped, and the defeat came as a shock. The War Guilt Clause was felt as a national humiliation, the loss of land left millions of German speakers under foreign rule, and the reparations were seen as an attempt to keep Germany poor for generations. Wilson's promise of self-determination, the right of peoples to choose their own government, seemed to apply to everyone except Germans.

Out of this anger grew the stab-in-the-back myth. Nationalists and former army leaders, including General Paul von Hindenburg, claimed that the army had not been defeated on the battlefield but betrayed at home by socialists, Jews and the politicians who signed the armistice and the treaty. They called those politicians the November Criminals. The myth was false: the army command itself had told the government in September 1918 that the war could not be won and that an armistice was needed. But it was widely believed, and it attached the shame of defeat to the democratic republic and its supporters.

The republic was therefore weak from birth. It was blamed for the treaty; it relied on judges, civil servants and army officers who had served the Kaiser and often despised democracy; it was attacked by communists who wanted a revolution like Russia's and by nationalists who wanted the Kaiser's empire back; and its coalition governments were unstable. Courts punished left-wing rebels harshly and right-wing rebels lightly; political murders by right-wing groups numbered in the hundreds in the early 1920s, among them the foreign minister Walther Rathenau, who was Jewish, in 1922.

Historians still debate whether the treaty was too harsh. Some argue that it was no harsher than the treaty Germany itself imposed on Russia at Brest-Litovsk in 1918, and that Germany could have paid; others argue that it humiliated Germany without weakening it permanently, the worst combination. Both positions can be defended with evidence.

Take this section's questions now: why Germans rejected the treaty, the stab-in-the-back myth, the November Criminals, and the weaknesses of the republic.

## Subtopic: The Crisis of 1923, Recovery and the Error Museum

In January 1923, after Germany fell behind with its reparations payments, French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr, Germany's main industrial region, to take coal and goods directly. The German government called on workers to resist passively by going on strike, and paid their wages by printing money. The result was hyperinflation, an extremely rapid rise in prices. The mark, which had been about 4 to the American dollar in 1914, fell to about 4,2 trillion to the dollar by November 1923. Workers were paid twice a day and rushed to spend their wages before prices rose again; a loaf of bread cost about 200 billion marks. People who had saved all their lives, pensioners and the middle class, saw their savings wiped out, and many never forgave the republic. In November 1923 Adolf Hitler attempted to seize power in Munich, the Beer Hall Putsch, which is part of the next lesson.

Recovery came under Gustav Stresemann, chancellor in 1923 and then foreign minister until 1929. He ended passive resistance, introduced a new currency, the Rentenmark, which stopped the inflation, and agreed to the Dawes Plan of 1924, under which reparations payments were reduced and spread out and American banks lent money to Germany. The French left the Ruhr. Under the Locarno Treaties of 1925 Germany accepted its western borders, and in 1926 it joined the League of Nations. From 1924 to 1929 Germany enjoyed its golden years of relative prosperity and cultural creativity. But the recovery rested on American loans, and when the American economy collapsed in 1929 those loans were recalled.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: the Weimar Republic was named after its first president; it was named after the town where the Assembly met. Two: Germany negotiated the treaty; it was excluded and given the terms to sign. Three: the army was defeated by politicians at home; the army command itself admitted defeat, and the stab in the back was a myth. Four: the treaty abolished the German army; it limited it to 100 000 men. Five: hyperinflation was caused only by reparations; the immediate cause was the government printing money to pay striking workers during the Ruhr occupation.

The questions for this section are ready: the Ruhr occupation, hyperinflation, Stresemann's recovery, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same story again, through a losing team handed a list of penalties, the four letters of a lamb, and a wheelbarrow full of money — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Losing Team Gets a New Captain

Imagine a soccer team that has lost a huge, bitter final. Its old captain, the Kaiser, runs away to another country. The players are hungry and exhausted. A new captain takes over and tries to run the team in a completely new way: everyone gets a vote, women too, and the players choose who leads them. That new way of running Germany was called the Weimar Republic, named after the small town of Weimar where its leaders first met, because the capital, Berlin, was too dangerous at the time.

It was a real democracy. Everyone over 20 could vote. Parliament, the Reichstag, got seats in proportion to votes, so a party with a tenth of the votes got about a tenth of the seats. Sounds fair, and it was, but it meant there were lots of small parties and nobody ever had a majority, so governments kept falling apart. There was also an emergency rule, Article 48, that let the president make laws on his own in a crisis. Remember that one; someone will misuse it later.

And from day one, people tried to knock the new captain over. Communists tried to take over Berlin in 1919, wanting a revolution like Russia's. Nationalists tried in 1920, wanting the old empire back. The republic survived both, just.

The worst part: the new captain was the one who had to sign for the defeat, so many Germans blamed him for losing, even though he had not played in the final.

Keep the losing team in mind and try the questions for this part: why the name Weimar, everyone gets a vote, and the dangerous emergency rule.

## Subtopic: Four Letters: L A M B

The winners met in Paris in 1919 to decide Germany's punishment. Germany was not allowed into the room. It was handed the list and told: sign, or the war starts again. On 28 June 1919, in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, Germany signed. That is the Treaty of Versailles.

Remember the punishments with one word: LAMB. L is for land: Germany lost about 13 percent of its land in Europe and about a tenth of its people. Alsace-Lorraine went to France, a strip called the Polish Corridor went to Poland, cutting Germany in two, and every one of its colonies was taken away. Here is the South African part: German South West Africa, today Namibia, was given to South Africa to run. A is for army: only 100 000 soldiers, no tanks, no air force, no submarines, and no soldiers at all in the Rhineland, the strip of Germany next to France. M is for money: reparations, payments for war damage, eventually set at 132 billion gold marks. B is for blame: Article 231, the War Guilt Clause, made Germany accept the blame for the whole war.

Germans were furious. Many had been told they were winning, no enemy soldier stood in Germany when the fighting stopped, and now they were losing land and money and taking all the blame. So a story spread: the army had not really lost; it had been stabbed in the back by politicians, socialists and Jews at home. It was a lie. The army's own generals had admitted the war was lost. But many people believed it.

Hold LAMB in mind and try this part's questions: land, army, money, blame, and the stab-in-the-back lie.

## Subtopic: A Wheelbarrow Full of Money

In 1923 Germany fell behind on its payments. French and Belgian soldiers marched into the Ruhr, Germany's industrial heartland, to take coal instead. The German government told the workers to go on strike and paid them by printing more and more money. When you print money without making more goods, each note buys less. Prices went crazy. By November 1923 a loaf of bread cost about 200 billion marks. People were paid twice a day and ran to the shops before prices rose again. Some carried their wages in wheelbarrows. A grandmother who had saved all her life could now buy one stamp with her savings. That is hyperinflation.

Then a capable leader, Gustav Stresemann, brought in a new currency, made a deal to pay less each year, and borrowed money from America. From 1924 to 1929 Germany recovered: jobs, shops, cinemas and music. But it was all built on American loans. If America ever got into trouble, Germany would fall with it. In 1929, it did.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Germany lost the war, the Kaiser ran away, and the Weimar Republic, a real democracy with some weak spots, took over. The Treaty of Versailles punished Germany: land, army, money, blame. Germans felt humiliated, and many believed the stab-in-the-back lie. In 1923 money became worthless. Stresemann rescued the economy with American loans. In Grade 11 History you will see how all this anger was used by the Nazis.

Hold the wheelbarrow in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: the Ruhr, hyperinflation, and the recovery that depended on America.
