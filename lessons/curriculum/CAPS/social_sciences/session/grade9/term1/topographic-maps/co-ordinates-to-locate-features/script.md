# Part 1 — Expert

This session teaches the use of geographical co-ordinates to locate features on topographic maps. It reviews latitude and longitude and the units of degrees, minutes and seconds; explains how the frame of a 1:50 000 map is marked; sets out the method for reading the co-ordinates of a feature to the nearest second, with the direction of measurement that differs between latitude south and longitude east; and works in reverse, from given co-ordinates to a feature. The anchor example is a trigonometrical station whose position is found as 25 degrees 45 minutes 24 seconds south, 28 degrees 12 minutes 36 seconds east. The governing idea is that a co-ordinate is a precise, unique address, and that every step in finding it is a measurement that can be checked.

## Subtopic: The Global Grid of Latitude and Longitude

The earth is covered by an imaginary grid of lines used to describe position. Lines of latitude, also called parallels, run east to west around the earth, parallel to the equator. The equator is latitude 0 degrees, and latitude is measured north or south of it up to 90 degrees at each pole. Lines of longitude, also called meridians, run from the North Pole to the South Pole. The prime meridian, which passes through Greenwich in London, is longitude 0 degrees, and longitude is measured east or west of it up to 180 degrees.

Latitude and longitude are angles, measured from the centre of the earth, so they are given in degrees. Each degree is divided into 60 minutes, and each minute into 60 seconds. The symbols are a small circle for degrees, a single stroke for minutes and a double stroke for seconds, but in speech and in many answers they are written out. One degree of latitude is about 111 kilometres on the ground, one minute about 1 852 metres, and one second about 31 metres. A degree of longitude is also about 111 kilometres at the equator but shrinks towards the poles, because the meridians converge; at 26 degrees south it is about 100 kilometres.

South Africa lies entirely south of the equator and east of the prime meridian, roughly between 22 and 35 degrees south and between 16 and 33 degrees east. Every co-ordinate in the country is therefore written with S for latitude and E for longitude. Latitude is always given first, then longitude, and both are needed: one number alone describes a whole line, not a point.

A full co-ordinate looks like this: 25 degrees 45 minutes 24 seconds south, 28 degrees 12 minutes 36 seconds east. Each part has a unit and the two hemispheres are named.

The questions for this section are with you now: parallels and meridians, the equator and prime meridian, the sixty-minute and sixty-second divisions, and South Africa's hemispheres.

## Subtopic: Reading Degrees and Minutes from the Map Frame

A 1:50 000 topographic sheet covers 15 minutes of latitude by 15 minutes of longitude. Its four corners are labelled with their full co-ordinates in degrees and minutes. Along the frame, the margin is marked at every minute with a short tick, and the minute values are printed at intervals. Lines may be drawn across the map joining matching ticks, dividing it into a grid of one-minute blocks; where no lines are drawn, you draw them lightly in pencil by joining the ticks on opposite sides.

Each one-minute block on the 1:50 000 map is about 3,7 centimetres from north to south, because 1 minute of latitude is about 1 852 metres. Its width from west to east is a little less, about 3,3 centimetres in Gauteng, because the meridians converge. That is why the blocks are slightly taller than they are wide.

To read the degrees and minutes of a feature, find the minute block it lies in. For latitude, read the minute lines to the north and south of the feature: if it lies between 25 degrees 45 minutes and 25 degrees 46 minutes, its latitude is 25 degrees 45 minutes and some seconds. For longitude, read the minute lines to the west and east: between 28 degrees 12 minutes and 28 degrees 13 minutes gives 28 degrees 12 minutes and some seconds.

The direction of increase matters. In the southern hemisphere latitude increases towards the south, so on the map the minute values get larger downward: 25 degrees 46 minutes is south of 25 degrees 45 minutes. In the eastern hemisphere longitude increases towards the east, so values get larger to the right. A learner who forgets this reads the wrong minute line as the starting line.

This section's questions are ready: the size of a sheet in minutes, the size of a minute block in centimetres, and the direction in which latitude and longitude increase.

## Subtopic: Calculating Seconds and Writing the Co-ordinate

Seconds are found by measuring how far the feature lies into its minute block and expressing that as a fraction of 60 seconds. The formula is: seconds equal the distance from the starting minute line to the feature, divided by the length of the whole minute block, multiplied by 60. The starting line is the minute line with the smaller value: for latitude south, the line to the north of the feature; for longitude east, the line to the west.

Work the anchor example. The trigonometrical station lies between 25 degrees 45 minutes and 25 degrees 46 minutes south. The block measures 3,7 centimetres from north to south, and the station is 1,5 centimetres south of the 25 degrees 45 minutes line. Seconds equal 1,5 divided by 3,7 times 60, which is 24,3, so 24 seconds. Latitude: 25 degrees 45 minutes 24 seconds south. For longitude, the station lies between 28 degrees 12 minutes and 28 degrees 13 minutes east. The block measures 3,3 centimetres from west to east, and the station is 2,0 centimetres east of the 28 degrees 12 minutes line. Seconds equal 2,0 divided by 3,3 times 60, which is 36,4, so 36 seconds. Longitude: 28 degrees 12 minutes 36 seconds east.

Always measure the block yourself rather than using a remembered figure, because the width of longitude blocks varies with latitude and exam extracts may be enlarged or reduced. Measure from the centre of the symbol, and round seconds to the nearest whole number. Because one second of latitude is only about 31 metres, an error of a millimetre on the map changes the answer by about 1 or 2 seconds, so memoranda accept a small range.

Set out the answer in the exam layout: the formula, the substitution for latitude, the latitude with S, the substitution for longitude, the longitude with E. Without the hemisphere letters the co-ordinate is incomplete, because 25 degrees 45 minutes north is a place in India.

Take this section's questions now: the seconds formula, the starting line for latitude south and longitude east, and the full written co-ordinate.

## Subtopic: Working in Reverse and the Error Museum

The reverse question gives a co-ordinate and asks which feature is found there. Read the degrees and minutes to find the correct block. Then convert the seconds into a distance: distance equals seconds divided by 60, multiplied by the length of the block. For a latitude of 25 degrees 45 minutes 45 seconds south, measure 45 divided by 60 times 3,7, which is 2,8 centimetres, south of the 25 degrees 45 minutes line, and draw a light horizontal line. For a longitude of 28 degrees 12 minutes 16 seconds east, measure 16 divided by 60 times 3,3, which is 0,9 centimetres, east of the 28 degrees 12 minutes line, and draw a light vertical line. The feature at the intersection is the answer, perhaps a dam wall or a school.

Co-ordinates also give quick estimates without measuring. If a feature lies almost exactly halfway across a minute block, it is at about 30 seconds; a quarter of the way is about 15 seconds; three quarters about 45 seconds. Such estimates are a useful check on a calculated answer: a feature visibly near the top of its block cannot have 50 seconds of latitude in the southern hemisphere.

Co-ordinates have wide uses beyond the exam. Every GPS receiver in a cellphone gives position as latitude and longitude, emergency services use them to find accident scenes, and surveyors, farmers and the defence force use them to record boundaries and positions. Learning to read them from a paper map builds the understanding behind every digital map.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: longitude is written first; latitude is always first. Two: latitude south increases northwards on the map; it increases southwards, downward on the sheet. Three: seconds are the distance in centimetres; they are the fraction of the block multiplied by 60. Four: the hemisphere letters can be left out in South Africa; S and E are part of every answer. Five: every minute block is the same width; longitude blocks narrow towards the poles and must be measured.

The questions for this section are ready: finding a feature from co-ordinates, estimating seconds, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same address system again, through the seat number on a stadium ticket, a ruler on the map's edge, and a friend's location pin on your phone — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Seat Number for Every Place on Earth

When you go to a match at the stadium, your ticket says something like row 25, seat 28. With two numbers, anyone can find your exact seat. The whole earth works the same way. It has imaginary rows and seats drawn over it, and every place has two numbers.

The rows are lines of latitude. They run around the earth from side to side, like the rings on a barrel. The middle one, around the fat middle of the earth, is the equator, number 0. They count up going north or going south, all the way to 90 at the poles. The seats are lines of longitude. They run from the top of the earth to the bottom, like the segments of an orange. Seat 0 runs through a place called Greenwich in London, and they count up going east or going west, to 180.

South Africa is south of the equator and east of Greenwich. So every South African address says S for south and E for east. We sit roughly between 22 and 35 south, and between 16 and 33 east.

One degree is a big jump, about 111 kilometres. So degrees are cut into 60 smaller pieces called minutes, and each minute into 60 tiny pieces called seconds, just like an hour on a clock. One minute of latitude is about 1,85 kilometres; one second is about 31 metres, about the length of a classroom block.

Always say the row first, then the seat: latitude first, then longitude.

Keep the stadium ticket in mind and try the questions for this part: rows and seats, the equator and Greenwich, and 60 minutes in a degree.

## Subtopic: A Ruler on the Map's Edge

Now find a real address on your map. Look at the edges of the topographic map: there are little tick marks all around it, one for every minute. Join the ticks with light pencil lines and the map is cut into boxes, each box one minute tall and one minute wide. On the 1:50 000 map each box is about 3,7 centimetres tall.

Say the trig station sits inside the box between 25 degrees 45 minutes and 25 degrees 46 minutes south. Here is the trap: in the south, numbers get bigger as you go down the map. So the top line of the box is the smaller one, 45 minutes, and that is where you start measuring.

Put your ruler on the top line and measure straight down to the trig station: 1,5 centimetres. The whole box is 3,7 centimetres tall. So the station is 1,5 out of 3,7 of the way down the box. A minute has 60 seconds, so: 1,5 divided by 3,7, times 60, is about 24. The latitude is 25 degrees 45 minutes 24 seconds south.

Now the other direction. The box runs from 28 degrees 12 minutes to 28 degrees 13 minutes east. East numbers get bigger to the right, so start at the left line, 12 minutes. Measure across to the station: 2,0 centimetres. The box is 3,3 centimetres wide. 2,0 divided by 3,3, times 60, is about 36. The longitude is 28 degrees 12 minutes 36 seconds east.

Hold the ruler in mind and try this part's questions: start at the top for south, start at the left for east, and part of the box times 60.

## Subtopic: The Location Pin on Your Phone

When a friend shares a location pin on WhatsApp, your phone shows two numbers behind it: latitude and longitude. Your phone got them from satellites. On the paper map, you are doing the same thing by hand.

And you can do it backwards. Someone gives you the address 25 degrees 45 minutes 45 seconds south, 28 degrees 12 minutes 16 seconds east, and asks what is there. Find the box: between 45 and 46 minutes south, between 12 and 13 minutes east. Now, 45 seconds out of 60 is three quarters of the way down the box. Three quarters of 3,7 centimetres is about 2,8 centimetres, so draw a light line 2,8 centimetres down from the top. 16 seconds out of 60 is about a quarter of the way across, so about 0,9 centimetres from the left line. Where the two pencil lines cross, that is your feature, maybe a dam wall or a school.

Why bother, when the phone does it for you? Because phones run out of battery, lose signal in the mountains and sometimes put the pin in the wrong place. Hikers, farmers and rescue teams still carry paper maps for exactly that reason, and the person who can read the numbers off the edge of the map is the one who finds the way home.

Quick check trick: halfway across a box is 30 seconds, a quarter is 15, three quarters is 45. If your answer says 50 seconds but the feature is near the top of the box, you went the wrong way.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Every place has two numbers: latitude, the rows, first; longitude, the seats, second. South Africa is S and E. A degree has 60 minutes, a minute has 60 seconds. Read degrees and minutes from the edge of the map. For seconds: measure into the box, divide by the box, times 60. Start at the top line for south, the left line for east. In Grade 10 Geography you will find co-ordinates on almost every map you open.

Hold the location pin in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: working backwards, the quarter and half checks, and S and E every time.
