# Part 1 — Expert

This session teaches scale and the measurement of distance on topographic and orthophoto maps. It covers the definition of scale; the word, ratio and line scales and conversion between them; large and small scales; measuring straight-line distance with a ruler and converting it with the ratio scale; measuring curved distance with a paper strip or string and reading it on the line scale; and using distance to calculate travel time. The anchor example is a learner's journey along a winding road between a village and a town on a 1:50 000 map, measured both as the crow flies and along the road. The governing idea is that every map distance is meaningless until it has been multiplied by the scale and written with a unit.

## Subtopic: Three Ways to Write a Scale

Scale is the relationship between a distance on a map and the corresponding distance on the ground. Because the earth cannot be drawn full size, every map reduces it by a fixed amount, and the scale states that amount. It can be written in three ways.

A word scale, also called a verbal or statement scale, states the relationship in words: one centimetre represents half a kilometre, or one centimetre represents 500 metres. It is easy to understand but tied to particular units.

A ratio scale, also called a numerical or representative fraction scale, states it as a ratio with no units: 1:50 000. It means that one unit of any kind on the map represents 50 000 of the same units on the ground: 1 centimetre represents 50 000 centimetres, 1 millimetre represents 50 000 millimetres. Because it has no units it can be used in any system of measurement, which is why it is printed on every topographic sheet. The 1:10 000 orthophoto means 1 unit represents 10 000 of the same units.

A line scale, also called a graphic or linear scale, is a ruled line divided into equal sections, each labelled with the ground distance it represents, usually in kilometres on the topographic map and in metres on the orthophoto. On the 1:50 000 sheet, each 2 centimetres of the line is labelled 1 kilometre. To the left of the zero, one section is usually subdivided into smaller units, such as 100 metres, for accurate reading of part-kilometres.

Converting between them uses the metric relationships: 100 centimetres in a metre, 1 000 metres in a kilometre, so 100 000 centimetres in a kilometre. For 1:50 000: 50 000 centimetres divided by 100 is 500 metres, and 500 metres divided by 1 000 is 0,5 kilometres, so the word scale is one centimetre represents 0,5 kilometres. For 1:10 000: 10 000 centimetres is 100 metres, or 0,1 kilometres.

Conversions can also run from a word or line scale to a ratio, and Grade 9 questions set them. If a word scale says that 1 centimetre represents 2 kilometres, express the ground distance in centimetres: 2 kilometres is 2 times 100 000, which is 200 000 centimetres, so the ratio is 1:200 000. If a line scale shows 4 centimetres for 1 kilometre, then 1 centimetre is a quarter of a kilometre, 25 000 centimetres, and the ratio is 1:25 000. The rule is always the same: both sides in the same unit, then simplify so that the map side is 1. Only then may the units be dropped, because a ratio compares like with like.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three types of scale, the meaning of 1:50 000, and conversion to a word scale.

## Subtopic: Large and Small Scale, and the Photocopier Problem

Scale is described as large or small by comparing the fractions. One ten-thousandth is larger than one fifty-thousandth, so 1:10 000 is a larger scale than 1:50 000. A large-scale map shows a small area in great detail; a small-scale map shows a large area in less detail. In order from largest to smallest scale: a building plan at 1:100, the orthophoto at 1:10 000, the topographic map at 1:50 000, a regional map at 1:250 000, and an atlas map of South Africa at about 1:10 000 000.

Each type of scale has an advantage. The ratio scale is universal and exact and is used for calculation. The word scale is the easiest for a reader to grasp. The line scale has a practical advantage the others lack: it remains correct when the map is enlarged or reduced. If a 1:50 000 map is photocopied at half size for an exam paper, every distance on the copy is halved, so 1 centimetre now represents 1 kilometre, and the printed ratio 1:50 000 is wrong for the copy; the true scale is 1:100 000. The line scale is printed on the map and shrinks with it, so it is still correct: the 1 kilometre section is now only 1 centimetre long, exactly matching the map. That is why exam questions on reduced extracts often instruct you to use the line scale, and why every South African topographic map carries one.

A related warning applies to screens: a map viewed on a phone or computer has no fixed size, so only a line scale, which zooms with the image, can be trusted there. Online maps show a small line scale in the corner for this reason.

This section's questions are ready: ordering scales, small and large scale, and why the line scale survives a photocopier.

## Subtopic: Measuring Straight and Curved Distances

Straight-line distance, also called distance as the crow flies, is the shortest distance between two points. Measure it with a ruler from the centre of one symbol to the centre of the other, in centimetres to one decimal place, then multiply by the scale. The calculation is set out in four lines. Formula: actual distance equals map distance times scale. Substitution: 6,3 centimetres times 50 000. Answer: 315 000 centimetres. Conversion: divided by 100 000 gives 3,15 kilometres. The shortcut on the 1:50 000 map is to multiply centimetres by 0,5 to get kilometres; on the 1:10 000 map, multiply centimetres by 100 to get metres, so 4,2 centimetres on the orthophoto is 420 metres.

Curved distance, along a road, railway, river or boundary, is longer than the straight line and must be measured along the feature. Place the straight edge of a sheet of paper at the starting point, along the first straight stretch of the road. Mark the paper and the map where the road begins to bend, pivot the paper with a pencil point at that mark so that the edge lies along the next stretch, mark again, and continue to the end point. The distance from the first mark to the last on the paper is the total length. Alternatively, lay a piece of string along the curves and then straighten it. Measure the paper or string with a ruler and convert with the ratio scale, or lay it directly against the line scale and read the kilometres.

In the anchor example the village and the town are 6,3 centimetres apart in a straight line, which is 3,15 kilometres. Along the winding road the paper strip measures 9,6 centimetres, which is 4,8 kilometres. The road is more than one and a half kilometres longer than the straight line because it follows a valley and swings around spurs, a direct link to the landforms of the previous lesson.

## Subtopic: Distance, Time and the Error Museum

Distance is often combined with speed to find time, or with time to find speed. The relationship is time equals distance divided by speed. A learner who walks the 4,8 kilometre road at 5 kilometres per hour takes 4,8 divided by 5, which is 0,96 hours; multiplied by 60 this is about 58 minutes. A taxi covering 6,2 kilometres at 80 kilometres per hour takes 6,2 divided by 80, which is 0,0775 hours, or about 4,7 minutes. Always convert the map distance to kilometres first, so that the units match the speed.

Distance questions can also run backwards: how long on the map is a road that is 3 kilometres on the ground? On the 1:50 000 map, 1 kilometre is 2 centimetres, so 3 kilometres is 6 centimetres. On the orthophoto, 1 kilometre is 10 centimetres, so 3 kilometres would need 30 centimetres, larger than the sheet, which shows why long distances are measured on the topographic map.

Precision matters. A measurement of 6,3 centimetres on the 1:50 000 map can be wrong by a millimetre in either direction, which is 50 metres on the ground, so answers to two decimal places of a kilometre are realistic, and more would be false precision. Measure from the centre of each symbol, not its edge, and read the ruler at eye level.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: 1 centimetre on a 1:50 000 map is 50 kilometres; it is 500 metres. Two: the ratio scale stays correct on a reduced photocopy; only the line scale does. Three: curved distance can be measured with a straight ruler from end to end; that gives the straight-line distance, which is shorter. Four: an answer of 3,15 without a unit earns full marks; the unit is part of the answer. Five: time equals speed divided by distance; it is distance divided by speed.

The questions for this section are ready: travel time, reverse conversions, sensible precision and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same scale work again, through a toy car next to a real one, a piece of string laid along a taxi route, and a walk timed on a cellphone — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Toy Car and the Real Car

Hold a toy car next to a real one. The toy is exactly the same shape, just much smaller. If the toy is 10 centimetres long and the real car is 4 metres, the toy is 40 times smaller. A map is the same idea: a perfect copy of the land, just shrunk. The scale tells you how many times smaller.

On the topographic map, the scale is written 1:50 000. Say it as: one on the map is fifty thousand on the ground. One centimetre on the map is 50 000 centimetres in real life. That is a big number of centimetres, so turn it into something you can feel. 50 000 centimetres is 500 metres, which is about a ten-minute walk at an easy pace. So one centimetre on the map is half a kilometre, and two centimetres is one kilometre. On the orthophoto, the scale is 1:10 000, so one centimetre is 10 000 centimetres, which is 100 metres, about the length of a soccer field.

The scale can be written three ways, and they all say the same thing. In words: one centimetre represents half a kilometre. As a ratio: 1:50 000. As a line: a little ruler printed on the map, with marks that say 0, 1, 2, 3 kilometres. Each kilometre on that line is 2 centimetres long.

Here is the clever part about the line. If someone photocopies the map smaller, everything on the map shrinks, including the little line ruler, so the line still matches. But the number 1:50 000 printed on the copy is now wrong. So if the map has been made smaller or bigger, trust the line, not the number.

Keep the toy car in mind and try the questions for this part: one centimetre for 500 metres, the three ways to write it, and trusting the line.

## Subtopic: String Along the Taxi Route

Now measure. If you want the distance as the crow flies, straight from your village to town, put a ruler from one to the other and read the centimetres. Say it is 6,3 centimetres. Each centimetre is half a kilometre, so 6,3 times 0,5 is 3,15 kilometres. Write it properly: 6,3 centimetres times 50 000 equals 315 000 centimetres, which is 3,15 kilometres.

But the taxi does not fly. It follows the road, which bends around hills and along the river. To measure the road, take a piece of string and lay it carefully along every bend of the road on the map, from the village to town. Then pull the string straight and measure it with the ruler. Or use the edge of a sheet of paper: put it along the first straight bit of road, make a mark where the road bends, turn the paper to follow the next bit, mark again, and keep going to the end. Then measure from the first mark to the last.

Say the road measures 9,6 centimetres. That is 9,6 times 0,5, which is 4,8 kilometres. The road is longer than the straight line, of course, because it winds. That difference is exactly why the taxi takes longer than you would expect from looking at the map.

You can also lay your string or paper directly against the line scale on the map and read the kilometres straight off it, with no sums.

Hold the string in mind and try this part's questions: straight distance with a ruler, curved distance with string or paper, and why the road is longer.

## Subtopic: Timing the Walk

You know the road to town is 4,8 kilometres. You walk at about 5 kilometres every hour. How long does it take? Time is distance divided by speed: 4,8 divided by 5 is 0,96 of an hour. Times 60 to get minutes: about 58 minutes. Just under an hour, which matches what your cellphone stopwatch would tell you if you walked it.

The taxi does 6,2 kilometres on the main road at 80 kilometres an hour: 6,2 divided by 80 is a tiny fraction of an hour, times 60 is under 5 minutes.

Going backwards is just as easy. The town is 3 kilometres away; how far on the map? Two centimetres for each kilometre, so 6 centimetres.

And three rules that save marks. Always measure from the middle of one symbol to the middle of the other. Always change centimetres into kilometres or metres before you work out time. Always write the unit: 3,15 on its own means nothing; 3,15 kilometres is an answer.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Scale is how much the map was shrunk. 1:50 000 means 1 centimetre is 500 metres, so 2 centimetres is a kilometre. 1:10 000 means 1 centimetre is 100 metres. Three ways to write it: words, ratio, line, and the line survives a photocopier. Ruler for straight, string or paper for curved. Time is distance over speed. In Grade 10 Geography you will add area calculations to these same steps.

Hold the stopwatch in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: distance to time, map to ground and back, and the unit every time.
