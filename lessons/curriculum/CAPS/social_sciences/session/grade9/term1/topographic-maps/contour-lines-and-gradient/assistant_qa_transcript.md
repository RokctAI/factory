### subtopic_1 — Height Clues on a Topographic Map
**Assistant:** Why are heights measured from mean sea level and not from the ground where I'm standing?

**Tutor:** Because a height is only useful if everyone measures from the same starting level, and the ground under different people is at different heights. Mean sea level is the average height of the sea surface between high tide and low tide, measured over many years at tide gauges along the coast, and it gives the whole country one common zero. With that zero, the trig station on a hill near Pretoria at 1 500 metres and a spot height in the Drakensberg at 3 000 metres can be compared directly, and the difference between any two heights on any map is just a subtraction. If heights were measured from the local ground, every map would have its own zero and nothing could be compared. In South Africa the reference is set from tide gauge records, and surveyors carried that zero inland by levelling, measuring height differences step by step from benchmark to benchmark, which is why benchmarks appear on bridges and buildings. Trigonometrical stations were then fixed by measuring angles between hilltops. The result is that every spot height and contour on a 1:50 000 map is part of one national framework. Johannesburg, at roughly 1 750 metres, is high enough that water boils at a slightly lower temperature than in Durban, and the effect of height on temperature and rainfall is a theme you will meet in Grade 10 Geography in the atmosphere topic.

### subtopic_2 — The Rules of Contour Lines and Reading Height
**Assistant:** If contour lines never cross, how does the map show an overhanging cliff?

**Tutor:** In practice it does not need to, because true overhangs are rare and small, and at 1:50 000 a cliff is shown by contour lines that run very close together or merge, together with a cliff symbol. A contour line joins points of the same height, so two lines of different heights can only touch where the ground is vertical: at a vertical cliff of 60 metres, the lines for 1 400, 1 420, 1 440 and 1 460 metres are all at the same horizontal position and appear to merge into one thick line. Where the cliff is not quite vertical, they are drawn just separated, packed tight. The four rules of contour lines describe the normal earth surface, and they work because a point on the ground can have only one height. An overhang, where the top of a cliff projects beyond its base, would technically make a higher contour pass outside a lower one, and the lines would appear to cross; but such features are usually only a few metres deep, far less than the width of a contour line at this scale, so the cartographer draws the cliff symbol instead. The same rules hold the other way: two different contour lines never split from one line, and every line closes on itself, either on the sheet or beyond its edge. For exam purposes, the rule is simply that contour lines never cross, and very closely spaced or merging contours mean a cliff or very steep slope.

### subtopic_3 — Steep and Gentle Slopes from Contour Spacing
**Assistant:** How do I tell a convex slope from a concave one on the map?

**Tutor:** By where the contour lines are crowded together along the slope, read from the bottom to the top. Choose a line from the foot of the slope to its crest and look at the spacing of contours along it. If the contours are close together near the bottom and spread out near the top, the slope is steep low down and gentle high up, which bulges outwards: a convex slope. If the contours are spread out near the bottom and close together near the top, the slope is gentle low down and steep high up, which curves inwards: a concave slope. If they are evenly spaced all the way, the slope is uniform. A practical test of convexity is intervisibility: from the top of a convex slope you cannot see the foot, because the bulge blocks your line of sight; on a concave slope you can see all the way down. That is why exam questions ask whether one point is visible from another and expect you to check the slope shape between them. Many hills combine the two: convex near the summit, where weathering rounds the top, and concave near the base, where material washed down collects into a gentle footslope. In Grade 10 the cross-section, a side view drawn from the contour heights along a line, makes these shapes visible, and in Grade 11 Geography slopes are studied as landforms in their own right.

### subtopic_4 — Calculating Gradient and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why must the horizontal equivalent be in metres before I divide?

**Tutor:** Because gradient is a ratio of two lengths, and a ratio only means something when both lengths are in the same unit. The vertical interval comes from contour heights, which are in metres. The distance you measure on the map is in centimetres of paper, and a centimetre of paper is not a centimetre of ground. If you divided 260 metres by 4 centimetres you would get 65, a meaningless number. The scale converts the map distance into ground distance: on a 1:50 000 map, 4 centimetres times 50 000 equals 200 000 centimetres on the ground, divided by 100 gives 2 000 metres. Now both lengths are ground lengths in metres, 260 and 2 000, and their ratio, 1 to 7,7, says something real: you rise 1 metre for every 7,7 metres you travel. A quick shortcut on the 1:50 000 map is to multiply centimetres by 500 to get metres; on the 1:10 000 map, multiply by 100. To write the answer as 1:x, divide both parts by the vertical interval: 260 over 260 is 1, and 2 000 over 260 is about 7,69, rounded to one decimal place as 7,7. Show every step in the exam: formula, VI with its subtraction, HE with its conversion, the substitution and the final 1:x. Most of the marks are for the method, so even a slip in the final division keeps most of them.
