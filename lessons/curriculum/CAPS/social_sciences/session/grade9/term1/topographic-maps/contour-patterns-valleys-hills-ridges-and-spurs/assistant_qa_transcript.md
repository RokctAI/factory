### subtopic_1 — Hills and Mountains
**Assistant:** Why does a contour line bend upstream when it crosses a river?

**Tutor:** Because a contour line must keep to one height, and the ground beside a river slopes down towards it, so the line has to move upstream to find the point where the river bed is at that same height. Picture the 1 400 metre contour running along a hillside. Ahead of it is a valley with a river whose bed, at the point directly in front of you, is only 1 360 metres high. If the contour went straight across it would pass through air above the river, which is impossible, because a contour runs along the ground surface. So it turns and runs up the valley side, staying at 1 400 metres, until it reaches the place upstream where the river bed itself has risen to 1 400 metres. There it crosses the river and runs back down the other side of the valley. Seen from above, the contour has drawn a V with its point upstream. The deeper and narrower the valley, the longer and sharper the V; a shallow, wide valley makes a gentle U. Every contour crossing the same river does the same thing, so a valley shows as a series of nested V's along the river, all pointing towards the source. This is also why rivers on a map always cross contour lines at the point of the V, and why you can read the direction of flow: the river flows away from the point of the V, from higher contours to lower ones.

### subtopic_2 — Ridges and Saddles
**Assistant:** How can a ridge be a watershed if water flows everywhere on a mountain?

**Tutor:** Water does flow in every direction on a mountain, but it always flows downhill, so the highest line of a ridge decides which way each raindrop goes. A watershed is the boundary between two drainage basins, the areas that drain into two different rivers. Rain falling on the north side of a ridge's crest runs down the northern slopes into the northern valley and its river; rain falling a metre to the south of the crest runs into the southern valley and a different river. The crest line itself, joining the highest points along the ridge and passing through the saddles, is therefore the dividing line, and on a map you can trace it by following the tops of the elongated contour loops. In South Africa the most important watershed is the Great Escarpment and the high ground of the interior: rivers rising on the inland side of the Drakensberg, such as the Orange River and its tributaries, flow west across the country to the Atlantic, while those rising on the eastern side, such as the Tugela, flow a short distance east to the Indian Ocean. On a smaller scale, every ridge on a 1:50 000 sheet divides two catchments. Watersheds matter for water management, which is a Grade 10 Geography topic, because a pollutant or a dam on one side of a ridge affects only the rivers downstream on that side.

### subtopic_3 — River Valleys and Spurs
**Assistant:** Is there an easy way to remember which way the V points for a valley and a spur?

**Tutor:** Yes: valleys point to the source, spurs point to the sea, but always confirm with the heights, because memory tricks fail when a question is drawn unusually. The phrase works because the source of a river is high in the mountains and the sea is the lowest level of all, so a valley's V pointing to the source points uphill and a spur's V pointing to the sea points downhill. The confirming test takes ten seconds. Pick any V in the pattern and read the contour label at its point and at its arms, or compare it with the next contour in the series. If the contours get higher as you move towards the point of the V, the point is uphill and the feature is a valley. If they get lower towards the point, the point is downhill and it is a spur. A river symbol confirms a valley, because rivers run along valley floors, and the river flows away from the points of the V's. A spot height or trig station sitting on high ground inside the V's indicates a spur. In an exam question that asks you to identify a feature in a given block, write the name and one piece of evidence: valley, because the contours form a V pointing upstream towards higher ground and a river flows through it. Two pieces of evidence earn the full answer even when the drawing is unusual.

### subtopic_4 — Putting the Landforms Together and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why do roads cross mountains at a saddle and not just go straight over the top?

**Tutor:** Because the saddle is the lowest and usually the shortest crossing of the high ground, which means less climbing, gentler gradients and lower cost. A road engineer wants to keep the gradient within what heavy trucks can manage, usually much gentler than 1:10, and to minimise the height that has to be gained and lost. A ridge's summits may be several hundred metres higher than the saddles between them, so going over a summit would add a long climb and a long descent for no purpose. The saddle, the waisted point in the contours, offers the lowest crossing, and the valleys on either side usually lead up to it, giving a natural route. Even so, the slopes approaching a saddle are often steeper than the road can take directly, so the road zigzags across the contours in hairpin bends to spread the climb over a longer distance; on the map the road crosses contour lines at a shallow angle rather than straight. South Africa is full of passes built this way through the Cape Fold Mountains and the escarpment, and many of their names include the word nek. Railways need even gentler gradients, so they follow valleys more closely and sometimes tunnel under a ridge instead of crossing it. Reading route choices like this on the map, why the road goes where it does, is part of the Grade 10 Geography interpretation of landscapes and settlements.
