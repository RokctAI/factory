# Part 1 — Expert

This session teaches the recognition of landforms from contour patterns on topographic maps: hills, mountains, ridges, river valleys and spurs, with the saddle and the plateau as related forms. It covers the pattern that identifies each landform, the evidence that confirms it, and the method that separates a valley from a spur, which share the same V shape but point in opposite directions. The anchor example is a hill with a river valley cutting into one side and a spur running down the other, so that the two V patterns can be compared on one sheet. The governing idea is that a contour pattern must always be read together with its height labels, because the same shape can describe high ground or low ground.

## Subtopic: Hills and Mountains

A hill is an area of high ground that rises above the surrounding land, usually with a rounded top. On the map it appears as a set of closed contour lines, one inside the other, decreasing in size towards the centre, with the innermost loop at the highest height. The summit is often marked by a spot height or a trigonometrical station, which gives its exact height. If the loops are roughly circular the hill is roughly round; if they are irregular, the hill's outline follows them.

A mountain is shown by the same pattern of concentric closed contours, but it rises much higher above its surroundings and its slopes are usually steeper, so the contours are more numerous and more closely spaced. There is no fixed height that turns a hill into a mountain; the difference is relative, and a map answer describes what the contours show: a high, steep-sided area rising about 600 metres above the valley floor, with closely spaced contours and a summit trig station at 2 140 metres. Where several summits are joined, the high ground forms a mountain range.

Two related forms are useful. A plateau is an extensive area of high, fairly flat land with steep edges: its top shows few contour lines and its edges show tightly packed contours or cliffs. A conical hill has evenly spaced, almost circular contours and a pointed summit. The closed loops of a hill must be distinguished from those of a depression, a hollow without an outlet, whose contours are marked with small ticks pointing inwards and whose heights decrease towards the centre; the labels always decide which it is.

To describe a hill in an exam, give its location, its height and its shape: a steep conical hill north of the river, rising to a trig station at 1 712 metres.

The questions for this section are with you now: the pattern of a hill, the difference between a hill and a mountain, plateaus and depressions.

## Subtopic: Ridges and Saddles

A ridge is a long, narrow stretch of high ground, often with steep sides, running for some distance across the landscape. On the map it appears as a set of elongated closed contours, stretched out in one direction like a series of narrow ovals or sausage shapes, with the highest contours along the crest line. A ridge may have several summits along its length, each with its own small closed loop at the top. Ridges often form where a resistant layer of rock has been left standing while softer rock on either side has been worn away.

Ridges matter on a map because they divide drainage. Rain falling on one side of a ridge flows into one valley and rain on the other side into another valley, so the crest of a ridge often forms a watershed, the boundary between two river catchments. Ridges also block movement: roads and railways either follow the valleys on either side or cross the ridge at its lowest point.

That lowest point is a saddle, also called a col or, in South Africa, a nek. A saddle is a dip in a ridge between two higher points. On the map it appears as two sets of closed contours, the two summits, with the contours between them forming an hourglass or waisted shape, lower in the middle. Many mountain passes in South Africa cross ranges at a nek for exactly this reason, because it gives the lowest and shortest crossing. A road that climbs in zigzags up a slope and crosses a ridge at a waisted point in the contours is crossing at a saddle.

South African examples help to fix the pattern. The Magaliesberg, running for about 120 kilometres from near Pretoria westwards towards Rustenburg, is a classic ridge: on a 1:50 000 sheet it appears as a band of long, narrow closed contours with a steep, cliff-edged southern face and a gentler northern slope, cut through at intervals by poorts where rivers have broken through the resistant quartzite. A poort is therefore a gap through a ridge cut by a river, which differs from a saddle because a river flows through it at the lowest level, while a saddle is a dip in the crest that rivers flow away from on both sides.

To tell a ridge from a hill: the hill's loops are roughly as wide as they are long; the ridge's loops are much longer than they are wide.

This section's questions are ready: the pattern of a ridge, the watershed, and the saddle as a crossing point.

## Subtopic: River Valleys and Spurs

A river valley is a long, low area of land between higher ground on either side, usually with a river flowing along its floor. On the map, contour lines crossing a valley bend sharply in a V or U shape. The key rule is that in a valley the point of the V points upstream, towards higher ground. This happens because the contour line, following a constant height, must travel up the valley until it reaches the river at that height, cross it, and come back down the other side. A V-shaped pattern points to a narrow, steep-sided valley, typical of the upper course of a river; a broad U-shaped pattern shows a wider valley with a flat floor.

A spur is a tongue of high ground that projects from a hill or mountain down into lower ground, usually between two valleys. Its contours also form a V or U shape, but the point of the V points downhill, towards lower ground. A spur and a valley are mirror images: the valley is a tongue of low ground pushing up into the high ground, and the spur is a tongue of high ground pushing down into the low ground. Rivers wind around spurs, and a road following a valley floor swings around the ends of the spurs, producing interlocking spurs where they alternate from either side.

The method for telling them apart has three steps. First, read the contour labels on the V: if the heights increase towards the point of the V, the V points uphill, and the landform is a valley. If the heights decrease towards the point, the V points downhill, and it is a spur. Second, look for a river: a blue line running through the points of the V's confirms a valley. Third, use spot heights: a spot height on high ground inside the V's indicates a spur.

The sharpness of the V also tells you something about the river. Sharp, narrow V's with closely spaced contours on both sides show a youthful valley in the upper course, where the river cuts downward vigorously into steep ground. Broad U's with a flat floor and widely spaced contours show a mature valley further downstream, where the river swings from side to side and widens its floor. Downstream again, the contours leave the river altogether and the floodplain shows none at all. Term 3 returns to these river stages in the topic on erosion and deposition.

Take this section's questions now: the direction of the V in a valley, the direction of the V in a spur, interlocking spurs, and the three-step method.

## Subtopic: Putting the Landforms Together and the Error Museum

Real landscapes combine these forms. Picture a sheet on which a mountain range runs east to west, made of several elongated ridges with summits marked by trig stations and saddles between them. Rivers rise on its slopes; each cuts a valley whose contours make sharp V's pointing up into the range; between neighbouring valleys, spurs project down from the ridges with their V's pointing down towards the plain. Further from the range the valleys widen into broad U's and then a flat floodplain with no contours at all, where the main river meanders.

Reading this landscape is a matter of method. Find the highest ground first, using closed loops, trig stations and labels. Trace the rivers downhill from it; they mark the valleys. Everything that projects between them is a spur. Elongated high ground is a ridge, and a waist in it is a saddle. Then relate land use to landform: settlements and cultivation on the valley floors and gentle lower slopes, roads along valleys and over saddles, plantations or natural vegetation on the steep spurs and ridges, and a reservoir sited on a hill or spur to supply a town by gravity.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: every set of closed contours is a hill; closed loops with inward ticks and decreasing heights are a depression. Two: in a valley the V points downstream; it points upstream, towards higher ground. Three: a spur and a valley have different shapes; they share the V shape and differ only in direction. Four: a ridge is the same as a hill; a ridge is long and narrow, a hill roughly as wide as it is long. Five: a saddle is the highest point of a ridge; it is the lowest point between two summits.

The questions for this section are ready: reading a whole landscape, linking land use to landform, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same landforms again, through your hand on a table, the drain of a roof, and a walk across a mountain pass — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Your Fist and Your Hand on the Table

Put your fist on the table, knuckles up. That is a hill. Now imagine drawing a line all the way around it at one height, then another a bit higher, and another higher still. From above, you see rings inside rings, getting smaller towards the top. That is the contour pattern of a hill: closed loops, one inside the other, and the smallest one is the top. If there is a triangle or a dot with a number in the middle, that is the exact height of the summit.

A mountain is the same picture, just bigger, higher and steeper, so there are more rings and they are squeezed closer together. There is no magic height where a hill becomes a mountain; a map answer just says what you see: high, steep, rings close together.

Now lay your forearm flat on the table. That is a ridge: long, narrow high ground. From above, its contour rings are stretched out long and thin, like sausages, not round like the fist. Rain on one side of your arm runs to one side of the table, rain on the other side runs the other way; a ridge splits the water between two valleys.

Put two fists side by side with a small gap between them. The dip between the two knuckles is a saddle, which South Africans often call a nek. It is the lowest point between two high points, so it is exactly where a road or a footpath crosses over. On the map the contours pinch in there like a waist.

Keep your fist and your arm in mind and try the questions for this part: rings for a hill, sausages for a ridge, and the waist for a saddle.

## Subtopic: Which Way Does the V Point?

Now the trickiest pattern on the map: the V. Both valleys and spurs make V-shaped contours, and you have to say which is which.

Think about water. A river cuts a groove into the hillside, a valley. Now walk along one contour line, staying at the same height. When you reach the valley, you cannot step straight across, because the ground drops away into the groove. To stay at the same height you have to walk up into the valley, upstream, until you reach the river at your height, cross it, and walk back out the other side. Your path makes a V that points upstream, up into the higher ground. So: in a valley, the V points uphill.

A spur is the opposite: a finger of high ground poking down into the low land, between two valleys. Walk along your contour and you have to go out around the end of the finger, so the V points downhill, towards the low ground.

How do you check? Three ways. Read the numbers: if they get bigger towards the point of the V, the V points uphill, valley. If they get smaller towards the point, spur. Look for a blue river line running through the points of the V's: valley. Look for a spot height in the middle of the V's on high ground: spur.

Here is a line to memorise: valleys point to the source, spurs point to the sea. The source of a river is high up, the sea is low down.

Hold the V in mind and try this part's questions: valley V uphill, spur V downhill, and the three checks.

## Subtopic: A Walk Across the Mountain Pass

Let us put it all together on a walk. You start on a flat plain where the river winds lazily; there are no contour lines at all, because the land is flat. Farms and fields are spread out here, and the road is straight.

Ahead is a mountain range: long ridges with rings on top and trig stations on the summits. The road heads for the range and follows a river valley up into it, where the contours make V's pointing up into the mountains. On either side, spurs poke down, with V's pointing back towards you, and the road swings around the end of each one. The valley gets narrower and steeper, the V's get sharper.

Then the road starts to zigzag up the slope, because going straight up would be far too steep, and it crosses the ridge at the nek, the waist in the contours between two summits. That is the pass. From the top you look down the other side into the next valley, where rain runs the other way, because the ridge is a watershed.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Rings inside rings: a hill or mountain, the smallest ring the top. Long thin rings: a ridge. A pinched waist between two tops: a saddle or nek, where roads cross. V pointing uphill with a river: a valley. V pointing downhill: a spur. Always check the numbers. In Grade 10 Geography you will draw these shapes as cross-sections, slicing the land sideways.

Hold the walk over the pass in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: the plain, the valley, the spurs, the zigzag and the nek.
