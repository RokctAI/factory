# Part 1 — Expert

This session teaches the reading of symbols on 1:50 000 topographic maps for both natural and constructed features. It covers the purpose of conventional symbols and the key, the three forms of symbol and the colour code, the main natural features and their symbols, the main constructed features and their symbols, and the interpretation of groups of symbols to describe land use and settlement. The anchor example is a stretch of farmland along a river, where a furrow, cultivated lands, a windmill and a dam together reveal irrigation farming. The governing idea is that a symbol is not the end of the reading but the beginning: identifying a symbol is worth one mark, and explaining what a set of symbols shows about how people use the land is worth the rest.

## Subtopic: Conventional Symbols, the Key and the Colour Code

A conventional symbol is a standard sign, agreed and used on every sheet of a map series, that represents a feature on the ground. Symbols are necessary because at 1:50 000 most features are too small to draw to scale: a house 10 metres long would be 0,2 millimetres on the map and a road 8 metres wide would be a hair. Symbols are therefore drawn larger than life and simplified, and the key, also called the legend, in the margin of the map explains every one. Because the symbols are the same on all sheets, a reader who learns them once can read any map of South Africa.

Symbols take three forms. Point symbols mark features at a single place: a building as a small black square, a windmill, a trigonometrical station as a triangle with a dot, a spot height as a dot with a number. Line symbols show features that are long and narrow: rivers, roads, railways, power lines, fences and boundaries. Area symbols cover a region with a colour, a shading or a repeated pattern: a dam's water surface, a plantation, cultivated land, an orchard, a built-up area, a marsh.

The colour code helps the eye to sort features before reading the key. Blue is water: rivers, dams, marshes, pans and reservoirs. Brown is relief: contour lines and the figures that give their heights. Green is vegetation: plantations, natural woodland, orchards and vineyards. Red is used for main roads. Black is used for most other constructed features, buildings, railways, minor roads, power lines, and for names and lettering. A reader scanning a sheet sees at once a blue river winding through brown contours, green plantations on the slopes and black buildings clustered at a red road junction.

The marginal information also matters: the sheet name and number, the scale and line scale, the contour interval, the edition date, the grid of latitude and longitude, and the index to neighbouring sheets. A map question that asks when the map was compiled, or what the contour interval is, is answered from the margin.

The questions for this section are with you now: why symbols are used, the three forms of symbol, and the colour code.

## Subtopic: Natural Features and Their Symbols

Natural features are those formed by nature without human construction. Drainage features are shown in blue. A perennial river flows throughout the year and is drawn as a continuous blue line; a non-perennial river flows only for part of the year, usually after rain in the wet season, and is drawn as a broken blue line of dashes and dots. Most rivers in the dry western half of South Africa are non-perennial, which on a map is a strong clue about climate. A marsh or vlei, ground that is waterlogged for most of the year, is shown by small blue tufts. A pan is a shallow natural depression in flat land that holds water after rain and dries out, often leaving a white salt crust; dry pans are common in the Free State and the Northern Cape.

Relief features are shown in brown and sometimes in black. Contour lines give the shape and height of the land, and the next lesson studies them in detail. Steep rock faces are shown as cliffs, with a hatched black or brown edge. Erosion gullies, called dongas in South Africa, are shown as a jagged line with short ticks. Areas of sand, such as dunes along the coast, are shown by fine dots.

Natural vegetation appears in green as an area symbol: natural woodland or bush, and on some sheets indigenous forest. Height points are partly natural and partly surveyed: a spot height is a point whose height above sea level has been measured and printed beside a dot, and a trigonometrical station is a surveyed beacon, usually on a hilltop, marked by a triangle with a dot and its height. A benchmark is a height marked by surveyors on a wall or building, shown by the letters BM and a figure.

The reading of natural symbols already tells a story. A sheet with many dashed non-perennial rivers, pans and few dams is in a dry region; a sheet with dense perennial rivers, marshes and indigenous forest is in a wet one. Clusters of dongas on the lower slopes point to soil erosion, a Term 3 topic.

This section's questions are ready: perennial and non-perennial rivers, marshes and pans, cliffs and dongas, and the three ways height is marked.

## Subtopic: Constructed Features and Their Symbols

Constructed features are those built or made by people. Transport features are line symbols. Roads are graded by importance: national and main roads in red, secondary and other roads thinner, and tracks and footpaths as broken black lines. The national road number, such as N1 or N3, is printed beside it. A railway is a solid black line; a station is marked where it stops. Where a road or railway passes through high ground in a cutting, or across low ground on an embankment, special edge symbols show it. An airfield or landing strip is shown with its runway.

Water supply features are partly constructed. A dam is a wall built across a river to store water, and the dam wall is a constructed feature even though the water behind it is blue. A reservoir is a constructed tank for storing water, often on high ground above a town so that water flows down by gravity. A furrow is a man-made channel that carries water from a river or dam to fields. A windmill pumps groundwater from a borehole, and a cluster of windmills across a sheet points to stock farming in a dry area.

Settlement and land-use features complete the set. Buildings are black squares or rectangles; in towns a built-up area is shaded and only important buildings are shown separately. Particular buildings carry their own symbols: place of worship, school, police station, post office, hospital. A cemetery, sewage works, mine dump, excavation, golf course and recreation ground each have their own symbols. Cultivated land, orchards and vineyards are area symbols, and power lines and telephone lines are thin black lines with pylons. Boundaries of provinces, municipalities and protected areas are broken lines.

The difference between natural and constructed is sometimes subtle. The river is natural; the dam wall is constructed; the water in the dam is natural water stored by construction, so the dam as a whole is classed as a constructed feature. A plantation of pine or gum trees is planted by people and is a constructed land use, while indigenous forest is natural.

Take this section's questions now: roads and railways, dams, reservoirs, furrows and windmills, and the classification of plantations and dams.

## Subtopic: Reading Symbols Together and the Error Museum

The skill that earns the higher marks is reading symbols in groups. Take a river valley on which the map shows a perennial river, a weir across it, a furrow leading off it, rectangles of cultivated land between the furrow and the river, and farm buildings on the slope above. Together these show irrigation farming: water is diverted from the river along the furrow to the fields, which lie on the flat floodplain where the soil is deep, and the farmhouses are on the slope, above the flood level. A question asking for evidence of irrigation is answered by naming the furrow and the cultivated land beside the river.

Other groups tell other stories. Windmills, scattered farmsteads, few cultivated lands and fenced camps suggest extensive stock farming in a dry area. A railway, a station, a grain silo and cultivated land suggest commercial grain farming. A mine dump, an excavation and a railway siding suggest mining. Plantations on steep slopes near a sawmill suggest forestry. A dense grid of buildings in a built-up area with a reservoir on a hill above it is a town supplied by gravity. Settlement can be described as nucleated, with buildings clustered, or dispersed, with farmsteads spread out, and the symbols show which.

The answer format matters. For a question that asks for evidence, name the symbol and say where it is: a furrow on the east bank of the river. For a question that asks for a reason, link the evidence to a cause: the town is located at the junction of two main roads and a railway, which makes it a route centre.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: a non-perennial river is a dried-up river; it flows for part of the year, usually in the rainy season. Two: a dam is a natural feature because it holds water; the dam is constructed. Three: a trigonometrical station and a spot height are the same; a trig station is a surveyed beacon shown by a triangle, a spot height is a measured point shown by a dot. Four: symbols are drawn to scale; most are enlarged and simplified. Five: one symbol proves a land use; evidence is stronger when several symbols agree.

The questions for this section are ready: irrigation evidence, stock farming and mining evidence, nucleated and dispersed settlement, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same symbols again, through the walk from your house to school, a farm seen from a hill, and a set of clues read like a detective — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Map's Alphabet on the Way to School

Think about your walk to school. You pass houses, cross a road, maybe step over a stream, walk past the church, the clinic and the soccer field, and duck under a power line. If you had to draw that walk from above, you could not draw every brick. You would draw a little square for each house, a line for the road, a blue line for the stream, a small sign for the church. That is exactly what a topographic map does. The little signs are called symbols, and the box in the corner that explains them all is the key.

There are only three kinds of symbol. Dots and small shapes for things at one spot, like a house, a windmill or a school. Lines for long thin things, like roads, rivers, railways and power lines. Patches of colour or pattern for things that cover an area, like a dam, a forest, a field or a town.

And there is a colour code that does half the work for you. Blue is water. Brown is the shape and height of the land, the contour lines. Green is trees and plants. Red is the main roads. Black is most of the things people build, plus all the writing. So before you even read the key, you can glance at a map and see the blue river, the green forest on the brown hills and the black town on the red road.

The symbols are drawn bigger than real life, otherwise a house would be a speck you could not see. Every map in the country uses the same symbols, so once you know them, you can read any map.

Keep the walk to school in mind and try the questions for this part: the three kinds of symbol, the colour code, and why symbols are drawn bigger.

## Subtopic: Nature's Things and People's Things

Now sort everything you saw on your walk into two piles. Nature's things and people's things.

Nature's things: the stream, the hill, the cliff, the wet patch where the reeds grow, the trees that grew there on their own. On the map, a river that flows all year round is a solid blue line: perennial, meaning it lasts through the years. A river that only flows after rain, and is dry sand for the rest of the year, is a broken blue line: non-perennial. It is not dead; it is just seasonal. A wet, reedy patch is a marsh, shown with little blue tufts. A shallow dip in flat land that fills after rain and dries out white is a pan. A gully torn into the land by running water, which we call a donga, has its own jagged symbol.

People's things: houses, roads, the railway, power lines, the school, the cemetery, the furrow carrying water to a field, the windmill pumping water from underground for the cattle, the fields themselves, and the dam wall built across the river. Here is the tricky one. The dam holds natural water, but people built the wall, so a dam counts as a people's thing, a constructed feature. A plantation of pine trees planted in neat rows is also a people's thing, but a natural forest is nature's.

The map also tells you heights in three ways: a dot with a number is a spot height, a triangle on a hilltop is a trigonometrical station, a beacon surveyors use, and BM with a number is a benchmark painted on a wall.

Hold the two piles in mind and try this part's questions: solid and broken blue lines, nature's things against people's things, and the dam trick.

## Subtopic: Reading the Clues Like a Detective

A detective does not just spot one clue; he puts the clues together. Do the same on a map. Look at a river with a thin line running off it beside the fields: that thin line is a furrow, and the fields next to it are cultivated land. Put those together and the story is irrigation: the farmer takes water from the river along the furrow to water his crops. Farmhouses sit higher up the slope, safe from floods. That is the answer the examiner wants when he asks for evidence of irrigation: the furrow and the fields beside the river.

More clue sets. Lots of windmills, farmhouses far apart and few fields: cattle or sheep farming in a dry area, with water pumped from underground. A railway, a station and a big grain silo among fields: grain farming, with the crop sent away by train. A mine dump and a big hole called an excavation: mining. Rows of pine trees on steep slopes: forestry. Buildings packed close together: a nucleated settlement, a village or town. Farmhouses spread far apart: a dispersed settlement.

When you write the answer, name the symbol and say where it is, then say what it means. Not just furrow. Say: there is a furrow on the east bank of the river, so the farmer is irrigating.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Symbols are the map's alphabet, explained in the key. Points, lines and areas. Blue water, brown land, green plants, red main roads, black buildings and writing. A solid blue line flows all year, a broken one only after rain. Dams and plantations are made by people. Put clues together to tell the story of how people live. In Grade 10 Geography you will read land use and settlement off maps exactly like this.

Hold the detective's notebook and take the final questions of the lesson: irrigation clues, farming and mining clues, and clustered against spread-out settlements.
