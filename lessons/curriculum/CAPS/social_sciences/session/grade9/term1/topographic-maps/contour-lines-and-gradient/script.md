# Part 1 — Expert

This session teaches how height and slope are shown and measured on topographic maps. It covers the height clues, spot heights, trigonometrical stations, benchmarks and contour lines; the definition and rules of contour lines and the contour interval; reading the height of a point; the link between contour spacing and steep and gentle slopes; and the calculation of gradient as vertical interval over horizontal equivalent, expressed as 1:x. The anchor example is a walk from a spot height of 1 420 metres to a trigonometrical station of 1 680 metres four centimetres away on a 1:50 000 map. The governing idea is that contour lines turn height into a pattern on the page, and gradient turns that pattern into a single comparable number.

## Subtopic: Height Clues on a Topographic Map

All heights on South African topographic maps are measured in metres above mean sea level, the average level of the sea between high and low tide. Four clues give height. A spot height is a point whose height has been measured, shown by a dot with the height printed beside it, for example 1 420. A trigonometrical station is a surveyed beacon, usually a concrete pillar on a hilltop from which surveyors measured angles to build the map's framework, shown by a triangle with a dot and its number and height. A benchmark is a height mark cut into a wall, bridge or building by surveyors, shown by the letters BM and the height. The fourth clue, and the one that shows the whole shape of the land, is the contour line.

A contour line is a line on a map that joins all points of the same height above mean sea level. Imagine slicing through a hill with horizontal planes at regular heights, say every 20 metres, and looking down from above at the edge of each slice: each edge is a contour line. On the 1:50 000 map contour lines are drawn in brown and labelled with their height at intervals along their length, with the numbers written so that they read facing uphill.

The contour interval is the constant difference in height between two neighbouring contour lines. On South African 1:50 000 topographic maps it is 20 metres; on 1:10 000 orthophoto maps it is 5 metres. The interval is printed in the margin and must be checked before any calculation. If one contour is labelled 1 400 and the next one uphill is unlabelled, it is 1 420; the one after it is 1 440.

The questions for this section are with you now: mean sea level, the four height clues and the contour interval of each map.

## Subtopic: The Rules of Contour Lines and Reading Height

Contour lines obey four rules, and every reading of a map depends on them. First, a contour line joins points of exactly the same height. Second, contour lines never cross or split, because a point cannot have two heights; they merge only at a vertical cliff, where they are drawn very close or touching. Third, every contour line eventually closes on itself, forming a loop, although the loop may run off the edge of the sheet. Fourth, the closed innermost loops mark the highest points of hills, unless they are marked as depressions.

To find the height of a point, locate the contour lines on either side of it. A point lying exactly on the 1 460 metre contour is at 1 460 metres. A point between the 1 460 and 1 480 metre contours is higher than 1 460 and lower than 1 480; for a map answer, either state that range or estimate by its position: halfway between is about 1 470 metres. A point inside the innermost closed contour of a hill, labelled 1 680, is higher than 1 680 but lower than 1 700, which is why trig stations and spot heights on summits are useful: they give the exact figure.

To tell whether the land rises or falls in a given direction, read the contour labels: if the numbers increase, the land rises. Where labels are sparse, use rivers, which always flow downhill, and spot heights. A river flowing from the 1 500 metre contour to the 1 300 metre contour tells you which way the valley falls.

A depression, a hollow with no outlet such as a pan or a quarry, also forms closed loops, so a closed loop is not automatically a hill. Depression contours are marked with short ticks pointing inwards, downhill, and the heights inside them are lower than the surrounding contour. Always read the labels before calling a loop a summit.

The difference in height between two points, the vertical interval, is found by subtraction: a trig station at 1 680 metres and a spot height at 1 420 metres have a vertical interval of 260 metres.

This section's questions are ready: the four rules, reading the height of a point between contours, and the vertical interval.

## Subtopic: Steep and Gentle Slopes from Contour Spacing

Because neighbouring contour lines are always 20 metres apart in height on the 1:50 000 map, the horizontal spacing between them shows how steep the land is. Where contour lines are close together, the land rises 20 metres over a short horizontal distance, so the slope is steep. Where they are far apart, the land takes a long distance to rise 20 metres, so the slope is gentle. Where there are no contour lines over a large area, the land is flat or nearly flat, such as a floodplain or a plateau surface.

The pattern of spacing also shows the shape of a slope. Evenly spaced contours show a uniform slope of constant steepness. Contours closely spaced at the bottom and widely spaced at the top show a convex slope, steep below and gentle above, so a person at the top cannot see the foot of the slope. Contours widely spaced at the bottom and close at the top show a concave slope, gentle below and steep above. Where contours almost merge, there is a cliff or a very steep scarp.

Reading slope has practical value. Roads and railways follow gentle slopes and swing in long curves or zigzags to avoid steep ones; settlements and cultivated land are found on gentle slopes and flat land; plantations and natural vegetation remain on steep slopes; steep slopes with little vegetation are exposed to soil erosion.

To describe a slope in an exam, give three things: its steepness, its shape and its direction. For example: the slope east of the river is steep and convex, rising from about 1 300 metres at the river to 1 520 metres at the crest. Using the contour labels on either side gives the figures; using the spacing gives the shape. When two slopes are compared, say which is steeper and give the evidence: the western slope is steeper because the contours are more closely spaced there. Evidence plus comparison is what turns a one-mark observation into a two-mark answer. A cliff deserves its own mention, because closely merged contours often coincide with a cliff symbol and a change in vegetation.

Take this section's questions now: close and wide spacing, uniform, convex and concave slopes, and why roads follow gentle slopes.

## Subtopic: Calculating Gradient and the Error Museum

Gradient is the steepness of a slope, expressed as the ratio of the vertical rise to the horizontal distance over which it occurs. The formula is gradient equals vertical interval divided by horizontal equivalent. The vertical interval, VI, is the difference in height between the two points, in metres. The horizontal equivalent, HE, is the horizontal distance between them on the ground, measured on the map and converted to metres using the scale. The answer is written as 1:x, meaning that for every 1 metre of rise you travel x metres horizontally.

Work the anchor example in the exam layout. Point A is a spot height of 1 420 metres; point B is a trig station of 1 680 metres. Vertical interval: 1 680 minus 1 420 equals 260 metres. The map distance is 4 centimetres on a 1:50 000 map, so the horizontal equivalent is 4 times 50 000 equals 200 000 centimetres, which is 2 000 metres. Gradient equals 260 over 2 000. Divide top and bottom by 260 to make the top 1: 2 000 divided by 260 is 7,69, so the gradient is 1:7,7, about 1 metre of rise for every 7,7 metres travelled. Both numbers must be in the same unit, metres, before dividing.

A second example: two contours 120 metres apart in height are 3 centimetres apart on the map. Horizontal equivalent: 3 times 500 metres is 1 500 metres. Gradient: 120 over 1 500 equals 1:12,5. The smaller the second number, the steeper the slope: 1:7,7 is steeper than 1:12,5. On a 1:10 000 orthophoto, a rise of 40 metres over 1,5 centimetres gives a horizontal equivalent of 150 metres and a gradient of 1:3,75, very steep.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: contour lines can cross; they never cross, because a point has only one height. Two: widely spaced contours mean steep land; they mean gentle land. Three: gradient is horizontal equivalent over vertical interval; it is vertical interval over horizontal equivalent. Four: the horizontal equivalent can stay in centimetres; it must be converted to metres to match the vertical interval. Five: 1:20 is steeper than 1:5; the larger x, the gentler the slope.

The questions for this section are ready: the gradient formula, the conversion of map distance, the comparison of two gradients and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same idea again, through a hill cut into slices like a loaf of bread, the walk up the steep path behind the school, and a bicycle that tells you the gradient — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Slicing the Hill Like a Loaf

Imagine a hill made of bread. Now cut it into slices, flat across, every 20 metres of height from the bottom to the top. Lift off each slice and look down from above at its edge. You see a ring at the bottom, a smaller ring above it, a smaller one above that, and a tiny one near the top. Draw all those rings on paper and you have contour lines: lines joining all the places at the same height above the sea.

That is all a contour line is. Walk along one and you never go up or down; you stay at the same height the whole way. Step off it uphill and you are higher; step off downhill and you are lower. Because the slices were cut every 20 metres, neighbouring lines are 20 metres apart in height on the topographic map. That 20 is called the contour interval. On the orthophoto they cut thinner slices, every 5 metres.

The map gives you exact heights in other ways too. A dot with a number is a spot height, a point someone measured. A triangle on top of a hill is a trigonometrical station, a concrete beacon surveyors built to make the map. BM and a number is a benchmark, a height mark on a wall or a bridge. All the heights are measured from the average level of the sea.

And because the slices never touch each other, contour lines never cross. Every ring closes into a loop, and the smallest ring is the top of the hill.

Keep the sliced loaf in mind and try the questions for this part: what a contour line joins, the 20 metre interval, and why the lines never cross.

## Subtopic: The Steep Path Behind the School

Think of two ways up the hill behind the school. One is the steep path straight up: you climb fast, your legs burn, and in a few steps you are 20 metres higher. The other is the long, gentle road that winds around: you walk a long way before you have climbed the same 20 metres.

Now look at the contour lines. On the steep path, the lines are packed close together, because you go up 20 metres in a short distance. On the gentle road, the lines are far apart, because it takes a long walk to go up 20 metres. That is the whole secret: close lines, steep; far lines, gentle; no lines, flat.

The spacing also shows the shape of the slope. Lines evenly spaced: the same steepness all the way. Lines close at the bottom and far apart at the top: steep first, then gentle, a slope that bulges out, so from the top you cannot see the bottom; that is convex. Lines far apart at the bottom and close at the top: gentle first, then steep, a slope that curves in like the inside of a bowl; that is concave.

And notice what people do. Roads, railways and houses go where the lines are far apart, because building and driving on gentle land is easy. Steep land, with lines squeezed together, is left for forest and grass, and if it is bare, the rain washes the soil off it.

Hold the steep path in mind and try this part's questions: close and far lines, convex and concave, and where roads go.

## Subtopic: The Bicycle Test

Here is the question the examiner really asks: could you cycle up that hill? To answer it, you need a number, the gradient.

Gradient is how much you go up compared with how far you go along. Up divided by along. The up part is called the vertical interval: subtract the two heights. The along part is called the horizontal equivalent: measure on the map and change it into metres.

Try it. You start at a spot height of 1 420 metres. You want to reach the trig station at 1 680 metres. Up: 1 680 minus 1 420 is 260 metres. On the map they are 4 centimetres apart, and on the 1:50 000 map each centimetre is 500 metres, so along is 2 000 metres. Gradient: 260 over 2 000. Make the top number 1 by dividing both by 260: 2 000 divided by 260 is about 7,7. Gradient 1:7,7. For every metre you climb, you ride 7,7 metres along. That is a tough climb for a bicycle, but possible in a low gear.

Rule for comparing: the smaller the second number, the steeper. 1:3 is like a staircase. 1:10 is a hard ride. 1:50 is barely a slope at all.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A contour line joins places of the same height; neighbours are 20 metres apart on the topographic map and 5 metres on the orthophoto. Lines never cross. Close lines steep, far lines gentle. Gradient is up over along, both in metres, written 1:x. In Grade 10 Geography and right through to matric, gradient is one of the calculations that appears in almost every map paper.

Hold the bicycle in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: up and along, the 1:x format, and which gradient is steeper.
