### subtopic_1 — The Global Grid of Latitude and Longitude
**Assistant:** Why are there 60 minutes in a degree instead of 100?

**Tutor:** Because the system comes from ancient Babylonian astronomers who counted in sixties, and it was kept because it divides neatly and because it was built into navigation, clocks and astronomy long before the metric system existed. About four thousand years ago the Babylonians used a base-sixty number system, and they divided the circle into 360 parts, close to the number of days in a year, with each part split into 60 smaller parts and those into 60 again. Greek astronomers such as Ptolemy adopted it for mapping the heavens and the earth, and it spread to every navigator who used the stars. Sixty is convenient because it divides exactly by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20 and 30, so a half, a third or a quarter of a degree is a whole number of minutes. The same system gives us 60 minutes in an hour and 60 seconds in a minute, and the two are linked: the earth turns 360 degrees in 24 hours, which is 15 degrees per hour, so time zones and longitude go together, a topic you will meet in Grade 10. There is a decimal alternative: GPS devices and online maps often show positions in decimal degrees, such as minus 25,7567 for latitude, where the minus means south. To convert, divide the minutes by 60 and the seconds by 3 600 and add them to the degrees. Exams in Grade 9 and the National Senior Certificate still use degrees, minutes and seconds, so learn both but write answers in that form.

### subtopic_2 — Reading Degrees and Minutes from the Map Frame
**Assistant:** Why is a minute of longitude shorter on the map than a minute of latitude?

**Tutor:** Because lines of longitude converge towards the poles, so the east to west distance covered by one minute of longitude shrinks as you move away from the equator, while a minute of latitude stays almost the same everywhere. Lines of latitude are parallel circles, evenly spaced, so the north to south distance between two of them one minute apart is always about 1 852 metres, wherever you are. Lines of longitude are not parallel: they are half-circles that all meet at the North and South Poles. At the equator they are furthest apart, and one minute of longitude there is also about 1 855 metres. At latitude 26 degrees south, near Johannesburg, the distance is reduced by a factor equal to the cosine of the latitude, about 0,9, so a minute of longitude is about 1 670 metres. At Cape Town, near 34 degrees south, it is about 1 540 metres; at the poles it is zero. On a 1:50 000 map this is why each one-minute block is about 3,7 centimetres tall but only about 3,3 centimetres wide in Gauteng and a little narrower in the Western Cape. The practical lesson for the exam is never to assume a block size: always measure the block on the map you are given, in the direction you are calculating. The same convergence explains why maps of the whole world have to stretch or squash shapes, which is the subject of map projections in later grades.

### subtopic_3 — Calculating Seconds and Writing the Co-ordinate
**Assistant:** Which minute line do I measure from, and why does it differ for latitude and longitude?

**Tutor:** You always measure from the line with the smaller value, the minute you have already passed, and the direction differs because latitude south increases downward on the map while longitude east increases to the right. In the southern hemisphere, latitude counts up as you move south, away from the equator. On a map with north at the top, that means the values increase towards the bottom of the sheet: 25 degrees 45 minutes is above 25 degrees 46 minutes. A feature between them has a latitude of 25 degrees 45 minutes plus some seconds, so you measure from the 45 minute line, the one to the north, downward to the feature. In the eastern hemisphere, longitude counts up as you move east, so values increase towards the right, and you measure from the line to the west, the smaller value, rightward to the feature. In the northern hemisphere the latitude rule reverses, because values increase upward, so a learner who has memorised always measure from the top would be wrong in Europe. The rule that never fails is: start from the smaller value. A useful check is that the seconds should be small if the feature is close to the starting line and large if it is close to the far line. If your feature is just below the 45 minute line and you get 55 seconds, you measured from the wrong line, and the correct answer is about 60 minus 55, which is 5 seconds.

### subtopic_4 — Working in Reverse and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How accurate can my co-ordinates be when I measure with a school ruler?

**Tutor:** To within a few seconds, which is roughly 50 to 100 metres on the ground, and that is good enough to identify any feature on a 1:50 000 map. A school ruler can be read to about half a millimetre, and the symbol you are measuring to is itself about a millimetre across. On the 1:50 000 map, one second of latitude is about 31 metres on the ground, which is about 0,6 millimetres on the map, so a measurement error of one millimetre gives an error of nearly two seconds. That is why memoranda allow a small range, typically two or three seconds either side of the expected answer. To keep your error small, sharpen your pencil, draw the minute lines precisely by joining the ticks with a ruler, measure the block length rather than assuming it, measure to the centre of the symbol, and keep the ruler at right angles to the minute line. Round your seconds to whole numbers; writing 24,32 seconds suggests a precision your ruler cannot give. A GPS receiver is far more precise, usually within a few metres, and surveyors with professional equipment work to centimetres, which is how the trigonometrical stations on your map were fixed. In Grade 10 Geography the same method is used on both the topographic map and the orthophoto, where the larger scale makes each second about 3 millimetres long, so you can measure more precisely.
