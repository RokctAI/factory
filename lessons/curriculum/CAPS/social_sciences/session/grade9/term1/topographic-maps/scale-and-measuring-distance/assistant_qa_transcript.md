### subtopic_1 — Three Ways to Write a Scale
**Assistant:** Why does a ratio scale have no units, and how can it work for centimetres and kilometres?

**Tutor:** Because a ratio compares two lengths measured in the same unit, and the units cancel, so the ratio is a pure number that works in any unit you choose. The scale 1:50 000 says that every length on the ground is 50 000 times the matching length on the map. If you measure the map in centimetres, the ground is 50 000 centimetres; in millimetres, 50 000 millimetres; in inches, 50 000 inches. It never mixes units: 1 centimetre is never 50 000 metres. That independence is why the ratio scale is printed on maps used internationally and why it is the form used in calculation. The trap is that the numbers it produces are in the same small unit you measured in, so they must be converted to a sensible unit at the end. Measuring 7,5 centimetres on a 1:50 000 map gives 375 000 centimetres; divide by 100 for 3 750 metres, or by 100 000 for 3,75 kilometres. A word scale has already done that conversion for you, one centimetre represents half a kilometre, which is why it feels easier, but it only works if you measure in centimetres. In exam working it is best to start from the ratio, write map distance times scale, and then convert in a separate line, because each step earns a mark and a mistake in one step can then be followed through. Grade 10 Geography also asks you to convert a word scale or line scale into a ratio, which simply means expressing the ground distance in the map unit.

### subtopic_2 — Large and Small Scale, and the Photocopier Problem
**Assistant:** If the exam map is reduced, how do I know what scale it really is?

**Tutor:** Use the line scale to measure the reduction, and then you can work out the new ratio if a question needs it. When a 1:50 000 map is reduced on a photocopier, every length on the paper shrinks by the same percentage, including the line scale printed in the margin. Measure the line scale's 1 kilometre section on the copy with a ruler. On the original it was 2 centimetres; suppose on the copy it is only 1,6 centimetres. Then 1,6 centimetres on the copy represents 1 kilometre, which is 100 000 centimetres, and the true ratio of the copy is 1 to 100 000 divided by 1,6, which is 1:62 500. The printed 1:50 000 is now simply wrong, and using it would underestimate every distance by a fifth. Most exam papers avoid the calculation by telling you to use the line scale: lay your ruler or paper strip directly against it and read off kilometres, or measure the line scale's kilometre in centimetres and divide your map distance by it. For example, a road measuring 8 centimetres on the reduced copy is 8 divided by 1,6, which is 5 kilometres. Instructions such as reduced to 75 percent of its original size printed on the paper are a hint that the line scale must be used. The same reasoning applies to maps on screens, where zooming changes the size constantly, which is why digital maps show a line scale that zooms with them.

### subtopic_3 — Measuring Straight and Curved Distances
**Assistant:** How accurate is the paper-strip method compared with string?

**Tutor:** Both can be accurate to within a millimetre or two of map distance, which at 1:50 000 is 50 to 100 metres on the ground, and the method you choose matters less than the care you take. The paper strip works by breaking a curved route into a chain of short straight segments. On a road with long straight sections and few bends, it is very accurate. On a tightly winding mountain road or a meandering river, each straight segment cuts a little off every curve, so the strip slightly underestimates the true length; the fix is to use more, shorter segments wherever the route bends sharply. String follows curves smoothly, but it can stretch or slip, and it is hard to hold at the start point while laying it along the route, so mark the start on the string with a pen and lay it in short sections while holding the part already laid. Whichever method you use, measure from the centre of the starting symbol to the centre of the end symbol, and read the result against the ruler or the line scale at eye level. In an exam, a range of answers is accepted for curved distances, typically a few millimetres either side of the memorandum, because examiners know that measurements vary; showing the method, the measured map distance and the conversion ensures you earn the method marks even if your measurement is slightly off. A computer measuring along a digitised road is more precise, and Grade 10 learners meet that idea in geographical information systems.

### subtopic_4 — Distance, Time and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why do travel time answers depend so much on using kilometres first?

**Tutor:** Because speed is given in kilometres per hour, and you can only divide a distance by a speed when the distance is in the same unit as the speed's distance part. If you divided a map distance of 9,6 centimetres by 5 kilometres per hour, the units would not cancel and the number 1,92 would be meaningless. Converting first, 9,6 centimetres at 1:50 000 is 4,8 kilometres, and then 4,8 kilometres divided by 5 kilometres per hour leaves hours, 0,96 hours, because kilometres cancel. Converting hours to minutes is a separate step: multiply by 60, giving about 58 minutes. For short distances it is sometimes easier to work in metres and metres per minute: walking at 5 kilometres per hour is 5 000 metres in 60 minutes, about 83 metres per minute, so a 420 metre path measured on the orthophoto takes 420 divided by 83, about 5 minutes. The reverse questions work the same way: speed equals distance divided by time, and distance equals speed times time, each with consistent units. Real travel times are usually longer than these calculations because of gradient, stops and traffic, and a good answer may say so: the calculated time assumes a constant speed. Map questions that combine distance, gradient and time, such as whether a learner can walk to school within an hour, return throughout Grade 10 Geography.
