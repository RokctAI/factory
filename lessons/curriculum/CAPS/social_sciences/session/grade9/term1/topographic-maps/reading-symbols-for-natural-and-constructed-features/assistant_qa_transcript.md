### subtopic_1 — Conventional Symbols, the Key and the Colour Code
**Assistant:** Why are symbols drawn larger than the features they show, and doesn't that make the map inaccurate?

**Tutor:** They are enlarged because at 1:50 000 most real features would be too small to see, and the enlargement is controlled so that position stays accurate even though size does not. At this scale 1 millimetre on the map is 50 metres on the ground. A typical farmhouse of 15 metres would be 0,3 millimetres wide, a two-lane road of 8 metres would be 0,16 millimetres, and a footpath would vanish entirely. If the map drew these to true scale it would be unreadable. So the cartographer draws a house as a black square of about a millimetre and a main road as a red band of about a millimetre, which on the ground would be 50 metres wide. The centre line of the road and the centre of the house are placed in their true positions, so distances measured along the road or between houses are still correct, but you must never measure the width of a road or the size of a building from the map and expect a true answer. This process of simplifying and enlarging is called generalisation, and it also involves leaving things out: in a town the individual houses are replaced by a shaded built-up area and only important buildings are shown. That is why the orthophoto at 1:10 000, which shows real roofs and real road widths, is used when size matters. A good habit is to read the key's description rather than the size of the symbol, and to treat the map as accurate for position and approximate for size.

### subtopic_2 — Natural Features and Their Symbols
**Assistant:** How can a non-perennial river be shown on the map if it is dry most of the time?

**Tutor:** Because the river channel exists all year even when the water does not, and that channel shapes the land, carries floods and affects where people can build and farm. A non-perennial river is one that flows only during part of the year, usually in the rainy season or for days after a storm, and its bed is dry sand or rock for the rest of the time. It is drawn as a broken blue line so that the reader knows not to rely on it for a water supply. The distinction is very useful in South Africa, where rainfall decreases from east to west. On sheets in KwaZulu-Natal or Mpumalanga most rivers are perennial, solid blue lines fed by summer rain and by groundwater. On sheets in the Karoo or the Northern Cape most rivers are non-perennial, and the map compensates by showing dams, windmills and reservoirs that store water for the dry months. A dry riverbed can still be dangerous: a thunderstorm upstream can send a flash flood down a channel where no rain fell, which is why roads cross such rivers on raised causeways or bridges and why farmhouses are built above the channel. When a question asks how you know a region is dry, a good answer combines evidence: many non-perennial rivers, many windmills and farm dams, few cultivated lands. When it asks about water supply problems, the broken blue line is the first piece of evidence.

### subtopic_3 — Constructed Features and Their Symbols
**Assistant:** Why is a dam counted as a constructed feature when the water in it is natural?

**Tutor:** Because the classification is about what made the feature exist, and without the wall built by people there would be no lake there, only a river. Natural features are produced by natural processes: rivers, marshes, pans, cliffs, dunes, indigenous forest. Constructed features are produced by human building or planting: roads, railways, buildings, power lines, furrows, plantations, cultivated land and dams. A dam is built by constructing a wall of earth, rock or concrete across a river valley so that water collects behind it; the stored lake is the result of that construction, so the whole dam is classified as constructed. A pan, by contrast, is a natural depression that fills with rain, so a pan is natural even though both look like patches of blue water on the map. You tell them apart by the dam wall symbol, a thick line across the outlet end, and by the shape: a dam is usually elongated up a valley with a straight wall at one end, while a pan is a roundish shape on flat ground with no outlet. The same reasoning classifies a plantation of pine or gum trees as constructed, because people planted it in rows for timber, while an indigenous forest is natural. This matters in exam questions that ask for one natural and one constructed feature in a given block of the map, because naming the dam as natural loses the mark. When in doubt, ask: would this be here if people had never come?

### subtopic_4 — Reading Symbols Together and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How many symbols do I need before I can say what the land use is?

**Tutor:** Strictly, one symbol can be offered as evidence, but a strong answer uses two or three that agree, because single symbols are often ambiguous. Cultivated land on its own tells you that crops are grown but not whether they are watered by rain or by irrigation. Add a furrow leading from the river to those fields and the land use becomes irrigation farming; add a weir across the river upstream and the case is very strong. A windmill on its own tells you that groundwater is pumped, but that water might be for a farmhouse; many windmills spread over a large area with few cultivated lands is clear evidence of extensive livestock farming. An excavation could be a quarry for road gravel or a mine; with a mine dump, a railway siding and processing buildings it is clearly mining. The examiner usually asks for a specific number of pieces of evidence, two marks for two, and expects each one to be a symbol you can point to, with its location if possible. Then a further mark often goes to a reason linking evidence and land use: the fields lie on the flat floodplain where the soil is deep and water can be led to them by gravity. In Grade 10 Geography and later in the Grade 12 topics on rural and urban settlement and on agriculture, the same method is used on full map sets, so building the habit of evidence plus location plus reason now pays off for four years.
