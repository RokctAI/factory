### subtopic_1 — Grade 9 Geography and How It Is Assessed
**Assistant:** What does a source-based question in Geography actually ask me to do?

**Tutor:** It gives you a source, a map, an orthophoto, a graph, a table, a photograph, a cartoon or a short newspaper extract, and asks a set of questions that climb from finding information to explaining it. The first questions are usually recall and reading: name the feature at a given point, read the height of a spot height, state what a graph shows in 2015. The middle questions ask you to apply a skill: calculate a distance, give the direction from one place to another, describe the pattern of settlement. The last questions ask you to explain or evaluate: why is the settlement located beside the river, what problem might the farmer face, what would you recommend. Marks are usually given per correct fact, so a question worth 4 marks wants four separate points or two points each with a reason. A paragraph question asks for about eight to ten connected sentences on one theme, with an opening sentence that answers the question directly, supporting sentences that each add a fact or an explanation, and a closing sentence; it is marked on content, not on decoration, so lists of single words earn little. The habit to build in Term 1 is to read the source before the questions, note the title, the scale, the key and the date, and then answer exactly what the verb asks: name means a word, describe means what it is like, explain means why. Grade 10 Geography and the National Senior Certificate use the same structure, so the skill grows with you.

### subtopic_2 — What a Topographic Map Is
**Assistant:** Why does South Africa number its map sheets with latitude and longitude instead of town names?

**Tutor:** Because a number built from latitude and longitude is unique, permanent and tells you where the sheet is before you even open it, while names repeat, change and say nothing about position. The reference starts with the degree square: 2628 means the square whose north-west corner lies at 26 degrees south and 28 degrees east, which is the area around Johannesburg. That one-degree square is too big for a single sheet at 1:50 000, so it is split into four quarters lettered A, B, C and D, reading left to right along the top and then left to right along the bottom, and each quarter is split again into four with the same letters. Two letters after the four digits therefore pin down one fifteen-minute square out of sixteen in the degree square, which is exactly one 1:50 000 sheet. The orthophoto series follows on from the same system, dividing each topographic sheet into 25 smaller sheets, so a mapping office, a surveyor or a learner in another province can name any sheet without ambiguity. A neighbouring sheet is easy to find too: the sheet east of 2628AB is 2628BA. The name printed beside the number, usually the biggest town on the sheet, helps people, but the number is the true address. In Grade 10 you will use latitude and longitude in degrees, minutes and seconds to give the exact position of a feature, and the sheet reference is the first step of that same system.

### subtopic_3 — What an Orthophoto Map Is
**Assistant:** How can I tell what something is on an orthophoto if there is no symbol in the key?

**Tutor:** By the same five clues an air-photo interpreter uses: tone, texture, shape, size and pattern, together with where the feature is. Tone is how light or dark it appears: deep water is dark, a dry sandy riverbed or a gravel road is pale, a freshly ploughed field is darker than a field of dry grass. Texture is how smooth or rough it looks: a plantation of pine trees is rough and even, a natural bushveld is rough and patchy, a lawn or a dam surface is smooth. Shape is often the strongest clue, because people build in straight lines and regular forms while nature makes curves: a rectangular field, a round reservoir, a sports ground with an oval track, a straight railway with gentle curves, while a river meanders. Size helps you separate a house from a factory or a farm dam from a reservoir, using the scale of 1:10 000 to estimate. Pattern is the arrangement: rows of houses on a grid suggest a formal township, a scatter of round huts with kraals suggests a rural village, and rows of dots in a field suggest an orchard. Finally use association: a feature beside a railway line with long rectangular sheds is likely a factory or a station; a dark irregular patch where a stream leaves a hill may be a wetland. Then confirm on the topographic map, where the same feature has a symbol and often a name. Grade 10 and Grade 12 Geography lean heavily on exactly this skill.

### subtopic_4 — Comparing the Two Maps and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why is the orthophoto called large scale when it covers a smaller area?

**Tutor:** Because scale describes how big things are drawn on the map compared with reality, not how much land the map covers, and on the orthophoto things are drawn bigger. Write each scale as a fraction: 1:10 000 is one ten-thousandth and 1:50 000 is one fifty-thousandth. One ten-thousandth is the larger fraction, five times larger, so a house that is 10 metres long appears as 1 millimetre on the orthophoto but only 0,2 millimetres on the topographic map. Large scale therefore means features look large, which leaves room on the sheet for only a small area. A useful picture is the zoom on a phone map: zooming in enlarges the streets and buildings, the scale becomes larger, and you see less of the city at once; zooming out shrinks everything, the scale becomes smaller, and you see the whole province. The terms are relative, so in South African school Geography the orthophoto at 1:10 000 is the large-scale map and the topographic sheet at 1:50 000 is the small-scale map, while a wall map of Africa at 1:10 000 000 is very small scale indeed. Exam questions often ask which of the two maps is larger scale and why; the full answer names the orthophoto, gives its scale and says that it shows a smaller area in more detail. Getting the reasoning right matters because in later lessons you will use it to choose which map to measure on.
