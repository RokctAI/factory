# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces Grade 9 Geography and the two maps on which the term's work is built. It sets out what Geography studies and how the year is assessed, then introduces the 1:50 000 topographic map and the 1:10 000 orthophoto map: their purpose, their producer, the area a sheet covers, the sheet reference system, the meaning of their scales, and the difference between a map drawn from symbols and a map built from a corrected aerial photograph. The anchor example is a single place shown twice, once on a topographic sheet and once on an orthophoto sheet, so that every difference between the two maps can be pointed to. The governing idea is that a map is a reduced, selective and precisely located model of the earth's surface, and that the two maps answer different questions at different levels of detail.

## Subtopic: Grade 9 Geography and How It Is Assessed

Geography is the study of the earth's surface: its physical features, the processes that shape them, and the ways people use, change and depend on them. In Grade 9 Social Sciences the subject is taught alongside History, with one Geography topic and one History topic in each term. Term 1 is map skills; Term 2 is development issues; Term 3 is the surface forces that shape the earth, weathering, erosion and deposition by rivers, and soil erosion; Term 4 is resource use and sustainability, including fishing and food security. Map skills come first because every later topic is shown, measured and explained on maps.

The assessment programme follows the Annual Teaching Plan. Geography carries a test of 50 marks at the end of Term 1, a controlled test of 75 marks in Term 2 that covers both Term 1 and Term 2 content, a test of 50 marks in Term 3, and an end-of-year examination of 75 marks in Term 4 that covers Term 3 and Term 4 content. The Term 1 test is a map skills test on topographic and orthophoto maps. The later tests include source-based questions, in which you interpret a map, graph, photograph or extract, and paragraph writing, in which you explain a process or an issue in connected sentences.

Three habits earn marks in every Geography question from here to the National Senior Certificate. First, answer in the format asked: if the question wants a distance in kilometres, the answer carries the unit; if it wants a direction, it is a compass point. Second, show the working for every calculation: formula, substitution, answer, unit. Third, use the subject's vocabulary precisely: a ridge is not a spur, weathering is not erosion, and a renewable resource is not the same as a sustainable one. These habits are built this term on the maps and used for the rest of the year.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four Geography topics of the year, the marks attached to each assessment, and the three habits that earn marks.

## Subtopic: What a Topographic Map Is

A topographic map is a detailed, accurate map of a small area showing both natural features and features made by people, drawn to a fixed scale with standard symbols. The word comes from the Greek for describing a place. Natural features include relief, the shape and height of the land, and drainage: rivers, dams, marshes and pans. Constructed features include roads, railways, buildings, power lines, cultivated fields, airfields and boundaries. In South Africa the national topographic map series is produced by the Chief Directorate: National Geo-spatial Information, the state mapping office in Mowbray, Cape Town.

The standard sheet is drawn at a scale of 1:50 000. That ratio means that one unit on the map represents 50 000 of the same units on the ground, so 1 centimetre on the map is 50 000 centimetres on the ground, which is 500 metres. Two centimetres therefore represent one kilometre, a conversion worth knowing by heart. Each sheet covers fifteen minutes of latitude by fifteen minutes of longitude. Fifteen minutes of latitude is about 27,8 kilometres from north to south; the east to west width shrinks slightly towards the south because lines of longitude converge, and is about 25 kilometres at the latitude of Gauteng.

Each sheet is identified by a reference built from latitude and longitude. The first two digits give the degrees of latitude south and the next two the degrees of longitude east of the north-west corner of a one-degree square. That square is divided into four quarters lettered A, B, C and D, reading from the top left, and each quarter into four again, also A to D. The reference 2628AB therefore names a sheet in the one-degree square from 26 degrees south and 28 degrees east, in the top-left quarter A, and in the top-right part B of that quarter. A sheet also carries a name, usually the largest town on it.

Relief is shown by contour lines drawn in brown at a vertical interval of 20 metres, by spot heights and by trigonometrical stations. Water is shown in blue and vegetation in green. Everything on the map is generalised to some extent, because a road 10 metres wide would be only 0,2 millimetres wide at true scale, so roads, rivers and buildings are drawn as symbols larger than life, and the key in the margin explains each one.

This section's questions are ready: the definition, the meaning of 1:50 000, the area of a sheet, and how a sheet reference is built.

## Subtopic: What an Orthophoto Map Is

A vertical aerial photograph is taken from an aircraft with the camera pointing straight down at the ground. It shows every roof, field, tree and path as they actually look from above, but it is not yet a map, because its scale is not uniform. The centre of the photograph is closer to the camera than the edges, so objects near the edges are slightly displaced, and hilltops are closer to the camera than valley floors, so high ground appears at a slightly larger scale than low ground. A building on a hill looks a little bigger than an identical building in the valley.

An orthophoto is an aerial photograph that has been corrected for these distortions, so that it has a uniform scale and every point lies in its correct position, exactly as on a map. The prefix ortho means straight or correct. In South Africa the orthophoto map series is drawn at a scale of 1:10 000, so 1 centimetre represents 10 000 centimetres, which is 100 metres, and 1 kilometre is 10 centimetres on the map. A 1:10 000 sheet covers three minutes of latitude by three minutes of longitude, so 25 orthophoto sheets fit inside the area of one 1:50 000 topographic sheet, five across and five down.

An orthophoto map is the corrected photograph with map information printed over it. Contour lines are added, at a vertical interval of 5 metres rather than the 20 metres of the topographic map, together with spot heights, names of places, rivers and roads, a grid of co-ordinates and a margin with the scale and key. The result combines the realism of the photograph with the measurability of the map. Because the image is a photograph, features appear in their real shapes, colours and textures, and the reader must recognise them by tone, texture, shape, size and pattern rather than by a symbol in the key.

Because the scale is five times larger, the orthophoto shows five times more detail along every line: individual houses, the shape of a sports field, rows in an orchard, the edge of a gully. A 1:50 000 sheet shows a town as a grey block of streets; the orthophoto of the same town shows each street and each roof.

Take this section's questions now: vertical and oblique photographs, why an aerial photograph must be corrected, the meaning of 1:10 000, and what is printed over the photograph.

## Subtopic: Comparing the Two Maps and the Error Museum

The two maps are designed to be used together. The topographic map is the small-scale overview: it covers a large area of about 700 square kilometres in less detail and uses symbols. The orthophoto map is the large-scale close-up: it covers about 28 square kilometres in much more detail and uses the photographic image. A small-scale map shows a large area with little detail; a large-scale map shows a small area with much detail. Because 1:10 000 is a larger fraction than 1:50 000, the orthophoto is the larger-scale map.

In a mapwork paper the two are given as a pair, and on the topographic map the area covered by the orthophoto is outlined with a block. Questions then ask you to find a feature on the orthophoto, identify it from its appearance, find the same feature on the topographic map, and use whichever map is more useful for the task. Distances over many kilometres, the route of a main road between towns and the general relief of a region are read from the topographic map; the shape of a meander, the number of houses in a village or the type of farming in a field are read from the orthophoto.

Each map has limits. A topographic map is generalised and dated: it shows what was there when it was compiled, and a new suburb or dam may be missing. An orthophoto shows only what can be seen from above at the moment the photograph was taken, in that season; it cannot show an underground pipeline, the name of a farm or the boundary between two municipalities unless that information is printed over it.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: 1:50 000 is a larger scale than 1:10 000; it is the other way round, because one fifty-thousandth is a smaller fraction than one ten-thousandth. Two: an orthophoto is just an aerial photograph; it is a corrected photograph with a uniform scale and map information printed on it. Three: the contour interval is the same on both maps; it is 20 metres on the 1:50 000 map and 5 metres on the 1:10 000 orthophoto. Four: 1 centimetre on a 1:50 000 map is 50 kilometres; it is 50 000 centimetres, which is 500 metres. Five: a large-scale map covers a large area; a large-scale map covers a small area in great detail.

The questions for this section are ready: small and large scale, which map to use for which task, the limits of each map, and the five exhibits of the museum.

## Subtopic: Standing on the Hill Above Town

Picture yourself standing on a hill above your town early in the morning. You can see the river curling through the valley, the main road heading out towards the next town, the railway line, the school's sports field, the dam the farmers use and the line of pylons marching over the ridge. Geography is the study of exactly that view: the land, the water, the weather, and what people have built on it and done to it. This year you study it in four parts: maps in Term 1, how rich or poor places are and why in Term 2, how rivers and weather carve the land in Term 3, and how we use and waste the earth's resources in Term 4.

Now, you cannot carry the hill home. So people draw it. A topographic map is a very careful drawing of that view from straight above, with everything shrunk by the same amount and every feature drawn with a standard little symbol. The river is a blue line, the road a coloured line, the buildings small black squares, and the shape of the hills is drawn with brown lines called contours. A key in the margin tells you what each symbol means.

How much is it shrunk? The scale says 1:50 000. That means everything on the ground is 50 000 times bigger than on the map. So 1 centimetre on the map is 50 000 centimetres on the ground, which is 500 metres, about five rugby fields end to end. Two centimetres on the map is one whole kilometre. Hold on to that: two centimetres, one kilometre.

The tests this year are the same every term: you get maps, photos, graphs or short texts and answer questions on them, and sometimes you write a paragraph to explain something. Answer what is asked, show your working, give the unit.

Keep the view from the hill in mind and try the questions for this part: what Geography studies, what a topographic map is, and two centimetres for one kilometre.

## Subtopic: The Phone Camera Pointed Straight Down

Now imagine you fly over the same town in a small aeroplane and hold a phone camera pointing straight down. Click. That is a vertical aerial photograph: the real roofs, the real trees, the real brown river, exactly as a bird sees them. If you had held the phone at an angle, out of the side window, you would get an oblique photograph, with the horizon showing, which is nice to look at but useless for measuring.

Even the straight-down photo has a problem. The hilltop was closer to the camera than the valley, so things on the hill look a little bigger. Things at the edges of the photo lean outwards a little. So you cannot measure accurately on a raw photo. Experts fix this with computers: they stretch and straighten the photo until every part is at exactly the same scale. That corrected photo is called an orthophoto. Ortho means straight, or correct.

Then they print map information on top: the names of places and rivers, a grid, and contour lines, brown lines joining points of the same height, every 5 metres of height instead of every 20. The scale is 1:10 000, so 1 centimetre on the orthophoto is only 100 metres on the ground. That is five times more zoomed-in than the topographic map. On the topographic map a village is a few black dots; on the orthophoto you can count the houses, see which ones have trees in the yard, and see the footpath the children walk to school.

Hold the phone camera in mind and try this part's questions: vertical against oblique, why the photo has to be corrected, and 1 centimetre for 100 metres.

## Subtopic: The Drawing Pinned Next to the Photograph

In an exam, the two are given to you together, like a drawing pinned next to a photograph of the same place. The topographic map is the zoomed-out drawing: a big area, about 25 kilometres across, with less detail and lots of symbols. The orthophoto is the zoomed-in photograph: a small area, about 5 kilometres across, with lots of detail and no symbols, just the real picture. In fact 25 orthophoto sheets fit inside one topographic sheet, five across and five down. On the topographic map, a little block is drawn to show you exactly which piece the orthophoto covers.

Which one do you use? For anything long or big, the distance between two towns, the route of a main road, the general shape of the mountains, use the topographic map. For anything small and close, how many houses there are, what kind of crops are in a field, the exact bend of a river, use the orthophoto.

Here is the trap that catches almost everyone. The orthophoto at 1:10 000 is called the larger-scale map, even though it shows the smaller area. Think of zoom: zooming in makes things look larger. Large scale, zoomed in, small area, lots of detail. Small scale, zoomed out, big area, less detail.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Geography studies the land and what people do with it, and this year starts with maps. The 1:50 000 topographic map is a drawing with symbols, 2 centimetres for a kilometre, contours every 20 metres. The 1:10 000 orthophoto is a corrected aerial photograph, 10 centimetres for a kilometre, contours every 5 metres. Use the drawing for the big picture and the photo for the close-up. In Grade 10 Geography these two maps open the year, and they are in every Paper 2 until you write matric.

Hold the drawing and the photograph side by side and take the final questions of the lesson: which map for which job, large scale against small scale, and the 25 sheets in one.
