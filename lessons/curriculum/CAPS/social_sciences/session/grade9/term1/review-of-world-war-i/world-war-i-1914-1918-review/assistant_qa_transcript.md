### subtopic_1 — The Causes of World War I
**Assistant:** If the assassination was only a trigger, could the war have been avoided?

**Tutor:** Historians still debate it, but most agree that the long-term tensions made a major war likely, while the particular decisions of July 1914 made it happen when and how it did. The assassination itself did not make war inevitable: Austria-Hungary could have punished Serbia in a limited way, and other Balkan crises in 1908 and 1912 to 1913 had been settled by diplomacy. What made July 1914 different was a series of choices. Germany gave Austria-Hungary what is called a blank cheque, a promise of unconditional support, which encouraged Austria-Hungary to send Serbia an ultimatum with demands it expected to be rejected. Russia chose to mobilise its huge army in support of Serbia. Germany's war plan, designed for a two-front war, required it to attack France quickly through Belgium as soon as Russia mobilised, which brought Britain in. Each step was taken by leaders who believed the war would be short, perhaps over by Christmas. So the long-term causes, the arms race, the alliance system, imperial rivalry and nationalism, created a situation in which a local crisis could become a world war, and the decisions made after Sarajevo turned that possibility into reality. A good History essay shows this layering: it explains the long-term causes, the trigger and the decisions in between, and it weighs which mattered most. That skill, explaining causation with evidence and argument, is exactly what the Grade 10 History essay requires.

### subtopic_2 — Trench Warfare and the Western Front
**Assistant:** Why didn't generals just stop attacking if trench attacks kept failing?

**Tutor:** Because they believed that the war could only be won by attacking, that a breakthrough was always one more push away, and because the technology of defence was ahead of the technology of attack in ways they took years to understand. A defender in a trench was protected by earth, wire and machine guns, while the attacker crossed open ground. Artillery bombardments were supposed to destroy the wire and the defenders before an attack, but shells often failed to cut wire, defenders sheltered in deep dugouts, and the bombardment warned that an attack was coming. Commanders also faced political pressure: France wanted the Germans driven off its soil, and allies demanded action to relieve pressure elsewhere, as the Somme was partly launched to relieve the French at Verdun. Some historians argue that the generals were callous and unimaginative; others point out that they were learning a completely new kind of war and that, by 1918, the Allies had developed tactics combining artillery, tanks, aircraft and infantry that did break the deadlock. Both views can be supported with evidence, which is why this is a classic essay debate. The human cost shaped memory: British and French soldiers' poems and memoirs described the futility of the attacks, and after the war many people in Britain and France resolved never to fight such a war again. That feeling, as you will see this term, influenced how Britain and France responded to Hitler in the 1930s.

### subtopic_3 — South Africa in World War I
**Assistant:** Why weren't Black South Africans allowed to carry weapons in World War I?

**Tutor:** Because the white minority government feared that arming Black South Africans and training them as soldiers would threaten white political control at home. The Union of South Africa, formed in 1910, excluded most Black South Africans from the vote and was already passing segregation laws, including the Natives Land Act of 1913, which restricted African land ownership to a small fraction of the country. The government reasoned that men who had fought, and killed white enemy soldiers, would return with skills and expectations of equal treatment. So the official policy was that Black South Africans could serve only as labourers. About 25 000 joined the South African Native Labour Corps for service in France, many hoping that loyal service would win recognition and rights. Leaders of the South African Native National Congress, founded in 1912 and later renamed the African National Congress, supported recruitment for that reason. In France the men were kept in closed compounds and paid less than white soldiers, and after the war they received no campaign medals. The Mendi tragedy came to stand for this unrecognised service. The hope that loyalty would be rewarded was disappointed, and the post-war years saw further segregation. You will meet the Natives Land Act as Topic 7 in Grade 10 History, and the long struggle for equal rights is the subject of Terms 3 and 4 this year.

### subtopic_4 — The End of the War, Its Cost and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do I know whether a source about the war is reliable?

**Tutor:** By asking a set of questions about who made it, when, why and for whom, and then deciding how far it can be trusted for your particular question. Start with the origin. A soldier's letter from Delville Wood in 1916 is a primary source written by an eyewitness, so it can tell you what the fighting felt like, but it was written by one man who saw only a small part of the battle and may have been censored by the army or have softened the truth to protect his family. A recruiting poster from 1915 is a primary source, but its purpose was persuasion: it shows what the government wanted people to believe, not what war was really like, which makes it unreliable as a description of war but very useful as evidence of propaganda. A history textbook is a secondary source, written later with the benefit of many sources and hindsight, but it reflects the author's selection and viewpoint. Reliability is not a fixed property of a source; it depends on the question you ask. Then cross-check: if several independent sources, a letter, a war diary and a photograph, agree, your conclusion is stronger. In a source-based answer, explain your judgement: this source is reliable because it was written by an eyewitness at the time, but limited because it shows only one man's view. That structure is used in every source-based question from Grade 9 to the National Senior Certificate.
