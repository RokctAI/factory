# Part 1 — Expert

This session reviews World War I, fought from 1914 to 1918, as the foundation for the Grade 9 study of the events leading up to World War II. It covers the long-term and short-term causes of the war; the two alliances; the nature of trench warfare and the new weapons on the Western Front; South Africa's participation, including German South West Africa, Delville Wood, the South African Native Labour Corps and the SS Mendi; and the end of the war, its human cost and its consequences. Throughout, it practises the use of historical sources. The anchor example is the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo on 28 June 1914, a single event that set off a chain of declarations across Europe in five weeks. The governing idea is that great events have both deep causes and immediate triggers, and that the settlement of one war can plant the seeds of the next.

## Subtopic: The Causes of World War I

Historians distinguish between long-term causes, the conditions that made war likely, and the short-term cause, the event that started it. For World War I the long-term causes are often remembered as MAIN: militarism, alliances, imperialism and nationalism. Militarism was the belief that a country should build up strong armed forces and be ready to use them; between 1900 and 1914 the great powers expanded their armies, and Britain and Germany raced to build battleships, so that each side felt threatened by the other. Alliances divided Europe into two armed camps: the Triple Alliance of Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy, and the Triple Entente of Britain, France and Russia. A quarrel between two countries could therefore drag in all six.

Imperialism was the competition for colonies and empire. By 1914 Britain and France held vast empires in Africa and Asia; Germany, united only in 1871, had arrived late and wanted more, which caused crises over Morocco in 1905 and 1911. Nationalism was intense pride in one's nation and, among peoples ruled by others, the demand for a nation of their own. In the Balkans, Slavic peoples ruled by Austria-Hungary looked to Serbia and its ally Russia, while Austria-Hungary saw Serbian nationalism as a threat to its survival.

The short-term cause came on 28 June 1914. Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the throne of Austria-Hungary, was shot dead with his wife Sophie in Sarajevo, the capital of Bosnia, by Gavrilo Princip, a young Bosnian Serb nationalist linked to a secret society called the Black Hand. Austria-Hungary, backed by Germany, declared war on Serbia on 28 July. Russia mobilised to defend Serbia; Germany declared war on Russia on 1 August and on France on 3 August, and invaded neutral Belgium to reach France. Britain, pledged to protect Belgium, declared war on Germany on 4 August. Within five weeks of the shooting, the great powers were at war, and their empires with them.

Italy did not join its allies in 1914 and in 1915 entered the war on the side of the Entente. The Ottoman Empire and Bulgaria joined Germany and Austria-Hungary, forming the Central Powers. The Entente and its partners became known as the Allies.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four long-term causes, the assassination at Sarajevo, the chain of declarations, and the two sides.

## Subtopic: Trench Warfare and the Western Front

Germany planned a quick victory in the west, sweeping through Belgium to capture Paris within weeks, before turning east against Russia. The plan failed when French and British forces stopped the German advance at the Battle of the Marne in September 1914. Both sides then dug in, and by the end of 1914 a line of trenches stretched about 700 kilometres from the North Sea coast of Belgium to the border of Switzerland. This was the Western Front, and for more than three years it barely moved.

Trench warfare was a deadlock produced by defensive weapons. Machine guns could fire hundreds of rounds a minute, barbed wire slowed attackers, and heavy artillery could destroy anything above ground. An attack meant climbing out of a trench and crossing no man's land, the ground between the opposing trenches, under fire. At the Battle of the Somme, which began on 1 July 1916, the British army suffered about 57 000 casualties, killed, wounded and missing, on the first day alone; by November the battle had cost more than a million casualties on both sides for a gain of a few kilometres. At Verdun in 1916 the French and Germans fought for ten months with similar losses.

New weapons were introduced to break the deadlock. Poison gas, first used on a large scale by Germany at Ypres in 1915, choked and blinded soldiers until gas masks were issued. Tanks, armoured vehicles that could crush barbed wire and cross trenches, were first used by Britain at the Somme in September 1916. Aircraft moved from scouting to dogfights and bombing. At sea, German submarines, called U-boats, sank merchant ships to starve Britain.

Life in the trenches was miserable: mud, rats, lice, cold, constant shelling and the disease called trench foot. Soldiers' letters, diaries and poems are among the most powerful primary sources of the war, written by people who were there.

This section's questions are ready: the failure of the quick victory, trench deadlock, the Somme, and the new weapons.

## Subtopic: South Africa in World War I

The Union of South Africa, formed in 1910, was a dominion of the British Empire, so when Britain declared war in August 1914 South Africa was at war too. The government of Prime Minister Louis Botha and his deputy Jan Smuts agreed to invade German South West Africa, today Namibia. The decision split white South Africa: some Afrikaners, only twelve years after the South African War against Britain, refused to fight for the British Empire, and in late 1914 a rebellion led by figures such as Christiaan de Wet and Manie Maritz broke out. It was put down by government forces. In 1915 South African troops conquered German South West Africa, whose forces surrendered in July.

South Africans also fought in German East Africa, today Tanzania, in a long campaign against a small German force, and on the Western Front. In July 1916, during the Battle of the Somme, the South African Brigade was ordered to capture and hold Delville Wood in France. For six days, from 15 to 20 July, about 3 000 men held the wood under some of the heaviest shelling of the war; fewer than 800 answered the roll call when they were relieved. Delville Wood became the most remembered South African battle of the war, and a national memorial stands there today.

Black South Africans were not permitted to carry arms in the war, by government policy. About 25 000 men volunteered for the South African Native Labour Corps and served in France, loading ships, building roads and railways and cutting timber, often in segregated camps. On 21 February 1917, the troopship SS Mendi, carrying a battalion of the Corps to France, was struck by another ship, the SS Darro, in thick fog off the Isle of Wight in the English Channel and sank within about twenty minutes. More than 600 men died, most of them members of the Labour Corps. According to accounts passed down from survivors, the Reverend Isaac Wauchope Dyobha called on the men to face death with courage, and the event became a symbol of sacrifice. For decades the Mendi received little official recognition; today South Africa's highest award for bravery is named the Order of Mendi for Bravery.

Coloured South Africans served in the Cape Corps, which fought in East Africa and Palestine, and Indian South Africans served as stretcher-bearers and in labour units. Their service did not bring them political rights after the war.

Take this section's questions now: the dominion at war, the 1914 Rebellion, Delville Wood, the Labour Corps and the Mendi.

## Subtopic: The End of the War, Its Cost and the Error Museum

Two events in 1917 changed the war. In April the United States entered on the side of the Allies, angered by German submarine attacks on shipping and by the Zimmermann Telegram, in which Germany proposed an alliance with Mexico. In Russia, war defeats and hunger led to revolution: the tsar abdicated in March 1917, and in November the Bolsheviks under Lenin seized power. Russia left the war in March 1918 by the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk. Germany launched a final great offensive in the west in spring 1918, but it was halted, and fresh American troops helped the Allies push the Germans back. With its allies collapsing, its people hungry because of the British naval blockade, and mutiny and revolution at home, Germany asked for peace. The Kaiser abdicated, and the armistice, the agreement to stop fighting, took effect at 11 o'clock on 11 November 1918.

The cost was enormous: about 9 million soldiers were killed and millions more wounded, and millions of civilians died from fighting, hunger and disease. Four empires fell: the German, the Austro-Hungarian, the Russian and the Ottoman. New states appeared in central and eastern Europe. In 1919 the victors met at Paris to decide the peace, and the treaty they imposed on Germany, the Treaty of Versailles, is the starting point of the next lesson and of the road to World War II.

A historian works with sources. A primary source was produced at the time by someone involved: a soldier's letter from Delville Wood, a photograph of a trench, a government poster. A secondary source was produced later by someone who studied the past: a textbook, a documentary. Every source has a point of view, so you ask who made it, when, why and for whom, and whether it is reliable and useful for your question.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: the assassination was the only cause of the war; it was the trigger, and the long-term causes made war likely. Two: the war was fought only in Europe; it was fought in Africa, the Middle East and at sea. Three: South Africa stayed neutral; as a British dominion it was at war from August 1914. Four: Black South Africans fought as armed soldiers; they were barred from carrying arms and served as labourers. Five: the war ended with a German surrender in 1919; an armistice ended the fighting on 11 November 1918, and the treaty followed in 1919.

The questions for this section are ready: 1917, the armistice, the cost, primary and secondary sources, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same war again, through a playground fight that pulled in everyone, a muddy ditch with no way forward, and two South African stories worth remembering — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Playground Fight That Pulled In Everyone

Picture a school playground where the learners have split into two big groups of friends who have promised to back each other up. One group is Germany, Austria-Hungary and, at first, Italy. The other is Britain, France and Russia. For years, each group has been showing off its muscles: bigger armies, more battleships. That showing off is militarism. They have also been competing to grab land all over the world, especially in Africa; that grabbing is imperialism. And everyone is fiercely proud of their own group and suspicious of the others; that pride is nationalism. Add the promises, the alliances, and you have the four long-term causes, M A I N: militarism, alliances, imperialism, nationalism.

Now someone throws the first punch. On 28 June 1914, in the city of Sarajevo, a young man named Gavrilo Princip shoots Archduke Franz Ferdinand, the man who will one day rule Austria-Hungary. Princip wants Bosnia to be free of Austria-Hungary and joined with Serbia. Austria-Hungary blames Serbia and declares war on it. Russia steps in to protect Serbia. Germany backs Austria-Hungary and declares war on Russia and France. Germany marches through Belgium to get to France, and Britain, which promised to protect Belgium, joins in. In five weeks, one shooting has pulled almost all of Europe into the fight, and their colonies too.

The shooting was the spark. But the room was already full of gas: the four long-term causes. That is how historians explain any big event: deep causes plus a trigger.

Keep the playground in mind and try the questions for this part: the four letters of MAIN, the shot in Sarajevo, and how promises pulled everyone in.

## Subtopic: Mud, Wire and Machine Guns

Germany thought it would win in a few weeks. It did not. By the end of 1914 both sides had dug long ditches facing each other, called trenches, stretching about 700 kilometres across Belgium and France. That line was the Western Front, and for more than three years it hardly moved.

Why? Because the new weapons were much better at defending than attacking. Imagine climbing out of your ditch and running across the open ground towards the enemy, the strip called no man's land, through barbed wire, while machine guns fire hundreds of bullets a minute at you. On the first day of the Battle of the Somme, 1 July 1916, the British army lost about 57 000 men killed, wounded or missing. In one day.

Both sides tried new tricks to break the deadlock. Poison gas, which choked and blinded. Tanks, metal boxes on tracks that could roll over wire and ditches. Aeroplanes, first for spying, then for fighting and bombing. Submarines that sank ships at sea.

And in between battles, the soldiers lived in the mud with rats, lice, cold and constant shellfire. Their letters home, their diaries and their poems are what historians call primary sources: written at the time by people who were there.

Hold the trench in mind and try this part's questions: why the front did not move, the Somme, and the new weapons.

## Subtopic: Two South African Stories

South Africa was part of the British Empire, so in 1914 it was at war too. First, South African troops invaded the German colony next door, South West Africa, today Namibia, and took it in 1915. Not everyone agreed: some Afrikaners, who had fought against Britain only twelve years earlier, rebelled, and the rebellion was crushed.

Story one: Delville Wood. In July 1916, in France, about 3 000 South African soldiers were ordered to take a small wood and hold it whatever happened. For six days the wood was smashed by shells. When they were finally relieved, fewer than 800 answered the roll call. A memorial stands there today.

Story two: the SS Mendi. Black South Africans were not allowed to carry guns in this war. About 25 000 went to France anyway, in the South African Native Labour Corps, to load ships and build roads. On 21 February 1917, in thick fog in the English Channel, another ship smashed into the Mendi, and it sank in about twenty minutes. More than 600 men died. For many years their sacrifice was hardly mentioned. Today South Africa's highest award for bravery is called the Order of Mendi.

The war ended at 11 o'clock on 11 November 1918. About 9 million soldiers were dead. Four empires had collapsed. And Germany, defeated, was about to be punished, which is where our next lesson begins.

South Africa at war as part of the empire: South West Africa, Delville Wood, the Labour Corps and the Mendi. The war ended in 1918 with an armistice.
