### subtopic_1 — Early ANC Methods and the Youth League
**Assistant:** Why did young ANC members feel that petitions and deputations had failed?

**Tutor:** By the 1940s, the ANC had been using petitions, letters and deputations for about thirty years, and young members could see that these methods had achieved almost nothing. The deputations to Britain in 1914 and 1919 were politely received but sent home without any change; Britain said that South Africa was a self-governing country and that it would not interfere. At home, the government continued to pass discriminatory laws regardless of the ANC's objections. The Natives Land Act of 1913 stayed in place, the Natives (Urban Areas) Act of 1923 restricted black people in towns, and in 1936 the Hertzog government removed black voters in the Cape from the common voters' roll, taking away one of the few political rights black South Africans still had. Petitions were easy for the government to ignore, because they did not cost it anything. The young members also criticised the ANC's structure. It was led by a small group of educated, middle-class men who often met only once a year and had little contact with ordinary workers, farmworkers and the urban poor, so it lacked the support of large numbers of people. The Youth League, influenced by Anton Lembede's African nationalism, argued that Africans must stop appealing to the goodwill of the government and instead build their own power through unity and mass action. They believed that only by involving millions of ordinary people, who could withdraw their labour and cooperation, could the liberation movement put real pressure on the state. Their frustration was increased by the election of the National Party in 1948, which made it clear that the situation would get worse, not better, unless the movement changed its approach.

### subtopic_2 — The Programme of Action of 1949
**Assistant:** How did civil disobedience differ from the ANC's earlier methods?

**Tutor:** The ANC's earlier methods, such as petitions, deputations and letters, all worked within the law and depended on the government being willing to listen and change. Civil disobedience was different in several important ways. First, it involved deliberately breaking laws that were considered unjust, such as pass laws or segregation regulations, openly and publicly. Second, it was non-violent: participants did not attack people or property, and they accepted arrest and punishment rather than trying to escape it. Third, it depended on large numbers of people, so it turned the struggle from the work of a few leaders into a mass movement. The idea behind civil disobedience was that unjust laws only work if people obey them. If thousands of people refuse to obey and fill the prisons, the courts and police are overwhelmed and the laws become difficult or impossible to enforce. Civil disobedience also had a moral power. By accepting punishment peacefully, protesters showed that they were prepared to suffer for their beliefs, exposed the injustice of the laws to the world, and challenged the conscience of those who supported them. The method had a long history in South Africa. Mahatma Gandhi had developed his approach of satyagraha, or soul force, while living in South Africa, leading Indian South Africans in passive resistance campaigns between 1906 and 1914, and the South African Indian Congress had used passive resistance again in 1946 to 1948. The ANC's Programme of Action brought this method into the African liberation struggle, and it was put into practice most fully in the Defiance Campaign of 1952, which is the subject of the next lesson.

### subtopic_3 — The Suppression of Communism Act of 1950
**Assistant:** What was it actually like to be banned?

**Tutor:** A banning order was a document issued by the Minister of Justice, without any trial, that placed severe restrictions on a person's life, usually for two or five years at a time, and it could be renewed again and again. The exact restrictions varied, but a banned person was typically forbidden to attend any gathering, which could be defined as a meeting of more than one other person at a time for a common purpose, so even a social visit or a family celebration could be illegal. They were often confined to a particular magisterial district, such as their town, and had to report regularly to a police station. They were forced to resign from political organisations, trade unions and sometimes even from jobs, such as teaching or journalism. Nothing they said or wrote could be published or quoted in South Africa, so they effectively disappeared from public life. They might be forbidden to enter factories, schools, universities or black townships. Some were later placed under house arrest, which confined them to their homes for many hours each day, or all day at weekends. Breaking a banning order was a criminal offence that could lead to imprisonment. Banning was intended to silence leaders without making them into martyrs through public trials. It caused great hardship: banned people often lost their income, were isolated from friends and colleagues, and were constantly watched by the security police. Their families also suffered. Albert Luthuli, Nelson Mandela, Lilian Ngoyi, Helen Joseph and Winnie Madikizela-Mandela were among the many who were banned, some for many years. Despite this, many banned people found ways to continue their political work secretly, at great risk to themselves.

### subtopic_4 — The Protests of 1950, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why is 26 June an important date in South African history?

**Tutor:** The date of 26 June became one of the most important days in the calendar of the liberation movement because several major events took place on it, deliberately chosen to build a tradition of resistance. The first was the National Day of Protest and Mourning on 26 June 1950. It was called by the ANC, the South African Indian Congress and other organisations to protest against the Suppression of Communism Act and to mourn the eighteen people killed by police after the May Day strike of 1 May 1950. Workers across the country were asked to stay at home, and the response was strong in several cities. It was one of the first national actions carried out under the Programme of Action, and it showed that different organisations could work together. Two years later, on 26 June 1952, the ANC and the South African Indian Congress launched the Defiance Campaign against Unjust Laws, the largest campaign of non-violent civil disobedience in South African history to that point. Then, on 26 June 1955, the Congress of the People, meeting at Kliptown near Johannesburg, adopted the Freedom Charter, which became the guiding document of the Congress Alliance. For decades, the liberation movement marked 26 June as South African Freedom Day. After 1994, the democratic government chose 27 April, the date of the first democratic election, as the official Freedom Day public holiday, but 26 June is still remembered by many as an important anniversary of the struggle. The story of 26 June shows how the liberation movement used symbols, dates and repeated action to build unity and keep the memory of resistance alive.
