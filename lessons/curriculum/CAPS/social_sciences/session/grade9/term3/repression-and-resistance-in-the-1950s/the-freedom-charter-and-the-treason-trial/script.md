# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the Freedom Charter of 1955 and the Treason Trial of 1956 to 1961. It covers the formation of the Congress Alliance and how ordinary people's demands were collected for the Congress of the People; the Congress of the People at Kliptown on 25 and 26 June 1955 and the main clauses of the Freedom Charter; the different responses to the Charter, including support, government hostility and criticism by Africanists; and the Treason Trial, from the arrests of December 1956 to the acquittal of all the accused in March 1961. The anchor idea is that the Freedom Charter was a vision of a free, democratic and non-racial South Africa, drawn up from the demands of ordinary people. The governing idea is that the apartheid government saw this peaceful vision as so threatening that it tried to prove in court that it amounted to treason, and failed.

## Subtopic: The Congress Alliance and the Call for Demands

After the Defiance Campaign, the organisations that opposed apartheid worked more closely together. By 1955, they had formed the Congress Alliance. It was made up of the African National Congress; the South African Indian Congress; the South African Coloured People's Organisation, later renamed the Coloured People's Congress; the South African Congress of Democrats, a small organisation of white opponents of apartheid; and, from its formation in March 1955, the South African Congress of Trade Unions, which organised workers of all races. Each organisation represented a different community, but they shared a commitment to a non-racial, democratic South Africa.

The idea of a Congress of the People was proposed in 1953 by Professor Z.K. Matthews, a respected ANC leader and academic at Fort Hare University. He suggested a national convention of the people of South Africa, of all races, to draw up a Freedom Charter for the democratic South Africa of the future. Because the existing Parliament represented only white voters, this would be a people's parliament, giving a voice to everyone.

In 1954 and 1955, the Congress Alliance launched a nationwide campaign to collect the demands of ordinary people. Thousands of volunteers, known as freedom volunteers, went from door to door in townships, villages, farms, factories and hostels, held meetings and handed out leaflets with the question: "If you could make the laws, what would you do?" People wrote their demands on scraps of paper, in exercise books and on the backs of leaflets, in many languages. They asked for land, for votes, for an end to passes, for decent houses, schools, wages and hospitals, for freedom to move and to work, and for equality. Thousands of these demands were sent to the organisers.

A committee then combined these demands into a single document, the draft Freedom Charter. Historians note that the final drafting was done by a small group, with members of the Congress of Democrats, including Lionel "Rusty" Bernstein, playing a leading role in writing the text, and that the ANC's national executive did not see the final draft before the Congress. This later became a point of criticism. Nevertheless, the Charter reflected many of the demands collected across the country.

So the next question is: what happened at Kliptown, and what does the Charter say?

## Subtopic: The Congress of the People and the Charter's Clauses

The Congress of the People met on 25 and 26 June 1955 on an open piece of ground in Kliptown, a township south-west of Johannesburg. About three thousand delegates attended, from all over the country and from all racial groups, along with thousands of observers. Many travelled long distances, despite police roadblocks that turned some back. Several important leaders, including Chief Albert Luthuli, Walter Sisulu and Nelson Mandela, were under banning orders and could not take part openly; Mandela and Sisulu watched from the edge of the crowd.

On the first day, the Congress awarded its highest honour, Isitwalandwe, named after the feather worn by the bravest warriors, to three people who had made outstanding contributions to the struggle: Chief Albert Luthuli, Dr Yusuf Dadoo of the Indian Congress, and Father Trevor Huddleston of Sophiatown.

The Charter was read out section by section, in English, Sesotho and isiXhosa, and each section was discussed and adopted by a show of hands and shouts of "Afrika!" and "Mayibuye!". On the afternoon of the second day, armed police surrounded the meeting, announced that they suspected treason, confiscated documents and banners, and took the names and addresses of every delegate. The delegates stayed calm, and the final sections of the Charter were adopted.

The Freedom Charter begins with a preamble declaring that "South Africa belongs to all who live in it, black and white, and that no government can justly claim authority unless it is based on the will of all the people". It then sets out ten main demands, each beginning with a short heading:

The People Shall Govern: every man and woman shall have the right to vote and to stand for election.

All National Groups Shall have Equal Rights: equal status in government, courts and schools, and the right to use one's own language and culture.

The People Shall Share in the Country's Wealth: the mineral wealth, the banks and monopoly industry shall be transferred to the ownership of the people as a whole.

The Land Shall be Shared Among Those Who Work It: an end to restrictions on land ownership on a racial basis.

All Shall be Equal Before the Law: no imprisonment without a fair trial.

All Shall Enjoy Equal Human Rights: freedom of speech, assembly, religion and movement, and an end to pass laws.

There Shall be Work and Security: fair wages, a forty-hour week, and the right to form trade unions.

The Doors of Learning and of Culture Shall be Opened: free, compulsory and equal education.

There Shall be Houses, Security and Comfort: decent housing, health care and an end to forced removals.

There Shall be Peace and Friendship: South Africa shall be a fully independent state that respects the rights of all nations.

The Charter ends with the words: "These freedoms we will fight for, side by side, throughout our lives, until we have won our liberty."

So the next question is: how did different groups respond to the Charter?

## Subtopic: Responses to the Charter

The ANC formally adopted the Freedom Charter at a special conference in 1956, and the other members of the Congress Alliance also adopted it. For the next forty years, it was the guiding document of the ANC and its allies, and it inspired generations of activists. Many of its ideas, such as equal voting rights, equality before the law, freedom of movement and expression, and rights to education, housing and health, were later reflected in the Constitution of 1996.

The government reacted with hostility. It saw the Charter's demands for majority rule and for transferring mines, banks and monopoly industry to the people as communist, and therefore, in its view, as part of a plan to overthrow the state. The police raid on the Congress of the People had already shown that the government regarded the gathering as possibly treasonous.

The Charter was also criticised from within the liberation movement. A group within the ANC known as the Africanists, influenced by the ideas of Anton Lembede and led by people such as Robert Sobukwe and Potlako Leballo, objected to it for several reasons. They rejected the statement that South Africa belongs to all who live in it, black and white, arguing that the land had been taken from Africans by conquest and should be returned to the indigenous African majority. They believed Africans should lead their own liberation without relying on alliances with other groups, and they were suspicious that white communists in the Congress of Democrats had too much influence over the Charter's wording. Some liberals, on the other hand, worried about the economic clauses calling for nationalisation. These debates contributed to the split that led to the formation of the Pan Africanist Congress in 1959, which you will study in the next lessons.

Historians today also debate the Charter. Some emphasise that it was a genuinely popular document, built from thousands of demands collected from ordinary people. Others point out that the final wording was written by a small group. Both points can be true: it reflected popular demands, shaped into a document by a few drafters.

So the final question is: what was the Treason Trial?

## Subtopic: The Treason Trial, and the Error Museum

Early on the morning of 5 December 1956, police across the country arrested 156 leaders of the Congress Alliance, and charged them with high treason, a crime that could carry the death penalty. The accused included 105 Africans, 21 Indians, 23 whites and 7 coloured people. Among them were Chief Albert Luthuli, Nelson Mandela, Oliver Tambo, Walter Sisulu, Lilian Ngoyi, Helen Joseph, Z.K. Matthews, Ahmed Kathrada, Joe Slovo and many others. They were flown or driven to Johannesburg and held in the Fort prison.

The state claimed that the accused were part of a nationwide conspiracy, inspired by communism, to overthrow the state by violence and replace it with a communist state. Much of the evidence consisted of the Freedom Charter, speeches made at meetings, and thousands of documents seized in police raids, including leaflets, posters and even signs from the Congress of the People reading "Soup with meat" and "Soup without meat".

The preparatory examination, to decide whether there was enough evidence for a trial, began in December 1956 at the Drill Hall in Johannesburg. Large crowds gathered outside singing "Senzeni na?", meaning "What have we done?". Supporters, including Bishop Ambrose Reeves and Alan Paton, set up a Treason Trial Defence Fund to pay for lawyers and support the families of the accused, many of whom lost their jobs. Charges against most of the accused were gradually dropped, including Luthuli and Tambo in 1957, until the main trial began in August 1958 in Pretoria, and from 1959 it focused on 30 accused.

The trial dragged on for more than four years. The defence, led by advocates such as Israel Maisels and Bram Fischer, argued that the Congress Alliance had always used non-violent methods, and the state could not prove that the accused had planned violence or that the Freedom Charter was a communist document. On 29 March 1961, the three judges found all the remaining accused not guilty. The presiding judge, Justice Frans Rumpff, stated that the state had failed to prove that the ANC had adopted a policy of violence or that it was a communist organisation.

The acquittal was a victory for the accused and a humiliation for the government. However, the trial had kept many leaders tied up in court for years, drained the resources of the movement and disrupted their lives. By the time it ended, the political situation had changed dramatically: the Sharpeville massacre had taken place in March 1960, and the ANC and PAC had been banned. Within months, Mandela and others would conclude that non-violent methods alone could no longer succeed.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "The Freedom Charter was written by a few leaders without consulting anyone." The demands were collected from thousands of ordinary people, though the final wording was drafted by a small group.

Exhibit two: "The Charter said South Africa belongs only to one racial group." It declared that South Africa belongs to all who live in it, black and white.

Exhibit three: "The Treason Trial ended with the accused being convicted." All the remaining accused were found not guilty on 29 March 1961.

Exhibit four: "Everyone in the liberation movement supported the Charter." The Africanists rejected parts of it, which contributed to the formation of the PAC in 1959.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a question on a scrap of paper, a field at Kliptown, and a trial that went on and on.

## Subtopic: A Question on a Scrap of Paper

Imagine someone knocking on your door and asking: "If you could make the laws, what would you do?"

In 1954 and 1955, thousands of volunteers did exactly that, all over South Africa. They visited townships, villages, farms and factories. People wrote their answers on scraps of paper, in notebooks and on the backs of leaflets, in many languages.

They wanted the vote. They wanted land. They wanted passes to end. They wanted good schools, houses, hospitals and fair wages. They wanted to be treated as equals.

The people collecting the answers belonged to the Congress Alliance, a team of organisations for African, Indian, coloured and white South Africans, and for workers. All of them dreamed of a South Africa without apartheid.

So what did they do with all those answers?

## Subtopic: A Field at Kliptown

On 25 and 26 June 1955, about three thousand people met on an open field at Kliptown, near Johannesburg. It was called the Congress of the People.

The answers had been turned into one document, the Freedom Charter. It was read out, part by part, and the crowd cheered each one.

It begins: South Africa belongs to all who live in it, black and white. Then it says: the people shall govern. Everyone shall be equal before the law. The land shall be shared. The doors of learning shall be opened. There shall be houses, work and peace.

On the second afternoon, armed police surrounded the field and wrote down everyone's name. But the people finished adopting the Charter.

Not everyone in the struggle agreed with it. Some believed Africans should lead on their own and that the land belonged to the African majority. They later formed their own organisation, the PAC.

So how did the government respond?

## Subtopic: A Trial That Went On and On

In December 1956, police arrested 156 people, including Albert Luthuli, Nelson Mandela, Lilian Ngoyi and Helen Joseph. They were charged with treason, which means trying to overthrow the government, and could have been sentenced to death.

The government said the Freedom Charter was part of a violent communist plot. The accused said they had always worked peacefully for equal rights for everyone.

The trial went on for more than four years. Slowly, most of the accused were released. Finally, on 29 March 1961, the judges declared all of the remaining accused not guilty.

It was a victory, and the crowd outside the court sang with joy. But by then the government had banned the ANC, and the struggle was about to change.

Here is the line to remember: the Freedom Charter of 1955 gathered ordinary people's dreams into a vision of a South Africa that belongs to all who live in it, and the government's attempt to call it treason ended in 1961 with every accused found not guilty.
