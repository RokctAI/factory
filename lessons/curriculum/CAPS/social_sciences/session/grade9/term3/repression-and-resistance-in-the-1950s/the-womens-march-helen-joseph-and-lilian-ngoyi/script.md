# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the Women's March of 9 August 1956 and the roles of Lilian Ngoyi and Helen Joseph in resistance to apartheid. It covers the history of women's resistance to passes and the reasons women opposed the extension of pass laws in the 1950s; the formation of the Federation of South African Women and the Women's Charter of 1954; the march to the Union Buildings in Pretoria on 9 August 1956 and its significance; and the lives, leadership and sacrifices of Lilian Ngoyi and Helen Joseph. The anchor idea is that black women faced a double oppression, of race and of gender, and that the extension of passes to women threatened their families, their homes and their ability to earn a living. The governing idea is that women were not followers in the struggle against apartheid, but organisers and leaders, and that the Women's March became one of its most powerful symbols.

## Subtopic: Women and the Pass Laws

For most of the early twentieth century, black men in South Africa had to carry passes, but black women generally did not. This was partly because of an early victory. In 1913, the authorities in the Orange Free State tried to force black women to carry passes. Black and coloured women in Bloemfontein and other towns protested, refused to carry passes and went to prison rather than pay fines. Their campaign, in which Charlotte Maxeke was a leading figure, was so determined that the authorities eventually backed down. It was one of the first successful campaigns of civil disobedience in South Africa.

In 1952, the Natives (Abolition of Passes and Co-ordination of Documents) Act introduced a single reference book for black people, and it made provision for black women to carry these books too. The government announced that women would be issued with reference books from 1956. For black women, this was a terrifying prospect, for several reasons.

First, the pass laws were used to control where black people could live and work. Under influx control, a black person needed permission to live in an urban area, and those without the right documents could be sent away to the reserves. Many women lived in towns with their husbands or worked as domestic workers, and passes would allow the authorities to separate them from their families and remove them from the cities.

Second, women had seen what passes did to men. Men were stopped and searched in the streets, arrested in night raids and jailed for not producing their passes. Women feared being arrested in the same way, leaving their children alone at home with no one to care for them.

Third, passes threatened women's ability to earn a living. Many women supported their families through domestic work, trading, brewing beer or other informal work, often without official permission, and passes would expose them to arrest.

Fourth, women experienced a double oppression. As black people, they suffered racial discrimination under apartheid. As women, they also faced discrimination under customary and common law, which often treated them as legal minors under the authority of their fathers or husbands, unable to own property or sign contracts in their own right.

So the next question is: how did women organise to resist?

## Subtopic: The Federation of South African Women

Women had long been active in political organisations. The ANC Women's League was formed in 1948, and women had taken part in large numbers in the Defiance Campaign of 1952. However, many women felt that the existing organisations, led mostly by men, did not give enough attention to women's issues.

On 17 April 1954, about 150 delegates, representing some 230 000 women from different organisations and all racial groups, met in Johannesburg to form the Federation of South African Women, known as FEDSAW. Its founders included Ray Alexander, a trade unionist; Lilian Ngoyi of the ANC Women's League; Helen Joseph of the Congress of Democrats; Amina Cachalia of the Transvaal Indian Congress; and Florence Mkhize and Frances Baard, among others. FEDSAW was a non-racial organisation, which brought together African, Indian, coloured and white women, and it became part of the Congress Alliance.

At its founding conference, FEDSAW adopted the Women's Charter. It demanded equal rights for women and men, including the right to vote, equal pay for equal work, equal rights in property, marriage and children, and the removal of laws that discriminated against women. It also called for the end of all racial discrimination and for women to take part fully in the struggle for national liberation. The Women's Charter linked women's freedom to the freedom of the whole nation, and it influenced the Freedom Charter of 1955.

As the government began issuing passes to women, FEDSAW and the ANC Women's League organised protests across the country. In October 1955, about two thousand women marched to the Union Buildings in Pretoria to protest against passes and other laws. Women in towns and rural areas held meetings, burned passes and protested at the offices where passes were issued. The organisers decided to plan a much larger national protest the following year.

So the next question is: what happened on 9 August 1956?

## Subtopic: The March to the Union Buildings

In the months before the march, organisers travelled the country and collected signatures on petitions against the pass laws. Women raised money for transport, and many travelled long distances by train, bus and on foot. The government tried to prevent the march: it banned gatherings, and railway officials refused to sell tickets to some groups of women, but women found other ways to travel to Pretoria.

On 9 August 1956, about twenty thousand women gathered in the amphitheatre of the Union Buildings in Pretoria, the seat of government. They came from all races and all walks of life: domestic workers, factory workers, nurses, teachers, farmworkers and housewives. Many African women wore traditional dress or the green, black and gold of the Congress, and some carried babies on their backs or brought the children of the white families they worked for.

The leaders of the march were Lilian Ngoyi, Helen Joseph, Rahima Moosa and Sophie Williams, who represented African, white, Indian and coloured women. They carried thousands of petitions, with more than one hundred thousand signatures, to the office of the Prime Minister, J.G. Strijdom, who had succeeded D.F. Malan in 1954. Strijdom was not in his office, and the leaders left the petitions with an official.

The leaders returned to the amphitheatre, and Lilian Ngoyi asked the women to stand in silence. For thirty minutes, twenty thousand women stood completely still and silent, many with their right fists raised in the Congress salute. Then they began to sing freedom songs, including a new song composed for the occasion. Its words included "Strijdom, wathint' abafazi, wathint' imbokodo, uzakufa", meaning "Strijdom, you have struck the women, you have struck a rock, you will be crushed". The march ended peacefully, and the women dispersed.

The march did not stop the government from extending passes to women. Resistance continued for years; there were further protests, and in some rural areas, such as Zeerust in 1957, women's resistance to passes was met with violent repression. By 1963, carrying passes was compulsory for black women. However, the march had enormous significance. It was one of the largest demonstrations in South African history to that time, it showed the discipline, courage and unity of women of all races, and it challenged the idea that women were passive or uninterested in politics. Today, 9 August is celebrated as National Women's Day, and the whole month of August is Women's Month. The words "you strike a woman, you strike a rock" have become a symbol of women's strength.

The march also showed how women's activism had grown out of their everyday lives. Many of the women who organised in the townships were members of church groups, burial societies, stokvels and trade unions, which gave them experience in organising meetings, collecting money and supporting one another. These networks made it possible to mobilise women across the country, often with very few resources. Women's resistance was not limited to passes; in the 1950s, women also led boycotts of Bantu Education, protested against municipal beerhalls that competed with home brewers, as at Cato Manor in Durban in 1959, and resisted cattle culling and other rural controls.

So the final question is: who were Lilian Ngoyi and Helen Joseph?

## Subtopic: Lilian Ngoyi and Helen Joseph, and the Error Museum

Lilian Ngoyi was born in Pretoria in 1911. Her family was poor, and she left school early, trained briefly as a nurse and later worked as a machinist in a clothing factory, where she joined the Garment Workers' Union and became a trade union leader. She joined the ANC during the Defiance Campaign in 1952 and was arrested for using facilities reserved for whites. She was a powerful and inspiring public speaker, known as "Ma Ngoyi". She rose quickly, becoming president of the ANC Women's League, and in 1956 she became the first woman elected to the ANC's National Executive Committee. She was also elected president of FEDSAW. She travelled overseas in 1955 to attend a women's conference in Switzerland and visited several countries, including China and the Soviet Union, despite not having a passport.

After the Women's March, Ngoyi was arrested in December 1956 and was one of the accused in the Treason Trial until her acquittal. She was detained during the state of emergency of 1960, spending much of her detention in solitary confinement. From 1962, she was banned almost continuously for the rest of her life, confined to Orlando in Soweto, where she supported her family by sewing. She died in 1980. A street in Pretoria and many other places are now named after her.

Helen Joseph was born Helen Fennell in England in 1905. She studied at the University of London, taught in India and came to South Africa in 1931, where she married. During World War II, she served as a welfare officer in the women's air force. Afterwards, she worked as a social worker in coloured communities in Cape Town and Johannesburg and became secretary of the Garment Workers' Union's medical aid society. Seeing the effects of apartheid at first hand turned her into an activist. She was a founder member of the Congress of Democrats and became national secretary of FEDSAW.

Joseph was also one of the accused in the Treason Trial. In 1962, she became the first person in South Africa to be placed under house arrest under the Sabotage Act, confining her to her home for long periods each day for many years. She survived assassination attempts and constant harassment, yet she continued her work, including travelling thousands of kilometres in 1962 to visit and document the conditions of people banished to remote parts of the country. She died in 1992, shortly after apartheid laws were repealed.

Both women showed extraordinary courage, both suffered banning, imprisonment and isolation, and both worked across racial lines for a non-racial South Africa. Their friendship and partnership on 9 August 1956 symbolised the unity of women of different backgrounds.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Black women always carried passes." Women successfully resisted passes from 1913, and passes were only extended to women from the 1950s.

Exhibit two: "The Women's March stopped passes for women." The government continued to issue passes, which became compulsory for black women by 1963.

Exhibit three: "Only African women took part in the march." Women of all races took part, and its leaders were African, white, Indian and coloured.

Exhibit four: "Women only supported men in the struggle." Women organised and led campaigns, and Lilian Ngoyi was the first woman on the ANC National Executive Committee.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a little book that could break a family, twenty thousand women in silence, and a rock that would not crack.

## Subtopic: A Little Book That Could Break a Family

Under apartheid, black men had to carry a pass book everywhere. If a policeman stopped them and they did not have it, they could be arrested and sent to jail.

For a long time, black women did not have to carry passes. Back in 1913, women in Bloemfontein had refused to carry them, and they won.

But in the 1950s, the government decided that black women must carry passes too. Women were frightened. A pass could be used to throw a woman out of the city and away from her husband and children. She could be arrested on her way to work and leave her babies alone at home. She could lose her job.

Black women faced two kinds of unfairness at once: they were treated badly because of their race and because they were women.

So women decided to fight back together. In 1954, they formed the Federation of South African Women, with members of every race.

## Subtopic: Twenty Thousand Women in Silence

On 9 August 1956, about twenty thousand women came to the Union Buildings in Pretoria, where the government worked. They came by train, by bus and on foot, from all over the country. Some carried babies on their backs.

Four leaders, Lilian Ngoyi, Helen Joseph, Rahima Moosa and Sophie Williams, carried piles of petitions, signed by many thousands of people, to the Prime Minister's office. He was not there, so they left the petitions behind.

Then something amazing happened. Lilian Ngoyi asked the crowd to stand in silence. For thirty minutes, twenty thousand women stood completely still, without a sound, many with their fists raised.

Then they sang: "You strike a woman, you strike a rock."

So what happened to the leaders?

## Subtopic: A Rock That Would Not Crack

The march did not stop the passes. But it showed the whole country that women were strong, united and brave, and that they would not be pushed aside.

Lilian Ngoyi was a factory worker and a brilliant speaker, called Ma Ngoyi. She became the first woman on the ANC's top committee. The government arrested her, put her in solitary confinement and banned her for most of her life. She never gave up, and she kept encouraging younger activists.

Helen Joseph was born in England, became a social worker in South Africa and was horrified by apartheid. She became the first person in South Africa placed under house arrest. People tried to kill her, but she kept going.

Today, 9 August is National Women's Day in South Africa, and August is Women's Month.

Here is the line to remember: on 9 August 1956, twenty thousand women of all races marched against passes for women, and leaders like Lilian Ngoyi and Helen Joseph showed that you strike a woman, you strike a rock.
