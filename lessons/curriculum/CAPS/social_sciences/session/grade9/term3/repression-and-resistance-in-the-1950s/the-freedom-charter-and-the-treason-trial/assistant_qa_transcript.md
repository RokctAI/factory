### subtopic_1 — The Congress Alliance and the Call for Demands
**Assistant:** Why was collecting demands from ordinary people so important for the Freedom Charter?

**Tutor:** Collecting demands from ordinary people was important for several reasons, both practical and symbolic. First, it gave the Charter legitimacy. The apartheid Parliament represented only white voters, so it could not claim to speak for the majority. By asking people across the country what laws they would make, the Congress Alliance could claim that the Charter expressed the will of the people, which was exactly what its preamble said a just government must be based on. Second, the campaign itself was a powerful form of political education and organisation. Thousands of freedom volunteers went from door to door, held meetings in townships, villages, farms, factories and hostels, and spoke with people about their grievances and hopes. This spread the message of the Congress Alliance, recruited new members and built networks of activists across the country, including in rural areas that earlier campaigns had not reached. Third, the demands made the Charter concrete and relevant to daily life. People did not just ask for abstract freedom; they asked for land, an end to passes, decent houses, schools, clinics, fair wages, and the right to move and work freely. The Charter's clauses reflected these practical concerns. Fourth, it involved people who were usually excluded, such as women, farmworkers, domestic workers and migrant workers, whose voices were rarely heard. Historians have also noted that the final text was drafted by a small group, so the Charter was a combination of popular demands and the ideas of the drafters. Even so, the process of collecting demands made the Congress of the People one of the most democratic events in South African history up to that time, and it explains why so many people felt that the Charter belonged to them.

### subtopic_2 — The Congress of the People and the Charter's Clauses
**Assistant:** Why did the Africanists object to the Freedom Charter?

**Tutor:** The Africanists were a group within the ANC who were strongly influenced by the African nationalism of Anton Lembede and the ANC Youth League. Their leaders included Robert Sobukwe, Potlako Leballo and others, and they were particularly strong in the Transvaal. They objected to the Freedom Charter for several connected reasons. The most important concerned the opening statement that South Africa belongs to all who live in it, black and white. The Africanists argued that the land of South Africa had been taken from its African inhabitants through colonial conquest, and that it belonged to the indigenous African people. In their view, the Charter's statement legitimised that conquest by giving equal claims to those who had benefited from it. Second, they objected to the Charter's idea of separate national groups, African, Indian, coloured and white, each represented by its own organisation in the Congress Alliance. They argued that this reflected apartheid thinking about separate races, and that there should simply be one African nation, to which anyone who gave their loyalty to Africa and accepted majority rule could belong. Third, they believed that Africans must lead their own liberation, relying on their own strength rather than on alliances with other groups. Fourth, they were suspicious of the influence of white communists, especially members of the Congress of Democrats, on the ANC and on the drafting of the Charter, and feared that the struggle was being shaped by an outside ideology. These disagreements grew sharper, and in 1958 the Africanists broke away. In April 1959, they formed the Pan Africanist Congress, with Robert Sobukwe as its president. The ANC and its supporters argued, in turn, that non-racialism was the only basis for a just future South Africa. This debate remains an important part of South African political history.

### subtopic_3 — Responses to the Charter
**Assistant:** Why did the government lose the Treason Trial?

**Tutor:** The government lost the Treason Trial mainly because it could not prove the central part of its case. The charge of high treason required the state to show that the accused had intended to overthrow the state by violence. The state argued that the Congress Alliance was part of a communist-inspired conspiracy to use violence, and it presented thousands of documents and speeches seized in police raids, as well as the Freedom Charter itself, as evidence. However, the evidence did not show a policy of violence. The ANC and its allies had consistently used non-violent methods, such as the Defiance Campaign, boycotts and stay-aways, and their leaders had repeatedly told their followers to avoid violence. Many of the seized documents were ordinary leaflets, minutes and posters, and the state relied heavily on police officers who had taken notes of speeches at meetings, often inaccurately, because some did not understand the languages being spoken. The defence team, which included some of the most able lawyers in the country, such as Israel Maisels, Bram Fischer and Sydney Kentridge, cross-examined state witnesses effectively and exposed weaknesses in the evidence. The state's expert witness on communism was also undermined, for example when he was shown to identify passages from his own writings as communist. Some accused, such as Nelson Mandela, gave evidence explaining the aims and non-violent methods of the ANC. The judges, led by Justice Frans Rumpff, concluded that the state had not proved that the ANC had adopted a policy to overthrow the state by violence, or that the Freedom Charter was a communist document. The verdict showed that, despite apartheid, the courts could still sometimes act independently, although Parliament later reduced their power through new security laws.

### subtopic_4 — The Treason Trial, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Did the Treason Trial help or harm the liberation movement?

**Tutor:** The Treason Trial both helped and harmed the liberation movement, and a balanced answer must consider both sides. On the one hand, it caused serious harm. For more than four years, many of the most experienced leaders of the Congress Alliance were tied up in court, first in Johannesburg and then in Pretoria. They had to attend court for long periods, which made it very difficult to lead their organisations, plan campaigns or earn a living. Many lost their jobs or businesses, and their families suffered financially. The trial drained the movement's money and energy. It also coincided with a period in which the government continued to pass repressive laws and in which the movement faced internal divisions, such as the Africanist breakaway in 1959. On the other hand, the trial had important benefits for the movement. Bringing so many leaders together for years gave them time to discuss strategy and strengthened the bonds between people of different races and organisations. Nelson Mandela later wrote that the trial helped to unite the leadership. The Treason Trial Defence Fund gathered support from churches, liberals and people overseas, and the trial attracted international attention to apartheid, presenting the accused as dignified people fighting for democracy. Most importantly, the final verdict was a public humiliation for the government. The court rejected the claim that the Freedom Charter was a communist document and that the ANC was committed to violence, which gave the movement moral credibility. However, the victory came at a time when the ANC had already been banned, after Sharpeville in 1960, and soon afterwards its leaders decided to begin an armed struggle. So the trial's legacy is mixed.
