# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the banning of the Communist Party and the ANC's Programme of Action at the start of the 1950s. It covers the early methods of the ANC and why young members became frustrated with them; the formation of the ANC Youth League and the adoption of the Programme of Action in 1949; the Suppression of Communism Act of 1950, the banning of the Communist Party and how the Act was used against opponents of apartheid; and the protests of 1950, including the May Day strike and the National Day of Protest on 26 June, which began a decade of mass, non-violent resistance. The anchor idea is that the early 1950s marked a turning point, when the liberation movement moved from polite appeals to mass action. The governing idea is that resistance and repression grew together: each new form of protest was met with new laws and harsher state power.

## Subtopic: Early ANC Methods and the Youth League

The South African Native National Congress was founded in Bloemfontein on 8 January 1912, partly in response to the Union of 1910, which had excluded most black South Africans from political power. It was renamed the African National Congress, or ANC, in 1923. Its early leaders, such as John Dube, its first president, Pixley ka Isaka Seme and Sol Plaatje, were mostly educated professionals, teachers, lawyers, journalists and church ministers. They believed in working within the law. They hoped to persuade the government and the British authorities through reasoned argument that black South Africans deserved equal rights.

The ANC's early methods were therefore moderate: petitions, letters, meetings with government officials and deputations, which were delegations sent to present a case. In 1914 and again in 1919, ANC deputations travelled to Britain to protest against the Natives Land Act of 1913 and to ask for rights, but they were turned away. In 1919, the ANC supported an anti-pass campaign on the Witwatersrand. Other organisations, such as the Industrial and Commercial Workers' Union led by Clements Kadalie in the 1920s, organised workers on a large scale.

The ANC was not the only organisation resisting segregation. In 1913, black and coloured women in Bloemfontein, led by figures such as Charlotte Maxeke, protested against attempts to force women to carry passes, and their campaign succeeded in delaying passes for women for decades. Maxeke went on to found the Bantu Women's League in 1918. The African Political Organisation, led by Dr Abdullah Abdurahman, represented many coloured South Africans, and the Natal Indian Congress, founded by Mahatma Gandhi in 1894, and later the South African Indian Congress, represented Indian South Africans. However, these organisations usually worked separately, and none of them had yet found a way to mobilise the majority of ordinary people.

Despite these efforts, the laws became steadily harsher: the Natives (Urban Areas) Act of 1923, the removal of black Cape voters in 1936, the Native Trust and Land Act of 1936 and others. Many younger members felt that the ANC's methods had achieved very little and that its leaders were too cautious and too willing to compromise.

In 1944, a group of young ANC members formed the ANC Youth League. Its leaders included Anton Lembede, its first president, who was a teacher and lawyer and a strong advocate of African nationalism, as well as A.P. Mda, Walter Sisulu, Oliver Tambo and Nelson Mandela. The Youth League argued that Africans must rely on their own strength and unity, that the ANC should become a mass movement of ordinary people rather than a small group of educated leaders, and that it should use more militant methods. Lembede died in 1947, but his ideas continued to inspire the League.

So the next question is: what was the Programme of Action?

## Subtopic: The Programme of Action of 1949

The election victory of the National Party in 1948 and its first apartheid laws made the Youth League's arguments more urgent. At the ANC's national conference in Bloemfontein in December 1949, the Youth League succeeded in persuading the organisation to adopt a new strategy, called the Programme of Action.

The Programme of Action called for national freedom, political independence and freedom from white domination. It rejected segregation and apartheid and demanded direct representation for Africans in all governing bodies. Most importantly, it changed the ANC's methods. Instead of petitions and deputations, the ANC would now use mass action: boycotts of apartheid institutions, such as the advisory councils and the Natives' Representative Council that gave black people only a token voice; strikes, including a national stoppage of work; civil disobedience, which means openly and peacefully breaking unjust laws and accepting the punishment; and non-cooperation with the government. It also called for the building of national unity and for raising funds and organising people.

The methods were non-violent. The ANC aimed to put pressure on the government through the united, disciplined action of large numbers of people, making apartheid laws unworkable rather than attacking anyone physically.

At the same conference, the ANC elected a new president-general, Dr James Moroka, who was seen as more willing to support the Programme of Action than the previous president, Dr A.B. Xuma. Walter Sisulu was elected secretary-general, and several Youth League members joined the national executive. The ANC had changed course, and it would soon put the new strategy into practice.

Putting the Programme into practice was not easy. Many ordinary people were poor and could not afford to lose a day's wages, let alone their jobs, by going on strike. Organising across a huge country without telephones in most homes, with many people unable to read, and under constant police watch, required patient work by local branches and volunteers. Some older leaders remained uncomfortable with the new methods. The Programme was therefore a statement of intent, and the ANC spent the next few years building the organisation it needed to carry it out.

So the next question is: how did the government respond to growing resistance?

## Subtopic: The Suppression of Communism Act of 1950

The Communist Party of South Africa had been formed in 1921. It was one of the first political parties in South Africa to accept members of all races, and from the late 1920s it called for majority rule. Many of its members were active in trade unions and worked closely with the ANC and the Indian congresses. For the National Party government, communism was a major enemy. This was the early Cold War, which you studied in Term 2, and the government presented itself as a defender of Western, Christian civilisation against Soviet communism. It also feared that communists were encouraging black workers to organise and demand rights.

In 1950, Parliament passed the Suppression of Communism Act. It declared the Communist Party an unlawful organisation. Before the Act came into force, the party dissolved itself in June 1950. In 1953, some of its members secretly re-formed it as an underground organisation, the South African Communist Party, or SACP, which remained banned until 1990.

The Act's real importance lay in how broadly it defined communism. It included any doctrine or scheme that aimed to bring about any political, industrial, social or economic change in South Africa by promoting disturbance or disorder, or by unlawful acts or omissions, or that aimed to encourage feelings of hostility between the white and other races. Under this definition, almost anyone who campaigned against apartheid could be labelled a communist, even if they had no connection to the Communist Party at all. Critics described the result as statutory communism, meaning communism as defined by statute, or law, rather than by its normal meaning.

The Act gave the Minister of Justice the power to ban people. A banned person could be prohibited from attending gatherings, restricted to a particular area, forced to resign from organisations, and forbidden to be quoted in the press, all without a trial. Over the following decades, hundreds of people, including many ANC leaders, trade unionists, journalists, teachers and church members, were banned under this Act and its successors. Banning was a powerful tool of repression, because it silenced leaders and cut them off from their organisations without the government needing to prove anything in court.

The Suppression of Communism Act was only the beginning. Over the following years, Parliament passed further laws that strengthened the government's power to act against opponents, such as the Public Safety Act and the Criminal Law Amendment Act, both of 1953, which you will meet in the next lesson. The security police, known as the Special Branch, grew in size and kept files on thousands of people. In this way, a pattern was set for the whole apartheid period: new forms of resistance were met by new laws that gave the state more power and reduced the rights of citizens, white and black.

So the final question is: how did the ANC put the Programme of Action into practice in 1950?

## Subtopic: The Protests of 1950, and the Error Museum

The first major mass action came on 1 May 1950, when the Communist Party, the Transvaal Indian Congress and the Transvaal branch of the ANC called a one-day strike on the Witwatersrand, known as the Freedom Day strike or May Day strike, to demand the repeal of the pass laws and other discriminatory laws and to oppose the proposed Suppression of Communism Act. Many workers stayed away from work. That night, police fired on crowds in several townships, and eighteen people were killed. At first, some Youth League members, including Mandela, opposed the strike because it was organised partly by communists, but the killings and the threat of the new Act brought the organisations closer together.

In response to the shootings and the Suppression of Communism Act, the ANC, together with the South African Indian Congress and others, called a National Day of Protest and Mourning on 26 June 1950. People across the country were asked to stay away from work and to pray and mourn for those who had been killed. The response was strongest in Port Elizabeth, Durban and parts of the Witwatersrand. The day 26 June became an important date in the liberation calendar, known as Freedom Day in the movement; the Defiance Campaign was launched on 26 June 1952, and the Congress of the People adopted the Freedom Charter on 26 June 1955.

The events of 1950 showed several things. They showed that mass action could mobilise large numbers of people. They showed that the government would respond with force and new laws. And they began a closer alliance between the ANC, the Indian congresses and other organisations, including former communists and some white democrats, which would later develop into the Congress Alliance. This cooperation was not without disagreement: some African nationalists in the ANC opposed working closely with other groups, a tension that would later lead to the formation of the Pan Africanist Congress in 1959.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "The ANC was always a mass movement using strikes." For its first decades, the ANC mainly used petitions and deputations; the Programme of Action of 1949 introduced mass action.

Exhibit two: "The Suppression of Communism Act was used only against communists." Its broad definition of communism allowed the government to ban almost any opponent of apartheid.

Exhibit three: "Banned people had first been convicted in court." Banning orders were issued by the Minister without any trial.

Exhibit four: "The Programme of Action called for armed struggle." It called for non-violent boycotts, strikes, civil disobedience and non-cooperation.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a polite letter nobody answers, a new plan from the young, and a net that catches everyone.

## Subtopic: A Polite Letter Nobody Answers

The ANC was formed in 1912 to fight for the rights of black South Africans. Its first leaders were teachers, lawyers, writers and ministers. They believed in being polite and reasonable.

So they wrote letters. They sent petitions. Twice they even sailed to Britain to ask for help. Each time, the answer was no.

Year after year, the laws got worse. Black people lost land, lost the vote in the Cape and were controlled by passes.

Imagine writing polite letters for thirty years and never getting a reply, while the people you are writing to keep making your life harder. You would start to wonder whether letters could ever work. That is how many young ANC members felt by the 1940s. In 1944, they formed the ANC Youth League. Its leaders included Anton Lembede, Walter Sisulu, Oliver Tambo and a young Nelson Mandela. They said: we need a new way.

So what was the new way?

## Subtopic: A New Plan From the Young

In 1949, a year after apartheid began, the Youth League persuaded the whole ANC to adopt a new plan, called the Programme of Action.

Instead of letters, the ANC would use the power of large numbers of ordinary people. Boycotts, which means refusing to take part in apartheid institutions. Strikes, which means staying away from work. And civil disobedience, which means openly breaking unfair laws, peacefully, and accepting arrest.

There would be no violence. The idea was to make apartheid laws impossible to run, by refusing to cooperate with them. A government cannot arrest millions of people, and a country cannot run if its workers stay at home.

In 1950, the first big protests took place. On 1 May, workers stayed away, and police shot and killed eighteen people. On 26 June, the ANC and its allies held a national day of protest and mourning. That date became a special day for the struggle.

So how did the government fight back?

## Subtopic: A Net That Catches Everyone

In 1950, the government passed the Suppression of Communism Act. It banned the Communist Party, which was one of the few parties that welcomed members of all races.

But the law was like a huge fishing net. It described communism so widely that almost anyone who wanted to change apartheid could be caught in it.

The government could ban people without a trial. A banned person could not go to meetings, could not leave their town and could not be quoted in newspapers. It was like being silenced and locked in an invisible cage, and it could last for years at a time.

Many leaders were banned, but the resistance kept growing. African, Indian and coloured organisations, and some white supporters, started working together, because they faced the same laws and the same police. Over the next few years, they would plan bigger campaigns than ever before.

Here is the line to remember: in 1949 the ANC turned from polite petitions to mass, non-violent action, and in 1950 the government answered with the Suppression of Communism Act, beginning a decade of resistance and repression.
