# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the Defiance Campaign of 1952 and the leadership of Albert Luthuli. It covers the influence of Mahatma Gandhi and his method of satyagraha, which he developed in South Africa; the planning and events of the Defiance Campaign against Unjust Laws; the government's response and the results of the campaign; and the life, leadership and ideas of Chief Albert Luthuli, who became President-General of the ANC at the end of 1952. The anchor idea is that the Defiance Campaign put the Programme of Action into practice through disciplined, non-violent civil disobedience by thousands of ordinary volunteers. The governing idea is that the campaign transformed the ANC into a mass movement and built unity between African and Indian South Africans, even though the government answered it with harsher laws.

## Subtopic: Gandhi, Satyagraha and South Africa

Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi, later known as Mahatma, meaning great soul, was born in India in 1869 and trained as a lawyer in London. In 1893 he came to South Africa to work on a legal case for an Indian business in Natal. Soon after arriving, he was thrown off a train at Pietermaritzburg station because he refused to leave a first-class compartment reserved for white passengers, even though he had a valid ticket. Gandhi later described this experience as a turning point that led him to resist racial injustice.

Gandhi stayed in South Africa for about twenty-one years, until 1914. In 1894 he helped to found the Natal Indian Congress to defend the rights of Indian South Africans, many of whom had come to Natal as indentured labourers on the sugar plantations or as traders. In 1906, the Transvaal government introduced a law, which Indians called the Black Act, requiring all Indians to register and carry certificates with their fingerprints. Gandhi led a campaign of resistance against it.

During this campaign, Gandhi developed the method he called satyagraha, which can be translated as truth force or soul force. Satyagraha meant resisting unjust laws by deliberately and openly breaking them, without using violence, and willingly accepting the punishment. Gandhi believed that suffering accepted with dignity could change the hearts of opponents and expose injustice to the world. Thousands of Indian South Africans, including women and mine workers, took part in marches, strikes and burnings of registration certificates, and many were imprisoned, including Gandhi himself. In 1914, the government agreed to some concessions, and Gandhi returned to India, where he used satyagraha to lead India's struggle for independence from Britain, achieved in 1947.

Gandhi's legacy in South Africa is debated. In his early years in South Africa, he campaigned only for the rights of Indians, and some of his writings at the time expressed prejudiced views about black Africans, which historians and critics have pointed out. His ideas developed over time. His method, however, had a lasting influence. The South African Indian Congress, led by Dr Yusuf Dadoo and Dr Monty Naicker, used passive resistance against the Asiatic Land Tenure Act from 1946 to 1948, and more than two thousand people were arrested. This experience, together with the example of India's independence, helped to inspire the ANC to adopt civil disobedience in its Programme of Action. Gandhi's son, Manilal Gandhi, who lived in South Africa, took part in the Defiance Campaign in 1952.

So the next question is: how was the Defiance Campaign organised?

## Subtopic: The Defiance Campaign Against Unjust Laws

After the protests of 1950, the ANC and the South African Indian Congress set up a Joint Planning Council in 1951 to organise a national campaign of civil disobedience. They wrote to Prime Minister D.F. Malan, demanding the repeal of six unjust laws by 29 February 1952: the pass laws, stock limitation regulations that forced rural people to reduce their cattle, the Group Areas Act, the Separate Representation of Voters Act, the Suppression of Communism Act and the Bantu Authorities Act. They warned that if the laws were not repealed, they would launch a campaign of defiance. Malan replied that the government would not repeal the laws and warned that it would use its full powers against any disturbance.

The campaign was planned carefully. Nelson Mandela was appointed the national volunteer-in-chief, responsible for recruiting and training volunteers, with Maulvi Cachalia of the Indian Congress as his deputy. Volunteers signed a pledge to obey the leaders, to remain non-violent even if provoked or attacked, and to accept arrest and punishment without paying fines. They were organised into small batches, each with a leader, which would break specific laws at announced times and places.

The campaign began on 26 June 1952, the anniversary of the National Day of Protest. In Port Elizabeth, volunteers entered a railway station through the entrance for Europeans only. In Johannesburg and Boksburg, volunteers led by Walter Sisulu and Nana Sita, an elderly follower of Gandhi, entered a location without permits. Elsewhere, volunteers broke curfew laws by staying out after the hours when black people had to be off the streets, sat on benches and used counters and post office entrances reserved for whites, and refused to carry passes. Volunteers often sang freedom songs and gave the thumbs-up sign of the ANC as they were arrested, and wore the colours of the ANC.

The campaign spread across the country, but it was strongest in the Eastern Cape, especially Port Elizabeth and East London, where trade unions and local branches were well organised. Over the following months, more than 8 000 volunteers were arrested. They came from all backgrounds: African, Indian and coloured, and a small number of white supporters, such as Patrick Duncan, who joined Manilal Gandhi in defying the law at Germiston in December 1952. The volunteers were overwhelmingly disciplined and non-violent.

So the next question is: how did the government respond, and what did the campaign achieve?

## Subtopic: Repression and Results of the Campaign

The government responded with arrests, trials and new laws. Many leaders, including Mandela, Sisulu, Moroka, Dadoo and others, were arrested and charged under the Suppression of Communism Act. In December 1952, they were convicted of statutory communism, but the judge accepted that they had consistently advised their followers to avoid violence, and they received suspended sentences.

In October and November 1952, riots broke out in Port Elizabeth, East London, Kimberley and Johannesburg. They began with incidents involving the police and were not part of the campaign, and the ANC condemned the violence, but several people, both black and white, were killed, including a nun, Sister Aidan, in East London. Many more black residents were shot dead by the police. The government blamed the campaign for the riots, and the violence made many people more cautious about taking part.

Early in 1953, Parliament passed two new laws designed to crush civil disobedience. The Public Safety Act allowed the government to declare a state of emergency and rule by decree. The Criminal Law Amendment Act made it a crime to break any law as a protest, or to encourage others to do so, with penalties of heavy fines, imprisonment of up to three years and whipping. With these penalties, the campaign gradually came to an end in early 1953.

The campaign did not achieve its stated aim, because none of the six laws was repealed. However, historians agree that it had very important results. ANC membership grew dramatically, from about 7 000 before the campaign to about 100 000 by its end, according to commonly cited estimates, turning the ANC into a genuine mass movement for the first time. It built strong cooperation between African and Indian organisations, laying the foundations for the Congress Alliance. It inspired the formation of the South African Coloured People's Organisation and the Congress of Democrats, a white organisation opposed to apartheid. It produced a new generation of leaders and gave thousands of people experience of organisation and sacrifice. It brought international attention to apartheid; in 1952, the United Nations General Assembly discussed apartheid for the first time as a separate issue and set up a commission to study it. And it led to a change in the ANC's leadership, with the election of Albert Luthuli.

The campaign also had limits that the leaders themselves recognised. It was much weaker in some regions than in others, it involved relatively few people in the countryside, and it depended on volunteers being willing to face prison again and again. Once the penalties became severe, it was difficult to continue. These lessons shaped the next stage of the struggle, which focused on building broad unity behind a shared vision of a free South Africa.

So the final question is: who was Albert Luthuli, and what did he stand for?

## Subtopic: Albert Luthuli's Leadership, and the Error Museum

Albert John Luthuli was born in about 1898 in what is now Zimbabwe, where his father was a missionary, and grew up in Groutville, a mission station in Natal. He trained as a teacher and taught for many years at Adams College, one of the leading mission schools in South Africa. He was a devout Christian and a lay preacher. In 1936, the people of Groutville elected him as their chief, a position he held for about sixteen years, dealing with the problems of land shortage and poverty facing his community.

Luthuli joined the ANC in 1944 and became President of the Natal branch in 1951. During the Defiance Campaign, he supported the volunteers. The government ordered him to choose between his position as chief and his membership of the ANC. He refused to leave the ANC and was dismissed as chief in November 1952. In response, he issued a famous statement called "The Road to Freedom is via the Cross", in which he explained that he had spent thirty years knocking patiently on a closed and barred door, and asked what the fruits of moderation had been. He declared that he would continue to work for freedom through non-violent means, even if it meant suffering, as Christ had suffered on the cross.

In December 1952, the ANC elected Luthuli as its President-General, replacing Dr Moroka, who had disappointed many members by distancing himself from other leaders during the trial. Luthuli led the ANC until his death in 1967. He was banned repeatedly, confined to the Groutville area for long periods and arrested in the Treason Trial, but he remained a respected, calm and principled leader. He believed in a non-racial South Africa in which all people would share equal rights, and in non-violence, and he worked to build the Congress Alliance.

In 1961, Luthuli was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize for 1960, becoming the first African to receive it. The government allowed him to travel to Oslo, Norway, to receive the prize, where he spoke about the struggle against apartheid and the hope for a peaceful, non-racial South Africa. He died in July 1967 after being struck by a train near his home, in circumstances that some people still question. His autobiography, "Let My People Go", was published in 1962.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Gandhi developed non-violent resistance in India and brought it to South Africa." Gandhi developed satyagraha in South Africa between 1906 and 1914, then used it in India.

Exhibit two: "The Defiance Campaign was violent." Volunteers pledged non-violence and were overwhelmingly disciplined; the riots of late 1952 were not part of the campaign.

Exhibit three: "The Defiance Campaign failed completely because no laws were repealed." It turned the ANC into a mass movement, built unity and drew international attention.

Exhibit four: "Albert Luthuli resigned as chief because he lost interest." He was dismissed by the government because he refused to leave the ANC.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a man thrown off a train, an open door marked "Whites only", and a chief who chose his people.

## Subtopic: A Man Thrown Off a Train

In 1893, a young Indian lawyer named Gandhi arrived in South Africa. One night, at Pietermaritzburg station, he was thrown off a train because he sat in a carriage meant only for white people, even though he had a ticket.

That night changed him. He stayed in South Africa for about twenty years and worked out a new way to fight unfair laws. He called it satyagraha, which means truth force.

Here is how it works. You break an unfair law on purpose, in the open, and you do not fight back or run away. You accept arrest and even prison. If enough people do this, the jails fill up, the law looks ridiculous, and the whole world sees how unfair it is.

Gandhi later used this method to help India win its freedom. In South Africa, the ANC and the Indian Congress decided to try it too.

So what did they do?

## Subtopic: An Open Door Marked "Whites Only"

On 26 June 1952, the Defiance Campaign began. Volunteers walked through doors marked "Whites only". They sat on benches meant for white people. They stayed out after curfew and refused to carry passes. Then they waited calmly to be arrested, singing freedom songs.

Nelson Mandela was in charge of the volunteers. Every volunteer promised never to use violence, even if attacked.

More than 8 000 people went to prison: African, Indian and coloured, and a few white supporters too.

The government did not change the laws. Instead, it made breaking a law as a protest a serious crime, with prison and whipping, and the campaign ended in 1953.

But something big had happened. The ANC grew from a few thousand members to about a hundred thousand. Ordinary people had shown that they were not afraid of prison.

So who led the ANC after this?

## Subtopic: A Chief Who Chose His People

Albert Luthuli was a teacher, a Christian preacher and the chief of Groutville in Natal. When he supported the Defiance Campaign, the government told him: give up the ANC, or stop being chief.

He would not give up the ANC, so the government took away his position as chief. Luthuli said he had spent thirty years knocking politely on a closed door, and that the road to freedom might be hard, like carrying a cross, but he would walk it.

In December 1952, the ANC chose him as its leader. He was calm, kind and firm, and people of all races respected him. He believed in a South Africa where all people would be equal, and in reaching it without violence.

In 1961, Luthuli received the Nobel Peace Prize, the first African ever to win it.

Here is the line to remember: the Defiance Campaign of 1952 used Gandhi's method of peaceful law-breaking to turn the ANC into a mass movement, and Albert Luthuli rose from it to lead the struggle for a non-racial South Africa.
