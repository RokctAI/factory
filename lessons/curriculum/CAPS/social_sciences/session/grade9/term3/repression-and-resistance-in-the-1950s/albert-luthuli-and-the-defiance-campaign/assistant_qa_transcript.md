### subtopic_1 — Gandhi, Satyagraha and South Africa
**Assistant:** Why do historians debate Gandhi's legacy in South Africa?

**Tutor:** Gandhi is admired around the world as a symbol of non-violent resistance, and his method of satyagraha, which he developed in South Africa, influenced liberation movements across the world, including the ANC and the American civil rights movement led by Martin Luther King Jr. In South Africa, his campaigns between 1906 and 1914 mobilised thousands of Indian South Africans and won some concessions, and his statue stands in Pietermaritzburg, near the station where he was thrown off the train. However, historians and critics have pointed out several complications. During his time in South Africa, Gandhi campaigned mainly for the rights of Indians, especially Indian traders and, later, indentured workers, and he did not campaign for the rights of black Africans. Some of his early writings and petitions used insulting language about black Africans and argued that Indians should not be classed with them, reflecting the racial prejudices common among many people of his time and class. He also supported the British during the South African War and during the suppression of the Bambatha Rebellion in 1906, when he led an Indian stretcher-bearer corps, although he later described being disturbed by the treatment of wounded Zulu people he cared for. Some scholars argue that his views developed over his life and that he later spoke against all forms of racial discrimination; others argue that his early attitudes should not be overlooked. A balanced view recognises both his important contribution to the idea and practice of non-violent resistance, which South African movements later adopted and adapted for their own struggle, and the limitations and prejudices of his early career. This kind of debate is a normal part of how historians evaluate significant figures.

### subtopic_2 — The Defiance Campaign Against Unjust Laws
**Assistant:** Why did volunteers refuse to pay fines when they were convicted?

**Tutor:** Refusing to pay fines was a deliberate part of the strategy of the Defiance Campaign, based on the ideas of satyagraha and civil disobedience. When volunteers were convicted of breaking apartheid laws, the courts usually gave them a choice between paying a fine and serving a short prison sentence. If volunteers had paid the fines, the campaign would have had little effect: the government would simply have collected money, and the unjust laws would have continued to work normally. By choosing prison instead, volunteers achieved several things. First, they filled the prisons and kept the police and courts busy, putting pressure on the system and showing that the laws could not be enforced easily if large numbers of people refused to obey them. Second, they showed their commitment and moral courage. Going to prison willingly for a just cause demonstrated that the volunteers were not criminals, but people prepared to suffer for their beliefs, which won respect and sympathy from many South Africans and from people abroad. Third, it was a matter of principle: paying a fine could be seen as accepting that the law was legitimate and that the volunteer had done something wrong. Fourth, it inspired others. When people saw their neighbours, teachers and church members going to prison with dignity, singing freedom songs, they were encouraged to join. Prison conditions for black prisoners were harsh, so this was a real sacrifice. The government understood the power of this strategy, which is why the Criminal Law Amendment Act of 1953 introduced much heavier penalties, including long prison sentences and whipping, designed to make the cost of defiance too high for most people.

### subtopic_3 — Repression and Results of the Campaign
**Assistant:** Why did ANC membership grow so much during the Defiance Campaign?

**Tutor:** Before the Defiance Campaign, the ANC was a relatively small organisation, with estimates of about 7 000 paid-up members, and it was often seen as an organisation of educated leaders rather than ordinary people. By the end of the campaign, commonly cited estimates put its membership at about 100 000. Several factors explain this growth. First, the campaign gave ordinary people something concrete to do. Instead of signing petitions or listening to speeches, they could take direct action against laws that affected them every day, such as pass laws and curfews. Second, it was well organised at the local level. Volunteers were recruited and trained through local branches, and in areas with strong branches and trade unions, such as Port Elizabeth and East London, participation was very high. Third, the sight of volunteers going to prison peacefully and with dignity inspired others. The courage of the volunteers made the ANC visible and respected in communities across the country. Fourth, the campaign was associated with hope. For the first time, many people felt that they could challenge apartheid together, and that the ANC was leading a real struggle rather than making polite requests. Fifth, the campaign received wide coverage in newspapers such as the Guardian and Drum, and in the international press, which spread awareness of the ANC. Finally, the government's harsh response, including the arrests of popular leaders, increased anger and support. It is worth noting that the growth was uneven across the country, and that the heavy penalties introduced in 1953, together with the riots of late 1952, caused participation to decline. But the campaign had permanently changed the ANC from a small organisation into a mass movement.

### subtopic_4 — Albert Luthuli's Leadership, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What did Luthuli mean by "The Road to Freedom is via the Cross"?

**Tutor:** Luthuli issued this statement in November 1952, after the government dismissed him as chief of Groutville because he refused to resign from the ANC. The title draws on his deep Christian faith. In Christianity, the cross is the symbol of Jesus's suffering and death, which Christians believe led to salvation. By saying that the road to freedom is via the cross, Luthuli meant that the struggle for freedom would involve suffering and sacrifice, which those who believed in justice must be prepared to accept without hatred or violence. In the statement, he explained that he had spent thirty years of his life knocking in vain, patiently, moderately and modestly, at a closed and barred door, and asked what the fruits of moderation had been. He pointed out that, in those years, Africans had received more laws restricting their rights and freedoms, until they had reached a stage where they had almost no rights at all. He therefore defended the decision of the ANC to use non-violent passive resistance through the Defiance Campaign, describing it as a legitimate and humane political pressure technique. He also made clear that he did not hate white South Africans and that he wanted a society in which all people could live together in equality. The statement showed several of Luthuli's key qualities: his moral courage, his willingness to lose his position and income for his principles, his commitment to non-violence, and his vision of non-racialism. It was widely published and made him a national figure, and the following month the ANC elected him as President-General. It remains one of the most important statements of the liberation struggle.
