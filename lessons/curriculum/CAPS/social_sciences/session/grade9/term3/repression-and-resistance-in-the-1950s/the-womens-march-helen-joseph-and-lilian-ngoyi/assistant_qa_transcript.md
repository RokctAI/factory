### subtopic_1 — Women and the Pass Laws
**Assistant:** Why did the women stand in silence for thirty minutes instead of making speeches?

**Tutor:** The thirty minutes of silence was one of the most powerful moments of the Women's March, and it was a carefully chosen form of protest. First, it showed extraordinary discipline and unity. Keeping twenty thousand people completely silent and still for half an hour required great self-control and organisation, and it demonstrated that the women were not a disorderly crowd but a determined, united movement. Second, silence was dignified and respectful, and it was very difficult for the government to criticise or attack. The government had banned gatherings and might have used speeches or noise as an excuse to declare the gathering unlawful or to use force against it. A silent crowd gave the authorities no excuse. Third, silence can be more powerful than words. Standing silently outside the seat of government, after delivering their petitions to an empty office, the women made a statement that the Prime Minister had refused to listen to them, while they, in turn, refused to go away. Many observers, including journalists, described the silence as deeply moving and even unnerving. Fourth, it gave the women themselves a moment of solidarity and reflection, and it built a shared memory that participants never forgot. Many women raised their right fists in the Congress salute during the silence, showing their commitment to the struggle. When the silence ended and the women began singing freedom songs, including the famous song warning Strijdom that he had struck a rock, the contrast between the silence and the singing made the moment even more dramatic. The silent protest has since become one of the best-remembered images of resistance to apartheid.

### subtopic_2 — The Federation of South African Women
**Assistant:** What does "you strike a woman, you strike a rock" mean?

**Tutor:** The phrase comes from the isiZulu words of a song composed for the Women's March of 9 August 1956: "Wathint' abafazi, wathint' imbokodo", which means "you have struck the women, you have struck a rock". In the full song, the women addressed the Prime Minister directly: "Strijdom, wathint' abafazi, wathint' imbokodo, uzakufa", which can be translated as "Strijdom, you have struck the women, you have struck a rock, you will be crushed". The imbokodo is a grinding stone, a hard, heavy rock used to grind maize and other grains, which was an important tool in many African homes. The image therefore carries several meanings. A rock is strong and unbreakable, so the song warns that women cannot easily be broken or defeated. Anyone who attacks them will find that they are as hard as stone, and may themselves be crushed. The grinding stone is also connected with women's daily work of feeding their families, so the image links women's strength to their role in sustaining homes and communities, which the pass laws threatened. By attacking women with passes, the song suggests, the government had made a serious mistake, because it had provoked people who would resist with great determination. Since 1956, the phrase has become a famous slogan of women's strength and resilience in South Africa, used every year on National Women's Day and in campaigns for women's rights and against gender-based violence. Strong women are often called imbokodo as a sign of respect. The phrase shows how songs were a vital part of the struggle against apartheid, helping people to express their feelings, build unity and remember important events.

### subtopic_3 — The March to the Union Buildings
**Assistant:** Why was it significant that the march's leaders came from different racial groups?

**Tutor:** The four leaders who carried the petitions to the Prime Minister's office, Lilian Ngoyi, Helen Joseph, Rahima Moosa and Sophie Williams, represented African, white, Indian and coloured women respectively. This was a deliberate and significant choice, for several reasons. First, it expressed the non-racial principles of the Federation of South African Women and the Congress Alliance. FEDSAW brought together women of all races in one organisation, and the leadership of the march showed that women of different backgrounds could work together as equals. Second, it directly challenged apartheid. The whole apartheid system was built on separating people by race and teaching them to see one another as different and unequal. Four women of different racial groups walking side by side to the seat of the apartheid government was a powerful rejection of that idea, especially at a time when such cooperation could lead to arrest and harassment. Third, although the pass laws applied only to black African women, women of other races joined the protest in solidarity. This showed that they saw an injustice against one group as an injustice against all, which strengthened the moral force of the protest. Fourth, it presented a picture of a future, united South Africa, in line with the Freedom Charter's statement that South Africa belongs to all who live in it. It should also be noted that the overwhelming majority of the marchers were African women, who were the most directly affected by the pass laws and who did most of the organising in townships and rural areas. The non-racial leadership did not replace their central role, but it added a strong message of unity to their protest.

### subtopic_4 — Lilian Ngoyi and Helen Joseph, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How did the government try to silence Lilian Ngoyi and Helen Joseph?

**Tutor:** The government used almost every tool of repression available against both women. Both were among the 156 people arrested in December 1956 and charged with high treason. The trial tied them up for years before the last accused were found not guilty in March 1961. During the state of emergency after the Sharpeville massacre in 1960, Ngoyi was detained for weeks, much of the time in solitary confinement, which she described as one of the hardest experiences of her life. Both women were repeatedly placed under banning orders, which prevented them from attending gatherings, from being quoted in newspapers and from taking part in political organisations. Ngoyi was banned from 1962, and her banning orders were renewed almost continuously until shortly before her death in 1980; she was confined to the township of Orlando in Soweto, cut off from the movement she had helped to lead, and she struggled to earn a living, working as a seamstress at home. Helen Joseph became, in October 1962, the first person in South Africa to be placed under house arrest under the Sabotage Act, which meant that she had to stay in her house every night and all weekend, and could not receive visitors. Her house arrest lasted for years, and she was later banned again. She received threatening phone calls, bullets were fired at her house, and a bomb was placed at her gate. Despite all this, neither woman stopped believing in the struggle. Ngoyi encouraged younger activists, and Joseph continued to write and speak whenever she was able. Their persistence under such pressure is one reason they are remembered as heroes of the struggle.
