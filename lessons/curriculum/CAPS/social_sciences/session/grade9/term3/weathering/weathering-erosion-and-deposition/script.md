# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the difference between weathering, erosion and deposition and how they work together. It covers the three processes and the sequence that links them; the agents of erosion, namely running water, wind, ice, waves and gravity; how rivers and wind transport material and why they deposit it; and a South African example that follows material from mountain to sea. The anchor idea is a three-step chain: weathering breaks rock down in place, erosion removes and transports the weathered material, and deposition lays it down in a new place. The governing idea is that the surface of the earth is constantly being worn down in some places and built up in others, and that energy is the key: material is eroded and carried where moving water, wind or ice has plenty of energy, and dropped where that energy runs out.

## Subtopic: Three Processes, One Chain

Weathering, erosion and deposition are often confused, so it helps to define each one carefully.

Weathering is the breaking down of rock in place, at or near the earth's surface. As you learnt in the previous lesson, it can be physical, chemical or biological. The important point is that weathering does not move material any significant distance. A boulder split by frost stays on the mountainside until something moves it.

Erosion is the wearing away and removal of rock and soil by a moving agent: running water, wind, moving ice, waves or gravity. Erosion includes two linked actions. First, the agent picks up or loosens material. Second, it transports that material away. Erosion can also wear away rock directly; for example, a river carrying stones scrapes and grinds its own bed. Many geographers use the term denudation to describe weathering and erosion together, the processes that lower and wear down the land.

Deposition is the laying down of material that has been transported. It happens when the agent carrying the material slows down and loses energy, so it can no longer carry its load. A river entering the sea, wind dropping behind a bush, or a wave running up a beach and sinking into the sand all deposit material. Over long periods, deposited sediments can be pressed and cemented together to form new sedimentary rocks, such as sandstone and shale.

The three processes form a chain. Weathering prepares the material, erosion moves it, and deposition puts it somewhere new. Erosion is much easier when rock has been weathered first, because loose, broken material can be picked up more easily than solid rock. Without weathering, there would be little for rivers and wind to carry. Without erosion, weathered material would pile up where it formed, creating thick soils but few new landforms. Without deposition, there would be no beaches, deltas, flood plains or sand dunes.

A simple way to remember the difference is to think of three verbs: weathering breaks, erosion takes, deposition drops. All three are driven, in the end, by energy from the sun, which drives the weather and the water cycle, and by gravity, which pulls water, ice and loose material downhill.

These processes also link to the rock cycle you studied in Natural Sciences. Rocks formed deep underground are pushed up to the surface, weathered, eroded and deposited as sediment, which becomes sedimentary rock, which may later be buried, heated and changed again.

So the next question is: what are the agents of erosion, and how do they work?

## Subtopic: The Agents of Erosion

There are five main agents of erosion.

Running water is the most important agent of erosion on land, including in South Africa. Rain falling on bare ground splashes soil particles loose, sheets of water flowing over the surface wash away thin layers of soil, small channels called rills cut into slopes, and these may grow into deep gullies, known in South Africa as dongas. Rivers erode their beds and banks in several ways. Hydraulic action is the sheer force of moving water pushing into cracks and loosening material. Abrasion, also called corrasion, happens when stones and sand carried by the river scrape and grind against the bed and banks. Attrition happens when the stones carried by the river knock against each other and become smaller and rounder. Solution, sometimes called corrosion, happens when the river dissolves certain minerals.

Wind is an important agent in dry areas with little vegetation, such as the Kalahari and the Namib. Deflation happens when wind lifts and blows away loose, dry sand and dust. Abrasion by wind happens when windblown sand blasts against rock surfaces, smoothing and shaping them, rather like sandpaper. Wind erosion can strip away fertile topsoil from ploughed fields during droughts.

Moving ice, in the form of glaciers, is a powerful agent in cold mountain regions and polar areas. Glaciers pluck rock from their beds and scrape the land, carving U-shaped valleys. South Africa has no glaciers today, but about 300 million years ago, when southern Africa lay near the South Pole as part of Gondwana, ice sheets covered much of the country and left deposits called the Dwyka tillite, which can still be seen in the Karoo.

Waves erode coastlines by hydraulic action and abrasion, forming cliffs, caves and arches, and they also transport and deposit sand to form beaches. You will meet coastal processes in more detail in later grades.

Gravity causes mass movement, the downhill movement of rock and soil without a separate carrying agent. It includes rockfalls, when weathered rock drops from cliffs; landslides, when a whole slope slides down, often after heavy rain; mudflows, when soil saturated with water flows like a thick liquid; and soil creep, the very slow movement of soil downslope that tilts fences, poles and walls. Rockfalls are a hazard on mountain passes such as Chapman's Peak Drive near Cape Town, which is protected by nets and catch fences.

So the next question is: how do these agents carry material, and why do they drop it?

## Subtopic: Transport and Deposition

Rivers carry their load in four ways. The largest material, such as boulders and pebbles, is rolled or dragged along the river bed; this is called traction. Smaller stones and coarse sand bounce along the bed in a series of hops; this is called saltation. Fine particles of silt and clay are carried along within the water, making it look muddy or brown; this is called suspension. Dissolved minerals are carried invisibly in the water; this is called solution. After heavy rain, many South African rivers turn brown because they carry huge amounts of soil in suspension.

How much a river can carry, and how large the pieces can be, depends on its energy, which depends mainly on the speed and volume of the water. A fast-flowing river in flood can move boulders the size of cars, while a slow river can carry only fine silt. When the river slows down, it loses energy and begins to deposit its load, starting with the heaviest material. Rivers deposit material where their speed drops: on the inside of bends, where the river leaves steep mountains and enters flat land, when floods spread over flat land, and where the river enters a lake or the sea. This sorting by size is an important clue for geographers: boulders are usually found in the upper course of a river, sand in the middle and fine mud near the mouth.

Wind transports material in similar ways. Sand grains bounce along by saltation, usually less than a metre above the ground, while fine dust can be carried in suspension for hundreds or even thousands of kilometres; dust from the Sahara sometimes reaches the Caribbean. When the wind slows down, for example behind a rock or a bush, or when it meets a slope, it drops its load, and sand piles up into dunes. The dunes of the Namib Desert, some of the highest in the world, and the red dunes of the Kalahari were formed this way.

Glaciers carry material of all sizes frozen in or on the ice and drop it in mixed, unsorted heaps when the ice melts. Waves carry sand along the coast and deposit it in sheltered bays to form beaches.

In every case, the same rule applies: more energy means more erosion and transport; less energy means deposition.

Deposition creates many important landforms, some of which you will study in the next lessons. Rivers build flood plains, broad flat areas of fertile silt on either side of the channel, as well as levees and deltas. Where a steep mountain stream reaches flat land, it may drop its load in a fan-shaped pile called an alluvial fan. Along the coast, deposition forms beaches, sandbars and spits. Many of the best farming areas in the world, such as the Nile valley in Egypt, are on deposited river sediment, because fine silt is fertile and easy to work. In South Africa, the flood plains of rivers such as the Pongola in northern KwaZulu-Natal support wetlands, fishing and farming.

So the final question is: how can we see the whole chain at work in South Africa?

## Subtopic: From Mountain to Sea and the Error Museum

The story of South Africa's diamonds shows the full chain of weathering, erosion and deposition. Diamonds form deep in the earth's mantle under enormous pressure and are brought to the surface in volcanic pipes of a rock called kimberlite, named after Kimberley, where the famous Big Hole was dug. Over many millions of years, the tops of kimberlite pipes in the interior were weathered and eroded. Rivers, especially the Orange River and its tributaries, carried the hard, heavy diamonds along with sand and gravel towards the Atlantic Ocean. Where the rivers slowed down, they deposited gravels containing diamonds along their old courses, and at the river mouth, diamonds were carried into the sea. Waves and currents then moved them along the coast and deposited them in beach gravels along the Namaqualand coast and southern Namibia, where they are mined today. Weathering released them, erosion moved them, and deposition concentrated them.

Another example is the Drakensberg. Its high basalt cliffs are weathered by frost, and the broken rock is moved downhill by rockfalls and by rivers such as the Tugela, which tumble down the escarpment. The rivers carry sediment across KwaZulu-Natal and deposit some of it on flood plains and at their mouths along the Indian Ocean coast.

Even the sand on a beach tells this story. Many South African beaches are made of quartz sand, the hard mineral grains left behind when rocks such as granite and sandstone were weathered. Rivers carried the grains to the coast, and waves and longshore currents spread them along the shore.

People are also affected. When rivers carry large amounts of eroded soil, dams fill up with sediment, a process called siltation, which reduces how much water they can store. South African dams lose storage capacity in this way every year, so erosion upstream affects water supplies downstream.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Erosion breaks rock down where it lies." That is weathering; erosion removes and transports material.

Exhibit two: "Deposition happens when a river speeds up." Deposition happens when the river slows down and loses energy.

Exhibit three: "Wind is the main agent of erosion everywhere in South Africa." Running water is the most important agent across most of the country; wind is important mainly in dry, bare areas.

Exhibit four: "Mass movement needs a river to carry the material." Mass movement is caused by gravity alone, without a separate carrying agent.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a crumbling biscuit, a broom, and a pile in the corner.

## Subtopic: The Crumbling Biscuit

Imagine a biscuit left on a windowsill for weeks. The sun, the damp and the ants slowly make it crumble. The crumbs are still lying on the windowsill. That is weathering: rock breaking down where it is.

Now someone comes along with a broom and sweeps the crumbs away. That is erosion: the broken pieces are picked up and carried off.

Finally the crumbs end up in a little pile in the corner of the room. That is deposition: the material is dropped in a new place.

Three short words help: weathering breaks, erosion takes, deposition drops. They happen one after another, again and again, all over the world. Over millions of years, they wear mountains down and build new land from the pieces.

If you remember nothing else, remember this test: has the material moved? If not, it is weathering. If it is moving, it is erosion. If it has stopped somewhere new, it is deposition.

So who holds the broom?

## Subtopic: The Broom

Nature has five brooms. The biggest broom in South Africa is running water. Raindrops splash soil loose, water runs down slopes and cuts little channels, and the channels can grow into huge ditches called dongas. Rivers push, scrape and grind their beds, using the stones they carry like sandpaper.

The second broom is wind. In dry places like the Kalahari, wind blows away loose sand and dust and blasts rocks smooth.

The third broom is ice. Glaciers are rivers of ice that scrape whole valleys. There are none in South Africa today, but millions of years ago there were.

The fourth broom is waves, which bash cliffs and move sand along beaches.

The fifth broom is gravity on its own. Rocks fall from cliffs, slopes slide after heavy rain, and soil creeps slowly downhill, tilting fence poles.

All five brooms are powered by the sun, which drives the wind and the rain, and by gravity, which pulls everything downhill.

So where does all the material go?

## Subtopic: The Pile in the Corner

Every broom drops its load when it runs out of energy. A fast river can roll big boulders. A slow river can only carry fine mud. So when a river slows down, on a bend, on flat land, or where it meets the sea, it drops what it is carrying: heavy stones first, fine mud last.

Rivers also make their stones smooth and round as they knock them against each other on the long journey downstream. That is why pebbles in a river are round, but rocks at the bottom of a cliff are sharp.

Wind does the same. When it slows down behind a bush or rock, it drops its sand, and dunes pile up, like the giant dunes of the Namib.

Here is a real South African story. Diamonds were once locked inside rock near Kimberley. Weathering broke the rock. The Orange River carried the diamonds hundreds of kilometres to the sea. Waves dropped them on beaches on the west coast, where people mine them today.

Here is the line to remember: weathering breaks rock where it lies, erosion carries it away, and deposition drops it where the moving water, wind or ice runs out of energy.
