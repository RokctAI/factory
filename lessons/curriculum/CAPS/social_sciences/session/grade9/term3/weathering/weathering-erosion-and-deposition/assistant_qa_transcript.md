### subtopic_1 — Three Processes, One Chain
**Assistant:** How can I tell in a question whether something is weathering or erosion?

**Tutor:** The easiest test is to ask one question: has the material moved? If the rock is being broken down or changed but is staying in the same place, it is weathering. If the material is being picked up, carried or worn away by a moving agent, it is erosion. For example, a boulder cracked by freezing water on a mountain slope is an example of weathering, even if the cracks are large. But when that broken rock tumbles down the slope as a rockfall, or a river carries pieces of it downstream, that is erosion and transport. A limestone rock being dissolved by rainwater where it lies is chemical weathering, but when a river dissolves minerals from its bed and carries them away, geographers count that as erosion by solution. Some processes involve both. A river that uses its load to scrape its bed is eroding, and in doing so it also breaks rock into smaller pieces. Look for key words in a question. Words such as breaking down, cracking, splitting, decaying, rusting and dissolving in place point to weathering. Words such as removing, carrying, transporting, wearing away, washing away and blowing away point to erosion. Words such as dropping, laying down, settling, building up and accumulating point to deposition. In longer answers, it is good to show that you understand the sequence, explaining that weathering usually happens first and makes erosion easier, and that eroded material must eventually be deposited somewhere. Using the correct term for each step is an important skill in Geography examinations.

### subtopic_2 — The Agents of Erosion
**Assistant:** Why are dongas such a problem in South Africa?

**Tutor:** Dongas are deep, steep-sided gullies cut into the land by running water, and they are one of the most visible signs of soil erosion in South Africa, especially in parts of KwaZulu-Natal, the Eastern Cape, the Free State and Limpopo. They often begin as small rills, little channels formed when rainwater flows over bare ground. If nothing slows the water, the rills join together and cut deeper, and during heavy summer thunderstorms they can grow very quickly into gullies several metres deep. Several factors make South Africa especially vulnerable. Much of the country receives its rain in short, intense summer storms, which produce a lot of fast-flowing surface runoff. Many soils, particularly in the former homelands where people were crowded onto small areas of land under apartheid, have been weakened by overgrazing, the clearing of vegetation and poor ploughing practices, which leave the soil bare. Footpaths, cattle tracks and poorly designed roads can also channel water and start gullies. Dongas cause serious problems. They destroy farmland and grazing, cut across roads and paths, and can undermine houses. The soil they remove is carried into rivers, which become muddy, and into dams, which slowly fill with sediment and store less water. Once a donga forms, it is difficult and expensive to repair. Methods include building small stone or gabion walls across the gully to slow the water and trap sediment, planting grass and trees, fencing areas to keep livestock out, and redirecting runoff. Preventing dongas by protecting vegetation is much easier than repairing them.

### subtopic_3 — Transport and Deposition
**Assistant:** Why does a river drop the biggest stones first?

**Tutor:** The reason is energy. To carry material, a river must use some of its energy to lift or push it along against gravity and friction. Larger and heavier particles need much more energy to move than small, light particles. When a river is flowing fast, especially in the steep upper course or during a flood, it has a great deal of energy, so it can roll boulders and carry pebbles, sand and fine silt all at the same time. As the river slows down, for example when the slope becomes gentler, when it flows into a wide, flat area, on the inside of a bend, or where it enters a lake or the sea, its energy decreases. The first particles it can no longer carry are the largest and heaviest, so these are dropped first. As the river continues to slow, it drops smaller pebbles, then sand, and finally, only in very still water, the finest silt and clay settle out. This process is called sorting, and it creates a pattern that geographers can use as evidence. Along a typical river, you find large angular boulders near the source, smaller and more rounded pebbles further downstream because of attrition, sandy banks in the middle course, and fine mud on flood plains and in deltas near the mouth. The same principle explains why fine clay stays suspended in a river for a long time, making it look brown, and why muddy water in a glass slowly clears as the particles settle. Engineers use this knowledge to design dams and harbours, because they need to predict where sediment will collect.

### subtopic_4 — From Mountain to Sea and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do people speed up erosion and deposition?

**Tutor:** Natural erosion usually happens slowly, but human activities can speed it up dramatically, often by removing the vegetation that protects the soil. Plant roots hold soil together, leaves and grass break the force of raindrops, and plant cover slows down running water and wind. When people clear forests, plough fields, allow too many animals to graze, or build roads and houses, the soil is left bare and exposed. Rain then washes it away much more quickly, and wind can blow dry topsoil away. Mining and construction move large amounts of rock and soil and create bare slopes and spoil heaps that are easily eroded. Paved surfaces in cities stop water soaking into the ground, so more water runs off quickly into rivers, increasing their energy and causing erosion of riverbanks and flooding. People also affect deposition. Dams trap sediment that would otherwise travel down to the coast, so reservoirs slowly fill with silt, while beaches and river mouths downstream may receive less sand and begin to erode. Building harbours and sea walls can change where waves deposit sand along the coast. On the other hand, people can slow down erosion by contour ploughing, building terraces, planting cover crops, rotating grazing and protecting wetlands, which trap sediment. In South Africa, the Working for Water and land care programmes aim to restore vegetation and reduce erosion. You will study human effects on erosion, and especially the role of agriculture, in more detail later this term, because soil lost in a few years can take hundreds of years to form again.
