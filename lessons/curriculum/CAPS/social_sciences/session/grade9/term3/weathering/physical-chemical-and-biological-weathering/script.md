# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces weathering, the first of the surface forces that shape the earth. It covers what weathering is and why it matters; physical weathering, including frost action, exfoliation and salt crystal growth; chemical weathering, including oxidation, carbonation and hydrolysis; and biological weathering by plants, animals and lichens, together with the role of climate. The anchor idea is a simple definition: weathering is the breaking down of rock in place, at or near the earth's surface, by the action of the weather, water, chemicals and living things. The governing idea is that weathering is the first step in shaping landscapes and making soil: rock that is weathered can then be moved by erosion and laid down elsewhere by deposition, so without weathering there would be no soil, no fertile farmland and far fewer of the landforms we see around us.

## Subtopic: What Is Weathering?

Rocks look permanent, but they are not. When rocks are exposed at the earth's surface, they are attacked by sun, rain, frost, air and living things. Over hundreds, thousands and millions of years, even the hardest rock breaks down. This process is called weathering.

The key words in the definition are in place. Weathering happens where the rock is. The rock is broken up or changed, but it is not carried away. If the broken material is then moved by water, wind, ice or gravity, that movement is called erosion, which you will study in the next lesson. Weathering is like the slow crumbling of a biscuit left out in the rain; erosion is like someone sweeping the crumbs away.

Geographers divide weathering into three main types. Physical weathering, also called mechanical weathering, breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing the minerals it is made of. A large granite boulder may be split into smaller blocks, but each block is still granite. Chemical weathering changes the minerals in the rock into new substances, often softer or more easily dissolved, so the rock is weakened and decays. Biological weathering is caused by living things, plants, animals, fungi and bacteria, and it can work either physically, for example by roots splitting rock, or chemically, for example by acids produced by lichens.

In nature the three types usually work together. Cracks made by physical weathering let water into the rock, which speeds up chemical weathering; chemical weathering weakens the rock, so it breaks more easily; and plant roots grow into the cracks and widen them further.

Weathering matters for several reasons. It is the first step in the formation of soil, which is made of weathered rock particles mixed with organic matter, air and water. Almost all the food we grow depends on soil. Weathering prepares material that rivers, wind and the sea can erode and later deposit to form landforms such as deltas and beaches. It shapes distinctive landscapes, such as rounded granite domes and rocky koppies. And it affects people directly: it damages buildings, roads, bridges and monuments, and causes rockfalls on mountain passes.

How fast weathering happens depends on several factors. Climate, especially temperature and rainfall, is the most important, as you will see at the end of this part. Rock type matters because some minerals are much more easily broken down than others. The number of joints and cracks matters because they let water and roots into the rock and increase the surface area that can be attacked. Vegetation can protect rock from heating and cooling, but it also adds roots and acids. And time matters most of all: the effects of weathering that we see today are the result of processes acting over thousands to millions of years.

So the next question is: how does physical weathering break rock apart?

## Subtopic: Physical Weathering

Physical weathering breaks rock into smaller fragments through forces that push, pull and crack it. The most important processes are frost action, exfoliation caused by temperature changes, salt crystal growth and pressure release.

Frost action, also called freeze-thaw weathering, happens where temperatures rise above and fall below freezing point, zero degrees Celsius, often in the same day. Rain or melted snow seeps into cracks in the rock. At night the water freezes, and as water turns to ice it expands by about nine percent in volume. This pushes against the sides of the crack like a wedge, widening it. In the day the ice melts, and more water seeps deeper. Repeated many times, the rock is split apart. The broken, angular pieces that collect at the foot of a cliff form a slope called scree or talus. In South Africa, frost action is common on the high peaks of the Drakensberg and the mountains of the Eastern Cape and Western Cape in winter.

Exfoliation, sometimes called onion-skin weathering, happens where there are large differences between day and night temperatures, as in deserts and semi-deserts. In the day, the outer layer of rock heats up and expands; at night it cools and contracts. Because rock is a poor conductor of heat, the outer layer expands and contracts more than the inside, so stress builds up, and over time thin outer layers peel off like the skin of an onion. Pressure release also contributes: when overlying rock is eroded away, the rock beneath expands slightly and cracks in layers parallel to the surface. Together these processes create rounded, dome-shaped granite hills, such as Paarl Rock in the Western Cape.

Salt crystal growth is common in dry areas and along coasts. Water containing dissolved salts seeps into cracks and pores in rock. When the water evaporates, salt crystals form and grow, pushing the rock apart grain by grain. This is why buildings and rocks near the sea often look pitted and crumbly.

Block disintegration happens when rocks that have joints, natural cracks, are broken along those lines into rectangular blocks by repeated heating and cooling or by frost. The dolerite koppies of the Karoo, covered in broken blocks and boulders, show the results of weathering along joints, together with the chemical weathering discussed next.

Notice what physical weathering produces: angular fragments and smaller pieces of the same rock. Physical weathering is most important in dry climates and in cold climates where freeze-thaw happens often.

So the next question is: how can chemicals change rock itself?

## Subtopic: Chemical Weathering

Chemical weathering changes the minerals that make up a rock, forming new substances. It needs water, and it works fastest where it is hot and wet, because chemical reactions speed up as temperature rises. The most important processes are oxidation, carbonation, hydrolysis and solution.

Oxidation happens when oxygen, usually dissolved in water, reacts with minerals that contain iron. The iron forms iron oxides, which is the same process as rusting. The rock turns red, orange or yellow-brown and becomes weaker and crumbly. The red soils found in many parts of South Africa, such as in parts of Limpopo, Mpumalanga and KwaZulu-Natal, get their colour from iron oxides formed by weathering.

Carbonation happens when carbon dioxide from the air and soil dissolves in rainwater to form a weak acid called carbonic acid. This acid reacts with rocks containing calcium carbonate, especially limestone, and the dissolved minerals are carried away in the water. Over thousands of years carbonation creates caves, underground passages and features such as stalactites and stalagmites, which form when dissolved minerals are deposited again from dripping water. The Cango Caves near Oudtshoorn in the Western Cape and the Sudwala Caves in Mpumalanga were formed in this way, as were the caves of the Cradle of Humankind near Johannesburg, where many important fossils have been found.

Hydrolysis happens when water reacts chemically with certain minerals. In granite, the mineral feldspar reacts with water to form clay, which is soft. As the feldspar turns to clay, the quartz grains in the granite are released, and the rock crumbles into a gritty sand. This is why weathered granite often looks rotten and can be dug with a spade.

Solution happens when minerals such as rock salt dissolve directly in water and are carried away. Human activities can speed up chemical weathering, for example where air pollution from burning coal produces acid rain, a topic explored in the lesson on people and weathering.

Chemical weathering often rounds the corners and edges of rocks, because the chemicals attack them from more than one side. This is called spheroidal weathering, and it helps explain the rounded boulders seen on many dolerite koppies in the Karoo and on granite hills in Limpopo and Zimbabwe.

So the final question is: how do living things weather rock, and how does climate decide which type matters most?

## Subtopic: Biological Weathering, Climate and the Error Museum

Biological weathering is weathering caused by living things. It can be physical or chemical. Plant roots grow into cracks in rock in search of water and nutrients. As the roots grow thicker, they press against the sides of the crack and widen it, sometimes splitting large rocks apart. You can see this in pavements and walls, where tree roots lift and crack concrete. Burrowing animals, such as moles, earthworms, termites and porcupines, dig into weathered rock and soil, exposing fresh rock surfaces to air and water and mixing the soil. In South Africa, termites move large amounts of soil and create termite mounds.

Biological weathering can also be chemical. Lichens, made of a fungus and an alga living together, grow on bare rock and produce weak acids that slowly dissolve the minerals beneath them. Decaying plant material forms humic acids in the soil, which speed up chemical weathering of the rock below. Bacteria in the soil also contribute.

Climate is the main factor deciding which type of weathering is most important in a place. In hot, wet climates, such as tropical rainforests and the subtropical east coast of South Africa, chemical weathering is fastest, so the rock may be weathered to great depths and soils can be thick. In hot, dry climates, such as the Namib and the Karoo, there is little water for chemical reactions, so physical weathering by temperature changes and salt crystals is more important. In cold climates with frequent freezing and thawing, frost action dominates. In general, warmth and moisture increase chemical weathering, while large temperature ranges and freezing increase physical weathering. Rock type also matters: limestone is especially vulnerable to carbonation, while hard quartzite and sandstone resist weathering and often form high cliffs, such as those of Table Mountain.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Weathering and erosion are the same thing." Weathering breaks rock down in place; erosion moves the broken material away.

Exhibit two: "Physical weathering changes the minerals in rock." Physical weathering only breaks rock into smaller pieces; chemical weathering changes the minerals.

Exhibit three: "Frost action works best where it is always frozen." It works best where temperatures go above and below freezing repeatedly, so the ice keeps forming and melting.

Exhibit four: "Chemical weathering is fastest in deserts." It is fastest where it is hot and wet; deserts lack the water needed for chemical reactions.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a hammer, a rusty nail, and a stubborn root.

## Subtopic: The Hammer

Rocks may look as if they will last forever, but they are always breaking down very slowly. This breaking down, where the rock lies, is called weathering. If the pieces are then carried away by water or wind, that is a different process called erosion.

The first kind of weathering is like an invisible hammer. It breaks rock into smaller pieces, but the pieces are still the same rock. We call it physical weathering.

One hammer is ice. Water gets into a crack in the rock. At night it freezes, and ice takes up more space than water, so it pushes the crack wider, like a wedge. Day after day, night after night, the rock splits. This happens on cold mountains such as the Drakensberg in winter.

Another hammer is heat. In a desert, rock gets very hot by day and cold at night. The outside layer swells and shrinks again and again until it peels off like the skin of an onion. That is how smooth, rounded rock domes such as Paarl Rock are formed.

A third hammer is salt. Near the sea or in dry places, salty water soaks into tiny holes in the rock. When the water dries up, salt crystals grow and push the grains of rock apart, so the surface crumbles like sugar.

Every one of these hammers leaves sharp, broken pieces behind, and at the bottom of a cliff these pile up as loose slopes of stones.

So what about rocks that change into something new?

## Subtopic: The Rusty Nail

Leave an iron nail outside in the rain, and it rusts. Rock can rust too. This is chemical weathering: the rock is not just broken, it is changed into new substances that are weaker.

When rock with iron in it meets water and air, it rusts and turns red or brown. That is why so much South African soil is red.

Rainwater is very slightly acidic, a bit like weak fizzy cold drink. Over thousands of years, it slowly dissolves limestone, hollowing out huge caves like the Cango Caves near Oudtshoorn. Drips inside the caves leave behind stone icicles, called stalactites and stalagmites.

Granite can go soft too. Water turns some of its crystals into clay, and the rock crumbles like stale bread.

Chemical weathering loves heat and water. It works fastest in warm, wet places. It also rounds off the sharp corners of rocks, because corners are attacked from more sides than flat faces. That is why so many boulders on koppies look smooth and round.

So what part do living things play?

## Subtopic: The Stubborn Root

Have you seen a tree root lift and crack a pavement? Roots grow into tiny cracks in rock looking for water. As they grow thicker, they push the crack wider until the rock splits. This is biological weathering, weathering by living things.

Have you noticed grey, orange or green crusty patches on old rocks and gravestones? Those are lichens, and they are weathering the stone right now.

Animals help too. Termites, earthworms, moles and porcupines dig and burrow, letting air and water reach fresh rock. Small flat patches called lichens grow on bare rocks and make weak acids that slowly eat into the stone.

The weather in a place decides which kind of weathering wins. In hot, wet places the rust-and-dissolve kind wins. In deserts the heat hammer wins. On freezing mountains the ice hammer wins. And everywhere, roots and animals lend a hand.

Here is the line to remember: weathering breaks down rock where it lies, by physical force, by chemical change or by living things, and it is the first step in making the soil we all depend on.
