### subtopic_1 — What Is Weathering?
**Assistant:** Why does water expanding when it freezes matter so much for weathering?

**Tutor:** Most substances shrink when they change from a liquid to a solid, but water is unusual: when it freezes into ice, it expands by about nine percent in volume. This simple fact makes ice a powerful natural wedge. When water fills a crack in rock and then freezes, the ice needs more space than the water did. Because the ice is trapped by the walls of the crack, it presses outwards on them with great force, enough to widen the crack slightly. When the ice melts, the water can seep a little deeper into the widened crack, and the next time it freezes, it pushes again. Repeated over hundreds or thousands of freeze-thaw cycles, this process splits rock into angular blocks and fragments. The important point is that the temperature must keep crossing zero degrees Celsius. In places that are frozen all the time, the ice simply stays in the crack and does not keep working. In places that never freeze, there is no ice wedge at all. Freeze-thaw weathering is therefore most active in mountains and high-latitude areas where days are above freezing and nights are below, as on the high Drakensberg and the mountains of the Cape in winter. The broken fragments fall and collect at the foot of slopes as scree. People also see this process in everyday life: water pipes can burst when the water inside them freezes, and potholes in roads grow when water in cracks freezes and thaws. Road engineers in cold areas must design roads to cope with this.

### subtopic_2 — Physical Weathering
**Assistant:** How exactly does carbonation create caves in limestone?

**Tutor:** Carbonation is a chemical weathering process that is especially important in areas of limestone, a rock made mainly of calcium carbonate. It begins in the air and soil. As rain falls, it dissolves a small amount of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, and as it seeps through the soil, it picks up much more carbon dioxide produced by decaying plants and soil organisms. The carbon dioxide combines with water to form carbonic acid, a weak acid. When this slightly acidic water reaches limestone, it reacts with the calcium carbonate and turns it into calcium bicarbonate, which dissolves in water and is carried away. Limestone has many joints and bedding planes, the natural cracks and layers in the rock, so water flows along these lines of weakness and dissolves them wider. Over thousands of years, narrow cracks become passages, and passages become large caverns, often with underground streams. Inside the caves, the process can reverse. When water carrying dissolved calcium bicarbonate drips from the ceiling, some carbon dioxide escapes into the cave air and some water evaporates, so calcium carbonate is deposited again as a tiny ring of crystal. Drip by drip, these build stalactites hanging from the ceiling, and where the drips land, stalagmites grow upwards. Where the two meet, they form columns. Stalactites grow very slowly, often only a fraction of a millimetre to a few millimetres in a year, so large formations take a very long time to form. The Cango Caves, the Sudwala Caves and the caves of the Cradle of Humankind are all South African examples.

### subtopic_3 — Chemical Weathering
**Assistant:** Why are the corners of rocks often rounded by weathering?

**Tutor:** When you look at weathered rocks in places such as the Karoo or Limpopo, you often see rounded boulders rather than sharp-edged blocks. This rounding is mainly the result of chemical weathering, and it is called spheroidal weathering, because the rocks become more like spheres. It starts with joints, the natural cracks that divide many rocks, such as dolerite and granite, into rough rectangular blocks. Water seeps along these joints and begins to weather the rock chemically from the outside of each block inwards. Think about a single block. A flat face of the block is attacked by water from one side only. An edge, where two faces meet, is attacked from two sides. A corner, where three faces meet, is attacked from three sides. So corners weather fastest, edges next, and flat faces slowest. Gradually the corners and edges are rounded off, and the block becomes a rounded core of fresh rock surrounded by layers of weathered material, sometimes peeling off in thin shells. When erosion later removes the loose weathered material, the rounded cores are left behind as boulders, sometimes stacked on top of each other in striking piles. Physical weathering by temperature changes can add to the effect by causing the weakened outer layers to flake off. The result is the typical landscape of rounded boulders on many South African koppies, which shows that weathering has been at work for a very long time beneath the surface, even before the rocks were exposed.

### subtopic_4 — Biological Weathering, Climate and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why is climate so important in deciding the type of weathering?

**Tutor:** Climate controls weathering because it determines the two things that drive most weathering processes: temperature and moisture. Chemical weathering needs water, because the reactions of oxidation, carbonation and hydrolysis all take place in water. It also speeds up as temperature rises, because chemical reactions happen faster when molecules have more energy. So where it is both hot and wet, as in tropical rainforests or the warm, humid coast of KwaZulu-Natal, chemical weathering is fast and rock can be decomposed to depths of tens of metres, producing thick, deep, often reddish soils. Physical weathering depends more on temperature changes and freezing. In hot deserts, there is little water, so chemical weathering is slow, but the large difference between hot days and cold nights causes rock to expand and contract, and salts left behind by evaporation grow in cracks. So exfoliation and salt crystal growth are important, and rocks tend to break into angular fragments, while soils are thin. In cold mountain and polar regions, freeze-thaw is the main process. Where it is cold and dry, weathering of all kinds is very slow. Geographers sometimes show these relationships on a diagram with mean annual temperature on one axis and mean annual rainfall on the other, with zones of strong chemical weathering, strong frost action and slight weathering. Rock type, slope and vegetation also matter, but climate gives the broad pattern. This is why soils in different parts of South Africa, from the wet east to the dry west, are so different.
