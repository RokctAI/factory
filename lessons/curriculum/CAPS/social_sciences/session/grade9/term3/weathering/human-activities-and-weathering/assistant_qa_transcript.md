### subtopic_1 — Air Pollution and Acid Rain
**Assistant:** Why does the pH scale make acid rain sound less serious than it is?

**Tutor:** The pH scale measures how acidic or alkaline a substance is, from 0, very acidic, to 14, very alkaline, with 7 being neutral, like pure water. The tricky part is that the scale is logarithmic, which means that each step of one unit represents a tenfold change in acidity. So a difference that looks small on the scale is actually large. Natural rain has a pH of about 5,6, slightly acidic because carbon dioxide dissolves in it. Rain with a pH of 4,6 is not just a little more acidic; it is ten times more acidic than natural rain. Rain with a pH of 3,6 is one hundred times more acidic than natural rain, because ten multiplied by ten is one hundred. In some heavily polluted regions of the world, rain and fog with a pH below 4 have been recorded. This explains why acid rain can weather limestone, marble and other building materials so much faster than natural rain, and why it can harm lakes and soils. Many fish cannot survive when lake water becomes too acidic, and acidic soils can release harmful metals such as aluminium, which damage plant roots. When you read about acid rain, look carefully at the pH numbers and remember that every whole number lower means ten times more acidic. This is also a good reminder for Geography and Natural Sciences in general: graphs and scales must be read carefully, because the way a scale is designed affects how big a change looks.

### subtopic_2 — Mining, Quarrying and Construction
**Assistant:** Why is acid mine drainage such a long-lasting problem?

**Tutor:** Acid mine drainage is long-lasting because the chemical weathering that causes it can continue for decades or even centuries after mining stops. On the Witwatersrand, gold-bearing rock contains pyrite, an iron sulphide mineral. Deep underground, where there is little oxygen, pyrite is stable. But mining brought enormous amounts of this rock to the surface and crushed it into fine particles, which were piled into mine dumps and tailings dams, and it also exposed pyrite in the walls of underground tunnels. When pyrite meets oxygen and water, it oxidises, forming sulphuric acid and dissolved iron. The acidic water then dissolves other metals from the rock, some of them toxic, such as heavy metals and, in some places, uranium. While mines were working, they pumped water out of the tunnels to keep them dry. When mines closed, pumping stopped, and groundwater slowly filled the interconnected tunnels, reacting with the exposed pyrite. Eventually the rising acidic water can reach the surface and decant, or flow out, into streams and rivers. As long as pyrite remains exposed to oxygen and water, the reaction continues, and there are enormous quantities of it in old mines and dumps. The water must therefore be pumped and treated, for example by adding lime to neutralise the acid, at great and continuing cost. The problem also shows that the environmental costs of mining are often paid long after the profits have been made, which raises important questions about who should pay for the clean-up.

### subtopic_3 — Agriculture, Vegetation and Cities
**Assistant:** How can I protect rock art when I visit a site?

**Tutor:** Visiting a rock art site is a privilege, because the paintings and engravings are irreplaceable records of the beliefs and lives of San people and other communities over thousands of years. Natural weathering already threatens them, so visitors must take great care not to add to the damage. The most important rule is never to touch the paintings or the rock surface around them. The natural oils and dirt on our fingers leave marks and weaken the paint, and the combined effect of many visitors touching the same place can be severe. Never wet the paintings with water or any other liquid, even if you have heard that it makes the colours look brighter. Wetting dissolves salts in the rock, which then crystallise beneath the paint and push it off, and it can also encourage the growth of algae and fungi. Do not scratch, chalk, trace or write on the rock, and never remove anything from the site, including stones, bones or soil, which may hold archaeological information. Do not light fires or cook near shelters, because smoke and heat damage the paintings and the rock. Stay behind barriers and on paths, which protect fragile floors where archaeological deposits are buried, and keep dust down. Visit only sites that are open to the public, preferably with a trained guide, who can explain the meaning of the art. Take photographs without flash where requested, and report any damage you see to the site managers. In South Africa, rock art sites are protected by the National Heritage Resources Act, so damaging them is a criminal offence.

### subtopic_4 — Protecting Heritage, Reducing Damage and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Can we ever completely stop human-caused weathering?

**Tutor:** We cannot stop weathering completely, because it is a natural process that has shaped the earth for billions of years, and it will continue as long as there is air, water, sunshine and life. But we can greatly reduce the extra weathering that human activities cause, and history shows that this is possible. For example, in Europe and North America acid rain was a severe problem in the 1970s and 1980s, damaging forests, lakes and historic buildings. Governments passed laws limiting sulphur dioxide emissions, power stations fitted scrubbers that remove sulphur from smoke, and cleaner fuels were introduced. Sulphur dioxide emissions in many of these countries fell by more than three quarters, and rainfall became less acidic. South Africa has set minimum emission standards for power stations and industries, although meeting them has been slow and expensive, and moving towards renewable energy such as solar and wind will reduce pollution further. Mining companies can rehabilitate dumps by covering them with soil and plants and treating acidic water. Farmers can protect vegetation and manage irrigation carefully. Heritage managers can protect rock art and historic buildings by controlling visitors, improving drainage and monitoring damage. There are trade-offs, however. Cleaner technology costs money, and mining and coal power provide jobs and electricity that many people depend on. A balanced answer recognises that reducing human-caused weathering requires both government action and individual choices, and that the benefits, such as cleaner air, safer water and protected heritage, are usually worth the costs in the long run.
