# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the impact of human activities on weathering. It covers how air pollution and acid rain speed up chemical weathering; how mining, quarrying and construction expose rock and speed up both physical and chemical weathering, including the problem of acid mine drainage; how agriculture, clearing vegetation and urban areas change weathering; and the damage people cause to buildings, monuments and heritage sites, with ways to reduce it. The anchor idea is that weathering is a natural process, but people change the conditions that control it, such as the chemistry of rainwater, the amount of rock exposed and the protection given by vegetation, and so they can make it happen much faster. The governing idea is that human impacts on weathering have costs for the economy, for the environment and for our heritage, and that understanding the processes helps us to reduce the damage.

## Subtopic: Air Pollution and Acid Rain

Rainwater is naturally slightly acidic, because it dissolves carbon dioxide from the air to form weak carbonic acid. As you learnt earlier, this natural acidity slowly weathers rocks such as limestone through carbonation. Human activities can make rain much more acidic.

When coal, oil and petrol are burnt in power stations, factories and vehicles, they release gases called sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides into the air. These gases react with water vapour, oxygen and sunlight in the atmosphere to form sulphuric acid and nitric acid, which fall to the ground dissolved in rain, snow, mist or fog, or as dry particles. This is called acid rain or, more broadly, acid deposition. Acidity is measured on the pH scale, on which 7 is neutral and lower numbers are more acidic. Natural rain has a pH of about 5,6, while acid rain usually has a pH below 5, and in badly polluted areas it can be much lower. Because the pH scale is logarithmic, rain with a pH of 4,6 is ten times more acidic than rain with a pH of 5,6.

Acid rain speeds up chemical weathering. It attacks rocks containing calcium carbonate, such as limestone and marble, much faster than natural rain does, dissolving the stone and turning its surface into a crumbly crust of gypsum that washes away. Statues lose their faces and fine details, inscriptions on gravestones become unreadable, and building stone crumbles. Acid rain also corrodes metals, such as iron and steel in bridges and railways, and damages paint. It harms the environment too, acidifying soils, lakes and rivers and damaging plants.

South Africa produces most of its electricity by burning coal, and many large coal-fired power stations are concentrated on the Mpumalanga Highveld, together with a large coal-to-fuel plant at Secunda. The government declared the Highveld a Priority Area for air quality in 2007 because of high levels of pollution, including sulphur dioxide. Studies have found acidic rainfall in parts of Mpumalanga and Gauteng. However, acid rain does not stay where it is produced: winds can carry the pollutants hundreds of kilometres, so its effects can be felt far from the factories. Many countries have reduced acid rain by fitting equipment called scrubbers to power stations, using cleaner fuels, and moving to renewable energy such as solar and wind power.

Acid rain also shows how weathering connects to economics. Repairing and replacing damaged stonework, metal structures and paint costs cities and governments large sums every year, and the loss of fine detail on historic monuments cannot be fully repaired at all. The cost of pollution is therefore paid not only by the people who breathe the air but by everyone who maintains buildings and infrastructure. Geographers call such hidden costs externalities, because they are not included in the price of the electricity or fuel that caused them.

So the next question is: how do mining, quarrying and construction affect weathering?

## Subtopic: Mining, Quarrying and Construction

Mining, quarrying, road building and construction expose large amounts of fresh rock that was previously protected underground or beneath soil and vegetation. Once exposed to air, water and temperature changes, this rock begins to weather quickly. Blasting with explosives creates many new cracks, so water can seep in, freeze, and react with minerals, speeding up both physical and chemical weathering. Road cuttings through hillsides, for example, often suffer from rockfalls as the freshly exposed rock weathers and loosens, which is why engineers build nets, catch fences and retaining walls along mountain passes.

Mining also produces huge heaps of crushed waste rock and fine material called tailings, stored in mine dumps. Because the rock has been crushed into small pieces, it has a very large surface area exposed to air and water, so it weathers far faster than solid rock.

One of the most serious examples is acid mine drainage on the Witwatersrand in Gauteng, where gold has been mined since 1886. The gold-bearing rock contains a mineral called pyrite, sometimes known as fool's gold, which is made of iron and sulphur. When pyrite in mine dumps and old underground workings is exposed to oxygen and water, it oxidises, a chemical weathering process, producing sulphuric acid and dissolved iron. As mines closed and stopped pumping water out, the old tunnels slowly flooded, and the water became acidic and full of dissolved metals, some of them toxic. This acid mine drainage can seep or decant into rivers and groundwater, harming plants, animals and people, and it corrodes pipes and concrete. The government has built treatment plants on the Witwatersrand to pump and neutralise the acidic water, but the problem will continue for many years. Coal mines in Mpumalanga cause similar problems.

Quarries that supply crushed stone, sand and lime for building are found near most South African towns, and abandoned quarries often have steep, unstable walls that continue to weather and shed rock for many years. Asbestos mining in the Northern Cape and Limpopo left dumps of fibrous waste that weathered and released dangerous fibres into the air, a serious health hazard for nearby communities, which shows that weathering of mine waste can directly affect human health.

Large construction projects, such as dams, highways and housing developments, also expose rock and soil. When weathered material is then eroded from these bare surfaces, rivers become loaded with sediment.

So the next question is: how do farming, vegetation clearing and cities affect weathering?

## Subtopic: Agriculture, Vegetation and Cities

Vegetation affects weathering in two ways. Plant cover protects rocks and soil from extreme temperatures and from the direct impact of rain, while plant roots and decaying plant matter add to biological and chemical weathering. When people clear natural vegetation for farming, firewood, settlements or plantations, bare rock and soil are exposed to stronger heating by day and cooling by night, increasing physical weathering, and the weathered material is then much more easily eroded.

Farming can change weathering in other ways. Ploughing breaks up and turns over the soil, exposing fresh material to air and water. Some fertilisers can make soils more acidic over time, speeding up chemical weathering of minerals in the soil. Irrigation in hot, dry areas can cause salinisation: water evaporates quickly, leaving salts behind in the soil and on rock surfaces, where salt crystal growth weathers rock and damages buildings and roads. Salinisation affects some irrigated land along the lower Orange River and in other dry parts of South Africa, and it reduces crop yields.

Cities create their own conditions for weathering. Air pollution from traffic and industry makes rain more acidic, and soot settles on buildings. Cities are often warmer than the surrounding countryside, an effect called the urban heat island, and buildings and pavements heat up and cool down every day. Salt used on roads in cold countries to melt ice speeds up salt weathering of concrete and stone. Even water leaking from pipes or gutters can speed up the weathering of building stone and brick.

Climate change, caused mainly by the burning of fossil fuels, is also likely to affect weathering. Higher temperatures and more intense storms may speed up chemical weathering in some places, while changes in rainfall may increase physical weathering and erosion in others.

It is important to notice that many of these human impacts work together. Clearing vegetation exposes rock, which weathers faster; the weathered material is then eroded more easily; and polluted rain speeds up chemical weathering of whatever rock remains. Geographers therefore look at the whole system rather than at one cause alone.

So the final question is: how do people damage heritage through weathering, and what can be done?

## Subtopic: Protecting Heritage, Reducing Damage and the Error Museum

Weathering threatens cultural heritage around the world. Famous examples include the marble of the Taj Mahal in India, which has been yellowed by air pollution, and medieval cathedrals in Europe whose carved stone figures have lost their features. In South Africa, the uKhahlamba-Drakensberg Park, a World Heritage Site, contains thousands of rock art sites with tens of thousands of paintings made by San people over many centuries. These paintings are on sandstone shelters and are naturally affected by weathering, such as water seeping through the rock, salt crystals forming beneath the paint, and the flaking of the rock surface. Human activities have made this worse. In the past, some visitors splashed water or other liquids on paintings to make the colours brighter for photographs, which damaged the paint and sped up weathering. Others touched the paintings, lit fires in the shelters, or scratched graffiti. Today, rock art sites are protected by law, many can only be visited with trained guides, and visitors are asked never to touch or wet the paintings.

Historic buildings and monuments also need protection. Ways to reduce human-caused weathering include reducing air pollution by cleaning emissions from power stations and vehicles and moving to renewable energy; choosing building stone that resists weathering; repairing gutters and drains so that water does not soak into walls; using protective coatings on some surfaces; and carefully cleaning soot and pollution from buildings. In mining areas, mine dumps can be covered with soil and planted with vegetation, and acidic water can be collected and treated before it reaches rivers. On farms, protecting vegetation cover, using irrigation carefully and avoiding over-fertilising reduce weathering and erosion. Individuals can help by saving electricity, which reduces coal burning, by using public transport, and by respecting heritage sites.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Natural rain is completely neutral." Natural rain is slightly acidic because it contains carbon dioxide; human pollution makes it much more acidic.

Exhibit two: "Acid rain only falls next to the factory that caused it." Winds can carry pollutants hundreds of kilometres before they fall as acid rain.

Exhibit three: "Splashing water on rock art protects it." Wetting rock art damages the paint and speeds up weathering of the rock surface.

Exhibit four: "Mining only causes physical weathering." Mining also causes chemical weathering, such as the oxidation of pyrite that produces acid mine drainage.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a smoky chimney, a blasted hillside, and a fingerprint on a painting.

## Subtopic: The Smoky Chimney

Weathering happens naturally, but people can make it go much faster. The first way is through smoke.

When power stations burn coal, and cars burn petrol, gases go up into the sky. Up there, they mix with water and turn into acids. Later they fall back down in the rain. We call this acid rain.

Acid rain is like a weak version of the vinegar in your kitchen falling from the sky. It eats into stone, especially limestone and marble, much faster than normal rain. Statues lose their noses and faces, and the writing on old gravestones fades away. It rusts metal bridges and harms lakes, soil and trees.

South Africa gets most of its electricity from coal, and many power stations stand together in Mpumalanga, so pollution there is high. The wind can carry the gases far away before they fall as rain.

Acid rain also makes the soil sour, so some plants struggle to grow, and fish can die in lakes that become too acidic. So the damage is not only to stone but to living things as well.

So what happens when people dig into the ground?

## Subtopic: The Blasted Hillside

When people dig mines, cut quarries or blast roads through hills, they uncover fresh rock that was safely hidden underground. Explosions crack it. Now sun, rain and frost can attack it, and it weathers much faster. That is why loose rocks sometimes fall onto mountain roads.

Mines also leave behind huge heaps of crushed rock. Crushed rock weathers very fast, because so many tiny surfaces touch air and water. Near Johannesburg, old gold mine rock contains a mineral called pyrite. When air and water reach it, it makes acid. Flooded old mines fill with this sour, metal-filled water, which can leak into rivers. This is called acid mine drainage.

It is a bit like peeling an orange. While the skin is on, the fruit stays fresh. Peel it, and it dries out and spoils quickly. Digging and blasting peel the protective skin off the land.

Farmers can speed up weathering too, by clearing plants that protect the ground, and by watering dry land so heavily that salt is left behind to crumble the rock.

In cities, smoke and car fumes blacken buildings, and old stone churches and town halls slowly crumble. Even a leaking gutter, dripping on the same wall for years, can rot the bricks.

So how do people harm precious places?

## Subtopic: A Fingerprint on a Painting

In the Drakensberg there are thousands of rock paintings made by San people long ago. They are part of our shared heritage. Nature slowly weathers them, but people have made it worse. Some visitors once threw water on the paintings to make them look brighter in photographs. Others touched them, lit fires near them, or scratched their names into the rock. Every fingerprint and splash speeds up the damage, and once a painting is gone, it is gone forever.

Rock paintings are not the only treasures at risk. Old buildings, statues and gravestones in our towns and cities also tell the stories of the people who came before us, and pollution is slowly wearing them away.

The good news is that we can help. Cleaner power from the sun and wind means less acid rain. Mines can cover their dumps with plants and clean their water. Farmers can protect plant cover. And all of us can save electricity and look at rock art with our eyes only.

Weathering will always happen, because it is part of how the earth works. Our job is not to stop nature, but to stop making it worse.

Here is the line to remember: people speed up weathering through pollution, mining, farming and carelessness, and people can slow it down by making smarter choices.
