### subtopic_1 — Segregation Before 1948
**Assistant:** How could the National Party win the 1948 election with fewer votes than the United Party?

**Tutor:** South Africa used a constituency system, similar to Britain's at the time. The country was divided into electoral divisions, or constituencies, and each constituency elected one member of parliament. The party that won the most constituencies, or seats, formed the government, regardless of the total number of votes cast across the whole country. This means a party can win many seats by small margins and lose others by large margins, and so win more seats with fewer votes overall. In addition, the South Africa Act of 1910 allowed rural constituencies to have fewer voters than urban constituencies, up to 15 percent below the average quota, while urban seats could have up to 15 percent more. This was called loading and unloading, and it was originally intended to give rural areas fair representation. In practice, it meant that a vote in a rural area carried more weight than a vote in a city. The National Party's support was strongest among Afrikaans-speaking voters in rural areas and small towns, while the United Party's support was concentrated in the cities, where it often won seats by huge majorities, so many of its votes were in a sense wasted. In 1948, the United Party and the Labour Party together won more votes than the National Party and the Afrikaner Party, but the Nationalist alliance won 79 seats to their 71, giving it a narrow majority. It is also important to remember that the overwhelming majority of South Africans, black South Africans, had no vote at all, and Indian South Africans could not vote either, so the election was not democratic in the modern sense.

### subtopic_2 — The 1948 Election and the Meaning of Apartheid
**Assistant:** Why was the Population Registration Act the foundation of apartheid?

**Tutor:** The Population Registration Act of 1950 was the foundation because almost every other apartheid law depended on it. Laws such as the Group Areas Act, the Prohibition of Mixed Marriages Act, the Immorality Amendment Act, the Separate Amenities Act, Bantu Education and the pass laws all treated people differently according to their race. For these laws to work, the government needed an official way to decide which race every person belonged to. The Population Registration Act provided this by creating a national register in which every person was classified as white, coloured, native, later called Bantu or black, or, after a later amendment, Indian or Asian, with subgroups. Classification was supposed to be based on appearance, general acceptance in the community and descent, but these criteria were vague, and officials often made arbitrary or humiliating decisions. Hearings were held, and some people were examined for features such as hair texture, the colour of their skin or nails, or the language they spoke at home. Each year, some people were reclassified, sometimes after applying to be moved to a group with more rights. The consequences were enormous. A person's classification decided where they could live, which schools and universities they could attend, which jobs were open to them, which hospital would treat them, whom they could marry, which beach they could use and whether they could vote. Families were sometimes torn apart when relatives were classified differently. Without the Population Registration Act, the rest of the apartheid system could not have functioned, which is why its repeal in June 1991 was seen as one of the most important steps in dismantling apartheid.

### subtopic_3 — The Main Apartheid Laws
**Assistant:** What was Bantu Education designed to do?

**Tutor:** The Bantu Education Act of 1953 took control of black education away from the provinces and from the mission schools, which had educated many black leaders, and placed it under the Department of Native Affairs, headed by Hendrik Verwoerd. Verwoerd explained its purpose openly in parliament. He argued that black people should be educated for their place in the apartheid system, and said that there was no place for them in the European community above the level of certain forms of labour, so there was no point in teaching a black child mathematics that they could not use in practice. Bantu Education was therefore designed to prepare black learners for unskilled and semi-skilled work and for life in the reserves, not to give them the same opportunities as white learners. Funding was kept very low: for many years, government spending on black education was tied to the taxes paid by black people themselves, and spending per black learner was a small fraction of spending per white learner. Classes were overcrowded, many teachers were underqualified and many schools lacked basic resources. Mission schools that refused to accept the system lost their subsidies, and many closed. The ANC organised a boycott of Bantu Education in 1955 and set up alternative schools, though these could not last long. However, the number of black children attending school did increase under Bantu Education, because the government needed a basic level of literacy for the workforce, so it would be inaccurate to say that black education simply disappeared. The deep resentment of Bantu Education, especially the later policy of forcing Afrikaans as a medium of instruction, contributed directly to the Soweto uprising of 1976, which you will study in Term 4.

### subtopic_4 — Petty and Grand Apartheid, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What is the difference between petty apartheid and grand apartheid?

**Tutor:** Petty apartheid and grand apartheid are terms used to describe two levels of the apartheid system. Petty apartheid was the segregation of everyday life and public facilities. It was enforced mainly through the Reservation of Separate Amenities Act of 1953 and through local regulations. It meant separate entrances to post offices and banks, separate counters, separate buses, trains and taxis, separate beaches and swimming pools, separate parks and benches, separate toilets, separate ambulances and hospitals, and signs everywhere reading "Whites only" or "Non-whites". The ban on mixed marriages and relationships is also often included. Petty apartheid was deeply humiliating, because it reminded people every day that the law treated them as inferior. Grand apartheid was the large-scale political and territorial separation of the races. Its central idea, developed by Hendrik Verwoerd and his successors, was that South Africa should be divided into a white South Africa and a number of black homelands, or bantustans, based on ethnic groups such as Xhosa, Zulu, Tswana and Venda. Black South Africans would become citizens of these homelands, which would eventually be declared independent, and would lose their South African citizenship. Four homelands, Transkei, Bophuthatswana, Venda and Ciskei, were declared independent between 1976 and 1981, though no other country in the world recognised them. Grand apartheid required massive forced removals of people from areas declared white into the homelands. From the late 1970s and 1980s, the government relaxed some aspects of petty apartheid, opening some facilities to all races, while trying to preserve grand apartheid and white political control. This showed that the core of apartheid was political power and land, not just separate benches.
