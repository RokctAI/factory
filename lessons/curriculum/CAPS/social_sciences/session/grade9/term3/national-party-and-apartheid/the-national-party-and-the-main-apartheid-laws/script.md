# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the coming to power of the National Party in 1948 and the main apartheid laws in broad outline. It covers segregation in South Africa before 1948 and the background to the election; why the National Party won the 1948 election and what it meant by apartheid; the main apartheid laws passed in the late 1940s and 1950s, grouped by what they controlled; and the difference between petty apartheid and grand apartheid, together with the effects of these laws on people's lives. The anchor idea is that apartheid was a system of laws designed to separate South Africans by race and to keep political and economic power in the hands of the white minority. The governing idea is that apartheid did not begin from nothing: it built on decades of segregation, but it made racial separation far more systematic, complete and harsh.

## Subtopic: Segregation Before 1948

Racial discrimination in South Africa did not begin in 1948. From the arrival of the Dutch East India Company at the Cape in 1652, through slavery at the Cape, the wars of conquest of the nineteenth century and British colonial rule, the land and labour of the indigenous peoples were increasingly controlled by white settlers.

When the Union of South Africa was formed in 1910, uniting the four colonies of the Cape, Natal, the Transvaal and the Orange River Colony, the new state gave political power almost entirely to white South Africans. Only in the Cape could some black and coloured men vote, if they met property and education qualifications. In response, African leaders formed the South African Native National Congress in 1912, which was renamed the African National Congress, or ANC, in 1923.

The governments of the Union passed many segregation laws. The Natives Land Act of 1913 restricted black land ownership to reserves covering about 7 percent of the country, increased to about 13 percent by the Native Trust and Land Act of 1936. The Natives (Urban Areas) Act of 1923 controlled where black people could live in towns, treating them as temporary residents. Pass laws, which had existed in various forms since the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, restricted the movement of black men. In 1936, black voters in the Cape were removed from the common voters' roll. Job reservation kept many skilled jobs for white workers, and the Mines and Works Act of 1911 had already reserved certain skilled mining jobs for whites.

So by 1948, South Africa was already a deeply segregated society. What changed with apartheid was the scale, the thoroughness and the ideology. Apartheid aimed to separate the races in every area of life, and to do it through a complete system of laws.

So the next question is: why did the National Party win in 1948?

## Subtopic: The 1948 Election and the Meaning of Apartheid

During World War II, South Africa's economy grew quickly. Factories needed workers, and large numbers of black South Africans moved to the cities. Informal settlements grew around Johannesburg and other towns. Black workers organised and went on strike, most notably in the African Mine Workers' Strike of 1946, when about 70 000 miners stopped work and were forced back by the police, with at least nine killed.

The United Party government of Jan Smuts appointed the Fagan Commission, which reported in 1948 that the movement of black people to the cities could not be reversed and should be managed. Many white voters, however, were afraid of black urbanisation, of competition for jobs and of the growing demands of black political organisations. The National Party, led by Dr Daniel François Malan, played on these fears. It warned of the swart gevaar, meaning black danger, and promised a policy called apartheid, an Afrikaans word meaning separateness. Its own Sauer Commission recommended strict separation, with black people treated as temporary visitors in white areas.

The National Party also drew on Afrikaner nationalism. Many Afrikaners remembered the suffering of the South African War of 1899 to 1902, including the British concentration camps, and resented British influence. Many were poor and had moved to the cities, where they competed with black workers for jobs. Organisations such as the Afrikaner Broederbond, a secret society, promoted Afrikaner language, culture and economic power. The National Party promised to protect Afrikaner interests and white workers.

In the election of 26 May 1948, the National Party and its ally, the Afrikaner Party, won 79 seats, against 71 for the United Party and the Labour Party together. The result was a surprise, because the United Party and its allies had actually won more votes. The National Party won because rural constituencies, where many of its supporters lived, had fewer voters than urban ones, so each rural vote counted for more. Malan became Prime Minister. The National Party would govern South Africa without interruption until 1994.

Apartheid had several aims. Its supporters claimed that each racial group should develop separately in its own areas. In practice, it was designed to secure white political control, to protect white jobs and privilege, to control the movement of black workers so that their labour remained available to white-owned farms, mines and factories, and to prevent black South Africans from uniting politically.

So the next question is: which laws built apartheid?

## Subtopic: The Main Apartheid Laws

The National Party passed a large number of laws in a short time. They can be grouped by what they controlled.

Laws classifying people. The Population Registration Act of 1950 was the foundation of apartheid. It required every South African to be classified as white, coloured, Indian or Asian, or native, later called Bantu or black, and recorded on a national register. A person's classification decided almost every other right they had.

Laws controlling personal relationships. The Prohibition of Mixed Marriages Act of 1949 banned marriages between white people and people of other races. The Immorality Amendment Act of 1950 made sexual relationships between white people and people of other races a crime.

Laws controlling where people lived. The Group Areas Act of 1950 divided towns and cities into separate residential and business areas for each racial group. It led to the forced removal of hundreds of thousands of people, almost all of them black, coloured or Indian, from areas declared white. The next lesson examines it in detail.

Laws controlling movement. The Natives (Abolition of Passes and Co-ordination of Documents) Act of 1952 did not abolish passes, despite its name. It replaced the various passes with a single reference book, which every black man, and later every black woman, over the age of sixteen had to carry at all times. It recorded where the person could live and work. Failure to produce it on demand led to arrest. Hundreds of thousands of people were arrested under the pass laws every year.

Laws controlling public facilities. The Reservation of Separate Amenities Act of 1953 allowed separate public facilities, such as beaches, parks, buses, trains, post offices, toilets and benches, for different races, and stated that they did not have to be equal.

Laws controlling education. The Bantu Education Act of 1953 placed black schooling under the control of the Department of Native Affairs and created a separate, inferior system designed to prepare black learners for low-paid labour. The Minister of Native Affairs, Hendrik Verwoerd, said openly that there was no place for black people in the European community above the level of certain forms of labour. The Extension of University Education Act of 1959 created separate universities and stopped most black students from attending existing universities.

Laws controlling political rights and opposition. The Suppression of Communism Act of 1950 banned the Communist Party of South Africa and defined communism so broadly that it could be used against almost any opponent of apartheid. The Bantu Authorities Act of 1951 set up tribal authorities under government-approved chiefs. The Separate Representation of Voters Act of 1951 aimed to remove coloured voters in the Cape from the common voters' roll, which the government finally achieved in 1956 after a long legal and constitutional battle. The Promotion of Bantu Self-Government Act of 1959 laid the basis for the homelands.

These laws were enforced by a large and growing state machinery. The Department of Native Affairs, later renamed Bantu Administration, controlled almost every aspect of black people's lives, from where they could live to which jobs they could take. The police carried out pass raids, often at night, and courts processed pass offenders in large numbers, sometimes in a minute or two per case. Over time, Parliament also gave the government stronger powers to ban people, organisations and publications, so that opposition to the laws could itself be punished. The government faced legal challenges, especially over the removal of coloured voters, but it overcame them by enlarging the Senate and the Appeal Court bench with its own supporters, showing that it was prepared to change the constitution to get its way.

So the final question is: how did these laws work together, and what did they mean for people?

## Subtopic: Petty and Grand Apartheid, and the Error Museum

Historians often distinguish between two levels of apartheid.

Petty apartheid refers to the segregation of everyday life and public facilities: separate entrances to post offices and shops, separate buses, trains and taxis, separate beaches, parks and benches, separate hospitals and schools, signs reading "Whites only" or "Net blankes", and the ban on mixed marriages and relationships. Petty apartheid was humiliating and was a constant daily reminder of inequality.

Grand apartheid refers to the large-scale separation of land and political rights. Its central idea, developed under Hendrik Verwoerd, who became Prime Minister in 1958, was that black South Africans were not citizens of South Africa at all, but of separate ethnic homelands, or bantustans, which would eventually become independent states. Black people in the cities would then be treated as foreign migrant workers with no political rights in South Africa. Grand apartheid involved the forced removal of millions of people.

The effects of apartheid laws reached every part of life. Families were separated when relatives were classified differently or when men had to work in cities far from their families under the migrant labour system. Communities were destroyed by forced removals. Generations of learners received an inferior education. Black South Africans had no vote and no say in the laws that governed them. People who resisted faced banning, imprisonment, and later exile or death.

At the same time, the laws were never fully accepted. Black, coloured and Indian South Africans, together with some white allies, resisted them from the beginning, through petitions, boycotts, strikes, protests and campaigns of defiance, as the next lessons will show. Within the white population too, opinions differed: the United Party and later the Liberal Party and the Progressive Party opposed aspects of apartheid, and some white South Africans, such as members of the Black Sash, actively campaigned against it, though most white voters continued to support the National Party.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Racial segregation began in South Africa in 1948." Segregation existed long before 1948; apartheid made it more systematic and complete.

Exhibit two: "The National Party won the most votes in 1948." The United Party and its allies won more votes; the National Party won more seats because rural constituencies had fewer voters.

Exhibit three: "The Abolition of Passes Act ended the pass laws." It replaced several passes with a single reference book that black people had to carry at all times.

Exhibit four: "Separate amenities under apartheid were equal." The Separate Amenities Act stated that facilities did not have to be equal, and those for whites were usually far better.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: an old fence, a new wall, and a label on every person.

## Subtopic: An Old Fence

Long before 1948, South Africa was already divided. Think of it as an old fence running through the country.

When South Africa became one country in 1910, white people held almost all the power. In 1913, a law said black people could own land in only a small part of the country. Other laws controlled where black people could live in towns and forced black men to carry passes.

During World War II, many black people moved to the cities to work in factories. Many white voters were afraid. They worried about losing their jobs and their power.

In 1948, the National Party, led by D.F. Malan, promised to protect white people with a policy called apartheid, which means separateness. In an election where almost only white people could vote, it won. Strangely, it got fewer votes than the other side, but it won more seats.

Many Afrikaners also supported the party because they had been poor and felt looked down on by English-speaking South Africans, and the party promised to look after them. So fear, pride and self-interest all played a part.

So what did the National Party do with its power?

## Subtopic: A New Wall

The National Party tore down the old fence and built a high wall instead. Law after law, it separated people in every part of life.

One law banned people of different races from marrying. Another divided every town into separate areas for each race, and people were forced out of their homes. Black people had to carry a pass book everywhere, and could be arrested if they did not have it. Beaches, buses, parks, toilets and even benches were marked "Whites only". A law about schools gave black children a separate, poorer education, meant to prepare them only for low-paid work.

These everyday rules are called petty apartheid. The bigger plan, called grand apartheid, was to send black South Africans to separate homelands and treat them as foreigners in their own country.

So how did the government know which side of the wall each person belonged on?

## Subtopic: A Label on Every Person

The Population Registration Act of 1950 put a label on every person: white, coloured, Indian or black. That label decided where you could live, which school you went to, which job you could do, whom you could marry and whether you could vote.

The label was written in the population register and on identity documents. Sometimes officials used cruel tests, like pushing a pencil into a person's hair to see if it fell out. Some families were split apart when brothers and sisters got different labels.

But people never simply accepted the wall. From the start, they protested, boycotted, marched and defied the laws, as you will learn next.

Here is the line to remember: in 1948 the National Party took power and built apartheid, a system of laws that labelled every person by race and controlled where they could live, work, learn, travel and love.
