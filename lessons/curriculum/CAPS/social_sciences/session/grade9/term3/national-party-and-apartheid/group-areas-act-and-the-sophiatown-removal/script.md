# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a case study of the Group Areas Act of 1950 and the forced removal of Sophiatown. It covers what the Group Areas Act and related laws did and why the apartheid government introduced them; the history and life of Sophiatown before the removals, including its freehold rights and its famous culture; the removal of Sophiatown from 1955, the campaign of resistance against it and the move to Meadowlands; and the wider impact of forced removals in South Africa, including District Six, and how they are remembered today. The anchor idea is that the Group Areas Act divided every town and city into separate areas for each racial group, and that the best land, nearest to city centres, was usually declared white. The governing idea is that forced removals destroyed not only houses but whole communities, and their effects on people's lives and on the shape of South African cities are still felt today.

## Subtopic: The Group Areas Act and Its Purposes

The Group Areas Act was passed in 1950. It gave the government the power to declare any area of a town or city a group area for one racial group only, based on the classifications made under the Population Registration Act of the same year. Once an area was proclaimed for one group, members of other groups were not allowed to own property or live there, and could be forced to leave. The Act also controlled who could own or run businesses in each area. It was amended many times and reissued in 1957 and 1966, each time with stronger powers.

Residential segregation already existed before 1950. The Natives (Urban Areas) Act of 1923 had allowed towns to set up separate locations for black residents. However, in 1950 many towns and cities still had mixed areas, where black, coloured, Indian and sometimes white people lived side by side, often in older, inner-city neighbourhoods. The Group Areas Act aimed to eliminate these mixed areas completely.

The government gave several reasons for the Act. It claimed that separation would reduce friction between racial groups and that each group would be able to develop its own community. In reality, the Act served several other purposes. It protected white property values and gave white people the best residential land, usually near city centres, with the best services. It removed black, coloured and Indian communities from areas that white people wanted. It weakened Indian traders, who were often successful business owners in central areas, by moving them out. It made it easier to control the black population, by placing townships at a distance from white suburbs, separated by buffer zones such as open land, railway lines or industrial areas, with only a few roads in and out that could be easily controlled by the police and army. And it broke up mixed communities where people of different races lived, worked and organised politically together.

Together with other laws, such as the Natives Resettlement Act of 1954 and the Prevention of Illegal Squatting Act of 1951, the Group Areas Act was used to carry out forced removals across the country. The people moved were overwhelmingly black, coloured and Indian. Very few white people were ever moved.

So the next question is: what was Sophiatown, and why did the government want it removed?

## Subtopic: Sophiatown Before the Removals

Sophiatown lay about six kilometres west of the centre of Johannesburg. In 1903, a speculator named Herman Tobiansky bought the land and named the new township after his wife, Sophia. He intended it for white residents, but the town council built a sewage disposal site nearby, and white buyers lost interest. Tobiansky began selling plots to anyone who could pay, including black, coloured, Indian and Chinese buyers.

This was important, because Sophiatown became one of the very few places in urban South Africa where black people could own land under freehold title, meaning they owned their property permanently and could pass it on to their children. After the Natives Land Act of 1913, and with the Urban Areas Act of 1923, this was almost impossible elsewhere. Freehold rights gave residents a sense of security and independence. Many families rented out rooms or backyard shacks to tenants to earn an income, so the area became very crowded. By the late 1940s, tens of thousands of people lived in Sophiatown and the neighbouring areas of Martindale and Newclare, known together as the Western Areas.

Sophiatown was poor in many ways. Many houses were overcrowded, services were limited, and crime was a real problem, with gangs such as the Americans and the Berliners operating in the area. But it was also a vibrant, cosmopolitan community, often nicknamed Kofifi. People of many languages, cultures and backgrounds lived side by side. It was a centre of black urban culture in the 1940s and 1950s. Jazz and marabi music filled its shebeens and dance halls, where musicians such as the singer Dolly Rathebe performed, and where a young Hugh Masekela was given his first trumpet by Father Trevor Huddleston. Writers and journalists for Drum magazine, such as Can Themba, Bloke Modisane and Henry Nxumalo, lived there and wrote about its life. Father Trevor Huddleston, an Anglican priest at the Church of Christ the King in Sophiatown, became a well-known campaigner against apartheid.

For the apartheid government, Sophiatown represented everything it opposed: black people owning land in a city, people of different races mixing freely, and a strong, independent black urban culture that was a centre of political activity. White suburbs, such as Westdene, had grown up around it, and some white residents demanded that the "black spot" be removed.

Sophiatown also mattered politically. It was home to members of the ANC, the Communist Party and other organisations, and its meetings and rallies drew large crowds. During the Defiance Campaign of 1952, which you will study in the next lessons, many volunteers came from the Western Areas. A community in which black people owned property, organised freely and mixed with people of other races showed that a different South Africa was possible, which is exactly why the government wanted to destroy it.

So the next question is: how was Sophiatown removed, and how did people resist?

## Subtopic: The Removal and the Resistance

In 1953, the government announced the Western Areas Removal Scheme, under which the residents of Sophiatown and the neighbouring areas would be moved to a new township called Meadowlands, part of what later became Soweto, more than ten kilometres to the south-west. The Natives Resettlement Act of 1954 created a special board to carry out the removal. Owners were offered compensation, which was usually far below the real value of their property, and they lost their freehold rights; at Meadowlands, they could only rent houses from the government.

The residents, supported by the ANC, the Transvaal Indian Congress and other organisations, campaigned against the removal. Meetings were held, often in Freedom Square in Sophiatown, and speakers such as Nelson Mandela, then a young lawyer and ANC leader, and Father Huddleston addressed large crowds. The slogan of the campaign was "We won't move", and in the township's mixed street language, "Ons dak nie, ons phola hier", meaning "We won't go, we're staying here". The ANC planned a campaign of resistance on the day of the first removal.

The government moved first. Before dawn on 9 February 1955, three days earlier than announced, about two thousand armed police arrived in Sophiatown in heavy rain. They loaded the first families and their belongings onto government trucks and drove them to Meadowlands. Faced with this show of force, and with no clear plan for an alternative, the organisers called off the planned resistance, and the removal went ahead without violence. Some residents did not resist the move, because Meadowlands offered brick houses away from overcrowded rooms and the gangs of Sophiatown, especially for tenants who had no property to lose. Others felt devastated. The removals continued in stages for several years, until the last residents had left in the early 1960s. In total, tens of thousands of people were moved. Coloured, Indian and Chinese residents were moved to separate areas set aside for their own groups, so families and neighbours were split up.

As people left, their houses were bulldozed. The singer Strike Vilakazi recorded a song called "Meadowlands", which became famous; the government interpreted it as support for the move, while residents sang it with bitter irony. On the ruins, the government built a new suburb for working-class white residents and named it Triomf, the Afrikaans word for triumph.

Former residents often described what was lost in terms of people rather than buildings: neighbours who had known one another for decades, a church where families had been baptised and married, a school, a corner shop, a street where children played together regardless of colour. Meadowlands had new houses, but it was laid out in uniform rows, and residents were sorted by ethnic group into different sections, which apartheid planners preferred. Many residents said that the spirit of Sophiatown could not be moved with them.

So the final question is: what did forced removals mean for South Africa?

## Subtopic: Forced Removals and Their Legacy, and the Error Museum

Sophiatown was one of the first, but far from the last, forced removals. In Cape Town, District Six, a vibrant, mainly coloured inner-city community near the harbour, was declared a white group area on 11 February 1966. Over the following fifteen years or so, about 60 000 people were removed to the Cape Flats, an area of sandy, windswept land far from the city, and their homes were demolished. In Durban, tens of thousands of people were removed from Cato Manor. In Gqeberha, then called Port Elizabeth, the community of South End was destroyed. Similar removals took place in almost every town, from Pretoria's Lady Selborne to Kimberley and East London. The Surplus People Project, a research group, estimated that about 3.5 million people were forcibly removed in South Africa between 1960 and 1983 under all the various laws, including removals from farms and so-called black spots into the homelands.

The effects of forced removals were severe. People lost homes, property and, in the case of freehold owners, the security of owning land. Communities, friendships, churches, schools and businesses built up over generations were destroyed. People were moved far from their jobs and from city centres, so they had to spend long hours and much of their income travelling to work. New townships often lacked basic facilities such as shops, clinics and sports fields. Many people experienced deep trauma and a sense of loss that lasted for the rest of their lives.

The Group Areas Act was repealed in June 1991, along with the Population Registration Act and the Land Acts. After 1994, the democratic government introduced land restitution, under the Restitution of Land Rights Act of 1994, which allowed people who had lost land through racially discriminatory laws after 1913 to claim it back or receive compensation. In 2006, Triomf was officially renamed Sophiatown, and some former residents have returned to District Six. However, the apartheid shape of South African cities, with mainly black townships far from city centres, still largely remains and affects people's daily lives.

Sophiatown and District Six are remembered through museums, such as the District Six Museum in Cape Town and the Trevor Huddleston Memorial Centre in Sophiatown, and through books, plays, music and the oral histories of former residents.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "The Group Areas Act affected all races equally." The people removed were overwhelmingly black, coloured and Indian, and the best land was usually given to whites.

Exhibit two: "Residents of Sophiatown were paid the full value of their properties." Compensation was usually far below the real value, and owners lost their freehold rights.

Exhibit three: "Forced removals happened only in Sophiatown." About 3.5 million people were removed across South Africa between 1960 and 1983.

Exhibit four: "Repealing the Act in 1991 restored the old communities." The communities had been destroyed; restitution has been slow and apartheid city patterns largely remain.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a coloured-in map, a street full of music, and trucks in the rain.

## Subtopic: A Coloured-In Map

Imagine the government taking a map of every town in South Africa and colouring it in, one colour for each race. White areas here, coloured areas there, Indian areas somewhere else, black townships far away on the edges.

That is what the Group Areas Act of 1950 did. If your area was coloured in for another race, you had to leave, even if your family had lived there for generations.

The government said this would keep the peace. In reality, white people usually got the best land, close to town, and everyone else was pushed out to the edges, separated by empty land or railway lines. Townships with only a few roads in and out were easy for the police to control.

Millions of people were forced out of their homes across the country. Very few of them were white.

So which place became the most famous example?

## Subtopic: A Street Full of Music

Sophiatown was a crowded suburb just west of Johannesburg's city centre. It was special because black people could own their homes there, which was almost impossible anywhere else in a city.

People of all backgrounds lived side by side. It was poor and crowded, and it had gangs, but it was also full of life. Jazz played in the clubs and shebeens. Famous musicians and writers lived there. People called it Kofifi.

The government did not like it. Black people owning land in a city, different races mixing freely, and a strong, proud community that took part in politics, all went against apartheid. White people in nearby suburbs wanted it gone.

So the government decided to move everyone out.

## Subtopic: Trucks in the Rain

The people of Sophiatown said, "We won't move!" They held big meetings, and leaders like Nelson Mandela spoke to the crowds.

But before sunrise on 9 February 1955, in pouring rain, about two thousand armed police arrived. Families were loaded onto trucks with their furniture and driven to Meadowlands, many kilometres away. Over the next few years, every house in Sophiatown was knocked down.

On the ruins, the government built a suburb for white people and called it Triomf, which means triumph.

The same thing happened to District Six in Cape Town and many other places. After apartheid ended, some land was returned, and in 2006 Triomf got back the name Sophiatown. But many cities are still divided the way apartheid planned them.

Here is the line to remember: the Group Areas Act divided every town by race, and the destruction of Sophiatown showed how forced removals tore apart whole communities.
