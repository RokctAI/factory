# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains levees and deltas, two important landforms produced by deposition in a river's lower course. It covers the lower course and the flood plain; how natural levees form during floods and how people build artificial levees; how deltas form where rivers enter the sea or a lake and the different types of deltas; and the importance of levees and deltas for people, including their benefits and hazards. The anchor idea is that a river deposits its load when it loses energy, and that it loses energy suddenly in two places: when it spills over its banks in a flood, and when it flows into the still water of a sea or lake. The governing idea is that deposition builds new, fertile land that has supported great civilisations and millions of farmers, but that this land is low-lying and flat, so it is always at risk from floods and rising seas.

## Subtopic: The Lower Course and the Flood Plain

In its lower course, a river is usually at its widest and deepest, and it carries its largest volume of water, collected from all its tributaries. The land is very flat, and the river is close to sea level, which geographers call base level, the lowest level to which a river can erode. Because the gradient is so gentle, the river has little energy left for erosion, except during floods, and it carries mainly fine material, such as silt and clay, in suspension. Deposition is now the dominant process.

The valley in the lower course is very wide and flat. On both sides of the river lies the flood plain, a broad area of flat land that is covered by water when the river floods. Each time the river floods, it spreads a thin layer of silt over the flood plain. Over thousands of years, these layers build up into deep, fertile soils, called alluvium. Alluvial soils are rich in nutrients and easy to plough, which is why river flood plains have attracted farmers since the beginning of agriculture.

The great early civilisations of the world grew up on river flood plains: ancient Egypt on the Nile, Mesopotamia between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, the Indus Valley civilisation in what is now Pakistan and India, and ancient China on the Yellow River. Each year, floods watered the fields and renewed the soil with fresh silt, making it possible to grow enough food to support cities.

Rivers in their lower course also continue to meander, as you learnt in the previous lesson, so the flood plain is marked with meanders, oxbow lakes and meander scars. Two further landforms, levees and deltas, are produced by deposition here.

In South Africa, many rivers flow down from the Great Escarpment over a relatively short distance to the coast, especially in the east, so they have steep gradients and short lower courses. Large flood plains are therefore less common than in many other parts of the world, but there are important examples, such as the Pongola flood plain in KwaZulu-Natal and the lower Orange River near its mouth.

So the next question is: how do levees form?

## Subtopic: Natural and Artificial Levees

A levee is a raised bank along the side of a river channel. Natural levees are formed by deposition during floods.

When a river floods, water spills over its banks and spreads out across the flood plain. As soon as the water leaves the deep, fast-flowing channel and spreads out over the flat land, it becomes shallow, and friction with the ground and vegetation slows it down sharply. It suddenly loses energy and drops much of its load. The heaviest, coarsest material, such as sand and coarse silt, is dropped first, right next to the channel. Finer silt and clay are carried further across the flood plain and settle out more slowly, where the water is still. After many floods, the coarse material deposited next to the river builds up into long, low ridges along both banks. These are natural levees. They are higher than the flood plain behind them, and they slope gently away from the river.

Between floods, the river also deposits material on its own bed, so the bed slowly rises. In some rivers, the bed and the levees together build up so much that the river ends up flowing above the level of the surrounding flood plain. The Yellow River in China, which carries a huge load of fine, yellow sediment, is a famous example: in parts of its lower course, its bed is several metres above the land around it. This can be very dangerous, because if a levee breaks, water pours out onto the lower land and cannot easily drain back.

People build artificial levees, also called embankments or dykes, to protect land and settlements from flooding. They usually make existing natural levees higher and stronger, using earth, sand, rock or concrete. The Mississippi River in the United States has thousands of kilometres of artificial levees. However, artificial levees can create problems. They confine floodwater to the channel, so the water flows faster and higher, which can increase flooding downstream, and they stop fresh silt from reaching the flood plain. If they fail, the results can be catastrophic. In 2005, when Hurricane Katrina struck the city of New Orleans, which lies largely below sea level on the Mississippi delta, levees and flood walls failed in many places, about 80 percent of the city was flooded, and more than 1 800 people died in the disaster as a whole.

So the next question is: how do deltas form?

## Subtopic: How Deltas Form and Their Shapes

A delta is an area of new land formed by deposition at the mouth of a river, where it enters a sea, an ocean or a lake. The name comes from the Greek letter delta, which is shaped like a triangle, because the Greek historian Herodotus noticed that the land at the mouth of the Nile had a triangular shape.

Deltas form when a river carrying a large load of sediment flows into a body of still or slow-moving water. When the river water enters the sea or lake, it slows down suddenly and loses energy, so it deposits its load. Coarser material is dropped first, near the river mouth, and finer material is carried further out. In sea water, another process helps: salt causes the tiny clay particles to clump together, a process called flocculation, so they become heavier and sink more quickly. Over time, layers of sediment build up on the sea or lake floor until they reach the surface, forming new land. The river then has to find its way across these deposits. It often splits into several smaller channels, called distributaries, which spread out across the delta, each depositing sediment and building the delta further out.

For a delta to form, several conditions are needed. The river must carry a large load of sediment. The sea or lake must be relatively calm, with weak tides, waves and currents, so that the sediment is not washed away faster than it is deposited. A shallow continental shelf helps, because less material is needed to fill the water up to the surface.

Deltas have different shapes, depending on the balance between river deposition and the power of waves and tides. An arcuate delta, shaped like a fan or an arc, with many distributaries, forms where there is a moderate balance; the Nile Delta, with about 240 kilometres of coastline, is the classic example. A bird's-foot delta, with long, finger-like distributaries extending out into the sea, forms where the river carries a large load and waves are weak; the Mississippi Delta is the best-known example. A cuspate delta, shaped like a pointed tooth, forms where strong waves push sediment back on both sides of a single channel; the delta of the Tiber River in Italy is an example.

Not all deltas are at the coast. The Okavango Delta in northern Botswana is an inland delta. The Okavango River flows from the highlands of Angola but never reaches the sea. Instead, it spreads out across the flat Kalahari basin into a vast fan-shaped network of channels, lagoons, islands and swamps, where almost all its water evaporates or is used by plants. It is one of the largest inland deltas in the world, and it became a World Heritage Site in 2014.

So the final question is: why are levees and deltas so important to people?

## Subtopic: Living on Deltas, and the Error Museum

Deltas are among the most densely populated places on earth. Their soils are deep and fertile, the land is flat and easy to farm, water is plentiful for irrigation, and the location at the meeting of a river and the sea is ideal for fishing, ports and trade. The Nile Delta has been farmed for thousands of years and is still home to tens of millions of Egyptians. The Ganges-Brahmaputra Delta, shared by Bangladesh and India, is the largest delta in the world, and well over 100 million people live on or near it, growing rice and jute. Deltas also contain valuable wetlands, mangrove forests and fisheries; the Sundarbans mangrove forest on the Ganges-Brahmaputra Delta is home to the Bengal tiger.

However, deltas face serious hazards. They are low-lying and flat, so they are very vulnerable to river floods, storm surges and tropical cyclones; cyclones in Bangladesh have killed hundreds of thousands of people in the past, though improved warnings and shelters have greatly reduced deaths in recent decades. Many deltas are also slowly sinking, because the soft sediments compact under their own weight, and because people pump out groundwater and oil. Dams upstream trap sediment, so less new material reaches the delta to replace what is lost; since the Aswan High Dam was completed in 1970, the Nile Delta has received very little new sediment, and parts of its coastline are eroding. Rising sea levels caused by climate change make all these problems worse, threatening farmland with flooding and salt water.

South Africa has few large deltas, because most of its rivers enter the sea along a coast with strong waves, powerful currents and a narrow continental shelf, which carry sediment away. Instead, many South African rivers end in estuaries, where fresh water and sea water mix, or in lagoons separated from the sea by sandbars. Much of the sediment of the Tugela River, for example, is spread by currents across the seabed offshore. Nevertheless, the processes are the same, and South Africans benefit from the nearby Okavango Delta as a major tourist destination in southern Africa.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Levees are built only by people." Natural levees are built by rivers depositing coarse material next to the channel during floods; people build artificial ones.

Exhibit two: "Deltas form where the sea is very rough." Deltas form where waves, tides and currents are weak enough for sediment to build up.

Exhibit three: "All deltas are at the coast." Inland deltas, such as the Okavango Delta, form where rivers spread out over flat land.

Exhibit four: "Building dams makes deltas grow faster." Dams trap sediment, so deltas downstream receive less material and may shrink.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a spilt cup of muddy water, a fan of mud at the sea, and a city behind a wall.

## Subtopic: Spilt Muddy Water

Near the sea, a river is wide, slow and full of mud. The land around it is very flat. When heavy rain falls upstream, the river gets too full and spills over its banks, flooding the flat land beside it. That flat land is called the flood plain.

Imagine spilling a cup of muddy water on a flat table. Right where the water spills out, it slows down and drops the heaviest grit. Further away, only a thin film of fine mud is left.

A flooding river does the same. As soon as the water leaves the deep channel, it slows down and drops its heavier sand right beside the river. Flood after flood, these sand piles grow into long, raised banks along both sides of the river. These banks are called levees.

The fine mud spreads further over the flood plain, making the soil rich and good for farming. That is why people have farmed flood plains for thousands of years.

So what happens when the river reaches the sea?

## Subtopic: A Fan of Mud at the Sea

When a muddy river flows into a calm sea or lake, it suddenly slows right down. It cannot carry its mud any longer, so it drops it. Year after year, the mud piles up on the sea floor until it rises above the water and becomes new land. This new land is called a delta.

The river has to push through its own mud, so it splits into lots of smaller channels, spreading out like the fingers of a hand or the folds of a fan.

Deltas only grow where the sea is fairly calm. If big waves and strong currents keep washing the mud away, no delta can form. That is why South Africa, with its rough seas, has very few deltas.

One of Africa's most famous deltas is not even at the sea. The Okavango River in Botswana spreads out into the Kalahari and forms a huge green maze of channels, islands and swamps, full of elephants, hippos and birds.

Some deltas grow surprisingly fast. Parts of the Mississippi Delta pushed out into the sea by many metres a year when the river carried its full load of mud.

So why do people live on such flat, wet land?

## Subtopic: A City Behind a Wall

Deltas and flood plains have some of the richest soil on earth, flat land and plenty of water. So millions of people live there. Egypt's farms have fed people from the Nile Delta for thousands of years, and in Bangladesh, well over 100 million people live on a giant delta.

But living there is risky. The land is low and flat, so floods and storms from the sea can cover it quickly. People build high walls of earth, called artificial levees, to keep the water out. When they work, they protect homes. When they break, the results can be terrible, as in New Orleans in the United States in 2005, when much of the city flooded.

Dams upstream also trap mud, so some deltas are now shrinking instead of growing, and rising seas make the danger greater.

Here is the line to remember: when a river slows down in a flood it builds levees along its banks, and when it slows down at the sea or a lake it builds a delta of rich new land.
