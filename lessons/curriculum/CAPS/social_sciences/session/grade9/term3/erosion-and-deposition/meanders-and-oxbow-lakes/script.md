# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains meanders and oxbow lakes, landforms produced by both erosion and deposition in a river's middle and lower courses. It covers how rivers change from the upper to the middle and lower courses; how meanders form, with erosion on the outside of bends and deposition on the inside; how meanders migrate and how oxbow lakes form; and the importance of meanders and oxbow lakes for ecosystems and people, with South African examples. The anchor idea is that water flows fastest around the outside of a bend and slowest on the inside, so a river erodes its outer bank and deposits on its inner bank at the same time. The governing idea is that meanders are a balance between erosion and deposition, which constantly reshape the river's course across its flood plain.

## Subtopic: From the Upper Course to the Lowlands

As a river leaves the mountains and enters its middle and lower courses, its character changes. The gradient becomes gentler, but the river is joined by tributaries, so it carries more water. Its channel becomes wider and deeper, and its bed smoother, so there is less friction. As a result, the river often flows quite efficiently, even though the slope is gentle. It carries a large load of finer material, such as sand, silt and clay, much of it in suspension.

In the upper course, the river mostly erodes downwards. In the middle and lower courses, it erodes sideways into its banks more than downwards. This sideways erosion is called lateral erosion. It widens the valley, which becomes broad and flat-floored rather than V-shaped. At the same time, the river deposits material wherever it slows down. The flat land on either side of the river, covered by layers of silt deposited during floods, is called the flood plain.

Rivers rarely flow in perfectly straight lines for long. Even in a straight channel, small irregularities, such as a fallen tree or a bank of sand, deflect the flow towards one bank. The water erodes that bank a little, creating a small curve, and deposits material on the opposite side, where the water slows down. Once a bend starts, it tends to grow, because the water swings into the outside of the bend and is deflected towards the opposite bank further downstream, starting another bend. Over time, the river develops a series of large, regular loops. These loops are called meanders.

The word meander comes from the Büyük Menderes River in Turkey, known in ancient Greek times as the Maeander, which is famous for its winding course. Today the word is also used in everyday language to mean wandering slowly and aimlessly.

Meanders can be found on many South African rivers where they cross flat land, such as the lower Pongola River in northern KwaZulu-Natal, the Nyl River flood plain in Limpopo, the Breede River in the Western Cape and the Vaal and Orange rivers in places where they cross wide valleys.

Geographers sometimes describe the shape of a meandering river using its sinuosity, which compares the length of the river channel with the straight-line distance down the valley. A river that is 15 kilometres long along its channel but covers only 10 kilometres down the valley has a sinuosity of 1,5. The higher the number, the more the river winds. Rivers with a sinuosity above about 1,5 are usually described as meandering.

So the next question is: how exactly does a meander form and grow?

## Subtopic: Erosion and Deposition on a Bend

The key to understanding meanders is the way water flows around a bend. Think of what happens when a car goes around a corner, or when you run around the curve of an athletics track: the outside lane is longer than the inside lane, so to keep up, you must move faster on the outside. Similarly, as a river flows around a bend, the fastest-flowing water, called the thalweg, swings towards the outside of the bend. The water there is deeper and has more energy. On the inside of the bend, the water is shallower and flows more slowly, with less energy.

On the outside of the bend, the fast, deep water erodes the bank by hydraulic action and abrasion. The bank is undercut and collapses, forming a steep bank called a river cliff. On the inside of the bend, the slow, shallow water cannot carry all its load, so it deposits sand, gravel and silt. This builds up a gentle, sloping bank called a slip-off slope. The deposit on the inside of the bend is also called a point bar.

So if you draw a cross-section across a meander, from the inside to the outside of the bend, it is not symmetrical. It is shallow with a gentle slope on the inside, and deep with a steep cliff on the outside. This is an important diagram to be able to draw.

The water in a meander does not only flow downstream. It also moves in a corkscrew-like spiral, called helicoidal flow. Surface water flows towards the outer bank, dives down and erodes it, then flows back across the river bed towards the inner bank, carrying eroded material, which is deposited on the slip-off slope. This spiral flow helps explain why erosion and deposition happen on opposite sides at the same time.

Because erosion continues on the outer bank and deposition on the inner bank, the meander slowly becomes larger and more curved. Meanders also tend to move gradually downstream across the flood plain over time, because erosion is strongest on the downstream side of each outer bend. This movement is called meander migration, and it means that the river's course is never fixed. A farmer's field next to a river may slowly lose land on one side, while new land is built up on the other.

Meander migration can be measured. By comparing old aerial photographs and maps with recent ones, geographers can see how far a river bend has moved over several decades. On large, fast-changing rivers, outer banks can retreat by several metres in a single flood season, which shows how active these processes can be.

So the next question is: what happens when a meander becomes very curved?

## Subtopic: How Oxbow Lakes Form

As erosion continues on the outer banks of two neighbouring bends, the meander becomes more and more looped, until it almost forms a circle. The narrow strip of land between the two outer banks, at the start and end of the loop, is called the neck of the meander. As the outer banks on either side of the neck are eroded towards each other, the neck becomes narrower and narrower.

Eventually, usually during a flood when the river has extra energy and a large volume of water, the river breaks through the narrow neck. It now takes the shortest, straightest path, because water flows downhill along the steepest route. The new straight channel is called a cut-off.

The old loop of the meander is now no longer part of the main flow. The water in it is still and moves very slowly, so the river deposits sediment at the ends of the loop where it joins the new channel. Over time, these deposits seal off the old loop completely. The result is a curved, horseshoe-shaped lake lying beside the river, called an oxbow lake. The name comes from the U-shaped wooden collar placed around the neck of an ox pulling a plough or wagon, which the lake resembles.

Oxbow lakes do not last forever. Because no river flows through them, they slowly fill with sediment washed in during floods, with dead plant material and with soil blown or washed from the land around them. Water also evaporates, especially in hot, dry climates. Gradually they become marshes or swamps, and finally they may dry out completely, leaving a curved hollow in the flood plain called a meander scar. On aerial photographs and topographic maps, these scars and old lakes show where the river used to flow.

In summary, the formation of an oxbow lake follows a sequence: lateral erosion on the outer banks of a meander; narrowing of the neck; the river breaks through the neck during a flood; deposition seals off the old loop; an oxbow lake remains, which later fills in to become a marsh and then a meander scar.

So the final question is: why are meanders and oxbow lakes important?

## Subtopic: Why They Matter and the Error Museum

Meanders and oxbow lakes are important for ecosystems and people. Oxbow lakes and the marshes that form from them are valuable wetlands. They provide habitats for fish, frogs, water birds, crocodiles and hippos, and for plants such as reeds and water lilies. During floods, they store water and release it slowly, reducing flood peaks downstream, and they trap sediment and filter pollutants.

In South Africa, one of the best examples is the Pongola River flood plain in northern KwaZulu-Natal, near the border with Mozambique. Here the river meanders across a broad, flat plain and is lined with many pans, shallow lakes, several of them old meander loops and oxbow lakes. The Ndumo Game Reserve protects part of this flood plain, which is famous for its bird life, with over 400 species recorded. Local communities have long depended on the flood plain for fishing, grazing and growing crops on the fertile soils as the floodwaters recede. Since the Pongolapoort Dam was completed in the 1970s, managed releases of water from the dam have been used to try to imitate natural floods, to support both the ecosystem and the people who depend on it.

Meanders also create challenges. Because rivers migrate across their flood plains, they can erode farmland, roads, bridges and buildings on outer bends. Flood plains are flat and fertile, which attracts farming and settlement, but they flood regularly. In some countries, engineers have straightened meandering rivers to improve navigation or to drain water away quickly. This is called channelisation. However, straightening often increases the speed of the water, causing flooding and erosion further downstream and destroying wetlands, so many countries are now restoring meanders.

For mapwork, you should be able to recognise meanders and oxbow lakes on a 1:50 000 topographic map or an orthophoto: look for a river forming large, regular loops across an area with few contour lines, which shows flat land, and for curved lakes or marshes beside the river.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Water flows fastest on the inside of a bend." It flows fastest on the outside of a bend, where erosion forms a river cliff.

Exhibit two: "The slip-off slope is formed by erosion." It is formed by deposition on the inside of the bend, where the water is slow.

Exhibit three: "Oxbow lakes form in the upper course." They form in the middle and lower courses, where rivers meander across flat flood plains.

Exhibit four: "An oxbow lake lasts forever." Without a river flowing through it, it slowly fills with sediment and plants and becomes a marsh and then a meander scar.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a running track, a snake swallowing its tail, and a lake left behind.

## Subtopic: The Running Track

When a river leaves the mountains, the land becomes flat. The river slows down a bit, gets wider, and starts to swing from side to side in big loops. These loops are called meanders.

Think of a running track. On a bend, the runner in the outside lane has further to go, so they must run faster. A river bend works the same way. The water on the outside of the bend moves fastest and is deepest. The water on the inside moves slowly and is shallow.

The fast water on the outside eats into the bank, making a steep little cliff. The slow water on the inside cannot carry its load, so it drops sand and mud, building a gentle, sandy slope, like a small beach.

So one side of the bend is always being eaten away, while the other side is being built up. That is why the loops keep growing bigger.

Over many years, the whole river slowly wanders across the flat land, a little like a snake sliding sideways. A farmer may lose a strip of field on one bank while gaining new sandy ground on the other.

So what happens when a loop grows too big?

## Subtopic: A Snake Swallowing Its Tail

As the river keeps eating into the outside of its bends, the loops get bigger and curvier. Soon a loop curls almost all the way round, like a snake trying to swallow its own tail. Only a narrow strip of land, called the neck, is left between the start and the end of the loop.

The river keeps nibbling at both sides of the neck, so it gets thinner and thinner. Then, one rainy summer, a big flood comes. The extra water has so much power that it bursts right through the neck. Now the river has a short cut, a straight path. Water always likes the easiest way downhill, so it follows the short cut.

The old loop is now left out of the main flow, cut off from the river. Mud and sand pile up at its two ends and block them off.

This might happen in just a few days of flooding, after the river has spent many years slowly eating away at the neck.

So what is left behind?

## Subtopic: A Lake Left Behind

What is left is a curved lake beside the river, shaped like a horseshoe. It is called an oxbow lake, because it looks like the curved wooden collar once used to harness oxen to a plough.

Oxbow lakes become peaceful, still-water homes for fish, frogs, birds, hippos and reeds. In northern KwaZulu-Natal, the Pongola River winds across a flat plain with many pans and lakes, some of them old river loops, where hundreds of kinds of birds live.

Wetlands like these are like giant sponges. They soak up floodwater and let it out slowly, and they clean the water as it passes through the reeds.

But an oxbow lake slowly fills with mud and plants. Bit by bit, it turns into a marsh, and one day it may dry up, leaving only a curved dip in the ground to show where the river once flowed.

If you ever fly over flat land, look down at the rivers. You may spot meanders, oxbow lakes and old curved scars, all telling the story of a river on the move.

Here is the line to remember: a river erodes the outside of its bends and deposits on the inside, so meanders grow until a flood cuts through the neck and leaves an oxbow lake behind.
