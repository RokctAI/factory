Hey — this topic fits in three pictures: a running track, a snake swallowing its tail and a lake left behind.

The track. In the middle and lower course, rivers erode sideways and swing into loops called meanders. Water flows fastest on the outside of a bend, eroding a steep river cliff. On the inside, slow water deposits sand and silt, building a gentle slip-off slope or point bar. Helicoidal flow spirals across the channel. Meanders grow and migrate downstream.

The snake. Erosion on two outer bends narrows the neck of the meander. During a flood, the river breaks through the neck and takes the short cut.

The lake. Deposition seals the ends of the old loop, leaving a horseshoe-shaped oxbow lake. It slowly fills with sediment and plants, becomes a marsh, then a meander scar.

Oxbow lakes are valuable wetlands, like the pans of the Pongola flood plain and Ndumo Game Reserve. Straightening rivers can make floods worse downstream.
