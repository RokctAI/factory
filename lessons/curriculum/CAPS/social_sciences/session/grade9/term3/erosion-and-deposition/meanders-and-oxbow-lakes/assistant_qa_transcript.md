### subtopic_1 — From the Upper Course to the Lowlands
**Assistant:** How do I draw a cross-section across a meander?

**Tutor:** A cross-section across a meander shows what the river channel would look like if you cut through it from one bank to the other, and it is one of the most useful diagrams for this topic. Start by drawing a plan view, a bird's-eye view, of a single meander bend, with arrows showing the direction of flow. Mark a straight line across the bend from the inside bank to the outside bank and label its ends A and B. Then draw the cross-section along that line. Begin at the inside of the bend, where the land slopes gently down into the water: this is the slip-off slope, made of deposited sand and gravel, so draw a gentle slope and label it deposition, slow-flowing shallow water, point bar or slip-off slope. Then let the river bed deepen gradually towards the outside of the bend, so that the deepest part of the channel is near the outer bank. Mark this with a label such as fastest flow and deepest water, or thalweg. At the outside of the bend, draw a steep, almost vertical bank and label it river cliff, formed by erosion and undercutting. Add small arrows showing the corkscrew helicoidal flow, moving across the surface towards the outer bank, down, and back along the bed towards the inner bank. Remember that the cross-section is asymmetrical: one side is gentle, one side is steep. In your explanation, connect the shape to the processes: faster flow and more energy on the outside cause erosion; slower flow and less energy on the inside cause deposition. Labels with process words earn more marks than labels that only name features.

### subtopic_2 — Erosion and Deposition on a Bend
**Assistant:** Why does the river break through the neck during a flood?

**Tutor:** During normal flow, the river follows its looped path around the meander, because it is contained within its channel. The neck of land between the two outer bends may already be very narrow, but as long as the water stays in the channel, it keeps flowing the long way round. In a flood, the situation changes in three important ways. First, the volume of water increases enormously, and the river overflows its banks, spreading across the flood plain, including over the narrow neck. Water flowing across the neck is taking a much shorter and therefore steeper route downstream than water following the loop, so it flows faster and has more energy to erode. Second, the greater speed and volume of the flood give the river much more power for hydraulic action and abrasion, so it can quickly cut a channel through the soft, loose sediments of the neck. Third, once a small channel has been cut, more and more water is drawn into it, because it is the most efficient route, and it rapidly enlarges into the river's new main channel. After the flood, the river remains in this straighter channel. Water entering the old loop slows down, deposits its load at the entrance and exit, and the loop is sealed off. This is a good example of how a single extreme event can complete a change that has been building up slowly over many years. It also explains why people living on flood plains must be prepared for rivers to change their course suddenly, which can affect property boundaries, roads and even borders between countries that follow rivers.

### subtopic_3 — How Oxbow Lakes Form
**Assistant:** Why are wetlands like oxbow lakes so valuable?

**Tutor:** Wetlands are areas where land is covered by shallow water or is waterlogged for at least part of the year, and oxbow lakes and the marshes that develop from them are an important type. They are valuable for several reasons. Ecologically, they are among the most productive ecosystems on earth. They provide breeding and feeding areas for fish, frogs, insects and many water birds, as well as habitats for hippos, crocodiles and specialised plants. The pans of the Pongola flood plain, for example, are a haven for birds, including many that are rare elsewhere in South Africa. Hydrologically, wetlands act like sponges. During floods, they absorb and store water, slowing it down and reducing flood damage downstream; during dry periods, they release water slowly, helping to keep rivers flowing. They also recharge groundwater. Environmentally, wetlands filter water: plants and sediments trap silt and absorb nutrients and some pollutants, improving water quality downstream. Economically and socially, wetlands support people through fishing, grazing, flood-recession farming, reeds for weaving and thatching, medicinal plants and tourism. Despite these benefits, South Africa has lost a large proportion of its wetlands through drainage for farming, building, mining, dams and pollution, and many of those that remain are damaged. The country is a signatory to the Ramsar Convention on Wetlands, and several South African wetlands, including Ndumo Game Reserve, have been listed as Wetlands of International Importance. World Wetlands Day is celebrated on 2 February each year to raise awareness of their importance.

### subtopic_4 — Why They Matter and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Should engineers straighten meandering rivers?

**Tutor:** In the past, engineers in many countries straightened meandering rivers, a process called channelisation, for several reasons. A straight river is shorter, so floodwater drains away more quickly from the area being protected. Straightening can make rivers easier for boats to navigate, frees up land for farming and building, and stops meanders from eroding valuable property. Some of these benefits were real in the short term and for particular places. However, experience has shown that straightening rivers often creates serious problems. Because a straight channel is shorter and steeper, the water flows faster, so it has more energy to erode its bed and banks and carries floods downstream more quickly, increasing the risk of flooding for towns further down the river. Straightening also destroys the variety of habitats that a meandering river provides, such as deep pools, shallow bars and oxbow lakes, so fish and bird populations decline. Wetlands on the flood plain dry out, and their water storage and filtering functions are lost. Maintaining straightened channels can be expensive, as they often need concrete lining and repeated repairs. For these reasons, many countries, including several in Europe, are now spending money to restore meanders and reconnect rivers to their flood plains, a process known as river restoration. A balanced answer recognises that there may be places where controlling a river is necessary, for example to protect a city, but that working with natural river processes, by keeping buildings off flood plains and protecting wetlands, is often cheaper and more sustainable in the long run.
