Hey — this topic fits in three pictures: a spilt cup of muddy water, a fan of mud at the sea and a city behind a wall.

The cup. In the lower course, the river is wide, slow and carries fine silt. When it floods, water leaves the channel, slows down and drops coarse sand next to the river, building natural levees. Fine silt spreads over the flood plain as fertile alluvium.

The fan. Where a river enters a calm sea or lake, it slows and deposits its load, helped by flocculation. Sediment builds up into new land, a delta, and the river splits into distributaries. Types: arcuate, like the Nile; bird's-foot, like the Mississippi; cuspate, like the Tiber. The Okavango in Botswana is an inland delta. South Africa's rough coast has few deltas.

The wall. Deltas are fertile and crowded, as on the Nile and in Bangladesh, but they flood. Artificial levees protect land but can fail, as in New Orleans in 2005. Dams starve deltas of sediment, and sea levels are rising.
