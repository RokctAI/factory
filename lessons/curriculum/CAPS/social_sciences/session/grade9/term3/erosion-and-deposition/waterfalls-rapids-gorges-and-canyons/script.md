# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the river erosion features of waterfalls, rapids, gorges and canyons. It covers how rivers erode and where these features form in a river's course; how waterfalls form and retreat upstream; how rapids form; and how gorges and canyons form, with South African and African examples and the importance of these features for people. The anchor idea is differential erosion: where a river flows over rocks of different hardness, it erodes the softer rock faster than the harder rock, creating sudden steps in its bed. The governing idea is that the spectacular features of a river's upper course are made by the same processes of hydraulic action, abrasion and undercutting, acting over thousands of years on rocks of different strength.

## Subtopic: Rivers at Work in the Upper Course

A river and its valley change along its length, from its source to its mouth. Geographers often divide a river into three parts: the upper course, the middle course and the lower course. In the upper course, near the source, the river flows down steep slopes, often in mountains or on a plateau edge. It is usually narrow and shallow, with a rocky bed, but because the slope is steep, the water can move fast and has a great deal of energy, especially during floods.

In the upper course, the river uses most of its energy to erode downwards into its bed. This is called vertical erosion. It uses the processes you studied earlier. Hydraulic action is the force of the water itself, which pushes into cracks and loosens rock. Abrasion happens when the boulders, pebbles and sand carried by the river scrape and grind the bed, rather like sandpaper. Potholes form where swirling water spins pebbles round and round in hollows in the bed, drilling them deeper. Attrition breaks the river's load into smaller, rounder pieces as they collide. Solution dissolves some minerals.

Because the river is cutting downwards faster than the valley sides are being worn back, the valley in the upper course is usually narrow and steep-sided, with a V-shaped cross-section. Weathering and mass movement on the valley sides move material down into the river, which carries it away. The river often winds around spurs of high land, called interlocking spurs.

The landforms of the upper course are mainly erosional landforms, formed by the wearing away of rock. The most dramatic of these are waterfalls, rapids, gorges and canyons. All of them depend on the river's power of vertical erosion and on differences in the strength of the rocks the river flows across.

In South Africa, many rivers begin on the high interior plateau or on the Great Escarpment, the steep edge of the plateau that runs in a great arc around the country, including the Drakensberg in KwaZulu-Natal and Mpumalanga. Where rivers flow over this escarpment, they create many waterfalls and deep valleys. The Tugela River, for example, rises on the plateau of the Mont-aux-Sources area and drops over the edge of the Drakensberg escarpment in a series of falls.

So the next question is: how exactly does a waterfall form?

## Subtopic: How Waterfalls Form and Retreat

A waterfall is a place where a river drops vertically, or almost vertically, over a step in its bed. Most waterfalls form where a band of hard, resistant rock lies on top of softer, less resistant rock.

The process works in stages. First, the river flows over the hard rock, which it erodes only slowly. When it reaches the softer rock beyond or beneath it, it erodes that much faster, so a step develops in the river bed. This is differential erosion. Second, the water falling over the step hits the bed below with great force. Hydraulic action and abrasion by boulders swirling in the water erode a deep hollow at the foot of the falls, called a plunge pool. Third, the falling water and the spray splashing back erode the soft rock behind the waterfall, undercutting the hard rock above. This leaves the hard rock sticking out as an overhang, with a hollow behind the curtain of water. Fourth, the overhang becomes unsupported and eventually collapses under its own weight. The fallen blocks drop into the plunge pool, where they are used as tools for further abrasion. Finally, the whole process repeats, and with each collapse the waterfall moves a little further back upstream. This is called retreat or headward erosion.

As a waterfall retreats, it leaves behind it a steep-sided, narrow valley called a gorge. The gorge marks the path the waterfall has taken as it retreated upstream.

Not all waterfalls form in exactly this way. Some form where a river flows over the edge of a plateau or escarpment, where a fault in the earth's crust has created a sudden drop, or where a glacier has deepened a main valley so that side streams drop into it. But the hard-over-soft model explains many waterfalls.

South Africa has several famous waterfalls. The Tugela Falls in the Drakensberg drop about 948 metres in a series of five falls, and they are often listed as the second-highest waterfall in the world, though measurements are debated. The Howick Falls on the uMngeni River in KwaZulu-Natal drop about 95 metres over a ledge of hard dolerite. The Augrabies Falls on the Orange River drop about 56 metres into a granite gorge about 18 kilometres long. Just across our borders, the Victoria Falls on the Zambezi River, between Zambia and Zimbabwe, are about 1,7 kilometres wide and about 108 metres high, and are known locally as Mosi-oa-Tunya, "the smoke that thunders". Their gorges zigzag downstream along lines of weakness in the basalt rock, showing earlier positions of the falls.

On a topographic map, a waterfall is shown by a symbol across the river, and you can often recognise its position because contour lines crowd together very closely where they cross the river, showing a sudden drop in height. A gorge appears as a narrow valley where contours on both sides are packed close together. Being able to recognise these features on a 1:50 000 map links this lesson to the mapwork skills you learnt in Term 1.

So the next question is: how are rapids different from waterfalls?

## Subtopic: Rapids, Gorges and Canyons

Rapids are sections of a river where the bed is steep and rocky and the water flows fast and turbulently, with broken, foaming white water. They often form where a river crosses alternating bands of hard and soft rock, so that the softer bands are eroded more quickly, leaving a series of small steps and ridges of hard rock. Rapids may also form where boulders have fallen into the river from the valley sides, or below a waterfall where the river is still steep. Rapids can be thought of as small waterfalls or a series of steps rather than one big drop, and in time some rapids may develop into waterfalls. Rapids are dangerous for boats but attract river rafting and canoeing, for example on the Orange River, the Vaal and the Tugela.

A gorge is a narrow, steep-sided valley, much deeper than it is wide, often with almost vertical rock walls. Gorges form mainly in two ways. Many form, as you have seen, by the retreat of a waterfall, which cuts back through hard rock and leaves a deep slot behind it, such as the Augrabies gorge. Others form where a river cuts down very rapidly into hard rock because the land is rising or because the river has a great deal of energy, so that the valley sides do not have time to be worn back into a wider V-shape.

A canyon is a very large, deep gorge, usually many kilometres long and hundreds of metres deep. Canyons often form in dry or semi-arid regions. There, the river, fed by rain far away or by snowmelt, keeps cutting downwards, while the valley sides, receiving little rain, are weathered and worn back only slowly, so they stay steep. Canyons are also more likely where the land has been slowly uplifted, giving the river more energy to cut down, and where the rocks are in horizontal layers that form steep cliffs and ledges.

The Fish River Canyon in southern Namibia is one of the largest canyons in the world: about 160 kilometres long and in places about 550 metres deep. In South Africa, the Blyde River Canyon in Mpumalanga, on the edge of the Great Escarpment, is about 25 kilometres long and in places around 750 metres deep, and it is one of the largest green canyons on earth, because its slopes are covered with subtropical vegetation. The Grand Canyon in the United States, cut by the Colorado River, is about 450 kilometres long and up to about 1,8 kilometres deep, and its walls reveal rock layers covering hundreds of millions of years of earth history.

Gorges and canyons are also valuable to scientists. Their steep walls expose layers of rock that would otherwise be hidden underground, like the pages of a history book, so geologists can read the order in which rocks were laid down and find fossils. Canyons also provide sheltered habitats for plants and animals, and the Blyde River Canyon is home to a wide range of vegetation, birds and primates.

So the final question is: why do these landforms matter to people?

## Subtopic: Why They Matter, and the Error Museum

Waterfalls, rapids, gorges and canyons are valuable to people in many ways. Tourism is one of the most important. Visitors come to see the Victoria Falls, the Augrabies Falls National Park, the Blyde River Canyon with its viewpoints such as God's Window and the Three Rondavels, and the Tugela Falls in the Royal Natal National Park, bringing income and jobs to local communities. Adventure tourism, such as hiking the Fish River Canyon trail, abseiling, bungee jumping from the Victoria Falls Bridge and white-water rafting on rapids, has grown rapidly.

These landforms also provide sites for water supply and energy. Narrow gorges with hard rock walls are good places to build dam walls, because a short, strong wall can hold back a large volume of water, and the rock provides firm foundations. The fast fall of water can be used to produce hydroelectric power. The Kariba Dam on the Zambezi, built in a gorge, supplies electricity to Zambia and Zimbabwe, and South Africa's Drakensberg and Ingula pumped-storage schemes use the height of the escarpment to generate power at times of high demand. The Lesotho Highlands Water Project uses dams in deep mountain valleys to send water to Gauteng.

These landforms can also be hazards and barriers. Waterfalls and rapids block navigation, which is one reason why most African rivers were not used for transport deep into the interior. Gorges and canyons can be barriers to roads and railways, needing long bridges or detours. Rockfalls from gorge walls and flash floods in canyons can be dangerous for visitors.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Waterfalls stay in the same place forever." Waterfalls slowly retreat upstream as the overhang collapses, leaving a gorge behind them.

Exhibit two: "The plunge pool is made by rain falling into the river." It is eroded by the force of the falling water and by boulders swirling at the foot of the falls.

Exhibit three: "A waterfall forms where soft rock lies over hard rock." It usually forms where hard rock lies over softer rock, which is eroded and undercut.

Exhibit four: "Canyons form fastest in very wet areas." Canyons often form in dry areas, where the valley sides are worn back slowly while the river keeps cutting down.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a river with a saw, a crumbling ledge, and a deep slot in the land.

## Subtopic: A River With a Saw

Imagine a river high up in the mountains. The slope is steep, so the water rushes downhill fast. A fast river is strong. It carries stones and boulders, and it uses them like a saw, scraping and grinding its bed deeper and deeper.

In the mountains, the river cuts down more than it cuts sideways. So the valley it makes is narrow and steep, shaped like the letter V.

But rock is not all the same. Some rock is hard, like a concrete step. Some is soft, like a step made of hard-packed sand. The river saws through soft rock much faster than hard rock. Where the two meet, the river bed gets a sudden step. And that step is where the magic begins.

So what happens at the step?

## Subtopic: A Crumbling Ledge

At the step, the water falls over the edge. This is a waterfall. The falling water crashes down and digs a deep pool at the bottom, called a plunge pool.

The spray and splashing also eat away the soft rock behind the waterfall. Soon the hard rock on top is left sticking out like a shelf, with nothing holding it up underneath. One day, the shelf breaks off and falls into the pool.

Now the waterfall is a little further back upstream. Again and again, over thousands of years, the ledge crumbles and the waterfall creeps backwards. South Africa has some wonderful waterfalls, such as the Tugela Falls, one of the highest in the world, and the thundering Augrabies Falls on the Orange River.

Where the river tumbles over lots of small steps instead of one big one, the water foams white and fast. These are rapids, loved by river rafters.

Waterfalls also form where a river flows over the edge of a great cliff, such as the edge of the Drakensberg mountains, where the Tugela River leaps off the top.

So what does a retreating waterfall leave behind?

## Subtopic: A Deep Slot in the Land

As a waterfall creeps backwards, it leaves a deep, narrow valley behind it, with steep rock walls. This is called a gorge. At Augrabies, the gorge is about 18 kilometres long.

A really huge gorge, hundreds of metres deep and many kilometres long, is called a canyon. Canyons often form in dry places, where the river keeps sawing down but the sides stay steep because there is little rain to wear them back. Namibia's Fish River Canyon and the Blyde River Canyon in Mpumalanga are famous examples.

Some gorges are so deep and narrow that sunlight reaches the bottom for only a short time each day.

People love these places. Tourists come to stare over the edges, hike, raft and bungee jump. Engineers build dams in narrow gorges, and falling water can make electricity.

Here is the line to remember: where a river flows from hard rock onto soft rock, it carves a waterfall, which creeps upstream and leaves a gorge behind it; over a very long time, rivers can cut canyons hundreds of metres deep.
