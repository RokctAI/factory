### subtopic_1 — Rivers at Work in the Upper Course
**Assistant:** How can I draw and label the formation of a waterfall in a test?

**Tutor:** A clear labelled diagram is often the best way to explain waterfall formation, and examiners usually expect to see a cross-section showing the river from the side. Start by drawing the river bed as a line with a step in it. Above the step, shade a thick layer and label it hard, resistant rock, such as dolerite, sandstone or granite. Below and behind it, shade a different pattern and label it soft, less resistant rock, such as shale. Draw the river flowing from left to right over the hard rock and falling over the edge as a curtain of water. At the foot of the falls, draw a deep hollow and label it plunge pool, adding a few boulders in it and an arrow showing swirling water, with a label such as erosion by hydraulic action and abrasion. Behind the falling water, show a hollow cut into the soft rock and label it undercutting, with the hard rock sticking out above as an overhang. Then add a dotted line showing where the overhang will break and collapse. Finally, add an arrow pointing upstream and label it direction of retreat, and you may also draw a second diagram showing the gorge left downstream after several collapses. In your written explanation, use the correct sequence of stages: differential erosion of soft rock, formation of the plunge pool, undercutting, collapse of the overhang, and retreat upstream forming a gorge. Use geographical terms such as hydraulic action, abrasion, resistant rock and headward erosion. A neat, well-labelled diagram combined with a short explanation can earn more marks than a long paragraph alone.

### subtopic_2 — How Waterfalls Form and Retreat
**Assistant:** How fast do waterfalls actually retreat?

**Tutor:** The speed at which a waterfall retreats varies greatly, depending on the hardness of the rock, the volume and power of the river, the amount of sediment it carries to act as tools for abrasion, and the climate. Some waterfalls cut through very hard rock and retreat only a few millimetres or centimetres a year, so their position changes very little over a human lifetime. Others retreat much faster. Niagara Falls on the border between Canada and the United States is a famous example: before engineers began controlling the water flow, it retreated by roughly a metre a year on average, and over about 12 000 years since the end of the last ice age it has cut a gorge about 11 kilometres long. This calculation is a good example of how geographers use the length of a gorge and the rate of retreat to estimate how old a landform is. Today, much of the Niagara River's water is diverted to hydroelectric power stations, and the rock edge has been reinforced, so retreat has slowed greatly. The Victoria Falls show a different pattern: they have retreated through a series of zigzag gorges over hundreds of thousands of years, because the river has eroded back along lines of weakness filled with softer material in the hard basalt. For Augrabies and other South African falls, which cut through very hard granite, retreat is generally slow. The key point to remember is that waterfalls are not permanent; they are always moving upstream, but usually far too slowly to notice without careful measurement over many years.

### subtopic_3 — Rapids, Gorges and Canyons
**Assistant:** What is the difference between a gorge and a canyon?

**Tutor:** Gorges and canyons are both deep, steep-sided river valleys, and the words are sometimes used to mean the same thing, so it is useful to understand how geographers usually distinguish them. A gorge is a narrow valley with very steep, often vertical, rock walls, which is deeper than it is wide. Gorges are often relatively short, a few hundred metres to tens of kilometres long, and many form where a waterfall has retreated upstream through hard rock, such as the Augrabies gorge on the Orange River or the gorges below the Victoria Falls. A canyon is generally much larger: usually a very long and deep valley, many kilometres long and often hundreds of metres to more than a kilometre deep, sometimes with a series of cliffs and ledges stepping down the sides where harder and softer rock layers have been eroded at different rates. Canyons usually form over a very long time where a river has cut down steadily into a slowly rising plateau, often in a dry climate where the valley sides are weathered slowly and therefore stay steep. The Fish River Canyon, the Blyde River Canyon and the Grand Canyon are examples. In simple terms, you could say that every canyon is a kind of gorge, but a canyon is bigger and deeper and often formed by a river cutting into a plateau, while a gorge can be quite small and is often formed by a retreating waterfall. In an examination, if you are asked to describe either feature, focus on the shape, steep sides and depth, and explain the processes of vertical erosion that formed it.

### subtopic_4 — Why They Matter, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why are dams often built in gorges?

**Tutor:** Gorges are attractive places to build dams for several geographical and engineering reasons. First, a gorge is narrow, so the dam wall needed to block the river is relatively short, which saves a great deal of concrete and money compared with building a wall across a wide valley. Second, gorges are usually cut through hard, resistant rock, which provides strong, stable foundations and side walls for the dam, so the pressure of the water held behind it can be safely carried by the rock. Third, the deep valley upstream of the gorge can hold a large volume of water in a reservoir, while flooding a smaller area of land than a shallow valley would, which reduces the number of people and the amount of farmland affected. Fourth, the height difference between the water behind the dam and the river below can be used to generate hydroelectric power, because falling water turns turbines. The Kariba Dam on the Zambezi, the Katse Dam in Lesotho, part of the Lesotho Highlands Water Project, and many South African dams such as the Gariep Dam on the Orange River were built at narrow points in river valleys for these reasons. However, building dams in gorges also has costs. They can flood valuable ecosystems and heritage sites, displace communities, trap sediment that would otherwise reach the river's lower course and coast, and change the flow of water downstream, affecting fish and farmers. When dams are planned, these costs and benefits must be carefully weighed, and local communities should be consulted.
