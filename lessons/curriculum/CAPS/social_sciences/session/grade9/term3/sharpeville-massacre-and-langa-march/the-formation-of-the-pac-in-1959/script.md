# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the formation of the Pan Africanist Congress, or PAC, in 1959. It covers the ideas of African nationalism and Pan-Africanism and their roots in the ANC Youth League and in the independence of African countries; the growing disagreements within the ANC that led the Africanists to break away in 1958; the founding conference of the PAC in April 1959 and its aims, policies and slogans; and the life and leadership of Robert Sobukwe, together with the PAC's plans that led to the anti-pass campaign of 1960. The anchor idea is that the PAC was formed by African nationalists who believed that Africans must lead their own liberation and that the land belonged to the indigenous African majority. The governing idea is that the split between the ANC and the PAC was a disagreement about strategy and identity within the liberation movement, not about the goal of ending apartheid, and that competition between them helped to shape the dramatic events of 1960.

## Subtopic: Africanism and Pan-Africanism

African nationalism is the belief that African people should unite, take pride in their identity and history, and govern themselves. In South Africa, its most influential early thinker was Anton Lembede, the first president of the ANC Youth League in 1944. Lembede argued that Africans had been made to feel inferior by colonialism and that they needed to develop self-confidence and self-reliance. He believed that Africa was a black man's country, that Africans should lead their own struggle, and that they should be cautious about alliances with other groups, including communists, whose ideas came from outside Africa. Lembede died young in 1947, but his ideas influenced a generation of Youth League members, who became known as the Africanists.

Pan-Africanism is the idea that all people of African descent, in Africa and around the world, share a common history and destiny and should work together for freedom and unity. It developed in the early twentieth century among thinkers such as W.E.B. Du Bois in the United States and Marcus Garvey in Jamaica, and through a series of Pan-African Congresses. After World War II, Pan-Africanism became closely linked to the struggle against colonialism.

The late 1950s were a time of great excitement in Africa. In 1957, Ghana, led by Kwame Nkrumah, became the first sub-Saharan African country to win independence from colonial rule. In December 1958, Nkrumah hosted the All-African People's Conference in Accra, which brought together liberation movements from across the continent and called for the end of colonialism. Many other countries were moving towards independence; in 1960 alone, seventeen African countries became independent, so it became known as the Year of Africa. For South African Africanists, these events showed that African freedom was possible and coming soon, and they wanted South Africa to be part of a free, united Africa.

These ideas were not new in South Africa. African thinkers had written about African unity and pride since the late nineteenth century, and the founders of the ANC in 1912 had themselves called for Africans of different ethnic groups to unite as one people. Pixley ka Isaka Seme, one of the ANC's founders, had spoken in 1906 of the regeneration of Africa. What was new in the 1950s was the belief that liberation was close at hand.

So the next question is: why did the Africanists break away from the ANC?

## Subtopic: The Split in the ANC

Through the 1950s, tensions grew within the ANC between the Africanists and the majority of the leadership, who supported the Congress Alliance and the Freedom Charter. The Africanists, who were strongest in the Transvaal, especially in Orlando in Soweto, published their views in a journal called The Africanist. Their disagreements focused on several issues.

The first was the Freedom Charter. The Africanists rejected its statement that South Africa belongs to all who live in it, black and white. They argued that the land had been taken from Africans by conquest and that it belonged to the indigenous African people, so the Charter seemed to accept the results of colonial dispossession.

The second was the Congress Alliance. The Alliance was organised as a partnership of separate organisations for Africans, Indians, coloured people and whites. The Africanists argued that this was multiracialism, which treated South Africans as separate racial groups, just as apartheid did, and gave minorities, who were only a small part of the population, equal weight in decision-making with the African majority.

The third was the influence of communists. The Africanists believed that white and Indian communists, especially in the Congress of Democrats, had too much influence over ANC policy and had shaped the Freedom Charter. They argued that the struggle should be guided by African nationalism, not by an ideology imported from Europe.

The fourth was strategy. The Africanists felt that the ANC leadership was too cautious, that its campaigns were poorly organised and that the Treason Trial had paralysed it. They wanted bolder, more decisive action.

The ANC leadership, including Albert Luthuli, Oliver Tambo and Nelson Mandela, rejected these criticisms. They argued that non-racialism, uniting all opponents of apartheid regardless of race, was both morally right and strategically necessary, and that the Freedom Charter reflected the demands of the people.

Matters came to a head at the Transvaal ANC conference in November 1958, when there were disputes about leadership and procedure, and the Africanists were prevented from taking part fully. They announced that they were breaking away from the ANC and would form a new organisation.

So the next question is: how was the PAC formed, and what did it stand for?

## Subtopic: The Founding of the PAC and Its Aims

The Pan Africanist Congress held its founding conference in the Orlando Communal Hall in Soweto from 4 to 6 April 1959, and was launched on 6 April. The date was chosen deliberately, because 6 April was the anniversary of the arrival of Jan van Riebeeck at the Cape in 1652, which the white government celebrated as the founding of white South Africa. Robert Sobukwe was elected president and Potlako Leballo became national secretary.

The PAC's aims were set out in its manifesto and constitution. It aimed to unite and rally the African people into one national front on the basis of African nationalism; to fight for the overthrow of white domination; to establish and maintain a democratic government of the Africans, by the Africans, for the Africans; and to work for a united states of Africa. The PAC defined an African as anyone who owed loyalty only to Africa and accepted the democratic rule of an African majority, which meant that, in principle, people of other races could be counted as Africans if they accepted these conditions. It rejected multiracialism, but declared that it believed in one human race and would not discriminate against anyone on the basis of race in a free South Africa.

The PAC's slogans included "Izwe Lethu", meaning "Our land", and "Africa for the Africans". It adopted a flag showing a map of Africa with a gold star over Ghana, in green, symbolising its links with the rest of the continent.

The PAC grew quickly in some areas, especially in parts of the Transvaal, such as Sharpeville and Evaton near Vereeniging, and in the Western Cape, where many African migrant workers living in Langa and Nyanga were angered by influx control and the pass laws. By the end of 1959 it claimed tens of thousands of members, although it was much smaller than the ANC overall.

The PAC's economic policy was described as Africanist socialist democracy, which aimed at a fair distribution of wealth and the return of land to the people, though its details were less developed than its political programme. In the Western Cape, the PAC attracted many young migrant workers living in single-sex hostels in Langa and Nyanga, who faced constant pass raids and the threat of being sent back to the reserves under the government's policy of reducing the number of Africans in the region. One of its most energetic young organisers there was Philip Kgosana, a student at the University of Cape Town, who would play a central role in the events of March 1960. The PAC's message of self-reliance and quick results appealed especially to the young and to people who felt the older organisations had achieved too little.

So the final question is: who was Robert Sobukwe, and what did the PAC plan?

## Subtopic: Robert Sobukwe and the PAC's Plans, and the Error Museum

Robert Mangaliso Sobukwe was born in Graaff-Reinet in the Eastern Cape in 1924. His family was poor; his father was a labourer and his mother a domestic worker. He was a brilliant student and won a place at Healdtown, a Methodist mission school, and then at Fort Hare University College, where he became involved in the ANC Youth League and was elected president of the students' representative council in 1949. He worked as a teacher and, from 1954, as a lecturer in African languages at the University of the Witwatersrand, where students called him "Prof". He was known as a thoughtful, gentle and principled man and a powerful speaker who refused to hate his opponents.

Under Sobukwe's leadership, the PAC set itself the goal of achieving freedom by 1963. In 1959, it ran a status campaign, which encouraged Africans to demand courteous treatment in shops, for example by refusing to be called by insulting names. In December 1959, it decided to launch a campaign against the pass laws. The ANC had also planned an anti-pass campaign, to begin on 31 March 1960. The PAC announced that its campaign would begin earlier, on 21 March 1960.

The PAC's plan was that African men would leave their passes at home and present themselves at police stations, inviting arrest. Their slogan was "No bail, no defence, no fine". If enough men filled the prisons, the economy, which depended on African labour, would be disrupted and the government would be forced to abolish the pass laws. Sobukwe wrote to the Commissioner of Police to explain that the campaign would be non-violent, and instructed members to avoid violence. Historians debate whether the PAC was well enough organised for such a campaign, and whether competition with the ANC led it to act in haste. What is clear is that the campaign would change the course of South African history.

Sobukwe's own fate after 21 March 1960 shows how seriously the government took him. He was arrested that day, convicted of incitement and sentenced to three years in prison, and when that sentence ended in 1963, Parliament passed a special law, known as the Sobukwe clause, to keep him imprisoned on Robben Island, alone, until 1969.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "The PAC disagreed with the ANC about whether apartheid should end." Both wanted to end apartheid; they disagreed about strategy, alliances and the Freedom Charter.

Exhibit two: "The PAC said only people with black skin could ever be African." It defined an African as anyone who owed loyalty only to Africa and accepted African majority rule.

Exhibit three: "The PAC was formed in 1912." The ANC was formed in 1912; the PAC was formed on 6 April 1959.

Exhibit four: "The PAC's anti-pass campaign was planned to be violent." Sobukwe instructed members to remain non-violent and to invite arrest.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a continent waking up, a family argument, and a teacher called "Prof".

## Subtopic: A Continent Waking Up

In the 1950s, Africa was waking up. Country after country was winning freedom from European rule. In 1957, Ghana became independent, led by Kwame Nkrumah. In 1960 alone, seventeen African countries became free.

Many young South Africans watched this with excitement. They believed in Pan-Africanism, the idea that all African people share one history and should stand together, and in African nationalism, the idea that Africans should be proud of who they are and lead their own fight for freedom.

These ideas had been taught in South Africa by Anton Lembede of the ANC Youth League in the 1940s. His followers were called Africanists.

They asked: if Ghana can be free, why not South Africa? And why not soon? Many of them were young, and they were tired of waiting. They wanted action, and they wanted it quickly.

So why did they leave the ANC?

## Subtopic: A Family Argument

Imagine a family that agrees on where it wants to go, but argues about which road to take.

The Africanists and the ANC leaders all wanted to end apartheid. But they disagreed about how.

The Africanists did not like the Freedom Charter's words that South Africa belongs to all who live in it, black and white. They said the land had been taken from Africans and belonged to them. They did not like working in an alliance with organisations for Indian, coloured and white people. They felt communists had too much influence. And they felt the ANC was too slow.

The ANC leaders answered that uniting everyone against apartheid, whatever their race, was right and would make the struggle stronger.

Both sides believed they were right, and both had good reasons. In 1958, the argument split the family, and the Africanists walked out of a meeting of the ANC in the Transvaal. On 6 April 1959, the Africanists formed the Pan Africanist Congress, the PAC. Their slogan was "Izwe Lethu": our land.

So who led the new organisation?

## Subtopic: A Teacher Called "Prof"

The PAC's first president was Robert Sobukwe. He grew up poor in Graaff-Reinet, but he was so clever that he went to university at Fort Hare and later taught African languages at Wits University. People called him "Prof", and his students loved his lessons.

He was calm, gentle and a wonderful speaker, and he refused to hate anyone, even the people who would later imprison him. He said that in a free South Africa there would be only one race, the human race.

The PAC set a bold goal: freedom by 1963. It planned a campaign against the pass laws for 21 March 1960. Men would leave their passes at home, go to police stations and say: arrest us. No bail, no defence, no fine. Fill the jails, and the country cannot run, because factories, mines and homes all depended on African workers.

Sobukwe told everyone to stay peaceful, and he wrote to the police to explain that the protest would be non-violent. Nobody knew that the campaign would end in one of the darkest days in South African history.

Here is the line to remember: the PAC was formed on 6 April 1959 by Africanists who believed Africans must lead their own liberation, and under Robert Sobukwe it planned the anti-pass campaign of 21 March 1960.
