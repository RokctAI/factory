### subtopic_1 — Causes of the Protests
**Assistant:** Why do historians disagree about why the police opened fire?

**Tutor:** Historians and investigators have studied the Sharpeville massacre carefully, and although the main facts are clear, some details remain disputed. The police officers on the scene, and the government at the time, claimed that they had fired in self-defence. They said that the crowd was hostile and very large, that stones had been thrown, that some people were armed with sticks and other weapons, and that the police feared they would be overwhelmed, especially because nine police officers had been killed by a crowd at Cato Manor in Durban only two months earlier. Critics, survivors and many historians strongly reject the claim of self-defence. They point out that the crowd had been largely peaceful and good-humoured for hours, that no police officer was seriously injured, that the police were heavily armed and protected by armoured cars, and that no order to fire was given and no warning was given to the crowd to disperse. Most importantly, the medical evidence showed that the great majority of the dead and wounded had been shot in the back, which means they were running away, not attacking. Several explanations have been suggested for why the police fired: that inexperienced and nervous officers panicked when the crowd surged forward; that the police expected violence because of the tense atmosphere and their attitudes towards black protesters; that one or two officers opened fire and others followed without thinking; or that the police had come prepared to teach the protesters a lesson. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission later found that the police had deliberately opened fire on an unarmed crowd and that this was a gross violation of human rights. Whatever the exact trigger, there is no doubt that the response was grossly excessive and that it caused the deaths of sixty-nine unarmed people.

### subtopic_2 — The Sharpeville Massacre
**Assistant:** Why did Philip Kgosana send the marchers home?

**Tutor:** Philip Kgosana's decision to send the marchers home on 30 March 1960 has been debated ever since. At the time, he was only twenty-three years old, a student at the University of Cape Town and the regional leader of the PAC. He had led about thirty thousand people on a disciplined, peaceful march into the centre of Cape Town, and they were gathered near the police headquarters at Caledon Square, where armed police and soldiers were present. Kgosana's main demands were the release of arrested PAC leaders and a meeting with the Minister of Justice to discuss the pass laws and the grievances of the people. Colonel Terblanche, the senior police officer, promised to arrange the meeting with the Minister if Kgosana sent the crowd home. Kgosana accepted the offer for several reasons. He was committed to the PAC's policy of non-violence, and he feared that if the huge crowd stayed in the city centre, the police and army might open fire, causing another massacre like Sharpeville. He believed that a meeting with the Minister might achieve concrete results. He may also have trusted the word of a senior police officer. The marchers obeyed him and returned peacefully to Langa, which showed his authority and the discipline of the crowd. However, when he returned for the meeting, he was arrested, and the government used the time to surround the townships and crush the strike. Some critics have argued that he missed a historic opportunity to put real pressure on the government. Others argue that he saved many lives and that the government would have used force against the crowd. Kgosana later went into exile and returned to South Africa after 1994. He died in 2017.

### subtopic_3 — Langa and the March on Cape Town
**Assistant:** Why did Sharpeville lead the ANC and PAC to turn to armed struggle?

**Tutor:** Sharpeville convinced many leaders of the liberation movements that peaceful methods alone could no longer bring change, for several connected reasons. First, the massacre showed that the government was prepared to use deadly force against unarmed people protesting peacefully. Second, after Sharpeville, the government banned the ANC and the PAC under the Unlawful Organisations Act of April 1960. This made it illegal for them to hold meetings, recruit members or organise legal campaigns, so the non-violent methods they had used for decades, such as protests, boycotts and stay-aways, became very difficult and dangerous. Third, the state of emergency and the detention of thousands of people showed that the government would respond to any form of protest with greater repression, rather than reform. When the ANC organised a stay-at-home in May 1961 to protest against the declaration of a republic without consulting the majority, the government mobilised the army and police on a huge scale, and the stay-away had limited success. Leaders such as Nelson Mandela concluded that the government had closed all peaceful avenues of change. Mandela argued that if the movement did not provide leadership, frustrated people would turn to uncontrolled violence. In 1961, the ANC and the South African Communist Party formed Umkhonto we Sizwe, which began a campaign of sabotage on 16 December 1961. Its first strategy was to attack government installations, such as power lines and offices, while trying to avoid loss of life. The PAC's armed wing, Poqo, was more willing to target people. It is important to note that this decision was controversial, and that Albert Luthuli, who remained committed to non-violence, was uneasy about it. The armed struggle would continue, in different forms, until 1990.

### subtopic_4 — Short- and Longer-Term Consequences, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why did Mandela sign the Constitution at Sharpeville?

**Tutor:** On 10 December 1996, President Nelson Mandela signed South Africa's new Constitution into law at a ceremony in Sharpeville. The choice of place and date was deeply symbolic. The date, 10 December, is International Human Rights Day, the anniversary of the adoption of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights by the United Nations in 1948. As you learnt earlier this term, South Africa had abstained from voting for that Declaration, because it clashed with apartheid. Signing the Constitution on that date showed that South Africa had finally committed itself to the human rights that the world had declared almost fifty years earlier. The place, Sharpeville, was where sixty-nine unarmed people had been killed by police on 21 March 1960 while protesting against the pass laws, one of the most notorious violations of human rights under apartheid. By signing the Constitution there, Mandela honoured those who had died, acknowledged the suffering caused by apartheid, and connected the new democracy to the long struggle that had made it possible. The Constitution itself, with its Bill of Rights, guarantees freedom of movement, the right to protest peacefully, freedom of association and equality before the law, all rights that had been denied to the people of Sharpeville. In his speech, Mandela spoke about the sacrifices made by ordinary people and the need to build a society based on human dignity. Since then, 21 March has been marked in South Africa as Human Rights Day. These choices show how symbols and places of memory are used to help a nation reflect on its past and commit itself to a different future.
