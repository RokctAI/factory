### subtopic_1 — Africanism and Pan-Africanism
**Assistant:** What is the difference between non-racialism and the PAC's idea of Africanism?

**Tutor:** Both the ANC and the PAC opposed apartheid and racial discrimination, and both wanted a democratic South Africa with majority rule, but they understood the nation differently. The ANC, through the Congress Alliance and the Freedom Charter, promoted what it called non-racialism, though critics argued that its structure in the 1950s was really multiracial. In practice, the Alliance consisted of separate organisations for different groups, the ANC for Africans, the Indian Congress for Indians, the Coloured People's Congress for coloured people and the Congress of Democrats for whites, working together as partners. The Freedom Charter declared that South Africa belongs to all who live in it, black and white, and that all national groups should have equal rights. The PAC rejected this approach. It argued that organising people as separate racial groups, even in alliance, accepted the idea of separate races, which was the basis of apartheid, and that it gave small minorities the same weight as the African majority in decision-making. The PAC believed that there was only one nation in South Africa, the African nation, and that the land belonged to its indigenous people, who had been dispossessed by conquest. However, it defined an African not by skin colour but by loyalty: anyone who owed loyalty only to Africa and accepted African majority rule could be regarded as an African. Sobukwe stated that the PAC recognised only one race, the human race. Critics argued that, in practice, PAC rhetoric sometimes sounded anti-white and excluded allies; supporters argued that it was simply insisting that the African majority must lead. Today, South Africa's Constitution adopts non-racialism as a founding value, but debates about land, identity and belonging that the PAC raised remain important.

### subtopic_2 — The Split in the ANC
**Assistant:** How did African independence movements inspire the PAC?

**Tutor:** The late 1950s were a time of rapid change across Africa, and these changes had a strong influence on the Africanists who formed the PAC. In March 1957, Ghana, formerly the British colony of the Gold Coast, became independent under Kwame Nkrumah, who was a leading Pan-Africanist. Ghana's independence showed that African people could defeat colonial rule and govern themselves, and Nkrumah declared that Ghana's independence was meaningless unless it was linked to the total liberation of Africa. In April 1958, Nkrumah hosted a conference of independent African states, and in December 1958 he hosted the All-African People's Conference in Accra, which brought together nationalist movements from across the continent, including from countries still under colonial rule. Both South African Africanists and ANC members had links with these developments. At the same time, other countries were moving rapidly towards independence. Guinea became independent from France in 1958, and in 1960 seventeen African countries became independent, including Nigeria, Senegal, Somalia and the Belgian Congo. The PAC's founders saw South Africa as part of this wave of liberation. They adopted the name Pan Africanist Congress to show their commitment to African unity and to a future united states of Africa, and their flag showed a map of Africa with a star over Ghana. Their belief that freedom could be achieved quickly, expressed in the goal of freedom by 1963, was strongly influenced by the speed of decolonisation elsewhere. However, South Africa was different from most colonies, because it had a large, permanently settled white population that controlled a powerful, independent state, so the struggle would prove much longer and harder than the PAC's founders expected.

### subtopic_3 — The Founding of the PAC and Its Aims
**Assistant:** Why did the PAC set the date of its campaign before the ANC's campaign?

**Tutor:** In December 1959, both the ANC and the PAC held conferences at which they decided to launch campaigns against the pass laws. The ANC planned a series of actions, culminating in a national anti-pass day on 31 March 1960, which was meant to be the start of a longer campaign. The PAC then announced that its own campaign would start earlier, on 21 March 1960, and Sobukwe invited the ANC to join it, which the ANC declined, arguing that the PAC's campaign was poorly prepared. Historians suggest several reasons for the PAC's decision to start first. First, the PAC was a new organisation and needed to show that it was more decisive and active than the ANC, which it had criticised for being cautious and slow. Starting first would allow it to take the lead in the struggle against the pass laws, attract members and win support from the African public. Second, the PAC's leaders genuinely believed that mass, determined action could achieve rapid results, in line with their goal of freedom by 1963, and they wanted to seize the moment created by African independence and by anger over the pass laws. Third, competition between the two organisations was real, and each wanted to prove that its strategy was correct. Critics, including some historians and ANC leaders at the time, argued that the PAC acted in haste, that it lacked the organisation to sustain a national campaign, and that its call was supported strongly only in a few areas, such as the Vereeniging area and the Cape Peninsula. Supporters argue that the PAC's boldness forced the issue and brought the injustice of the pass laws to the attention of the world. Both views help to explain the events that followed.

### subtopic_4 — Robert Sobukwe and the PAC's Plans, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why was Robert Sobukwe so widely respected, even by opponents?

**Tutor:** Robert Sobukwe was respected by many people, including some who disagreed with him, because of his intellect, his integrity and his personal dignity. He was a brilliant scholar who had risen from a poor family in Graaff-Reinet to study at Fort Hare and to lecture at the University of the Witwatersrand, which was a remarkable achievement for a black South African at the time. His speeches were thoughtful and eloquent, and his students and followers admired his knowledge of African languages and literature. He was known for his gentle manner and his refusal to hate. Although he insisted that Africans must lead their own liberation, he declared that the PAC recognised only one race, the human race, and he spoke of a future in which a person's colour would be irrelevant. He was also principled and willing to suffer for his beliefs. On 21 March 1960, he led by example, walking to the Orlando police station without his pass to invite arrest. He was sentenced to three years in prison for incitement. When his sentence ended in 1963, Parliament passed a special provision, known as the Sobukwe clause, which allowed the government to keep him imprisoned on Robben Island without a new trial, where he was held alone, in isolation from other prisoners, until 1969. He was then banished to Kimberley under banning orders, where he studied law and practised as a lawyer, until his death from cancer in 1978. The government's decision to pass a special law just to keep one man in prison showed how much it feared his influence. His dignity under these conditions earned him widespread respect, and he is remembered as one of the great leaders of the liberation struggle.
