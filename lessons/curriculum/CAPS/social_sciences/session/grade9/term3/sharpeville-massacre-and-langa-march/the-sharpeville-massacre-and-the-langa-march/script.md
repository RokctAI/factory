# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the Sharpeville massacre and the Langa march of March 1960. It covers the causes of the protests, including the pass laws, economic hardship and the PAC's anti-pass campaign; the events at Sharpeville on 21 March 1960; the events at Langa and the march on Cape Town led by Philip Kgosana on 30 March 1960; and the short-term and longer-term consequences of these events for South Africa and the world. The anchor idea is that on 21 March 1960 the police shot dead sixty-nine unarmed protesters at Sharpeville, and that this massacre was a turning point in South African history. The governing idea is that the government responded to the crisis with greater repression rather than reform, banning the liberation movements, and that this convinced many in the liberation movements that non-violent protest alone could no longer succeed.

## Subtopic: Causes of the Protests

The most immediate cause of the protests was the pass system. Under the pass laws, every African man, and increasingly every African woman, had to carry a reference book at all times. It recorded where they were allowed to live and work. Police could stop anyone at any time and demand to see their pass. People who could not produce a pass, or whose pass was not in order, were arrested, fined or imprisoned, and could be endorsed out of the urban area, which meant being sent away to the reserves. Hundreds of thousands of people were prosecuted under the pass laws every year. Passes were a constant reminder of humiliation and control, and for many people they were the most hated symbol of apartheid.

In Sharpeville, there were additional grievances. Sharpeville was a relatively new township near the industrial town of Vereeniging, built in the 1940s to house African workers. Many residents were unemployed or poorly paid, and rents had been increased. The authorities had tightened influx control, refusing permits to young people who wanted to work in the area and issuing endorsement-out orders. Police raids for passes and for illegal liquor were frequent. In the Western Cape, the government was trying to reduce the number of Africans in the region under the Coloured Labour Preference Policy, which reserved many jobs for coloured workers. Many African men lived in single-sex hostels in Langa and Nyanga, separated from their families, and faced constant pass raids and the threat of being sent away.

The political cause was the anti-pass campaign of the Pan Africanist Congress. As you learnt in the previous lesson, the PAC, led by Robert Sobukwe, called on African men to leave their passes at home on 21 March 1960 and to present themselves at police stations for arrest, under the slogan "No bail, no defence, no fine". The PAC hoped that the jails would overflow and the economy would be disrupted, forcing the government to abolish the pass laws. The PAC was strongest in the Vereeniging area, including Sharpeville and Evaton, and in the Cape Peninsula, so these became the centres of the protest. The ANC had planned its own anti-pass campaign for 31 March and did not join the PAC's call.

So the next question is: what happened at Sharpeville on 21 March 1960?

## Subtopic: The Sharpeville Massacre

On Monday 21 March 1960, PAC activists in Sharpeville had been active since the early hours, going from house to house to urge people to join the protest, and some people were also prevented from going to work. By the morning, a large crowd had gathered at the Sharpeville police station. Estimates of its size vary, from about five thousand to about seven thousand people, with some estimates higher. The crowd included men, women and children. Many people came because they supported the campaign; others came out of curiosity or because they had heard rumours that an important announcement about passes would be made. The local PAC leaders included Nyakane Tsolo, who led the protest in Sharpeville.

During the morning, the crowd was mostly good-humoured. People sang freedom songs and shouted slogans such as "Izwe Lethu", and gave the thumbs-up salute. Some carried sticks. Police reinforcements arrived during the morning, bringing the number of police at the station to about three hundred, with five Saracen armoured cars. Military jet aircraft flew low over the crowd, apparently to frighten it into dispersing, but the crowd did not leave. Pressed against the low wire fence around the station, the crowd waited for a statement.

Shortly after one o'clock in the afternoon, there was a scuffle near the gate as police arrested some people, and the crowd surged forward, possibly to see what was happening. Some police later claimed that stones had been thrown at them. Without any clear order to fire, and without any warning to the crowd to disperse, police officers opened fire with rifles and submachine guns. The firing continued for about forty seconds. As people turned and ran, many were shot in the back.

When the firing stopped, sixty-nine people lay dead, including eight women and ten children, and about 180 were wounded, among them many women and children. Many of the dead and wounded had been shot in the back. The police claimed that they had acted in self-defence against a violent mob, but photographs and the evidence of witnesses, including journalists from Drum magazine such as Ian Berry, showed people fleeing. A commission of inquiry was held, but no police officer was ever prosecuted.

Elsewhere on 21 March, the response to the PAC's call was mixed. In Evaton and Vanderbijlpark, nearby crowds gathered and were dispersed, and at Vanderbijlpark police also fired, killing at least one person. In Orlando in Soweto, Robert Sobukwe and a small group of leaders walked to the police station without their passes and were arrested, but the response in most of Johannesburg was small. In much of the country there was little protest at all that day, which supports the view that the PAC was well organised only in certain areas. It was the events at Sharpeville and in the Cape Peninsula that turned the campaign into a national crisis.

So the next question is: what happened at Langa and in Cape Town?

## Subtopic: Langa and the March on Cape Town

On the same day, 21 March 1960, thousands of people gathered in Langa, a township near Cape Town, in response to the PAC's call. In the evening, police ordered a meeting of about six thousand people to disperse and then baton-charged the crowd and opened fire. At least two people were killed and others injured. Rioting followed, and buildings were set on fire. In the days that followed, many African workers in Langa and Nyanga stayed away from work in a strike that brought much of the Cape Town economy to a halt.

The leader of the PAC in the area was Philip Kgosana, a twenty-three-year-old student. On 25 March, he led about two thousand people to the police headquarters at Caledon Square in Cape Town. On 30 March, after the government had declared a state of emergency and police had raided Langa and Nyanga and beaten workers to force them back to their jobs, about thirty thousand people marched from Langa to the centre of Cape Town, heading for Caledon Square. The march was peaceful and orderly.

At Caledon Square, Kgosana met the senior police officer, Colonel Ignatius Terblanche. Kgosana demanded the release of arrested PAC leaders and a meeting with the Minister of Justice, F.C. Erasmus. Terblanche promised to arrange a meeting with the Minister if Kgosana sent the crowd home. Kgosana agreed and asked the marchers to return peacefully to Langa, which they did. When Kgosana returned later that day for the promised meeting, however, he was arrested. The authorities then surrounded Langa and Nyanga with police and soldiers and forced the strike to an end.

The Langa march showed the power of organised, disciplined mass action, with tens of thousands of people marching into the heart of a city without violence. It also showed how the government was prepared to break promises to restore control.

The Cape Town events also had a lasting influence on how people remember the crisis. For many white South Africans in Cape Town, the sight of tens of thousands of disciplined African marchers in the city centre was a shock that revealed how much the economy and society depended on people who had no political rights.

So the final question is: what were the consequences of Sharpeville and Langa?

## Subtopic: Short- and Longer-Term Consequences, and the Error Museum

In the short term, the massacre provoked anger and protest across South Africa. On 26 March, Chief Albert Luthuli publicly burned his pass and called on others to do the same, and the ANC called for a national day of mourning on 28 March, when hundreds of thousands of workers stayed away. The government temporarily suspended the pass laws on 26 March, which seemed briefly to be a victory, but it reintroduced them in early April. On 30 March, the government declared a state of emergency, which gave the police wide powers to arrest and detain people without trial; more than 11 000 people were detained, many in brutal conditions. On 8 April, Parliament passed the Unlawful Organisations Act, and the ANC and PAC were banned. They would remain banned for thirty years, until February 1990.

There were also economic and international effects. Foreign investors were alarmed, and money flowed out of the country; the stock market fell sharply, though the economy recovered in the following years. On 1 April 1960, the United Nations Security Council passed its first resolution on South Africa, Resolution 134, which blamed the government's policies for the shootings and called on it to abandon apartheid. Newspapers around the world published photographs of the dead and wounded, and Sharpeville became a symbol of apartheid's brutality. On 9 April 1960, a white farmer, David Pratt, shot and wounded Prime Minister Verwoerd at an agricultural show; Verwoerd survived, and Pratt was found to be mentally ill.

In the longer term, Sharpeville was a turning point. With their organisations banned and peaceful protest met with bullets, leaders of both the ANC and the PAC concluded that non-violent methods alone could no longer succeed. In 1961, the ANC and its allies formed Umkhonto we Sizwe, meaning Spear of the Nation, led by Nelson Mandela, which began a campaign of sabotage on 16 December 1961, aiming at government property rather than people. The PAC's armed wing, Poqo, which means "alone" or "pure", carried out attacks in 1962 and 1963, some of which targeted people. Many leaders went into exile to build the movements abroad, including Oliver Tambo, who left in 1960. Robert Sobukwe was sentenced to three years in prison and then held on Robben Island under the Sobukwe clause until 1969. The government became even more repressive, passing laws allowing detention without trial. In 1961, South Africa became a republic and left the Commonwealth, partly because of criticism of apartheid from other Commonwealth countries.

Sharpeville is remembered today in several ways. In 1966, the United Nations declared 21 March the International Day for the Elimination of Racial Discrimination. In South Africa, 21 March is Human Rights Day. On 10 December 1996, President Nelson Mandela chose Sharpeville as the place to sign South Africa's new Constitution into law.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "The protesters at Sharpeville attacked the police with weapons." The crowd was unarmed apart from some sticks, and many victims were shot in the back as they fled.

Exhibit two: "The ANC organised the Sharpeville protest." The protest was part of the PAC's anti-pass campaign; the ANC had planned a separate campaign for 31 March.

Exhibit three: "The government abolished the pass laws after Sharpeville." It suspended them briefly, then reintroduced them and banned the ANC and PAC.

Exhibit four: "Sharpeville had no effect outside South Africa." It led to the first UN Security Council resolution on South Africa and international condemnation.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a book left at home, forty seconds of gunfire, and a long walk into the city.

## Subtopic: A Book Left at Home

Every African man had to carry a pass book. If the police stopped you and you did not have it, you could be arrested, fined or sent away from the town where you lived and worked. People hated the pass book more than almost anything else about apartheid.

In Sharpeville, a township near Vereeniging, life was hard. Many people had no jobs, rents had gone up and police raids were common. In Cape Town, African men lived in crowded hostels far from their families, always afraid of being sent away.

The PAC, led by Robert Sobukwe, had a plan. On 21 March 1960, men would leave their pass books at home, walk to the police station and say: arrest us. No bail, no defence, no fine. If the jails filled up, the pass laws would collapse.

So what happened that day?

## Subtopic: Forty Seconds of Gunfire

On the morning of 21 March 1960, thousands of people gathered outside the police station in Sharpeville. They sang songs and waited. Some had come to protest; some had come to hear an announcement; some were just curious. There were women and children in the crowd.

More police arrived, with armoured cars. Fighter jets roared low overhead to scare the people away, but they stayed.

Just after one o'clock, there was pushing near the gate. Suddenly, without any warning, the police opened fire. The shooting lasted about forty seconds. People turned and ran, and many were shot in the back.

Sixty-nine people were killed, and about 180 were wounded. No police officer was ever punished.

Photographs of the dead went around the world.

So what happened in Cape Town?

## Subtopic: A Long Walk Into the City

In Langa, near Cape Town, police also shot at protesters on 21 March, killing at least two. Workers stopped going to work.

On 30 March, a young student named Philip Kgosana led about thirty thousand people on a peaceful march into the centre of Cape Town. A senior police officer promised that Kgosana could meet the Minister of Justice if he sent the crowd home. Kgosana agreed, and the marchers went home quietly. But when he came back for the meeting, he was arrested.

That same day, the government declared a state of emergency. More than 11 000 people were locked up. Soon, the ANC and PAC were banned. Their leaders decided that peaceful protest was no longer enough, and they began an armed struggle.

Today, 21 March is Human Rights Day in South Africa.

Here is the line to remember: at Sharpeville on 21 March 1960, police killed sixty-nine unarmed people protesting against the pass laws, and the government's response of banning and repression turned the struggle towards armed resistance.
