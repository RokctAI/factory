### subtopic_1 — Crop Farming and Bare Soil
**Assistant:** Why is the start of the rainy season such a dangerous time for ploughed fields?

**Tutor:** In South Africa's summer-rainfall regions, which cover most of the country except the Western Cape and parts of the Northern Cape, the rainy season begins in spring and early summer, from about September to November. Farmers usually plough and prepare their fields just before or as the rains start, so that seeds can be planted into moist soil. This means that the fields are at their most exposed exactly when the first rains arrive. The soil has just been loosened by ploughing, its structure has been broken up, and there are no crop leaves or roots yet to protect it. To make matters worse, the early-season rain in much of the interior often falls as thunderstorms, which produce short bursts of very intense rain with large raindrops. Large drops hit the bare soil with great force, causing splash erosion, and the rain falls faster than the soil can absorb it, so a lot of runoff is produced. The runoff carries away loose topsoil as sheet erosion and cuts rills, especially where furrows run downhill. A single storm at this time can remove more soil than many weeks of gentle rain later in the season, when the crop has grown and covers the ground. This is one reason why conservation agriculture is so useful: if farmers do not plough, and instead leave the remains of the previous crop on the surface, the soil is protected from those first heavy storms. It also explains why contour ploughing and contour banks are so important on sloping land, because they slow down and hold back the runoff during the most dangerous part of the farming year.

### subtopic_2 — Livestock, Overgrazing and South African Examples
**Assistant:** What does carrying capacity mean for a cattle farmer?

**Tutor:** Carrying capacity is the number of animals that an area of land can support for a long time without the vegetation and soil being damaged. It depends mainly on how much grass and other edible plants the land produces, which depends on rainfall, soil type and the kinds of plants growing there. In a high-rainfall grassland in KwaZulu-Natal, a hectare might support far more animals than a hectare in the dry Karoo, where farmers may need many hectares for each sheep or cow. For a farmer, the carrying capacity sets a limit on how many animals to keep. If they keep more animals than the land can support, which is called overstocking, the animals eat the grass faster than it can regrow. The best grasses are eaten first and may die out, bare patches appear, the soil is trampled and compacted, and erosion begins. In the short term, the farmer may earn more from extra animals, but in the long term the land produces less grass and the farm can support fewer animals than before. Carrying capacity also changes with the weather. In a drought, the land produces much less grass, so it can support fewer animals. A wise farmer therefore reduces the number of animals early in a drought, by selling some, rather than waiting until the land is badly damaged and the animals are starving. Farmers also improve carrying capacity by rotational grazing, which gives the grass time to recover between grazing periods, and by spreading water points across the farm. In communal areas, managing carrying capacity is harder, because many families share the same land and no single person can control the total number of animals.

### subtopic_3 — The Dust Bowl of the 1930s
**Assistant:** Could something like the Dust Bowl happen in South Africa?

**Tutor:** South Africa has many of the conditions that contributed to the Dust Bowl. Large parts of the country are semi-arid, droughts are common and can last for several years, and strong winds blow across the interior plateau, especially in late winter and spring. In the dry western and central parts of the country, such as the Northern Cape, North West and western Free State, sandy soils are easily eroded by wind when they are left bare. South Africa has in fact experienced serious wind erosion and dust storms during droughts, for example during the severe drought of the early 1930s, which happened at the same time as the American Dust Bowl, and more recently during dry years when maize fields on sandy soils in the western maize belt were left bare. However, several things make a disaster on the scale of the Dust Bowl less likely today. South African farmers, agricultural scientists and government departments learnt from the American experience and from local droughts, and laws such as the Conservation of Agricultural Resources Act of 1983 restrict the ploughing of land that is unsuitable for crops and require farmers to protect soil and vegetation. Many commercial farmers in the drier maize-growing areas have adopted conservation agriculture, keeping crop residues on the surface, and plant windbreaks. Climate change is a concern, because scientists project that parts of South Africa will become hotter and drier, with more intense droughts. So the risk has not disappeared, and it will only stay low if farmers, communities and government continue to treat the soil as a precious, limited resource.

### subtopic_4 — Soil Conservation Methods, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why did betterment schemes cause anger if they aimed to stop erosion?

**Tutor:** Betterment schemes were government programmes, introduced from the 1930s and expanded under apartheid in the 1950s and 1960s, that aimed to reduce soil erosion and improve farming in the reserves, later called homelands. Their aims sounded reasonable: they tried to reduce overgrazing by limiting the number of cattle, to separate land into residential areas, fields and grazing camps, and to stop cultivation of steep slopes. The way they were carried out, however, caused great anger, for several reasons. First, they treated the symptoms rather than the cause. The real cause of overcrowding and erosion was that the black majority had been confined to about 13 percent of the land by the Land Acts, and the schemes did nothing to give people more land. Second, they were imposed by officials without the consent of the communities affected. People were forced to move their homes into planned villages, sometimes losing good fields or gardens, and to sell or slaughter cattle. Cattle were not just a source of income; they were a store of wealth, a form of security and central to customs such as lobola and ceremonies, so forced culling was deeply resented. Third, the schemes were linked to apartheid control, including the authority of chiefs appointed by the government, so many people saw them as another form of oppression. Resistance to betterment and related policies contributed to rural uprisings, such as those in Sekhukhuneland in 1958 and in Pondoland in 1960. This history shows that conservation works best when communities are involved in decisions and when the underlying causes, such as unfair access to land, are addressed.
