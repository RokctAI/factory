### subtopic_1 — Soil, Topsoil and Natural Erosion
**Assistant:** Why is topsoil described as a non-renewable resource if soil forms naturally?

**Tutor:** Soil does form naturally, so in theory it is renewable, but the problem is the time it takes. Soil forms when rock is slowly broken down by physical, chemical and biological weathering, and when the resulting mineral particles are mixed with humus from decaying plants and animals. This mixing is done by plant roots, earthworms, insects, fungi and bacteria, and it depends on the climate, the type of rock, the slope of the land and the plants growing there. Under most conditions, all of this happens very slowly. Soil scientists estimate that it can take hundreds of years, and in dry or cold places even longer, for a single centimetre of topsoil to form. A human lifetime is about seventy or eighty years, so a farmer who loses ten centimetres of topsoil to erosion will never see it replaced, and neither will their children or grandchildren. In contrast, accelerated erosion can remove a centimetre of soil from bare ground in just a few heavy storms. When something is used up or lost much faster than it can be replaced, we treat it as non-renewable for practical purposes, in the same way that we treat coal and oil as non-renewable even though they also formed naturally over millions of years. This is why soil conservation is so important. It is far cheaper and more effective to protect the topsoil we have, by keeping plant cover on the land, than to try to restore land after the topsoil has gone. Once a field has lost its topsoil, adding fertiliser can replace some nutrients, but it cannot replace the structure, organic matter and living organisms that make soil truly fertile.

### subtopic_2 — Types of Soil Erosion by Water and Wind
**Assistant:** How does a small rill turn into a deep donga?

**Tutor:** A rill turns into a donga through a process that feeds on itself. It starts when runoff on bare soil collects in a small channel a few centimetres deep. Because the water is now concentrated in a narrow channel instead of spread out as a thin sheet, it is deeper and flows faster, so it has more energy to erode. It cuts the channel deeper and wider, and the bigger channel then collects even more water from the surrounding slope in the next storm. Several rills may join together, adding their water. Once the channel cuts through the topsoil into softer subsoil, erosion can speed up even more, because many subsoils, especially in parts of KwaZulu-Natal and the Eastern Cape, are dispersive, which means they fall apart easily when wet. At the upper end of the gully, called the head, water pours over the edge like a small waterfall and undercuts it, so the head moves back up the slope with every storm. This is called headward erosion. The sides of the gully collapse as they are undercut, making it wider. Within a few years, a channel that could have been ploughed over can become a donga several metres deep. Once this happens, it is very difficult and expensive to repair. Farmers and conservation workers try to stop dongas by diverting water away from the head, filling them with brushwood, building small walls of stone in wire baskets called gabions across them to slow the water and trap sediment, and planting grass. The best approach is to stop rills early, before they become dongas, by keeping vegetation on the land.

### subtopic_3 — Agriculture, Construction and Mining
**Assistant:** Why does building a town increase erosion even after construction is finished?

**Tutor:** It seems surprising, because once a town is built the soil is covered by roofs, roads and paving, so it cannot be eroded there. The problem is what happens to the water. In a natural grassland, a large share of the rain soaks into the soil, a process called infiltration, and the rest flows slowly over the surface, slowed down by plants. When the land is covered by impermeable surfaces, such as roofs, tar and concrete, almost no water can soak in. Instead, nearly all of the rain becomes runoff, and it flows quickly along smooth gutters, streets and stormwater pipes. This means that much more water reaches streams and rivers, and it reaches them much faster, causing sudden peaks in flow during storms. Where stormwater pipes empty into a stream or onto open ground, the fast, concentrated water erodes the stream banks and cuts gullies. Streams in towns often become deeper and wider, their banks collapse, and sediment is carried downstream. This is why planners now try to manage stormwater more carefully, by leaving open green spaces, building retention ponds that hold water and release it slowly, using permeable paving that lets water soak through, and protecting stream banks with vegetation or gabions. In informal settlements, where houses are often built quickly on steep slopes without proper drainage, runoff from roofs and paths can quickly create gullies between houses, which can even threaten homes. So construction affects erosion both during building, by exposing soil, and afterwards, by changing how water moves across the land.

### subtopic_4 — Effects of Soil Erosion, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Were the eroded homelands simply the result of bad farming?

**Tutor:** No, and this is an important example of why history matters in Geography. During the twentieth century, many officials and writers blamed erosion in the reserves, later called homelands, on African farming methods, claiming that people kept too many cattle and did not care for the land. This explanation ignored the main cause. The Natives Land Act of 1913 restricted black South Africans to owning land in reserves that made up about 7 percent of the country, and the Native Trust and Land Act of 1936 increased this to about 13 percent, while black South Africans made up the large majority of the population. Later, apartheid forced removals moved even more people into these areas. As a result, the homelands became severely overcrowded. Families had small plots and had to graze their cattle on shared land, so the land was used much more intensively than it could sustain. People often had to cut trees for firewood because they had no electricity. Many men were away working as migrant labourers on the mines and in the cities, leaving fewer people to farm and care for the land. In these conditions, overgrazing, deforestation and erosion were almost unavoidable. Government schemes to control erosion, such as forcing people to reduce cattle numbers or move into planned villages, were often imposed without consultation and caused great anger and resistance. So the erosion was real, but its root cause was land dispossession and overcrowding. It is also true that erosion affected some white-owned commercial farms, especially in the Karoo and Free State, through overgrazing and poor ploughing. A balanced answer recognises both the physical processes and the political history behind them.
