# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a case study of agriculture as a contributor to soil erosion. It covers how crop farming exposes soil to erosion; how livestock farming and overgrazing damage the land, with examples from South Africa; the Dust Bowl of the 1930s in the United States as an example of what happens when farming ignores the limits of the land; and the soil conservation methods that farmers use to reduce erosion. The anchor idea is that agriculture covers more land than any other human activity, so how farmers treat the soil decides the fate of most of the world's topsoil. The governing idea is that farming does not have to cause erosion: when farmers keep the soil covered, slow down runoff and match the number of animals to the grass available, they can produce food and protect the soil at the same time.

## Subtopic: Crop Farming and Bare Soil

Crop farming begins by clearing the natural vegetation, such as grassland, bush or forest, that has protected the soil for thousands of years. The cleared land is then ploughed. Ploughing turns over the topsoil, buries weeds and makes a loose seedbed for planting. However, it also breaks up the soil structure, which is the way soil particles are clumped together, and leaves the surface bare and loose. Until the new crop has grown enough leaves to cover the ground, rain falls directly on the soil and wind blows across it.

There are several times in the farming year when soil is especially exposed. After ploughing and before planting, the field may be completely bare. After harvest, if the crop remains are removed, burnt or eaten by animals, the soil is bare again. In South Africa's summer-rainfall areas, the first heavy thunderstorms of spring often fall when fields have just been ploughed, which is exactly when they can do the most damage.

Several farming practices make erosion worse. Ploughing up and down a slope makes furrows that act as channels, carrying runoff straight downhill and cutting rills. Planting the same crop on the same land year after year, called monoculture, can reduce the organic matter in the soil and weaken its structure, so it erodes more easily. Ploughing very steep land, or land with thin, sandy or easily eroded soils, exposes soil that should never have been cultivated. Removing hedges, windbreaks and strips of natural vegetation between fields to make larger fields for big machines allows wind to sweep across the land. Heavy machinery also compacts the soil, so less water can soak in and more runs off.

Irrigation can also cause problems if it is badly managed. Too much water applied too quickly can wash soil away, and in dry areas, poor irrigation can also make soils salty, which kills plants and leaves soil bare.

So the next question is: how does livestock farming affect the soil?

## Subtopic: Livestock, Overgrazing and South African Examples

About 80 percent of South Africa's agricultural land is used mainly for grazing, because much of the country is too dry or too steep for crops. Livestock farming, with cattle, sheep and goats, is therefore very important. Well-managed grazing can keep grassland healthy. The problem is overgrazing.

Overgrazing happens when there are more animals on an area of land than the vegetation can support, which is called exceeding the carrying capacity, or when animals graze the same area for too long without giving the plants time to recover. The animals eat the grass down to the ground and eat the most nutritious plants first, so these disappear and are replaced by less useful plants or by bare ground. Their hooves trample and compact the soil, so rain runs off instead of soaking in. Paths worn by animals walking to and from water points become channels for runoff, then rills and eventually dongas. Overgrazing is especially damaging during droughts, when plants are already stressed and farmers may be reluctant to sell their animals.

The Karoo, a vast semi-desert in the interior of South Africa, is one example. During the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, farmers kept very large numbers of sheep and goats there, and many areas were heavily overgrazed. Palatable grasses and shrubs were replaced by less useful plants, soil was lost to wind and water, and dams filled with silt. Since then, farmers have generally reduced stock numbers and improved grazing management, and parts of the Karoo have partly recovered.

The former homelands are another example. As you learnt in the previous lesson, the Natives Land Act of 1913 and the Native Trust and Land Act of 1936 confined the black majority to about 13 percent of the land. Overcrowding forced people to graze many animals on small areas of communal land and to cultivate steep slopes, and cattle were also an important form of wealth and security, used for lobola, ceremonies and savings. The result in many areas, such as parts of the former Transkei, Ciskei and KwaZulu, was severe overgrazing and some of the worst gully erosion in the country. Government betterment schemes from the 1930s to the 1960s tried to control erosion by culling cattle and reorganising land, but because they were imposed without consent, they caused resentment and resistance in rural areas.

Livestock farming is not only a problem, however. Grazing animals have always been part of South African grasslands, which evolved with large herds of wild antelope, buffalo and zebra that moved across the land. Carefully managed grazing, with animals moved regularly and given enough land, can keep grassland healthy by removing old, dry grass and spreading seeds and manure. The damage comes from too many animals kept in one place for too long, not from grazing itself.

So the next question is: what happens when erosion gets completely out of control?

## Subtopic: The Dust Bowl of the 1930s

The most famous example of agriculture causing soil erosion is the Dust Bowl in the southern Great Plains of the United States, in parts of Kansas, Oklahoma, Texas, Colorado and New Mexico.

The Great Plains were naturally covered by deep-rooted prairie grasses, which held the soil in place through regular droughts. From the late nineteenth century, settlers ploughed up the grassland to grow wheat. During the First World War, wheat prices were high, and in the 1920s new tractors made it possible to plough enormous areas quickly. Millions of hectares of grassland were ploughed, and when wheat prices fell, farmers ploughed even more land to try to earn the same income.

Then, from about 1930, a series of severe droughts struck the region. Crops failed, leaving the ploughed fields bare and dry. Strong winds, which are common on the plains, lifted the loose, dry topsoil into the air in enormous dust storms, known as black blizzards. On 14 April 1935, known as Black Sunday, one of the worst storms swept across the plains, turning day into darkness. Dust from the plains fell on cities along the east coast, more than two thousand kilometres away.

The effects were devastating. Huge amounts of topsoil were lost. Farms failed and families lost their land, made worse by the Great Depression that you studied in Term 1. Many people suffered from dust pneumonia, a lung disease caused by breathing in dust. Hundreds of thousands of people left the region, many travelling west to California in search of work.

The United States government responded by creating the Soil Conservation Service in 1935, which taught farmers methods such as contour ploughing, crop rotation and strip farming, and paid them to use these methods. Hundreds of millions of trees were planted as windbreaks across the plains. When rain returned at the end of the 1930s, these changes helped the land to recover. The Dust Bowl showed the world that farming that ignores the limits of the land can destroy it, and that good soil conservation can help to restore it.

It is worth noticing why the farmers of the plains acted as they did. Most were not careless. They were responding to the incentives around them: governments encouraged settlement, banks lent money for tractors and land, and when prices fell, ploughing more land seemed the only way to pay debts. A few wet years in the early twentieth century had also convinced many people that the plains were wetter than they really were. The disaster shows that soil erosion often has economic and political causes as well as physical ones, which is also true of the South African examples.

So the final question is: how can farmers protect their soil?

## Subtopic: Soil Conservation Methods, and the Error Museum

Soil conservation means protecting soil from erosion and keeping it fertile. Most methods do one or more of three things: they keep the soil covered, they slow down runoff, or they reduce the force of the wind.

On sloping cropland, contour ploughing means ploughing across the slope, following the contour lines, instead of up and down. Each furrow then acts as a small barrier that traps water and lets it soak in. On steeper slopes, farmers may build terraces, which are flat steps cut into the hillside, or contour banks, which are low ridges of earth along the contours that divert runoff safely off the field. Strip cropping means planting strips of different crops across the slope, so that a strip of dense grass or a cover crop catches soil washed from a strip of row crops above it.

Conservation agriculture, sometimes called no-till farming, is a modern approach that is spreading in South Africa. Instead of ploughing, farmers plant seeds directly into the remains of the previous crop, keep the soil permanently covered with crop residues or cover crops, and rotate different crops. This protects the soil from rain and wind, increases organic matter, and helps the soil hold more water.

Against wind erosion, farmers plant windbreaks, which are rows of trees or shrubs along the edges of fields, and leave crop stubble standing after harvest.

For grazing land, the key is to keep the number of animals within the carrying capacity of the land, to use rotational grazing, in which animals are moved between camps so that grass has time to recover, and to spread water points so that animals do not trample the same areas. Damaged areas can be fenced off and replanted.

Where gullies have formed, farmers can divert water away from the head, build small stone walls or gabions across them to slow the water and trap soil, and plant grass.

Many of these methods also save farmers money. Conservation agriculture, for example, reduces the fuel, labour and machinery needed for ploughing, and soils that hold more water help crops survive dry spells. This is why a growing number of grain farmers in the Western Cape, KwaZulu-Natal and the Free State have adopted it.

In South Africa, the Conservation of Agricultural Resources Act of 1983 sets rules to protect soil and vegetation, and the LandCare programme, started in the late 1990s, supports communities and farmers in conservation projects.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Farming always destroys soil." Farming can protect soil when farmers keep it covered, plough along the contours and avoid overgrazing.

Exhibit two: "The Dust Bowl was caused only by drought." Drought struck land that had been ploughed and left bare; the combination caused the disaster.

Exhibit three: "Ploughing up and down a slope is best because water drains away." Furrows down a slope channel runoff and cut rills; contour ploughing slows water down.

Exhibit four: "Erosion in the homelands was caused simply by poor farmers." Overcrowding caused by land dispossession forced people onto too little land.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a field with no coat, a black blizzard and a staircase on a hill.

## Subtopic: A Field With No Coat

To grow crops, farmers clear away the grass and plough the soil. Ploughing makes the soil soft and loose, which is good for planting seeds. But it also leaves the field with no coat. Until the crop grows, the soil lies bare under the sun, the wind and the rain.

If a big storm comes just after ploughing, the rain splashes the loose soil and washes it away. If the farmer ploughed straight up and down the hill, each furrow becomes a little river carrying soil downhill.

Animals can also take the coat off the land. If a farmer keeps too many cattle, sheep or goats on a piece of land, they eat the grass down to nothing and their hooves stamp the ground hard. Rain runs straight off, cutting paths into rills and dongas.

In South Africa, unfair laws once squeezed millions of people and their cattle onto small pieces of land. There was simply not enough grass, and the land eroded badly.

So what is the worst that can happen?

## Subtopic: A Black Blizzard

About ninety years ago, farmers in the middle of the United States ploughed up huge grasslands to grow wheat. The tough wild grass that had held the soil for thousands of years was gone.

Then the rain stopped for years. The wheat died. The ploughed fields dried into powder. Strong winds lifted the soil into the sky in giant dust storms, so thick that day turned into night. People called them black blizzards. On one terrible day in April 1935, called Black Sunday, a huge dust cloud swept across the land.

Families lost their farms. People got sick from breathing in dust. Hundreds of thousands moved away to find work.

The place became known as the Dust Bowl. It taught the world a hard lesson: if you take away the plants that hold the soil, the wind and rain will take the soil.

So how can farmers keep their soil safe?

## Subtopic: A Staircase on a Hill

Farmers have clever ways to protect their soil.

On a slope, they plough across the hill instead of up and down. Each furrow becomes a little wall that catches water. On steep hills, they cut flat steps into the hillside, like a giant staircase, called terraces.

Many farmers now hardly plough at all. They plant seeds straight into the old stalks of last season's crop, so the soil always has a coat.

They plant rows of trees as windbreaks to slow down the wind, so it cannot pick up the soil from open fields. On bare patches and in dongas, they plant grass and build little stone walls to catch the soil.

Cattle farmers keep only as many animals as the grass can feed, and move them from one camp to another, so the grass can grow back.

Here is the line to remember: farming can strip soil away, as the Dust Bowl showed, but farmers who keep the soil covered and slow down water and wind can grow food and protect the land at the same time.
