# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains how human activities contribute to soil erosion. It covers what soil is, why topsoil is so valuable and how soil erosion happens naturally; the types of soil erosion by water and wind, including sheet, rill and gully erosion; how agriculture, construction and mining expose soil and speed up erosion; and the effects of soil erosion on farming, rivers, dams and people, especially in South Africa. The anchor idea is that soil erosion is the removal of soil by water and wind faster than new soil can form. The governing idea is that vegetation protects soil, so whenever people remove plant cover and leave the ground bare, compacted or loose, erosion speeds up, sometimes many times over.

## Subtopic: Soil, Topsoil and Natural Erosion

Soil is the thin, loose layer of material that covers much of the earth's land surface. It is made of tiny particles of weathered rock, such as sand, silt and clay, mixed with humus, which is decayed plant and animal material, together with water, air and countless living organisms, such as earthworms, insects, fungi and bacteria. In the previous lessons, you learnt that weathering breaks rock down into smaller particles. Soil forms when these particles are mixed with organic matter over a very long time.

Soil forms in layers, called horizons. The top layer, the topsoil, is usually dark because it contains the most humus. It is the most fertile layer, rich in nutrients, and it is where most plant roots grow and most soil organisms live. Below it is the subsoil, which is lighter in colour, contains less humus and is less fertile. Below that lies weathered rock and then solid bedrock. Topsoil is usually only a few centimetres to a few tens of centimetres thick, and soil scientists estimate that a single centimetre of topsoil can take hundreds of years to form. For human purposes, therefore, soil is a non-renewable resource: once it is lost, it will not come back in our lifetimes.

Soil erosion is the removal of soil, especially topsoil, from one place by agents such as running water and wind, which carry it somewhere else. Like the erosion of rock you studied earlier, soil erosion is a natural process. Rain, rivers and wind have always moved some soil. Under natural conditions, however, the land is usually covered by grass, bushes or forest. Plants protect soil in several ways: leaves break the force of falling raindrops, roots bind the soil particles together, fallen leaves and stems cover the surface, and vegetation slows down water running over the ground and wind blowing across it. Under natural plant cover, soil is usually lost about as slowly as new soil forms, so the soil layer stays roughly the same thickness.

The problem begins when this balance is upset. Accelerated soil erosion is soil erosion that has been speeded up by human activities, so that soil is lost much faster than it can form. This is the kind of erosion that this lesson is about.

So the next question is: how exactly does water and wind erosion remove soil?

## Subtopic: Types of Soil Erosion by Water and Wind

Water erosion begins with a single raindrop. When a heavy raindrop hits bare soil, it strikes with surprising force and splashes soil particles into the air. This is called splash erosion. Loose particles are then easily carried away by water flowing over the surface.

When rain falls faster than the soil can soak it up, the extra water flows over the surface as runoff. Where runoff flows as a thin, even layer over a gentle slope, it removes a thin layer of topsoil evenly from a wide area. This is sheet erosion. It is dangerous because it is hard to see: a farmer may lose several millimetres of the most fertile soil each year without noticing, until stones and the paler subsoil begin to show.

On steeper or uneven ground, runoff gathers into many small channels, a few centimetres deep, called rills. This is rill erosion. Rills can usually be removed by ploughing, but if they are left alone, the water in them flows faster and cuts deeper.

When rills join and deepen into large channels that are too big to plough over, the result is gully erosion. Gullies can be metres or even tens of metres deep and can grow quickly in every storm, eating back into the land at their heads. In South Africa, these gullies are called dongas, a word taken from isiZulu. Dongas are common in parts of KwaZulu-Natal, the Eastern Cape, Limpopo and the Free State, especially on soils that erode easily. Once a donga forms, the land around it can no longer be farmed, and roads, fences and even houses may be undermined.

Wind erosion happens mainly in dry areas, where the soil is loose, dry and fine and there is little vegetation. Strong winds lift fine particles into the air, sometimes forming dust storms, and roll larger grains along the ground. The finest and most fertile particles, the silt, clay and humus, are carried furthest, so wind erosion leaves behind poorer, sandier soil. In South Africa, wind erosion affects dry, sandy areas such as parts of the Northern Cape, the North West and the western Free State, especially during droughts.

The amount of erosion depends on several factors: the intensity of the rainfall or the strength of the wind, the steepness and length of the slope, the type of soil, and, most importantly, how much plant cover protects the soil. People cannot easily change the rain, the wind or the slope, but they constantly change the plant cover.

So the next question is: which human activities make soil erosion worse?

## Subtopic: Agriculture, Construction and Mining

Agriculture is the human activity that affects the largest area of land, so it is the biggest human contributor to soil erosion worldwide. Clearing natural vegetation for fields removes the protective cover. Ploughing loosens the soil and leaves it bare, especially between harvesting and the time the next crop grows. Ploughing up and down slopes creates furrows that channel runoff downhill. Overgrazing, when too many cattle, sheep or goats graze an area for too long, removes the grass and tramples and compacts the soil, so that rain runs off instead of soaking in. Paths made by animals walking to water become rills and then dongas. Burning grassland too often also leaves the ground bare. Because agriculture is so important, the next lesson examines it as a case study.

Construction also contributes to erosion. When roads, houses, shopping centres and factories are built, bulldozers clear all vegetation and move large amounts of soil. During construction, the bare, loose soil is easily washed away by rain, and building sites can lose soil many times faster than farmland. Once construction is complete, roofs, tarred roads and paved areas are impermeable, which means water cannot soak into them. More rain therefore runs off, faster and in greater volumes, into stormwater drains and streams, causing erosion of stream banks and gullies where the water is released. Roads built along slopes cut through hillsides and can trigger gullies where culverts release water. Informal settlements built quickly on steep slopes without proper drainage, a common situation in many South African towns and cities, are especially vulnerable.

Mining is the third major contributor. Open-cast mining, used for example for coal on the Mpumalanga Highveld and for iron ore and manganese in the Northern Cape, removes all vegetation and soil over large areas to reach the minerals below. The removed soil and rock are piled up in heaps, and mines produce huge dumps of waste rock and tailings, the fine waste left after the minerals have been extracted. These dumps are often bare and steep, so water carves gullies into them and wind blows dust from them. The pale gold mine dumps of the Witwatersrand have for decades produced clouds of fine dust that blow over nearby suburbs and townships on windy days, causing health problems as well as erosion. Haul roads used by heavy trucks also expose and compact the soil.

Other human activities add to the problem: cutting down trees for firewood and timber, which is called deforestation; footpaths and off-road vehicles; and poorly planned forestry plantations. In each case, the pattern is the same: people remove the vegetation, expose the soil and change how water flows over the land.

So the final question is: why does soil erosion matter so much?

## Subtopic: Effects of Soil Erosion, and the Error Museum

The first effect is on farming. When topsoil is lost, the land loses its nutrients and organic matter and its ability to hold water, so crops grow less well. Farmers must use more fertiliser to grow the same amount of food, which costs money. Eventually, badly eroded land with deep dongas may have to be abandoned altogether. Over time this threatens food security, which you will study in Term 4.

The second effect is on rivers and dams. Eroded soil does not disappear; it is washed into streams and rivers as sediment. This is called siltation. Muddy water blocks sunlight and harms fish and other river life. Sediment fills up dams, reducing the amount of water they can store. In South Africa, which is a dry country that depends heavily on dams, this is a serious problem. The Welbedacht Dam on the Caledon River, completed in 1973, lost more than half of its storage capacity to silt within about two decades. Silt also makes water more expensive to purify, and it can block irrigation canals and harbours.

The third effect is on people and the environment more widely. Dust storms reduce visibility, damage crops and cause breathing problems. Gullies undermine roads, bridges and houses. Loss of soil and vegetation reduces biodiversity. Eroded land stores less carbon. In the long run, poor and rural communities who depend directly on the land are hit hardest.

In South Africa, soil erosion also has a history. Laws such as the Natives Land Act of 1913 and the Native Trust and Land Act of 1936 restricted black South Africans to a small share of the country's land, about 13 percent by the end of this process. These reserves, later called homelands, became severely overcrowded, and people had no choice but to farm and graze too many animals on too little land. Severe erosion in many former homelands is therefore not simply the result of poor farming, as was sometimes claimed, but also of land dispossession. At the same time, some commercial farms also suffered serious erosion, so soil loss has affected farmers of all kinds.

South Africa is estimated to lose hundreds of millions of tonnes of soil a year, far more than is formed, which is why soil conservation is a national concern.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Soil erosion is caused only by people." Soil erosion is a natural process; people speed it up, which is called accelerated erosion.

Exhibit two: "Soil can be replaced quickly." A single centimetre of topsoil can take hundreds of years to form, so it is non-renewable on a human timescale.

Exhibit three: "Sheet erosion is not serious because you cannot see it." Sheet erosion quietly removes the most fertile topsoil from wide areas.

Exhibit four: "Only farming causes soil erosion." Construction, mining, deforestation and roads also expose soil and increase runoff.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a blanket on the ground, a tap left running, and a bulldozer at work.

## Subtopic: A Blanket on the Ground

Imagine the land wearing a blanket. The blanket is the grass, bushes and trees that grow on it. Under the blanket lies the soil, and the very top of the soil, the dark topsoil, is the most precious part. It holds the food that plants need, and almost all our food grows in it.

Topsoil forms terribly slowly. It can take hundreds of years to build up just one centimetre, about the thickness of your finger. So we cannot make more of it when we need it.

The blanket of plants keeps the soil safe. Leaves soften the blow of falling rain. Roots hold the soil together like a net. Grass slows down water and wind moving across the ground.

Wind and rain have always moved a little soil. That is natural. But when people pull the blanket off, the soil is left naked, and it starts to disappear much faster. That is soil erosion made worse by people.

So what does rain do to bare soil?

## Subtopic: A Tap Left Running

Picture a tap left running onto a heap of sand. At first the water spreads out and carries away a thin layer of sand. Then it starts to cut little channels. Soon the channels join into a big groove.

Rain does the same to bare soil on a slope. First it washes away a thin layer from the whole surface. Farmers call this sheet erosion, and you hardly notice it happening. Then the water cuts small channels called rills. If nothing stops it, the rills grow into huge, deep ditches called dongas. Some dongas in South Africa are deeper than a house is tall.

In dry places, wind does the stealing instead. It lifts the fine, rich soil into the air as dust and leaves the poor sand behind.

The soil does not vanish. It washes into rivers and turns them brown, and it fills up dams with mud, so they hold less water. In a dry country like South Africa, that really matters.

So which human activities pull the blanket off?

## Subtopic: A Bulldozer at Work

Three big activities pull the blanket off the land.

The first is farming. Farmers clear plants to make fields and plough the soil, leaving it bare for part of the year. When too many cattle, sheep or goats eat the grass down to the ground and trample it hard, the rain runs straight off.

The second is building. When a road, a mall or a housing estate is built, bulldozers scrape away all the plants. Rain washes the loose soil away. Afterwards, roofs and roads stop water from soaking in, so even more water rushes off and cuts into stream banks.

The third is mining. Mines dig away whole hillsides and leave huge dumps of bare waste. Rain carves gullies into them, and on windy days, dust blows off the old gold mine dumps around Johannesburg.

In South Africa, unfair land laws long ago also crowded millions of people onto small pieces of land, which made erosion much worse there.

Here is the line to remember: soil erosion is natural, but when people strip away plant cover through farming, building and mining, soil is lost far faster than it can ever form again.
