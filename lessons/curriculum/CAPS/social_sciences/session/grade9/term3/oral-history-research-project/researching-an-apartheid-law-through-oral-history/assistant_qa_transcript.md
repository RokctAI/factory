### subtopic_1 — What Oral History Is and Why It Matters
**Assistant:** Why are official records alone not enough to understand apartheid?

**Tutor:** Official records are very valuable, because they show what the government decided, which laws it passed, how many people were removed from an area, or how many people were arrested under the pass laws. However, they have serious limitations. First, they were produced by the people in power, so they reflect the views and purposes of the apartheid government and its officials. They often described policies in positive language, for example calling forced removals "slum clearance" or presenting separate development as giving each group its own opportunities, which hid the suffering they caused. Second, they usually record events from the outside, as numbers and categories, rather than from the point of view of the people affected. A government file might record that a certain number of families were moved from Sophiatown to Meadowlands, but it will not tell you how a child felt when the family home was bulldozed, how a grandmother lost her neighbours and church, or how families coped with longer journeys to work. Third, many experiences were never recorded at all, especially those of women, rural people and the poor. During apartheid, newspapers were censored, many organisations were banned, and people who spoke out could be detained, so many people kept silent. Some records were also deliberately destroyed by the government in the early 1990s, before the transition to democracy. Oral history fills these gaps by recording the experiences, feelings and resistance of ordinary people. A balanced historian uses both kinds of sources together: official records for the structure of laws and policies, and oral and other personal sources for how those policies were experienced and resisted.

### subtopic_2 — Choosing and Researching an Apartheid Law
**Assistant:** What should I do if my interviewee does not want to talk about something?

**Tutor:** You must respect their wishes completely. An interview for a history project is a privilege, not a right, and the person is doing you a favour by sharing their memories. Before the interview, you should explain that they can choose not to answer any question, ask to pause or stop the interview at any time, and decide whether their name may be used. If they then decline to talk about a particular topic during the interview, simply thank them, move on to another question, and do not push or try to persuade them. Remember that many memories of apartheid involve trauma, humiliation or loss, such as being arrested, losing a home, the death of a family member, or violence. Talking about these experiences can bring back painful feelings. Watch for signs of distress, such as a change in voice, long silences or tears, and offer a break or the chance to stop. Silence and reluctance can themselves be historically meaningful, because they show how deeply some experiences affected people, and you can mention in your report, in general terms, that some topics were too painful to discuss. You should never invent or guess what they might have said. If you find you do not have enough information for your project, you could interview a second person, or rely more on written sources for that part. After the interview, you should also respect any conditions the person set, for example by using a pseudonym if they asked to remain anonymous, and by not sharing the recording beyond your teacher. Behaving ethically in this way is not just politeness; it is a core principle of oral history.

### subtopic_3 — Planning and Conducting an Ethical Interview
**Assistant:** How can I tell if an oral source is reliable?

**Tutor:** Reliability means how far you can trust a source to give an accurate account. For an oral source, there are several questions to ask. First, was the person directly involved in, or an eyewitness to, the events they describe, or are they repeating what others told them? First-hand accounts are generally more reliable for events, while second-hand accounts may contain more errors. Second, how long ago did the events happen, and how old was the person at the time? Memories of childhood may be vivid but incomplete, and details such as exact dates often fade. Third, what is the person's point of view? Their background, beliefs and experiences may lead them to emphasise some aspects and play down others, which is natural, but which you should recognise. Fourth, is the account internally consistent, meaning that its different parts agree with one another? Fifth, and most importantly, can it be corroborated, which means supported by other evidence? Compare the interview with your written sources, and if possible with other people's memories of the same events. If a person remembers that their family was removed from a particular area in a certain year, you can check when removals took place there. Where the oral account agrees with other evidence, you can treat it as more reliable. Where it differs, look for an explanation before deciding that it is wrong. Finally, remember that reliability is not the same as usefulness. Even when a person's dates are a little uncertain, their account of how the law affected their feelings, family and community may be extremely useful, because no other source can provide it.

### subtopic_4 — Evaluating Evidence and Writing Up, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Which apartheid law would be easiest to research in my area?

**Tutor:** The best choice depends on where you live and who you can interview, so it is worth asking your family and community before deciding. In cities and towns, the Group Areas Act of 1950 is often a good choice, because almost every urban area was divided into separate residential zones and many communities experienced forced removals. Examples include Sophiatown in Johannesburg, District Six in Cape Town, Cato Manor in Durban, South End in Gqeberha and many smaller towns, so there are often local stories, museums and books. The pass laws are another good choice almost everywhere, because millions of black South Africans were required to carry a pass, and many older people remember pass raids, arrests or the difficulties of finding work and living in towns. The Bantu Education Act of 1953 affected generations of learners and teachers, so it is a good option if you can interview a retired teacher or someone who attended school under that system, and it also links to the Soweto uprising of 1976, which you will study in Term 4. The Reservation of Separate Amenities Act affected everyday life, such as separate buses, beaches, entrances and toilets, and almost every older person who lived under apartheid will remember examples. In rural areas, laws about land, such as the Land Acts and homeland policies, may be more relevant. Whatever law you choose, make sure there are enough written sources about it, that you can find a person willing to be interviewed who was affected by it, and that the topic is one they are comfortable discussing.
