# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces oral history and the Term 3 oral history and research project, in which a learner researches an apartheid law and interviews a person who was affected by it. It covers what oral history is and why it is valuable for understanding apartheid; how to choose an apartheid law and research it using written sources; how to plan and carry out an ethical interview; and how to evaluate oral evidence and write up a referenced research report. The anchor idea is that history is built from evidence, and that the memories of ordinary people are an important kind of evidence, especially for periods when their voices were left out of official records. The governing idea is that oral evidence must be collected with respect and consent, and then checked against other sources, just like any other historical source.

## Subtopic: What Oral History Is and Why It Matters

Historians use sources to find out about the past. A primary source is a source produced at the time of the events being studied, or by someone who was directly involved, such as a letter, a diary, a photograph, a newspaper report from the time, an official document, or a law itself. A secondary source is produced later by someone who was not there, using primary sources, such as a textbook, a history book or a documentary.

Oral history is the collecting and study of people's memories of the past through recorded interviews. A person who lived through events, and who describes their own experiences, is giving primary evidence, even though they are speaking many years later. Oral history is one of the oldest forms of history in Africa. Long before written records, communities passed on their histories through oral traditions, such as praise poems, the knowledge of griots in West Africa and izibongo in southern Africa, and stories told by elders.

Oral history is especially important for the history of apartheid. Most official records from that time were written by the government, the police and other officials, and they reflect their point of view. Newspapers were censored, and many people's experiences were never written down. The voices of ordinary black, coloured and Indian South Africans, and particularly of women, rural people and the poor, are often missing from the written record. Oral history allows these voices to be heard. It shows how laws that look simple on paper affected real families: how people lost homes, were separated from relatives, were arrested for not carrying a pass, or were denied education and jobs. It also records how people resisted, coped and built communities despite apartheid.

Oral history has been used in important ways in South Africa. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission, set up after 1994, heard testimony from thousands of victims and perpetrators of human rights violations, much of which is now part of the historical record. Museums such as the District Six Museum in Cape Town have collected the memories of people forcibly removed from their homes.

Oral history also has a personal value. Many young South Africans know only a little about what their own families experienced under apartheid, because the memories are painful or because nobody asked. An oral history project creates a reason for the generations to talk, and it often helps young people understand where their families come from and why the present looks the way it does. For the person being interviewed, being listened to with respect can also be meaningful, because it recognises that their experiences matter.

So the next question is: how do you choose and research an apartheid law?

## Subtopic: Choosing and Researching an Apartheid Law

Apartheid was built on a large number of laws passed mainly from 1948 onwards, which you will study in more detail in the coming lessons. For this project, you choose one of these laws and research it. It is sensible to choose a law that affected people in your community, so that you can find someone to interview.

Some laws that are often chosen are the Population Registration Act of 1950, which classified every South African into a racial group; the Group Areas Act of 1950, which set aside separate residential and business areas for each racial group and led to forced removals; the pass laws, including the Natives (Abolition of Passes and Co-ordination of Documents) Act of 1952, which forced black men, and later black women, to carry a reference book, commonly called a pass, at all times; the Bantu Education Act of 1953, which created a separate and inferior education system for black learners; the Reservation of Separate Amenities Act of 1953, which segregated public facilities such as beaches, buses, parks and toilets; and the Prohibition of Mixed Marriages Act of 1949 and the Immorality Amendment Act of 1950, which outlawed marriages and relationships across the colour line.

Once you have chosen a law, use written sources to answer key questions about it. When was it passed and by which government? What did it say? Why did the government say it was needed, and what were its real purposes? How was it enforced? Who was affected and how? How did people resist it? When and why was it repealed? Useful sources include school textbooks, history books in libraries, reliable websites such as South African History Online and university or museum archives, and old newspapers. Always record the details of each source you use, including the author, title, date and publisher or website address, so that you can reference it later.

This written research gives you the background you need to understand your interviewee's story and to ask good questions. It also gives you facts that you can compare with what you are told.

It also helps to form a key question for your project, which is the main question your research will try to answer. A good key question asks how or why, not just what. For example, instead of asking "What was the Group Areas Act?", you could ask "How did the Group Areas Act change the lives of families in my town?" or "Why did people resist the pass laws, and how successful was their resistance?". A key question gives your research a clear focus and helps you decide which information is relevant. Keep in mind, too, that a law on paper and a law in practice can be very different. The government often presented its laws in neutral or even positive terms, as bringing order or allowing each group to develop separately, while their real effect was to protect white privilege and control the black majority. Your written research should look for both the official purpose and the real effects.

So the next question is: how do you plan and carry out the interview?

## Subtopic: Planning and Conducting an Ethical Interview

Choose an interviewee who was directly affected by your chosen law, such as a grandparent, relative, neighbour or community member. Explain clearly what the project is about, how the interview will be used and who will see it. Ask for their informed consent, preferably in writing, including permission to record the interview and to use their name. Respect their right to say no, to remain anonymous, to refuse to answer any question and to stop at any time.

Prepare your questions in advance. Begin with simple background questions, such as when and where the person was born and where they grew up. Then move to questions about the law: when they first became aware of it, how it affected them and their family, specific events they remember, how they felt, and how they and others responded or resisted. End by asking how things have changed and what they think young people should know. Use open questions that invite a full answer, such as "What do you remember about the day your family was moved?", rather than closed questions that can be answered with yes or no. Avoid leading questions that suggest an answer, such as "Weren't you angry?".

During the interview, choose a quiet place, record the conversation with a phone or recorder if you have permission, and take notes. Listen carefully, do not interrupt, and ask follow-up questions when something is interesting or unclear. Remember that many memories of apartheid are painful. Be patient and sensitive. If the person becomes upset, offer to pause or stop. Thank them at the end, and offer to share your finished project with them.

Afterwards, write a transcript or a detailed summary of the interview as soon as possible, while it is fresh in your mind, noting the date, place and the name of the interviewee, if they agreed to be named.

So the final question is: how do you judge oral evidence and write up the project?

## Subtopic: Evaluating Evidence and Writing Up, and the Error Museum

Every source must be evaluated, and oral sources are no exception. Memory is powerful but not perfect. People may forget details or dates after many years, mix up events, remember things in the light of what they learnt later, or emphasise some things and leave others out. Strong emotions can make certain memories very vivid and others vague. A person's own point of view, which depends on their age, gender, community and experiences, shapes how they tell their story. This does not mean that oral evidence is unreliable; it means that, like every source, it must be read carefully and checked.

To evaluate your interview, compare it with your written sources. Do the dates and events match what the books say? Where they differ, think about why: perhaps the person remembers how a law was actually enforced in their area, which may be different from what the law said on paper. Consider what the interview adds that written sources cannot, such as feelings, personal details and the experience of ordinary people. Think about the usefulness of the source, which means how much it helps you answer your question, as well as its reliability.

Finally, write up your project. A good research report usually includes an introduction that states your topic and key question; background on the law from your written research; a summary or extracts of the interview; your analysis, comparing the interview with written sources and explaining how the law affected people; a conclusion that answers your key question; and a bibliography listing all your sources, including the interview. Use your own words, and when you quote a source or your interviewee, use quotation marks and reference it. Copying someone else's work and presenting it as your own is called plagiarism, and it is dishonest.

Your interview should be listed in your bibliography like any other source. A simple way is to give the name of the interviewee, or a pseudonym if they asked to remain anonymous, a short description of who they are, the words "interviewed by" followed by your name, and the place and date of the interview. For example: Interview with a retired teacher, interviewed by the learner, Gqeberha, 12 August. This shows your teacher exactly where your evidence came from, which is what referencing is for.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Oral history is just stories, so it is not real evidence." Memories of people who lived through events are primary evidence, which must be evaluated like any other source.

Exhibit two: "If a memory differs from a textbook, the person must be lying." Differences may reveal how a law was enforced locally, or they may be errors of memory; they must be investigated.

Exhibit three: "You may record and publish an interview without asking." Interviewees must give informed consent and may remain anonymous or stop at any time.

Exhibit four: "Closed questions get the best answers." Open questions let people tell their own stories in their own words.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a library with legs, a map before a journey, and a jigsaw puzzle.

## Subtopic: A Library With Legs

Every older person you know is like a library with legs. Inside their memory are stories you will not find in any book.

Apartheid ended in the early 1990s, so many people who lived under it are still alive. They remember carrying passes, being moved from their homes, going to separate schools and being kept out of beaches and parks. Most of these stories were never written down, because the government that made the laws was the one keeping the records.

Oral history means collecting these memories by interviewing people and recording what they say. A person telling you about their own life is giving you first-hand evidence, called a primary source.

In Africa, people have passed on history by word of mouth for thousands of years, through stories and praise poems. Your project continues that tradition.

So how do you get ready?

## Subtopic: A Map Before a Journey

Before you go on a journey, you check the map. Before an interview, you do your research.

First, choose one apartheid law, such as the Group Areas Act, which forced people out of their homes, or the pass laws, which made black South Africans carry a pass book. Read about it in books and on reliable websites. Write down where you found each fact.

Then find someone the law affected, perhaps a grandparent or neighbour. Explain your project and ask their permission to interview and record them. They can say no, stay anonymous or stop at any time.

Write your questions down. Start easy: where did you grow up? Then ask open questions like "What do you remember about the day you had to move?" so they can tell their story in their own words.

Be gentle. Some memories hurt. Listen more than you talk, and if the person wants to stop, stop and thank them.

Afterwards, write down what they said while you still remember it clearly, and say thank you.

So what do you do with the answers?

## Subtopic: A Jigsaw Puzzle

Think of the past as a jigsaw puzzle. Your interview is one important piece, and your books and websites are other pieces.

Memories are precious, but they can blur over time. Someone might mix up a date or forget a detail. So you check the interview against your other sources. Where they agree, you can be more confident. Where they differ, ask why. Maybe things happened differently in their town.

Then write your report: what the law said, what your interviewee experienced, how the two fit together, and what you have learnt. List every source at the end, including your interview. Use your own words, and put quotation marks around anything you copy.

Here is the line to remember: oral history turns people's memories of apartheid into evidence, as long as we collect them with respect and check them against other sources.
