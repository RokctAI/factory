### subtopic_1 — Human Rights and the Lessons of World War II
**Assistant:** Why did World War II convince the world that human rights needed protection?

**Tutor:** Before World War II, most governments believed that how a country treated its own citizens was its own business, and that other countries had no right to interfere. The war shattered that belief. Nazi Germany had shown that a modern, educated country could use its own legal system to persecute and murder millions of people. The Nazis did not start with mass murder. They began by excluding Jews from the civil service and professions, then the Nuremberg Laws of 1935 removed their citizenship and banned marriages between Jews and other Germans, then property was confiscated, then people were forced into ghettos, and finally they were deported to extermination camps. At each step, the persecution was legal under German law. This showed that law alone does not protect people if the law itself is unjust, and that there must be basic rights that no government can take away. The war also showed that a government that abuses its own people is often a danger to its neighbours, because the Nazi regime's racism and aggression went hand in hand with its drive to conquer other countries. The scale of death, with tens of millions of civilians killed, shocked the world, and the images from the liberated concentration camps in 1945 made the horror impossible to ignore. Leaders concluded that lasting peace depended on respect for human dignity everywhere. That is why the United Nations Charter of 1945 included a commitment to human rights, why the Nuremberg trials introduced crimes against humanity and why the Universal Declaration was written so soon after the war. Its preamble directly refers to the "barbarous acts which have outraged the conscience of mankind".

### subtopic_2 — The United Nations and the Drafting of the Declaration
**Assistant:** Why is it surprising that Jan Smuts helped write the United Nations Charter's preamble?

**Tutor:** It is surprising because of the contrast between the words Smuts helped to write and the policies of his own government. Jan Smuts was Prime Minister of South Africa during World War II and was a respected international statesman. He had served in the British Imperial War Cabinet in World War I and helped to create the League of Nations, and he was a field marshal in the British army in World War II. At the San Francisco conference in 1945, he proposed wording for the preamble of the United Nations Charter, including a reference to faith in fundamental human rights and the dignity and worth of the human person. Yet at home, Smuts led a government that practised segregation. Black South Africans could not vote in national elections except for a declining number in the Cape, they were restricted to about 13 percent of the land under the Land Acts, pass laws controlled their movements, and Smuts's government had passed the Asiatic Land Tenure and Indian Representation Act of 1946, which restricted where Indian South Africans could own land and was opposed by passive resistance. This contradiction was quickly noticed. In 1946, India raised the treatment of Indian South Africans at the first sessions of the UN General Assembly, and South Africa faced growing criticism. Historians therefore see Smuts as a complex figure: an international champion of peace and human dignity in his speeches, who nevertheless believed in white leadership in South Africa and did not extend those rights to the black majority. His story shows how ideas of human rights were often applied selectively at the time, and how the global language of rights could later be used against South Africa's own racial policies.

### subtopic_3 — The Thirty Articles
**Assistant:** If the Declaration was not legally binding, did it actually make a difference?

**Tutor:** Yes, it made a great difference, even though it was not a binding treaty when it was adopted. First, it set a common standard. For the first time, the countries of the world had agreed on a single list of rights that applied to everyone, everywhere, which made it much harder for any government to claim that abusing its people was simply its own business. Second, it became the foundation for international law. In 1966, the UN adopted two binding treaties, the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, and later treaties dealt with racial discrimination, discrimination against women, torture and the rights of children. Together with the Declaration, these form what is often called the International Bill of Human Rights. Many people also argue that some of the Declaration's principles, such as the ban on slavery and torture, have become customary international law that binds all states. Third, it shaped national constitutions. Many countries that became independent after 1948, including many in Africa, included rights based on the Declaration in their constitutions, and South Africa's Bill of Rights of 1996 was strongly influenced by it. Fourth, it gave ordinary people and activists a powerful moral argument. Movements against colonialism, racial discrimination and apartheid used the Declaration to show that their demands were not extreme, but simply what the world had already agreed every person deserved. Of course, the Declaration did not stop all abuses, and many governments have violated it. But it gave the world a measuring stick, and it changed what governments could get away with.

### subtopic_4 — The Declaration, Apartheid and South Africa's Bill of Rights, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How is South Africa's Bill of Rights different from the Universal Declaration?

**Tutor:** South Africa's Bill of Rights, in Chapter 2 of the Constitution of 1996, was strongly influenced by the Universal Declaration, but it differs in several important ways. First, and most importantly, it is part of the supreme law of the country, so it is legally binding. Any law or action by the government that conflicts with it can be declared invalid by the courts, and the Constitutional Court has the final say. Second, it reflects South Africa's own history. Because apartheid was built on racial discrimination, the right to equality in Section 9 is very detailed, listing many grounds on which unfair discrimination is forbidden, including race, gender, sex, pregnancy, marital status, ethnic or social origin, colour, sexual orientation, age, disability, religion, conscience, belief, culture, language and birth. It also allows measures to advance people who were disadvantaged by past unfair discrimination. Third, it includes social and economic rights in a form that courts can enforce, such as the rights of access to adequate housing, health care, sufficient food and water, and social security, and the right to basic education. The state must take reasonable steps, within its available resources, to realise these rights progressively. Fourth, it includes rights that were not in the 1948 Declaration, such as the right to an environment that is not harmful to health or well-being, rights for children, and rights related to just administrative action and access to information. Finally, it includes a limitation clause, Section 36, which explains when and how rights may be limited in an open and democratic society. In these ways, the Bill of Rights takes the principles of 1948 and turns them into enforceable law suited to South Africa's needs.
