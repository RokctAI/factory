# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted by the United Nations in 1948. It covers the meaning of human rights and why the horrors of World War II led the world to set them out; the founding of the United Nations and the drafting and adoption of the Declaration; the main rights contained in its thirty articles; and the contrast between the Declaration and apartheid in South Africa, together with the Declaration's influence on South Africa's Bill of Rights. The anchor idea is that human rights are the basic rights and freedoms that belong to every person simply because they are human, regardless of race, sex, religion or any other status. The governing idea is that the Declaration set a common standard against which every government, including South Africa's apartheid government, could be judged.

## Subtopic: Human Rights and the Lessons of World War II

Human rights are the basic rights and freedoms that every person is entitled to, simply because they are a human being. They include the right to life, to equality and to freedom from torture and slavery, and freedoms such as the freedom to speak, to practise one's religion and to move from place to place. Human rights are universal, which means they apply to everyone, everywhere. They are also inherent, which means people are born with them; governments do not grant them as favours, but have a duty to respect and protect them.

The idea of rights has a long history. Many societies and religions taught that people should be treated with dignity and fairness. The African philosophy of ubuntu, often summarised in the isiZulu phrase "umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu", meaning "a person is a person through other people", expresses the idea that every person's humanity is bound up with the humanity of others. Documents such as the English Bill of Rights of 1689, the American Declaration of Independence of 1776 and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen of 1789 set out rights for citizens. However, these early documents usually excluded many people, such as women, enslaved people and colonised peoples.

World War II, which you studied in Term 1, changed the way the world thought about rights. About 70 to 85 million people died, most of them civilians. In the Holocaust, Nazi Germany murdered about six million Jews, as well as Roma and Sinti people, people with disabilities, political opponents and others, in a deliberate programme of genocide. The Nazi state had shown that a government could use its own laws, such as the Nuremberg Laws of 1935, to strip people of their rights step by step, and then to persecute and murder them. Japanese forces also committed terrible abuses against prisoners of war and civilians in Asia.

After the war, many people concluded that the way a government treated its own people could no longer be seen as purely its own business. If basic rights were not protected everywhere, the world could again be plunged into war and genocide. At the Nuremberg trials of 1945 and 1946, leading Nazis were tried for crimes against humanity, an idea that showed some acts are crimes no matter what a country's own laws say.

So the next question is: how was the Universal Declaration created?

## Subtopic: The United Nations and the Drafting of the Declaration

In 1945, as the war ended, the Allied nations created the United Nations, or UN, to keep international peace and security and to promote cooperation between countries. The United Nations Charter was signed in San Francisco on 26 June 1945 by fifty nations, with Poland signing later as an original member, and the UN officially came into existence on 24 October 1945. The preamble of the Charter reaffirmed "faith in fundamental human rights" and "in the dignity and worth of the human person". Interestingly, South Africa's Prime Minister, Jan Smuts, helped to draft the wording of the preamble, even though his own government denied equal rights to the black majority at home. This contradiction would soon become a major problem for South Africa at the UN.

The Charter did not say exactly which rights it meant, so in 1946 the UN set up a Commission on Human Rights to write a list. It was chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt, the widow of the American President Franklin D. Roosevelt and a well-known campaigner for human rights. The drafting committee included people from different parts of the world and different traditions of thought, such as René Cassin of France, a Jewish lawyer who had lost family members in the Holocaust; Peng Chun Chang of China, a philosopher who brought ideas from Confucian thought; Charles Malik of Lebanon; and John Humphrey of Canada, who prepared the first draft. Women delegates, such as Hansa Mehta of India, made sure the text spoke of "all human beings" rather than "all men". Delegates from many countries debated the text over almost two years.

On 10 December 1948, the UN General Assembly, meeting in Paris, adopted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Forty-eight countries voted in favour, none voted against, and eight abstained, which means they chose not to vote either way. The eight countries that abstained were the Soviet Union and five other communist states, Saudi Arabia, and South Africa. The communist states objected mainly to some of the political and civil freedoms; Saudi Arabia objected to articles on religious freedom and marriage; and South Africa objected to rights that clashed with its racial policies, such as equality, freedom of movement and political participation. Since then, 10 December has been celebrated around the world as Human Rights Day.

So the next question is: what does the Declaration actually say?

## Subtopic: The Thirty Articles

The Universal Declaration has a preamble, which explains its purpose, and thirty articles, which set out the rights. Article 1 states the central principle: "All human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights." Article 2 states that everyone is entitled to these rights "without distinction of any kind, such as race, colour, sex, language, religion, political or other opinion, national or social origin, property, birth or other status". In other words, discrimination is ruled out from the start.

The articles can be grouped into several kinds of rights. Civil rights protect the person and their freedom. These include the right to life, liberty and security of person in Article 3; freedom from slavery in Article 4; freedom from torture and cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment in Article 5; equality before the law in Article 7; freedom from arbitrary arrest, detention or exile in Article 9; the right to a fair trial; and the right to be presumed innocent until proved guilty.

Political rights allow people to take part in public life. These include freedom of thought, conscience and religion in Article 18; freedom of opinion and expression in Article 19; freedom of peaceful assembly and association in Article 20; and the right to take part in the government of one's country, directly or through freely chosen representatives, in Article 21, which also states that the will of the people, expressed in genuine elections by universal and equal suffrage, shall be the basis of the authority of government.

Other rights protect people's freedom in daily life, such as freedom of movement and residence within one's country in Article 13, the right to seek asylum from persecution in Article 14, the right to a nationality in Article 15 and the right to marry and found a family, with the free and full consent of both partners, in Article 16.

Economic, social and cultural rights include the right to work and to equal pay for equal work in Article 23, the right to rest and leisure, the right to an adequate standard of living, including food, clothing, housing and medical care, in Article 25, and the right to education in Article 26, with elementary education free and compulsory.

The Declaration was not a treaty, so it was not legally binding on countries. Instead, it was a statement of principles, "a common standard of achievement for all peoples and all nations". Later, in 1966, the UN adopted two binding treaties, the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, which turned many of its principles into international law. The Declaration has been translated into hundreds of languages, more than any other document of its kind.

The Declaration also contains duties as well as rights. Article 29 states that everyone has duties to the community, and that rights may be limited only by law and only to protect the rights of others and to meet the just requirements of morality, public order and the general welfare in a democratic society. Article 30 adds that nothing in the Declaration may be used to destroy any of the rights it sets out. Rights, in other words, come with a responsibility to respect the rights of others.

So the final question is: how did the Declaration relate to South Africa?

## Subtopic: The Declaration, Apartheid and South Africa's Bill of Rights, and the Error Museum

The year 1948 was a turning point for South Africa in two opposite directions. On 26 May 1948, the National Party, led by D.F. Malan, won the general election, in which only white voters, and a small number of coloured voters in the Cape, could vote. It began to introduce apartheid, a system of laws that separated people by race and gave the white minority power and privilege. Just over six months later, the UN adopted the Universal Declaration, which South Africa refused to support.

Almost every apartheid law contradicted the Declaration. The Population Registration Act of 1950 classified people by race, against Article 2. Pass laws restricted freedom of movement, against Article 13. The Prohibition of Mixed Marriages Act of 1949 limited the right to marry, against Article 16. Detention without trial violated Articles 9 and 10. The denial of the vote to the black majority violated Article 21. Bantu Education violated the right to equal education in Article 26.

The Declaration became a powerful tool for opponents of apartheid. Liberation movements, such as the African National Congress, which had already published a document called African Claims in 1943 demanding rights based on the Atlantic Charter, used the language of human rights to argue their case. The Freedom Charter of 1955, which you will study later this term, echoed many of the Declaration's rights. At the UN, newly independent African and Asian countries repeatedly condemned apartheid, and in 1973 the UN General Assembly declared apartheid a crime against humanity.

After apartheid ended, South Africa's democratic Constitution of 1996 included a Bill of Rights, in Chapter 2, which is one of the most comprehensive in the world. It protects equality, dignity, life, freedom and security of the person, freedom of movement, expression and association, political rights, and also social and economic rights, such as access to housing, health care, food, water and education. South Africa celebrates Human Rights Day on 21 March, in memory of the Sharpeville massacre of 1960.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Human rights are given to people by their governments." Human rights are inherent; governments have a duty to respect and protect them.

Exhibit two: "South Africa voted for the Universal Declaration in 1948." South Africa abstained, because the Declaration clashed with its racial policies.

Exhibit three: "The Universal Declaration is a binding law that every country must obey." It is a statement of principles; later treaties made many of its rights legally binding.

Exhibit four: "Human rights only means the right to vote." The Declaration includes civil, political, economic, social and cultural rights, such as education and an adequate standard of living.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a promise after a nightmare, a list of thirty, and two roads in 1948.

## Subtopic: A Promise After a Nightmare

World War II was a nightmare. Tens of millions of people died. In the Holocaust, the Nazis murdered six million Jews and many others. They did it step by step, using their own laws to take away people's rights, then their homes, then their lives.

When the war ended in 1945, the world was shocked. Leaders asked: how can we stop this from ever happening again? One answer was to agree, as a whole world, on the rights that every person has, just because they are human.

These are called human rights. Nobody gives them to you. You are born with them. A government's job is to protect them, not take them away.

In 1945, the countries of the world formed the United Nations to keep the peace. A team led by Eleanor Roosevelt, from the United States, worked with people from many countries to write a list of human rights.

So what was on the list?

## Subtopic: A List of Thirty

On 10 December 1948, in Paris, the United Nations adopted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. It has thirty articles, like thirty promises.

The very first one says: all human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights. The second says these rights belong to everyone, no matter their race, colour, sex, language or religion.

Other articles say that everyone has the right to life and safety, that nobody may be a slave or be tortured, that nobody may be locked up without a fair trial, and that everyone may speak freely, practise their religion, move around their country, marry the person they choose, vote for their government, work, and go to school.

The Declaration was a promise, not a law. But it became the measuring stick the whole world uses to judge how governments treat people. Every year, 10 December is Human Rights Day around the world.

So how did South Africa measure up?

## Subtopic: Two Roads in 1948

In 1948, South Africa took the opposite road. In May, the National Party won an election in which only white people, and a few coloured voters, could vote. It began building apartheid.

When the United Nations voted on the Declaration, South Africa refused to vote for it. Apartheid broke almost every promise on the list: people were sorted by race, forced to carry passes, banned from marrying across the colour line, given unequal schools, and denied the vote.

People fighting apartheid used the Declaration as proof that the world was on their side.

When apartheid ended, South Africa wrote a new Constitution in 1996 with a Bill of Rights that protects equality and dignity for everyone. South Africa now celebrates Human Rights Day on 21 March.

Here is the line to remember: in 1948 the world promised that all people are born free and equal in dignity and rights, while South Africa began apartheid, which denied those rights until democracy wrote them into the Constitution.
