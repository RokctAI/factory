# Part 1 — Expert

This session gives a brief definition and explanation of racism. It covers the key terms race, prejudice, stereotyping and discrimination, and how they combine in racism; the history of racist ideas, including so-called scientific racism and how it was used to justify slavery, colonialism, Nazism and segregation; the difference between personal racism and institutional or structural racism, with apartheid as an example of racism built into law; and how racism can be recognised and challenged today, including through South Africa's Constitution. The anchor idea is that racism is the belief that people can be divided into races that are superior or inferior to one another, together with the actions and systems that treat people unequally because of that belief. The governing idea is that racism is learnt, not natural, so it can be unlearnt and challenged.

## Subtopic: Race, Prejudice, Stereotyping and Discrimination

To understand racism, it helps to define a few terms carefully.

The word race has been used to divide human beings into groups based on physical features that can be seen, such as skin colour, hair texture and the shape of the face. For centuries, many people believed that these groups were biologically separate types of human being, each with its own fixed abilities and character. Modern genetics has shown that this is not true. All living people belong to one species, Homo sapiens, and any two people share about 99.9 percent of their DNA. There is usually more genetic variation within a so-called racial group than between different groups, and physical features such as skin colour change gradually across the world rather than falling into neat boxes. Skin colour itself is an adaptation to the amount of sunlight in the places where people's ancestors lived. For these reasons, scientists describe race as a social construct, which means it is a set of categories created by societies rather than a natural biological division. However, race is very real in its effects, because societies have used these categories to decide how people are treated.

Prejudice means prejudging: forming an opinion about a person or group before knowing the facts, usually a negative opinion, based on the group they belong to. Prejudice is an attitude, something that happens in a person's mind.

A stereotype is an oversimplified and fixed idea about what all members of a group are like, for example the belief that all members of a group are lazy, dishonest, good at sport or bad at mathematics. Stereotypes ignore the fact that every individual is different, and even so-called positive stereotypes can be harmful, because they reduce people to a label.

Discrimination is action: treating a person or group unfairly because of the group they belong to, for example by refusing them a job, a house, a place in a school, service in a shop, or the right to vote.

Racism combines these elements. It is the belief that some races are superior and others inferior, together with prejudice, stereotypes and discrimination based on that belief. Many writers emphasise that racism is most harmful when it is combined with power: the power to make laws, control jobs, land and education, and decide who belongs. Racism can be directed at anyone, and in South Africa people of every background have experienced prejudice, but historically it was used above all by those in power to justify the domination of black people.

It is also useful to know that racism is learnt. Very young children notice differences in appearance, but they do not naturally rank people as better or worse because of them. Children learn prejudice and stereotypes from the adults around them, from the media, from jokes and from the way society is organised. If they see that people of one group are always in charge and people of another always do the hardest, lowest-paid work, they may conclude that this is natural. Because racism is learnt in these ways, it can also be unlearnt, through education, contact between people as equals, and fair laws and institutions.

So the next question is: where did racist ideas come from?

## Subtopic: The History of Racist Ideas

People have always noticed differences between groups, and hostility between communities has a long history. However, the modern idea of race, which ranks humanity into a fixed hierarchy based on skin colour, developed mainly from the sixteenth century onwards, alongside European colonialism and the Atlantic slave trade.

Between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries, more than 12 million Africans were forcibly taken across the Atlantic Ocean as enslaved people. Slave traders and slave owners needed to justify treating human beings as property. Increasingly, they argued that Africans were inferior by nature, so that slavery could be presented as natural rather than as a crime. Racism, in other words, grew in part to justify exploitation that was already profitable.

In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, some European scientists tried to classify humans into races, measure skulls and rank groups by supposed intelligence. This is known as scientific racism, although it was not good science, because its conclusions were decided in advance and its methods were deeply flawed. Followers of an idea called social Darwinism misapplied Charles Darwin's theory of evolution to human societies, claiming that some races were more advanced and destined to rule others. These ideas were used to justify colonial conquest, including the scramble for Africa in the late nineteenth century, when European powers divided almost the entire continent among themselves. In southern Africa, the bones and bodies of some people were collected and displayed in European museums. Sarah Baartman, a Khoikhoi woman, was taken to Europe in 1810 and exhibited as a curiosity in London and Paris; after her death in 1815, her remains were displayed in a Paris museum until the 1970s and were returned to South Africa for burial in 2002.

In the twentieth century, racist ideas reached their most extreme form in Nazi Germany, which you studied in Term 1. The Nazis believed in a so-called Aryan master race and described Jews, Roma and Slavs as inferior. Their racial ideology led directly to the Nuremberg Laws and the Holocaust. In the United States, segregation laws in the southern states, known as Jim Crow laws, separated black and white Americans in schools, transport and public places until the 1960s.

After World War II, the horrors of Nazi racism discredited scientific racism. In 1950, UNESCO, the United Nations educational and scientific organisation, published a statement by leading scientists rejecting the idea that races could be ranked. Yet in South Africa, the opposite happened: from 1948, the National Party built racism into the law more completely than ever before.

So the next question is: how can racism be built into a whole system?

## Subtopic: Personal and Institutional Racism

Racism can operate at different levels.

Personal racism, sometimes called individual or interpersonal racism, is racism in the attitudes and behaviour of individuals: insults, jokes, name-calling, refusing to sit next to someone, or treating someone with suspicion because of their race. It can be open and deliberate, or it can be subtle and even unconscious, for example when a shop assistant follows some customers more closely than others without realising why.

Institutional racism, also called structural or systemic racism, is racism built into the laws, rules, practices and institutions of a society, such as the government, schools, the police, businesses and the economy. It does not depend on whether every individual involved is personally prejudiced. Even people who see themselves as fair may carry out unfair rules or benefit from them.

Apartheid is one of the clearest examples of institutional racism in history. From 1948 to the early 1990s, racism in South Africa was not just a matter of personal attitudes; it was the law. The Population Registration Act of 1950 classified every person as white, coloured, Indian or Asian, or black, and this classification decided where a person could live, which school they could attend, which jobs they could hold, whom they could marry, which beaches and buses they could use, and whether they could vote. Officials sometimes used crude tests, such as the pencil test, in which a pencil was pushed into a person's hair to see whether it fell out, to decide which group someone belonged to. Families were sometimes split when relatives were classified differently.

Institutional racism can continue to affect people long after racist laws are repealed. In South Africa today, decades of apartheid and earlier segregation mean that wealth, land ownership, quality of schooling and access to services are still very unequal, and these inequalities still largely follow racial lines. This is sometimes called the legacy of apartheid. It explains why the Constitution allows measures, such as affirmative action and land reform, intended to redress past discrimination, though people debate how well these measures work and how they should be designed.

Racism is also not unique to South Africa or to any one group. Racial and ethnic prejudice exists in every country, and in Africa, as elsewhere, it has sometimes taken the form of hostility between ethnic groups or against foreigners. In South Africa, attacks on African migrants, often called xenophobia, have caused deaths and displacement, notably in 2008 and 2015.

So the final question is: how can racism be recognised and challenged?

## Subtopic: Challenging Racism Today, and the Error Museum

South Africa's Constitution of 1996 makes non-racialism one of the founding values of the country, alongside human dignity, equality and the advancement of human rights. Section 9 of the Bill of Rights states that everyone is equal before the law and forbids unfair discrimination on grounds including race, colour and ethnic or social origin. The Promotion of Equality and Prevention of Unfair Discrimination Act of 2000, often called the Equality Act, allows people to take cases of discrimination and hate speech to Equality Courts. The South African Human Rights Commission investigates complaints about human rights violations, including racism.

Laws are important, but racism is also challenged through everyday actions. Individuals can examine their own assumptions and stereotypes, learn about other people's histories, languages and cultures, and get to know people as individuals rather than as members of a group. They can refuse to laugh at or share racist jokes and messages, speak up safely when they see racism, support people who are targeted, and report serious incidents to teachers, the school, the police or the Human Rights Commission. Schools can make sure that all learners are treated fairly, that the curriculum includes the histories of all South Africans, and that racist incidents are dealt with seriously.

History shows that people of all backgrounds have fought racism. In South Africa, the struggle against apartheid involved people of every racial group, from ordinary workers and students to leaders such as Albert Luthuli, Nelson Mandela, Helen Joseph, Ahmed Kathrada, Desmond Tutu and many others, whom you will meet in the coming lessons. Their vision of a non-racial South Africa, in which, as the Freedom Charter of 1955 put it, South Africa belongs to all who live in it, became part of the Constitution.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Human races are separate biological types." All people are one species and share about 99.9 percent of their DNA; race is a social construct.

Exhibit two: "Racism is natural, so it can never change." Racism is learnt from society, so it can be unlearnt and challenged.

Exhibit three: "If there are no racist laws, racism has ended." Institutional racism and its legacy can continue after laws are repealed, and personal prejudice can persist.

Exhibit four: "A positive stereotype is harmless." All stereotypes reduce individuals to a label and can lead to unfair treatment.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: boxes that do not exist, a lie that paid well, and a game with unfair rules.

## Subtopic: Boxes That Do Not Exist

For hundreds of years, people tried to sort humans into boxes called races, mostly by skin colour. They believed each box had its own abilities and character.

Science has shown that these boxes do not really exist. All people belong to one human family. Any two people on earth share about 99.9 percent of their DNA. Skin colour simply changed over thousands of years to suit how much sunlight there was where people's ancestors lived.

So race is something societies invented. But it has had very real effects, because people have been treated differently because of it.

Prejudice means judging someone before you know them. A stereotype is a fixed idea that everyone in a group is the same. Discrimination is treating someone unfairly because of their group. Racism is believing some races are better than others, and treating people unfairly because of that belief, often through laws and rules.

So where did the idea of superior races come from?

## Subtopic: A Lie That Paid Well

When Europeans began taking millions of Africans across the ocean as slaves, they needed an excuse for such cruelty. They told themselves, and everyone else, that Africans were less than fully human. That lie made slavery look acceptable, and it made a lot of money.

Later, some scientists measured skulls and ranked races, claiming to prove that white Europeans were superior. It was bad science, but it was used to justify taking over Africa.

Sarah Baartman, a Khoikhoi woman from South Africa, was taken to Europe in 1810 and put on display like an object. Her remains came home only in 2002.

The Nazis took racist ideas to their most terrible end in the Holocaust. After World War II, scientists around the world rejected the idea of superior races. But South Africa did the opposite.

So what happens when racism becomes the rules?

## Subtopic: A Game With Unfair Rules

Imagine a football match where the rules say one team may use its hands, gets two extra players and plays downhill. Even if every player on that team is friendly, the game is unfair, because the rules are unfair.

That is institutional racism: racism built into the rules. Apartheid wrote racism into the law. Your race decided where you could live, which school you went to, which job you could do and whether you could vote.

Apartheid ended, but a game played with unfair rules for decades leaves one team far ahead on the scoreboard. That is why South Africa is still very unequal today.

Today, South Africa's Constitution says everyone is equal, and racism is against the law. But laws are not enough. Each person can question their own stereotypes, refuse to share racist jokes, and stand up for people who are treated unfairly.

Here is the line to remember: racism is the false belief that some races are better than others, used to treat people unfairly, and because it is learnt, it can be challenged and unlearnt.
