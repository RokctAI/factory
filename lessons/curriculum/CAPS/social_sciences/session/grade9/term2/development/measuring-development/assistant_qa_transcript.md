### subtopic_1 — What Is a Development Indicator?
**Assistant:** Why do we use GDP per capita instead of total GDP?

**Tutor:** Total GDP measures the size of a whole economy, which is useful for comparing economic power, but it does not tell us how wealthy the average person is, because countries have very different populations. For example, India's total GDP is much larger than Switzerland's, but India has about 1,4 billion people and Switzerland fewer than 10 million, so the average Swiss person is many times better off in income terms. Dividing GDP by population corrects for this and gives GDP per capita, a figure that can be compared between countries of any size. The method is straightforward: GDP divided by population. With South African figures of about 380 billion US dollars and about 62 million people, the result is about 6 100 dollars per person. When you do these calculations in an exam, be careful with the zeros: write the numbers out in full or use powers of ten, and check your answer against what you know, since a GDP per capita of 6 dollars or 6 million dollars would obviously be wrong. Remember too that GDP per capita is an average. It does not mean every South African earns 6 100 dollars; it is total output divided equally in a calculation, while real incomes are very unevenly shared. To see the distribution, you need an inequality measure such as the Gini coefficient, or the income share of the richest and poorest tenths of the population.

### subtopic_2 — Economic Indicators: GDP, GNI and Per Capita Figures
**Assistant:** What is purchasing power parity and why does it matter?

**Tutor:** Purchasing power parity, or PPP, is a way of adjusting income figures so that they reflect what money can actually buy in each country. Normally GDP figures are converted into US dollars using market exchange rates. But prices of many everyday goods and services, such as food, transport, rent and haircuts, are much lower in poorer countries than in rich ones. So a salary that looks very small when converted to dollars might buy considerably more locally than you would expect. PPP calculations compare the cost of a standard basket of goods and services in each country and adjust incomes accordingly. A simple illustration is the price of a burger: if the same burger costs 5 dollars in the United States and the equivalent of 2,50 dollars in another country, then each dollar of income goes about twice as far in the second country for that item. When South Africa's GDP per capita is measured at market exchange rates it is about 6 000 US dollars, but in PPP terms it is roughly 15 000 dollars. PPP matters because it gives a fairer comparison of living standards between countries and reduces the distorting effect of exchange-rate changes, such as a sudden fall in the value of the rand. The Human Development Index uses GNI per capita in PPP terms for this reason. However, PPP figures are estimates based on price surveys, and they do not remove inequality within countries.

### subtopic_3 — Social and Demographic Indicators
**Assistant:** Why is infant mortality considered such a useful indicator?

**Tutor:** Infant mortality, the number of babies who die before their first birthday per 1 000 live births, is considered one of the most revealing indicators because it responds to many aspects of development at the same time. A baby's survival depends on the mother's health and nutrition during pregnancy; on whether she gives birth with a trained midwife or doctor present; on clean drinking water and sanitation, since diarrhoea from contaminated water is a major killer of infants; on vaccinations against diseases such as measles; on access to clinics and medicines; on the family's income and housing; and on the mother's education, because educated mothers are more likely to use health services and know about nutrition and hygiene. So when infant mortality falls, it usually means that several of these conditions have improved together. It also changes relatively quickly, so it can show the effects of new policies within a few years. In South Africa, for example, infant mortality fell significantly after the programme to prevent mother-to-child transmission of HIV was expanded in the 2000s. The indicator also has the advantage of being easy to understand and compare. A limitation is that where births and deaths are not registered, the figures must be estimated from surveys, and national averages hide differences between provinces and between rich and poor households. In exams, remember that a lower rate indicates higher development.

### subtopic_4 — Limitations, Inequality and Composite Measures, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What important things do development indicators fail to measure?

**Tutor:** Indicators are powerful but leave out a great deal. First, they often miss the informal and unpaid economy. Subsistence farming, caring for children and the elderly, collecting water and firewood, and much informal trade, such as spaza shops, are under-recorded in GDP, even though they support millions of South African households and are done largely by women. Second, they struggle with distribution: averages hide inequality between rich and poor, men and women, urban and rural areas and different groups. Third, many indicators measure quantity rather than quality: a high school enrolment rate does not show whether learners are actually learning to read with understanding, and a count of clinics does not show whether they have medicine and staff. Fourth, GDP ignores environmental damage and the depletion of resources: a country can raise its GDP by cutting down forests or overfishing, even though it is reducing its future wealth. Fifth, important aspects of quality of life, such as freedom, safety, dignity, cultural identity, community relationships and happiness, are hard to measure at all, although some surveys attempt to measure life satisfaction. Sixth, data from some countries may be unreliable or outdated. This does not mean indicators are useless; it means geographers must use several, combine numbers with fieldwork, interviews and photographs, and interpret data critically by asking who collected it, how and why.
