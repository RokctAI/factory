# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains how development is measured. It covers what a development indicator is and why geographers use several; economic indicators, including gross domestic product, gross national income, per capita figures and purchasing power parity; social and demographic indicators, including life expectancy, infant mortality, literacy, access to services and population structure; and the limitations of indicators, measures of inequality and composite indices. The anchor calculation is GDP per capita, found by dividing a country's GDP by its population: for example, a GDP of about 380 billion US dollars divided by about 62 million people gives about 6 100 dollars per person, close to South Africa's recent figure. The governing idea is that every indicator is a simplification, so geographers combine several indicators, compare them over time and space, and always ask what the numbers hide.

## Subtopic: What Is a Development Indicator?

An indicator is a measurable piece of information that tells us something about a larger idea we cannot measure directly. Development, as you learned in the previous lesson, is a complex process involving economic, social and environmental change. We cannot put development on a scale, but we can measure things that are linked to it: how much people earn, how long they live, whether they can read, how many babies survive their first year, how many homes have clean water.

Geographers use indicators for several reasons. They allow us to compare countries, provinces or towns with each other; to track change over time, such as whether access to electricity in South Africa improved between the 1996 and 2022 censuses; to identify where help or investment is most needed; and to evaluate whether a policy has worked. Governments, the United Nations, the World Bank and non-governmental organisations all rely on indicators when they plan and report.

Good indicators share certain qualities. They should be clearly defined, so that everyone measures the same thing; reliable, meaning they are collected accurately and consistently; available for many places and years, so comparisons are possible; and valid, meaning they actually reflect the aspect of development they claim to measure. In practice, data from poorer countries may be less reliable because census and registration systems are weaker, and some important things, such as happiness, freedom or dignity, are very difficult to measure at all.

Indicators can be grouped into types. Economic indicators measure wealth, income and the structure of the economy. Social indicators measure health, education and access to services. Demographic indicators measure population characteristics such as birth rates, death rates and age structure. Environmental indicators measure such things as carbon emissions per person or the proportion of forest cover. Political indicators may measure freedom of the press or the fairness of elections. Because each type reveals only one part of development, geographers prefer to use a set of indicators, or a composite index that combines several.

In South Africa, the main source of official indicators is Statistics South Africa, known as Stats SA. It runs the national census, most recently in 2022, as well as regular surveys such as the Quarterly Labour Force Survey, which measures unemployment, and the General Household Survey, which tracks access to water, sanitation, electricity and education. International bodies such as the World Bank and the United Nations Development Programme then use national data to publish comparable tables for almost every country. When you use a statistic in an assignment, always note its source and year, because figures are revised and conditions change. A number without a date or source is much less useful as evidence.

So the next question is: how do we measure the economic aspect of development?

## Subtopic: Economic Indicators: GDP, GNI and Per Capita Figures

The most widely used economic indicator is gross domestic product, or GDP: the total market value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders in one year. It includes the output of mines, farms, factories, shops, banks and government services. A closely related measure is gross national income, or GNI, which adds income earned by a country's residents and companies from abroad and subtracts income earned in the country by foreigners and sent out. For a country such as South Africa, where many mines and companies are partly foreign-owned, GNI is slightly lower than GDP.

Total GDP tells us the size of an economy but not how wealthy its people are, because countries have very different populations. India has a much larger GDP than Switzerland, but far more people. So geographers use per capita figures, meaning per person. The formula is simple: GDP per capita equals GDP divided by population. If a country has a GDP of 380 billion US dollars and a population of 62 million, then GDP per capita is 380 000 000 000 divided by 62 000 000, which is about 6 129 dollars, or roughly 6 100 dollars per person per year. The same method gives GNI per capita.

Differences between countries are enormous. In recent years Norway's GDP per capita has been about 87 000 US dollars, while Malawi's has been about 600 dollars; dividing 87 000 by 600 shows the average Norwegian produces about 145 times as much in dollar terms as the average Malawian.

However, prices differ between countries: a loaf of bread, a taxi ride or a haircut costs much less in Malawi than in Norway. To compare what incomes can actually buy, economists use purchasing power parity, or PPP, which adjusts figures to account for local prices. Measured in PPP, the gap between rich and poor countries is smaller, though still very large. South Africa's GDP per capita is about 6 000 US dollars at market exchange rates but roughly two and a half times higher, about 15 000 dollars, when adjusted for PPP.

Other economic indicators include the percentage of workers employed in primary, secondary and tertiary activities, since a large share in farming often indicates lower development; the unemployment rate; energy consumption per person; and the value of exports and the types of goods exported.

So the next question is: what about health, education and people's daily lives?

## Subtopic: Social and Demographic Indicators

Social and demographic indicators measure how well people live, not how much a country produces.

Life expectancy at birth is the average number of years a newborn baby can expect to live if current death rates continue. It reflects nutrition, health care, clean water, sanitation and safety. In Japan it is about 84 years; in many very poor countries it is in the low sixties or below. South Africa's life expectancy fell during the HIV and Aids epidemic and then recovered to the low to mid sixties.

The infant mortality rate is the number of babies who die before their first birthday for every 1 000 live births in a year. If a region records 1 200 infant deaths among 48 000 live births, the rate is 1 200 divided by 48 000, multiplied by 1 000, which equals 25 per 1 000, close to South Africa's recent level. In the richest countries the rate is below 5, and in some of the poorest it is above 50. Infant mortality is a sensitive indicator, because it falls quickly when mothers have access to clean water, vaccines, nutrition and skilled care at birth.

The adult literacy rate is the percentage of adults who can read and write, and related education indicators include mean years of schooling, school enrolment and the proportion of girls in school. Health indicators include the number of doctors per 1 000 people and the percentage of children vaccinated. Indicators of access to services include the percentage of households with piped water, flush toilets, electricity and internet.

Demographic indicators include the birth rate, the death rate and the total fertility rate, which is the average number of children born to each woman. Countries at lower levels of development usually have high birth rates, many young people and a population pyramid with a wide base, while highly developed countries tend to have low birth rates and ageing populations. Gender indicators, such as the proportion of seats in parliament held by women or the gap between male and female earnings, show whether development benefits everyone equally.

Notice how indicators often move together. Countries with high incomes usually also have high life expectancy, low infant mortality and high literacy, which is why these indicators are said to be correlated. But the link is not perfect, and the exceptions are interesting. Some countries with modest incomes, such as Sri Lanka, Costa Rica and Cuba, have achieved high life expectancy through strong public health and education, while some wealthy oil-exporting countries have lagged on social indicators. Looking for such exceptions is a good way to show deeper understanding in a paragraph answer.

So the final question is: what are the weaknesses of all these indicators?

## Subtopic: Limitations, Inequality and Composite Measures, and the Error Museum

Every indicator has limitations. GDP per capita is an average and hides inequality: in a highly unequal country, a few very rich people raise the average while most people earn far less. GDP counts only market activity, so unpaid work, such as caring for children, cooking and subsistence farming, is left out, though it is vital in rural South Africa. GDP also counts harmful activity as growth: cleaning up after a disaster or treating pollution-related illness raises GDP. It ignores whether resources are being used up sustainably. Informal trade, such as spaza shops and street vendors, may be under-recorded.

Social indicators have problems too. They may be estimates rather than counts in countries without full birth and death registration. They may be averages that hide differences between men and women, urban and rural areas, or provinces. A school enrolment rate may be high while the quality of learning is poor.

To measure inequality directly, geographers use the Gini coefficient, which ranges from 0, where everyone has exactly the same income, to 1, where one person has all the income. South Africa's Gini coefficient, at about 0,63, is among the highest in the world, while countries such as Norway and Slovakia have values around 0,25 to 0,28. Another way to show inequality is to compare the share of income received by the richest tenth and the poorest tenth of the population.

Because single indicators are limited, organisations combine several into composite indices. The best known is the Human Development Index, which combines health, education and income and is the subject of the next lesson. Others include the Multidimensional Poverty Index, which measures overlapping deprivations in health, education and living standards in households.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "A country with a large GDP must be wealthy per person." Total GDP ignores population size. Always divide by population to find GDP per capita.

Exhibit two: "A higher infant mortality rate means a country is more developed." The opposite is true: fewer infant deaths per 1 000 births indicates better health care and living conditions.

Exhibit three: "One indicator is enough to rank countries." Every indicator measures only part of development; a set of indicators or a composite index gives a fuller picture.

Exhibit four: "GDP per capita shows what a typical person earns." It is an average of total output divided by population, not the income of a typical person, and in unequal countries most people earn far less than the average.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a school report card, a cake being shared, and the things a calculator cannot count.

## Subtopic: A School Report Card

When your school wants to know how you are doing, it does not give you just one mark. You get a report card with marks for languages, maths, science, social sciences and more. One high mark does not mean you are doing well in everything.

Countries get report cards too. The marks are called indicators: facts we can count or measure that tell us about development. Some are about money, like how much a country produces. Some are about health, like how long people live. Some are about learning, like how many adults can read. Some are about services, like how many homes have clean water and electricity.

Just like a report card, we can use indicators to compare: is South Africa doing better than last year, or better than a neighbouring country? Which province needs more clinics? Did a new programme help?

But some important things are hard to put on a report card, like happiness, safety or freedom. That is why we always use several indicators, never only one.

So how do we measure the money part?

## Subtopic: Sharing a Cake

Imagine a huge cake. Its size is everything a country produces in one year: food, cars, phone calls, haircuts, everything. That is called GDP, gross domestic product.

But a big cake is not much use if a million people have to share it. So we divide the cake by the number of people. That gives GDP per capita, which means per person. For example, if a country's cake is worth 380 billion dollars and there are 62 million people, each person's slice is worth about 6 100 dollars. That is close to South Africa's figure. In Norway each slice is worth about 87 000 dollars, and in Malawi about 600 dollars.

Here is the catch: in real life, the cake is not cut into equal slices. In South Africa a few people get huge slices and many get tiny crumbs. So the average slice can fool you. Geographers use a number called the Gini coefficient to show how unequal the slices are. South Africa's is about 0,63, one of the highest in the world.

So what about things that are not about money?

## Subtopic: Counting What Matters

Some of the most important indicators are about life itself. Life expectancy tells us, on average, how many years a baby born today can expect to live. In Japan it is about 84 years.

Infant mortality tells us how many babies, out of every 1 000 born, die before their first birthday. If a town has 48 000 babies born and 1 200 of them die, that is 25 for every 1 000. A lower number is better, and it drops fast when mothers get clean water, vaccines and good care.

The literacy rate tells us what percentage of adults can read and write. Other indicators count doctors, schools, taps and toilets.

But even these have limits. An average can hide differences between a rich suburb and a poor village. And a calculator cannot count the love of a grandmother caring for children, or the work of a farmer growing food for her family, even though these matter a lot.

Here is the line to remember: use many indicators, divide by population, check whether lower or higher is better, and always ask what the numbers leave out.
