### subtopic_1 — Why the HDI Was Created and Its Three Dimensions
**Assistant:** Why does the HDI use life expectancy, schooling and income?

**Tutor:** The designers of the HDI wanted a small number of indicators that capture the most basic capabilities people need to live a good life and that are available for almost every country. A long and healthy life is perhaps the most fundamental of all; without life and health, no other choices are possible, and life expectancy is a widely recorded figure that reflects nutrition, clean water, health care and safety together. Knowledge allows people to understand their world, find work, participate in society and make informed decisions; the two schooling indicators capture both the education of today's adults and the opportunities for today's children. A decent standard of living provides the resources people need for food, shelter, transport and other choices; GNI per capita in PPP dollars is used because it reflects what income can buy in each country. Mahbub ul Haq admitted that the HDI was deliberately simple, designed to be as easy to communicate as GDP so that it could compete with GDP for the attention of governments and the media. He accepted that it left out important things, such as freedom and the environment, which the UNDP has addressed with additional indices. In an exam, if you are asked to name the three dimensions, give health, education and income; if asked for the four indicators, give life expectancy at birth, mean years of schooling, expected years of schooling and GNI per capita at PPP.

### subtopic_2 — Calculating the HDI Step by Step
**Assistant:** How do you calculate a dimension index for the HDI?

**Tutor:** Each dimension index converts an actual value into a number between 0 and 1 using fixed minimum and maximum values, called goalposts. The general formula is the actual value minus the minimum, divided by the maximum minus the minimum. Let us work through the health index. The goalposts for life expectancy are 20 years as the minimum and 85 years as the maximum. Suppose a country's life expectancy is 65 years. First subtract the minimum from the actual value: 65 minus 20 equals 45. Then subtract the minimum from the maximum: 85 minus 20 equals 65. Finally divide: 45 divided by 65 is about 0,692. If a country had life expectancy of exactly 85 years, the index would be 1; if exactly 20 years, it would be 0. The same method applies to schooling: expected years of schooling use goalposts of 0 and 18, so 13,6 years gives 13,6 divided by 18, about 0,756; mean years of schooling use 0 and 15, so 11,6 years gives about 0,773. The education index is the average of these two, about 0,764. For income, logarithms are used, which you will meet in higher grades. In exam calculations, always show the formula, substitute the numbers, show each step, round sensibly to three decimal places, and check that your answer lies between 0 and 1. If it does not, you have made an error.

### subtopic_3 — Reading HDI Categories, Rankings and Maps
**Assistant:** What is the inequality-adjusted HDI and why is South Africa's so low?

**Tutor:** The inequality-adjusted HDI, or IHDI, was introduced by the UNDP in 2010 to address a major weakness of the HDI: the fact that it is based on national averages. The IHDI adjusts each of the three dimensions according to how unequally health, education and income are distributed among the population. If everyone in a country had exactly the same life expectancy, education and income, the IHDI would equal the HDI. The more unequal the distribution, the further the IHDI falls below the HDI. The difference between the two, expressed as a percentage, is called the loss due to inequality. South Africa has one of the largest losses in the world, mainly because of income inequality: its Gini coefficient of about 0,63 is among the highest anywhere, with the richest tenth of the population receiving a very large share of national income while many households depend on social grants. There are also large inequalities in education quality and health outcomes between rich and poor, urban and rural, and between provinces, rooted in colonial and apartheid policies such as the homelands, the Group Areas Act and Bantu Education, and continued by high unemployment. The IHDI shows that South Africa's high HDI category describes an average that many South Africans do not experience. For geographers, it is a reminder to look beyond averages to how development is distributed.

### subtopic_4 — Limitations of the HDI, Related Indices and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why does the HDI use a geometric mean instead of an ordinary average?

**Tutor:** Until 2010 the HDI was calculated as a simple arithmetic average of the three dimension indices: add them and divide by three. The UNDP changed to the geometric mean, which multiplies the three indices and takes the cube root, for an important reason. With an arithmetic average, a very high score in one dimension could fully compensate for a very low score in another. A country with very high income but poor health could still achieve a high HDI. The geometric mean reduces this substitution: a low value in any one dimension pulls the overall result down more strongly, so balanced development across health, education and income is rewarded. You can see the effect with extreme numbers. Suppose a country scored 1,0 for income, 1,0 for education and only 0,1 for health. The arithmetic average would be 2,1 divided by 3, which is 0,70, a high score. The geometric mean would be the cube root of 1,0 times 1,0 times 0,1, which is the cube root of 0,1, about 0,46, a low score. The geometric mean better reflects the idea that people in that country are not well off if they die young, however rich the country is. In our more balanced worked example, the two methods give almost the same answer, about 0,734, because the three indices are close together. The difference matters most when development is lopsided.
