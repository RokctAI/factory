# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the meaning of development in Geography. It covers the definition of development as a process of change that improves people's quality of life; the economic, social and environmental aspects of development; the terms used to classify countries, including developed and developing, more economically developed and less economically developed, and the Global North and Global South; and development within South Africa, where great differences exist within one country. The anchor idea is that development is not the same as economic growth: a country can become richer while many of its people remain poor, unhealthy or unsafe, and growth that destroys the environment can undermine future development. The governing idea is that development is multi-dimensional and must be judged by its effects on people and places, now and in the future.

## Subtopic: Defining Development

In Geography, development is the process by which the quality of life of people in a place improves over time. Quality of life refers to people's well-being: whether they have enough food, clean water, safe housing, good health, education, work that pays a living wage, security, and the freedom to make choices about their own lives. Standard of living is a narrower idea, usually referring to material wealth and the goods and services people can afford. When a family moves from a shack with no electricity to a brick house with water and power, its standard of living rises; when its children can also attend a good school, feel safe and be treated with dignity, its quality of life rises too.

Development is a process, not a fixed state. No country is completely developed, and all countries continue to change. Development can also go backwards: war, disease, natural disasters, corruption or economic collapse can reverse gains. For example, life expectancy in South Africa fell sharply in the late 1990s and early 2000s during the HIV and Aids epidemic, from over sixty years to the low fifties, and then rose again after the national antiretroviral treatment programme expanded from 2004, reaching about 65 years by the late 2010s. This shows that development must be measured over time, not at a single moment.

The economist Amartya Sen, who won the Nobel Prize for Economics in 1998, described development as freedom: the expansion of the real choices people have to live lives they value. In his view, income is important because it gives people choices, but income alone is not the goal. A person who is rich but cannot read, vote or move freely is not fully developed in this sense. This idea strongly influenced the Human Development Index you will meet later in this topic.

It is useful to recognise that development is a contested idea. People in different places may value different things: one community may prioritise land and farming, another factory jobs, another protecting cultural heritage or forests. Development decisions often involve trade-offs, such as building a dam that provides electricity and water but floods villages and farmland. Geographers therefore ask: development for whom, decided by whom, and at what cost?

So the next question is: what are the different aspects that make up development?

## Subtopic: Economic, Social and Environmental Aspects of Development

Geographers usually describe three connected aspects of development.

Economic development concerns wealth, production and income. It includes growth in the size of the economy, measured by gross domestic product or GDP, which is the total value of goods and services produced in a country in a year; the creation of jobs; rising incomes; better infrastructure such as roads, ports, electricity and internet; and a shift in the structure of the economy. In many countries, development has involved a shift from primary activities, such as farming, fishing and mining, to secondary activities, such as manufacturing, and then to tertiary activities, such as services, banking, tourism and retail, and quaternary activities based on information and research. A country whose economy depends mainly on exporting one raw material, such as copper or cocoa, is vulnerable to falling world prices.

Social development concerns people's well-being and their relationships in society. It includes access to health care and clean water, literacy and education, adequate housing, gender equality, human rights, safety from crime and violence, and the freedom to participate in decisions. Social development can lag behind economic growth. A country may discover oil and become wealthier on average, but if the income is captured by a small elite, most citizens may see little improvement in schools or clinics.

Environmental development, often linked to the idea of sustainability, concerns how development affects the natural world and whether the resources that support life are protected for the future. Clean air and water, healthy soils, forests, fish stocks and a stable climate are part of quality of life. Development that pollutes rivers, causes soil erosion or contributes to climate change may raise incomes in the short term but harm people later, especially the poor, who depend most directly on natural resources and are most exposed to floods, droughts and pollution.

These aspects interact. Education, a social factor, increases the skills of workers and therefore economic productivity. Economic growth provides taxes to pay for clinics and schools. Environmental damage, such as drought made worse by deforestation, can destroy farming incomes and force people to migrate. Good development tries to improve all three together, an idea you will meet again in the lesson on sustainable development.

A South African example shows all three aspects at once. The Medupi and Kusile coal-fired power stations in Limpopo and Mpumalanga were built to end electricity shortages, which supports economic development by allowing factories and mines to operate and social development by bringing power to homes and schools. Yet they also emit large amounts of carbon dioxide and use scarce water in dry regions, raising environmental concerns. At the same time, the Renewable Energy Independent Power Producer Procurement Programme, started in 2011, has brought solar and wind farms to places such as the Northern Cape, creating local jobs and community trust funds with lower emissions. Comparing such projects is a good way to practise judging development on all three legs rather than one.

So the next question is: how do geographers group countries according to their level of development?

## Subtopic: Classifying Countries: Developed, Developing, North and South

Geographers and international organisations have used several systems to classify countries, and each has limitations.

The most common terms are developed and developing countries. Developed countries, such as Germany, Japan, Canada and Australia, have high incomes, advanced industries and services, good infrastructure and high levels of health and education for most people. Developing countries, such as Malawi, Bangladesh or Bolivia, have lower average incomes and face challenges in providing health, education and infrastructure for all. Some geographers use the terms more economically developed countries, or MEDCs, and less economically developed countries, or LEDCs, to make clear that the classification is mainly about economic measures.

In the 1980s the Brandt Report, written by a commission led by the former West German chancellor Willy Brandt, drew a line around the world dividing the wealthy Global North from the poorer Global South. Most rich countries lay in the northern hemisphere, although Australia and New Zealand were placed in the North. The line is still used, but many geographers now regard it as too simple, because countries such as South Korea, Singapore and the Gulf states have developed rapidly, and China has become the world's second-largest economy while still having many poor citizens.

There are also newer terms. Newly industrialised countries, or NICs, are countries that moved quickly from farming-based to manufacturing-based economies, such as South Korea, Taiwan, Malaysia and Mexico. Emerging economies is a term used for large, fast-growing economies such as Brazil, India, China and South Africa, which together with Russia form the BRICS group; South Africa joined in 2010. The World Bank classifies countries by gross national income per person into low-income, lower-middle-income, upper-middle-income and high-income groups; South Africa is classified as upper-middle-income.

The limitation of all these labels is that they treat a country as a single unit and can suggest that development is a ladder that every country climbs in the same way, with rich Western countries at the top. Critics argue that the word developing can be patronising, ignores the wealth of culture and community found in poorer countries, and hides the historical reasons for inequality, such as colonialism, which you will study in this term's lesson on factors affecting development.

So the next question is: what happens when we look closely inside a single country, such as our own country, South Africa?

## Subtopic: Development Within South Africa and the Error Museum

South Africa is one of the clearest examples of uneven development within one country. It has highly developed areas, such as the business districts of Sandton and Cape Town, world-class universities, hospitals and an advanced financial sector, alongside townships and rural villages where people lack running water, reliable electricity, good schools and jobs. By the World Bank's measure, South Africa has one of the most unequal income distributions in the world, with a Gini coefficient of about 0,63, where 0 means everyone has the same income and 1 means one person has everything.

These differences have strong historical roots. Colonial conquest, the 1913 Natives Land Act, which reserved about 7 per cent of land, later about 13 per cent, for the African majority, and apartheid's homelands and Group Areas Act deliberately concentrated investment in white areas and kept black South Africans in overcrowded, underdeveloped areas with poor services. You will study the history of these laws in Term 3. Since 1994, governments have built millions of subsidised houses and extended electricity, water and social grants, but unemployment has remained very high, at more than thirty per cent on the official definition in recent years, and is highest among young people.

Development also differs between provinces and between urban and rural areas. Gauteng and the Western Cape have higher average incomes and more services than Limpopo or the Eastern Cape. Within cities, wealthy suburbs may sit beside informal settlements. A geographer studying development in South Africa therefore uses maps, statistics and fieldwork at several scales: national, provincial, municipal and even ward level.

When you answer a source-based question on development in South Africa, look carefully at the scale of the data. A table of provincial unemployment rates, a map of households with piped water by municipality, or a photograph of one street each tells a different part of the story. Ask what the source shows, what it leaves out, who produced it and when. Census data from Statistics South Africa, collected in 1996, 2001, 2011 and 2022, is one of the most important sources, because it allows changes in services such as electricity, water and sanitation to be compared over almost thirty years.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Development means economic growth." Growth is one part of development, but development also includes health, education, rights and the environment. A country can grow richer while most people remain poor.

Exhibit two: "Developed countries have no poverty." All countries have inequality; even rich countries have homeless people and poor regions.

Exhibit three: "The Global North and South are exactly the northern and southern hemispheres." Australia and New Zealand are in the southern hemisphere but were placed in the North; the line is about wealth, not latitude.

Exhibit four: "A country's average income tells us how most people live." An average can be raised by a small, very rich group. In an unequal country like South Africa, the average hides very large differences.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a family's life getting better, a three-legged pot, and a map with a wavy line.

## Subtopic: A Family's Life Getting Better

Imagine a family in a village. Five years ago they carried water from a river, cooked on a wood fire, and the nearest clinic was two hours away on foot. Today there is a tap at the end of their street, electricity in the house, a clinic in the next village and a school where the children are learning to code. Their life has got better. That is what development means: a process where people's quality of life improves.

Quality of life is about more than money. It means being healthy, educated, safe, having a decent home, enough food and the freedom to make choices about your life. A family could earn more money, but if they live in fear of crime or their water is polluted, their quality of life is not much better.

Development can also go backwards. A war, a drought or a disease can undo years of progress. South Africa saw this with HIV and Aids, when people's average life span dropped for some years, and then rose again when treatment became widely available.

So what are the different parts of development?

## Subtopic: A Three-Legged Pot

Think of a traditional three-legged pot. If one leg is broken, the pot falls over. Development has three legs too.

The first leg is economic: money, jobs, businesses, roads and electricity. The second leg is social: health, education, housing, safety, rights and equality between men and women. The third leg is environmental: clean air and water, healthy soil, and using resources in a way that leaves enough for your children and grandchildren.

If a country only grows the money leg, for example by mining very fast, but the rivers get polluted and the schools stay poor, the pot wobbles. Good development keeps all three legs strong at the same time.

So how do we group countries by how developed they are?

## Subtopic: A Map With a Wavy Line

Look at a world map. People often split countries into developed countries, like Germany or Japan, and developing countries, like Malawi or Bangladesh. In the 1980s a report drew a wavy line around the world: rich countries above it were called the North, and poorer countries below it the South. But that line is about money, not the equator, because rich Australia was put in the North.

Countries do not stay in one box. South Korea was poor in the 1950s and is now rich. China is now the second-biggest economy in the world, even though many Chinese people are still poor.

South Africa is a country with two faces. Sandton has skyscrapers and luxury shops; a few kilometres away are informal settlements without proper toilets. This is partly because of history: under colonialism and apartheid, investment went mostly to white areas. Since 1994, millions of houses and electricity connections have been provided, but unemployment, especially for young people, is still very high.

Here is the line to remember: development is about people's whole quality of life, it has three legs, and an average can hide very big differences.
