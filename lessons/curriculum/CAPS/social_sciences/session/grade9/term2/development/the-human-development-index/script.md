# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the Human Development Index. It covers the origins and purpose of the HDI; its three dimensions and four indicators; how each dimension index is calculated and combined into a single value between 0 and 1; the four HDI categories and how to interpret rankings and maps, including South Africa's position; and the limitations of the HDI and related indices such as the inequality-adjusted HDI. The anchor calculation is a worked example: a country with life expectancy of 65 years, expected schooling of 13,6 years, mean schooling of 11,6 years and GNI per capita of 14 000 PPP dollars has an HDI of about 0,734, which places it in the high human development category, close to South Africa's recent value of about 0,72. The governing idea is that the HDI puts people, not just money, at the centre of development, but like every index it simplifies and must be read critically.

## Subtopic: Why the HDI Was Created and Its Three Dimensions

Before 1990, countries were usually ranked by GDP or GNI per capita alone. The Pakistani economist Mahbub ul Haq, working with the Indian economist Amartya Sen, argued that this was misleading, because income is a means to development, not its purpose. Two countries with similar incomes could offer their people very different lives: one might invest in schools and clinics, while another spent on weapons or allowed wealth to be captured by a few. In 1990 the United Nations Development Programme, the UNDP, published the first Human Development Report, introducing the HDI. Ul Haq described the purpose of development as enlarging people's choices, and Sen described it as expanding the freedoms people have to live lives they value.

The HDI measures three basic dimensions of human development.

The first is a long and healthy life, measured by life expectancy at birth. Longevity is valuable in itself and reflects nutrition, health care, clean water and safety.

The second is knowledge, measured by two indicators: mean years of schooling, which is the average number of years of education received by adults aged 25 and older, and expected years of schooling, which is the number of years of schooling a child entering school now can expect to receive if current enrolment patterns continue. The first reflects the education of the current adult population; the second reflects the opportunities available to today's children.

The third is a decent standard of living, measured by gross national income per capita, adjusted for purchasing power parity and expressed in PPP dollars. Income stands for the resources people need for food, shelter and other necessities and choices.

So the HDI has three dimensions but four indicators: life expectancy, mean years of schooling, expected years of schooling and GNI per capita. It deliberately excludes many things, such as political freedom, environmental sustainability and inequality, to keep the index simple and comparable across almost two hundred countries. The UNDP publishes additional indices to cover some of these, which we discuss later in the lesson.

The HDI is published in the annual Human Development Report, which also chooses a theme each year, such as climate change, gender, work or inequality, and includes detailed statistical tables for every country. South Africa also publishes provincial studies, and researchers have calculated HDI values for provinces and even municipalities, which show, for example, that the Western Cape and Gauteng score considerably higher than Limpopo or the Eastern Cape. This allows the same tool to be used at different scales, which is one of the key skills of geography.

So the next question is: how are these very different measurements combined into one number?

## Subtopic: Calculating the HDI Step by Step

Life expectancy is measured in years, schooling in years, and income in dollars, so they cannot simply be added together. The HDI solves this by converting each one into an index between 0 and 1, using minimum and maximum values called goalposts. The formula for each dimension index is: actual value minus minimum value, divided by maximum value minus minimum value.

For health, the goalposts are 20 years and 85 years. A country with life expectancy of 65 years has a health index of 65 minus 20, which is 45, divided by 85 minus 20, which is 65. Forty-five divided by sixty-five gives about 0,692. A country with life expectancy of 80 would score 60 divided by 65, about 0,923.

For education, expected years of schooling uses goalposts of 0 and 18 years, and mean years of schooling uses 0 and 15 years. A country with expected schooling of 13,6 years scores 13,6 divided by 18, which is about 0,756, and mean schooling of 11,6 years scores 11,6 divided by 15, about 0,773. The education index is the average of these two: 0,756 plus 0,773, divided by 2, which is about 0,764.

For income, the goalposts are 100 and 75 000 PPP dollars, but the calculation uses natural logarithms. This reflects the idea that each extra dollar matters more to a poor person than to a rich one: an extra 1 000 dollars transforms life for a family earning 2 000, but makes little difference to one earning 70 000. With GNI per capita of 14 000 dollars, the income index works out to about 0,746. You will not be expected to calculate logarithms in Grade 9, but you should understand why income is treated this way.

Finally, the three dimension indices are combined using the geometric mean: multiply them together and take the cube root. So HDI equals the cube root of 0,692 times 0,764 times 0,746, which is about 0,734. The geometric mean is used because it means poor performance in one dimension cannot be fully compensated by high performance in another. A country cannot achieve a very high HDI through income alone if its people die young.

The result, about 0,734, is close to South Africa's recent HDI of about 0,72, and our example values are close to South Africa's actual indicators.

It is worth pausing on what the example shows about South Africa. Its education indices are relatively strong, because most children now enrol in school for many years, and its income index is moderate, because it is an upper-middle-income country. Its health index is the weakest of the three, because life expectancy is still lower than in many countries with similar incomes. The HDI therefore points to where improvement would raise the index most: in South Africa's case, better health outcomes, through measures such as reducing HIV infections, tuberculosis, maternal deaths and violence. In this way, the HDI is not only a ranking but also a diagnostic tool for policy.

So the next question is: what do HDI values tell us, and where does South Africa stand?

## Subtopic: Reading HDI Categories, Rankings and Maps

The UNDP groups countries into four categories. Very high human development is an HDI of 0,800 or above; high is 0,700 to 0,799; medium is 0,550 to 0,699; and low is below 0,550. In recent reports Switzerland, Norway and Iceland have been at the top with values above 0,95, while countries such as Somalia, South Sudan and the Central African Republic have been at the bottom with values below 0,40. South Africa, at about 0,72, is in the high category and ranked around 110th out of about 190 countries.

On a choropleth map of HDI, where countries are shaded by category, a clear pattern appears. Very high human development dominates Europe, North America, Japan, South Korea, Australia and New Zealand, and some Gulf states. Low human development is concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa, together with countries such as Afghanistan, Yemen and Haiti. Latin America, North Africa, the Middle East and much of Asia fall mainly in the high and medium categories. Within Southern Africa, Mauritius and the Seychelles have very high or high HDI, Botswana and South Africa high, and Lesotho, Malawi and Mozambique low or medium.

Comparing HDI rank with GNI per capita rank reveals interesting stories. Some countries rank much higher on the HDI than on income, which means they convert income into health and education effectively; Sri Lanka and Cuba are often cited. Others rank much lower on the HDI than on income, which suggests that income is not reaching health and education for most people. South Africa's HDI rank has historically been lower than its income rank, because life expectancy fell during the HIV epidemic and because of educational and other inequalities inherited from apartheid.

Trends over time are also important. Most countries' HDI has risen since 1990, especially in East Asia. South Africa's HDI fell in the late 1990s and early 2000s as life expectancy dropped and then rose again with the rollout of antiretroviral treatment. In 2020 and 2021 the global HDI fell for the first time, because of the Covid-19 pandemic, before recovering. When interpreting an HDI graph, describe the trend, give values and years, and suggest reasons.

To answer an HDI data-response question well, use a clear method. First, identify what the source shows: the countries, the years and the units. Second, describe the pattern or trend with figures, for example that a country's HDI rose from 0,63 to 0,72 over a period. Third, compare, noting which countries are higher or lower and by how much. Fourth, explain, linking differences to factors such as health care, education, income, conflict or inequality. Finally, evaluate, considering what the HDI cannot show.

So the final question is: what does the HDI leave out?

## Subtopic: Limitations of the HDI, Related Indices and the Error Museum

The HDI is a major improvement on income alone, but it has limitations. First, it is an average and hides inequality. Two countries with the same HDI could distribute health, education and income very differently. To address this, the UNDP publishes the inequality-adjusted HDI, or IHDI, which discounts each dimension according to how unequally it is shared. South Africa's IHDI is much lower than its HDI, one of the largest losses due to inequality in the world, reflecting its very high Gini coefficient.

Second, the HDI says nothing about the quality of education or health, only quantities such as years of schooling. A learner may spend twelve years in school yet leave unable to read for meaning. Third, it ignores environmental sustainability: a country can reach a high HDI while emitting large amounts of carbon dioxide and using resources faster than they can be replaced. The UNDP has experimented with a planetary pressures-adjusted HDI to account for this. Fourth, it excludes political freedom, human rights, safety and gender equality. The Gender Development Index and the Gender Inequality Index cover some of these, and the Multidimensional Poverty Index looks at overlapping household deprivations such as lack of electricity, clean water, schooling and nutrition. Fifth, data quality varies and figures are often estimates, revised from year to year, so small differences in rank should not be over-interpreted.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "The HDI measures income only." It combines health, education and income, which is its main purpose.

Exhibit two: "The three indices are simply added together." They are combined using the geometric mean, which means a very low score in one dimension pulls the whole HDI down.

Exhibit three: "An HDI of 0,72 means 72 per cent of people are developed." The HDI is an index between 0 and 1, not a percentage of people.

Exhibit four: "A high HDI means everyone in the country lives well." The HDI is an average; in South Africa the inequality-adjusted HDI is much lower than the HDI.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three things: a three-part test, a recipe for a single score, and a league table.

## Subtopic: A Three-Part Test

Imagine judging how well a country looks after its people, but you are only allowed three questions. Which three would you choose? In 1990 two economists, Mahbub ul Haq from Pakistan and Amartya Sen from India, chose these.

First: do people live long and healthy lives? We measure this with life expectancy, how long a newborn baby can expect to live.

Second: do people get knowledge? We measure this with how many years adults have been to school, and how many years children starting school now are expected to stay.

Third: do people have a decent standard of living? We measure this with average income per person, adjusted for local prices.

These three questions make up the Human Development Index, or HDI. It was made because money alone does not tell you whether people are healthy or educated. A country can be rich but let people die young, or quite poor but look after health and schools very well.

Think about your own community for a moment. Do older people there live long lives? Do most young people finish school? Do families have enough income for food, transport and school uniforms? You are already thinking like the HDI.

So how do we turn three different answers into one score?

## Subtopic: A Recipe for One Score

Years and dollars are like apples and oranges: you cannot just add them. So each answer is turned into a score between 0 and 1.

For health, a life expectancy of 20 years counts as 0 and 85 years counts as 1. If people live 65 years on average, we work out 65 minus 20, which is 45, and divide by 85 minus 20, which is 65. That gives about 0,69.

We do the same for schooling and income. In our example, schooling scores about 0,76 and income about 0,75.

Then we combine the three scores in a special way: multiply them and take the cube root. This way, a country cannot get a great score just by being rich if its people die young. Our example comes out at about 0,73, which is close to South Africa's real score of about 0,72.

So what do these scores tell us?

## Subtopic: The League Table

Every year the United Nations publishes a league table of almost 200 countries. A score of 0,800 or more is very high, 0,700 to 0,799 is high, 0,550 to 0,699 is medium, and below 0,550 is low.

At the top are countries like Switzerland, Norway and Iceland, with scores above 0,95. At the bottom are countries like Somalia and South Sudan, below 0,40. South Africa is in the high group, around 110th place.

But remember the cake from the last lesson. The HDI is an average. In South Africa, where some people have a lot and many have very little, the score drops a lot when the United Nations adjusts it for inequality. And the HDI does not show whether learners are learning well, whether the air is clean, or whether people are free and safe.

Here is the line to remember: the HDI puts people first by combining health, education and income, but it is still an average, so always ask who is left out.
