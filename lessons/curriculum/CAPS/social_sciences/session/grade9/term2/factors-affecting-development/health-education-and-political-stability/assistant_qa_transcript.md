### subtopic_1 — Health and Welfare
**Assistant:** Why does malaria slow down a country's development?

**Tutor:** Malaria slows development through its effects on individuals, households and the whole economy. For individuals, repeated attacks of fever and anaemia make adults too weak to work for days or weeks, and severe malaria can kill, especially young children and pregnant women. Children who survive repeated infections may suffer lasting effects on their growth and learning, and they miss school during illness. For households, malaria means lost income, money spent on medicine and transport to clinics, and time spent caring for the sick, often by girls and women who then miss school or work. In farming communities, malaria season often coincides with the rainy planting and harvest season, so illness can cost a family its food supply for the year. For the economy, malaria reduces the productivity of the labour force, raises health spending and discourages tourism and foreign investment in affected regions. Economists have estimated that countries with intensive malaria grew more slowly than similar countries without it. Prevention is relatively cheap compared to these costs: insecticide-treated bed nets, indoor spraying, draining standing water and rapid testing and treatment have reduced malaria deaths substantially since 2000. In 2021 the World Health Organization recommended the first malaria vaccine for children in Africa. South Africa has malaria only in parts of Limpopo, Mpumalanga and northern KwaZulu-Natal, and has made strong progress towards elimination.

### subtopic_2 — Education and the Quality of Learning
**Assistant:** Why is educating girls described as one of the best investments in development?

**Tutor:** Because the benefits of girls' education spread far beyond the individual girl to her family, community and country. A girl who completes secondary school is more likely to earn an income and to earn more, which raises household incomes and gives her more say in family decisions. Educated women tend to marry later and have fewer children, which slows rapid population growth and allows families to invest more in each child. Their children are healthier: research across many countries shows that children of mothers with secondary education have much lower mortality rates and are better nourished, partly because educated mothers understand nutrition, hygiene and vaccination and are more confident using health services. Their children are also more likely to attend and complete school, so the benefits continue across generations. Educated women are better able to protect themselves from HIV, exploitation and violence, and to participate in community leadership and politics. Yet millions of girls worldwide are still out of school. Barriers include poverty, when families choose to educate sons; early marriage and pregnancy; household chores such as collecting water; long, unsafe journeys to school; lack of toilets and sanitary products; and, in some countries, laws or armed groups that ban girls from school. Solutions include free schooling, bursaries, safe transport, school toilets, free sanitary pads, programmes that keep pregnant learners in school, and campaigns against child marriage.

### subtopic_3 — Political Stability, Conflict and Governance
**Assistant:** How did Botswana avoid the resource curse?

**Tutor:** The resource curse describes countries rich in natural resources, such as oil or diamonds, that experience corruption, conflict and slow development. Botswana is often given as an example of a country that avoided it. Several factors help explain this. First, its political institutions were strong. Botswana has held regular democratic elections since independence in 1966, and its first president, Seretse Khama, and his successors built a professional civil service with relatively low corruption. Traditional institutions such as the kgotla, a community meeting where leaders consult the people, supported a culture of consultation. Second, the government negotiated a strong deal with the mining company De Beers. Their joint venture, Debswana, formed in 1969, gave the state a large share of diamond profits. Third, revenue was invested in long-term development, such as roads, schools, clinics and water, rather than spent on prestige projects or lost to corruption, and some was saved in a fund for the future. Fourth, the country was peaceful, with no civil war. As a result, Botswana grew rapidly for decades and became an upper-middle-income country. However, it still faces challenges: the economy depends heavily on diamonds, whose prices can fall, unemployment is high, inequality is large, and HIV infection rates were among the highest in the world, although its treatment programme has been successful. Botswana shows that resources plus good governance can drive development.

### subtopic_4 — Cycles of Poverty and Development, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What is a cycle of poverty and how can it be broken?

**Tutor:** A cycle of poverty is a situation in which the effects of poverty become causes of further poverty, so families and communities remain poor across generations. Consider a typical cycle. A poor household cannot afford nutritious food, so children may be stunted and frequently sick. Sick, hungry children struggle to concentrate and learn, and may leave school early to earn money or care for family members. Without good education, they find only insecure, low-paid jobs or none at all. With low incomes, they cannot afford good food, housing or health care for their own children, and the cycle repeats. At the community level, poor areas collect little tax, so local schools and clinics are weak, and lack of opportunity can lead to crime and conflict, discouraging investment. Breaking the cycle requires interventions at several points at the same time. Social grants and school feeding schemes protect children from hunger. Early childhood development programmes and strong foundational teaching of reading and mathematics give children a solid start. Clinics, clean water and vaccinations improve health. Bursaries, technical colleges and skills programmes link young people to jobs. Job creation through investment, small business support and public employment programmes provides income. Good governance ensures that public money reaches the intended services. In South Africa, combinations of these measures, such as grants, free schooling in poorer areas and school meals, have reduced extreme poverty, but high unemployment shows that the cycle is far from broken.
