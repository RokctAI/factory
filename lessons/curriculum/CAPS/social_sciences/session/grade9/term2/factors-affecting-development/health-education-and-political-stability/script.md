# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains how health and welfare, education and political stability affect development. It covers the effects of disease and poor health on individuals, households and economies, and the role of health care and social welfare; the contribution of education to income, health and social change, including the special importance of girls' education and the quality of learning; the effects of political stability, conflict, corruption and governance on development; and the way these factors reinforce one another in cycles of poverty or of development. The anchor examples are Botswana, which used diamond wealth under stable democratic government to become one of Africa's most developed countries, and countries in conflict, where war has reversed decades of progress. The governing idea is that people are both the means and the end of development: healthy, educated people living in peaceful, well-governed societies are able to build better lives, and better lives in turn make people healthier, better educated and more secure.

## Subtopic: Health and Welfare

Health is both a goal of development and a cause of it. A healthy population can work, learn and care for others; an unhealthy population loses income, schooling and life itself. The World Health Organization defines health not merely as the absence of disease but as a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being.

Disease has major economic effects. Malaria, spread by the Anopheles mosquito, killed more than 600 000 people in 2022, about 95 per cent of them in Africa, most of them children under five. In countries where malaria is common, workers lose days of work, children miss school, families spend scarce income on treatment, and tourism and investment are discouraged. HIV and Aids had an even greater impact in Southern Africa: because it mainly killed adults of working age, it reduced the labour force, left many children orphaned and raised the costs of health care. South Africa has the largest number of people living with HIV in the world, about 7,8 million, and also the world's largest antiretroviral treatment programme, which has greatly increased life expectancy since the mid-2000s. Tuberculosis remains one of the leading causes of death in South Africa, often linked to HIV.

Health depends on more than hospitals. Clean water and sanitation prevent diarrhoea and cholera; good nutrition prevents stunting, which affects a child's physical and mental development for life; vaccinations prevent measles and polio; and safe housing and clean air reduce respiratory disease. In South Africa, more than one in five children under five is estimated to be stunted, which shows how poverty and poor nutrition affect the next generation's ability to learn and earn.

Welfare refers to government and community support that protects people from poverty and hardship. It includes social grants, free health care, free basic services and school feeding. South Africa has one of the largest social grant systems in the developing world: by the early 2020s about 18 to 19 million people received regular grants, such as the child support grant, the old age grant and the disability grant, not counting the temporary Social Relief of Distress grant introduced during Covid-19. The National School Nutrition Programme provides a daily meal to about nine million learners. Research shows grants reduce hunger and help keep children in school, though critics point out they cannot replace jobs.

Health systems are themselves unequally distributed. Many doctors in developing countries emigrate to richer countries for better pay and conditions, a process called the brain drain. In rural South Africa, the ratio of doctors to people is far lower than in cities.

So the next question is: how does education affect development?

## Subtopic: Education and the Quality of Learning

Education is often described as the most powerful tool for development. It gives individuals skills and knowledge that raise their productivity and earnings; it enables people to understand their rights, participate in democracy and make informed decisions about health; and it allows societies to develop scientists, engineers, doctors, teachers and entrepreneurs.

The economic returns are well documented. Studies by the World Bank across many countries estimate that each additional year of schooling raises a person's earnings by around 9 to 10 per cent on average. At the national level, countries that invested heavily in education, such as South Korea, Singapore and Finland, achieved rapid development.

The education of girls has especially strong effects. Women who have completed secondary school tend to marry later, have fewer children, and have healthier children with better nutrition and higher vaccination rates. Their children are more likely to attend school, so the benefits pass to the next generation. Educated women are more likely to earn an income, to participate in decision-making and to be protected from exploitation. For this reason, gender equality in education is one of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals. In many countries girls still face barriers: early marriage, household chores, lack of sanitary products and toilets at school, the cost of fees and uniforms, and in some places violence or laws that restrict girls' education.

However, the number of years in school is not enough; what matters is what children learn. In South Africa almost all children now attend primary school, and the Grade 12 pass rate has risen, but international assessments show serious weaknesses in foundational skills. The Progress in International Reading Literacy Study of 2021 found that about 81 per cent of South African Grade 4 learners could not read for meaning in any language. Weak early reading and mathematics make later learning difficult, contributing to high dropout before Grade 12 and to youth unemployment. Causes include the legacy of Bantu Education, which deliberately limited black learners' education from 1953, overcrowded classes, shortages of books in African languages, teacher training and absenteeism, and the effects of poverty and hunger on children.

Education must also match the needs of the economy. Countries with too few graduates in fields such as engineering, technology, health and trades face skills shortages, while many school-leavers without these skills remain unemployed. Technical and vocational education and training colleges aim to close this gap.

So the next question is: why does political stability matter so much?

## Subtopic: Political Stability, Conflict and Governance

Political stability means that a country is governed peacefully, with power changing hands through agreed rules such as elections, rather than through violence, coups or civil war. Good governance includes the rule of law, accountable and honest officials, protection of property and human rights, an independent judiciary and effective public services.

Conflict is one of the strongest causes of underdevelopment. War destroys infrastructure such as roads, bridges, schools and clinics; kills and injures people; forces millions to flee as refugees or internally displaced people; disrupts farming and trade, leading to famine; and diverts government spending to the military. Investors avoid conflict zones. The economist Paul Collier described a conflict trap, in which poverty makes war more likely and war makes poverty deeper. The eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, for example, has suffered decades of conflict linked to armed groups, ethnic tensions and competition for minerals such as coltan, gold and tin, even though the country is extremely rich in resources. South Sudan, independent since 2011, fell into civil war in 2013, and Sudan into a devastating war in 2023.

Corruption also undermines development. When officials steal public money, take bribes or award contracts to friends, money meant for schools, hospitals, water and roads is lost, services fail, and citizens lose trust. In South Africa, the Zondo Commission, which sat from 2018 to 2022, investigated allegations of state capture and found widespread corruption in state-owned enterprises such as Eskom, Transnet and the South African Revenue Service, contributing to electricity shortages and weak economic growth.

Botswana offers a contrasting example. At independence in 1966 it was one of the poorest countries in the world, with very few kilometres of tarred road and few secondary school graduates. Diamonds were discovered at Orapa in 1967. Unlike some resource-rich countries, Botswana's governments managed the diamond revenue carefully through a partnership with the mining company De Beers, invested in roads, schools and health, and maintained regular democratic elections and low corruption. Botswana became an upper-middle-income country, although it still faces high inequality, unemployment and HIV rates. Rwanda shows another path: after the genocide of 1994, in which about 800 000 people, mostly Tutsi, were murdered, the country achieved rapid economic growth and improvements in health and education, although critics express concern about limits on political freedom.

So the final question is: how do these factors interact?

## Subtopic: Cycles of Poverty and Development, and the Error Museum

Health, education and political stability do not act separately. They reinforce one another in cycles. In a cycle of poverty, a poor family cannot afford good food or health care, so children are often sick and malnourished; sick, hungry children learn less and leave school early; with little education, they find only low-paid work or none, so they remain poor and their own children face the same disadvantages. At the national level, a poor country collects little tax, so it cannot fund good schools and clinics; an unhealthy, poorly educated workforce produces less; and frustration and inequality can fuel conflict, which deepens poverty.

The same links can create a cycle of development. Investment in clean water and vaccination reduces child deaths; when parents are confident their children will survive, families tend to have fewer children and can invest more in each child's education; educated workers earn more and pay more tax; governments use the tax to improve services; and a growing middle class demands accountable government, supporting stability. East Asian countries such as South Korea are often used as examples of this virtuous cycle.

A South African case study shows these links clearly. In former homeland areas of the Eastern Cape, many villages have high unemployment, households depend on old age and child support grants, and young adults migrate to Cape Town or Gauteng in search of work. Schools may be small and under-resourced, clinics far away and water supplies unreliable. When young people leave, villages lose energy and skills, while the migrants often settle in informal settlements on the edges of cities, where health and educational challenges continue. Improving rural schools, roads, water and local economic opportunities, such as agriculture and tourism, would affect several parts of the cycle at once.

Breaking a cycle of poverty requires action at several points at once: social grants and school feeding to protect the poorest, investment in early childhood development and foundational literacy, clinics and clean water, job creation, and honest, stable government. No single factor is enough.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Health only matters to doctors, not to the economy." Illness reduces work, schooling and investment; health is a major economic factor.

Exhibit two: "More years in school automatically means more development." The quality of learning matters: years in school without reading and numeracy skills bring limited benefits.

Exhibit three: "Countries rich in minerals will always develop." Without stable, honest government, mineral wealth can fuel corruption and conflict, as the contrast between Botswana and the eastern Congo shows.

Exhibit four: "Social grants make people lazy." Research in South Africa finds that grants mainly support children and the elderly, reduce hunger and help keep children in school, and that recipients often use them to search for work.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a worker with a fever, a ladder of learning, and a calm house where everyone can get on with life.

## Subtopic: A Worker With a Fever

Imagine a farmer who gets malaria. For two weeks she has a high fever and cannot work in her fields. Her daughter stays home from school to look after her. The family spends its savings on medicine. By the time she is better, the planting season has passed. One mosquito bite has cost the family its harvest and the daughter some school.

Now multiply that by millions of people. Malaria killed more than 600 000 people in 2022, mostly young children in Africa. In Southern Africa, HIV and Aids made many adults sick at the age when they should have been working and raising children. South Africa now has the biggest HIV treatment programme in the world, and people are living much longer as a result.

Health is about more than hospitals: clean water, good food, vaccines and safe homes all keep people healthy. When people are poor, the government can help through welfare, such as South Africa's child support grants, old age grants and the school meals that about nine million learners eat every day.

Think about the clinic nearest to your home. How far is it, how long is the queue, and does it have the medicine people need? These everyday questions are part of development too.

So what about learning?

## Subtopic: A Ladder of Learning

Think of education as a ladder. Every step a person climbs, every extra year of good schooling, helps them reach better jobs and earn more. Studies suggest each extra year of school raises a person's earnings by around a tenth.

When girls climb the ladder, the whole family benefits. Women who finish school usually have healthier children, and those children are more likely to go to school too. But in some places girls are kept home to work, married young, or miss school because there are no proper toilets or sanitary pads.

But here is the catch: a ladder only helps if the steps are strong. If you sit in class for years but never learn to read properly, the steps break. In 2021, a study found that most South African Grade 4 learners could not yet read for meaning. That is why reading in the early grades is so important.

So why does peace matter so much for a country and its people?

## Subtopic: A Calm House

Imagine trying to do your homework in a house where people are fighting, the roof is falling in, and someone keeps stealing the family's money. You would not get much done. Countries are the same.

War destroys schools, clinics, roads and farms, and forces people to flee their homes. Corruption, when leaders steal public money, means fewer books, medicines and working electricity.

Botswana shows the opposite. In 1966 it was one of the poorest countries in the world. Then diamonds were found. Its leaders managed the money carefully, held fair elections and built roads and schools. Today it is much better off.

All three things connect. Sick children learn less, children who learn less earn less, and poor, angry countries are more likely to fight. But it works the other way too: healthy, educated people in a peaceful country can build a better life.

Here is the line to remember: health, learning and peace hold each other up, like the poles of a tent.
