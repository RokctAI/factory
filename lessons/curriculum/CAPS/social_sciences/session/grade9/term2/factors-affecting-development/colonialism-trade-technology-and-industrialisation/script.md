# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains historical and economic factors that cause differences in development between countries. It covers colonialism and its lasting effects on economies, borders and societies; patterns of world trade that disadvantage exporters of primary products, including terms of trade, tariffs and subsidies; the role of technology and industrialisation in raising productivity, and the gap between countries that industrialised early and those that did not; and South Africa's own development path from mining to manufacturing and services. The anchor idea is the colonial economy: a structure in which a colony exported raw materials cheaply and imported manufactured goods expensively, a pattern that in many countries continued after independence. The governing idea is that differences in development are not natural or accidental but have historical causes, and that understanding these causes is necessary to change them.

## Subtopic: Colonialism and Its Legacy

Colonialism is the control of one territory and its people by a foreign power, which governs it, settles it or exploits its resources. European colonialism expanded from the fifteenth century, when Portugal and Spain built empires in the Americas, Africa and Asia, followed by the Netherlands, Britain and France. In the late nineteenth century, a rapid period of conquest called the Scramble for Africa saw European powers divide almost the entire continent between them. At the Berlin Conference of 1884 to 1885, representatives of European states, with no Africans present, agreed on rules for claiming African territory. By 1914 only Ethiopia and Liberia remained independent.

Colonial powers organised their colonies' economies to serve their own needs. Colonies supplied raw materials, such as cotton, rubber, cocoa, palm oil, copper, gold and diamonds, and were markets for manufactured goods from the colonising country. Africans were often forced to grow cash crops for export instead of food, through taxes that had to be paid in cash, or through direct forced labour. In the Congo Free State, the personal possession of King Leopold II of Belgium from 1885 to 1908, Congolese people were forced to collect wild rubber under a brutal system of quotas, mutilation and hostage-taking; historians estimate that millions died through violence, famine and disease.

Infrastructure was built mainly to move raw materials to the coast. Railways in many African countries run from mines or plantations straight to ports, rather than connecting neighbouring towns and countries, which still makes trade within Africa difficult and expensive. Education and health services were limited, so at independence many countries had very few trained doctors, engineers or administrators. The Democratic Republic of the Congo became independent in 1960 with only a handful of African university graduates, by most accounts fewer than thirty, to run a country of about fifteen million people.

Colonial borders were drawn with little regard for the people living there. Some ethnic groups were split between several countries, and rival groups were combined within one, contributing in some cases to later conflicts. Land was taken for settlers in colonies such as Kenya, Zimbabwe and South Africa, leaving many people with small, poor plots. Some historians also emphasise that colonial rule disrupted existing African systems of trade, government and knowledge.

Historians debate how much present-day underdevelopment is due to colonialism and how much to other factors, including governance after independence, the Cold War, debt and conflict. Some former colonies, such as Singapore and South Korea, a Japanese colony from 1910 to 1945, developed rapidly. But the colonial legacy remains a major factor in explaining why many countries started independence with economies dependent on a few raw materials, weak infrastructure and few skilled people.

When you answer a question on colonialism, remember to be specific: name a colony, a resource, a policy and a consequence, rather than writing in general terms about Africa as a single place.

So the next question is: why did this pattern continue after colonies became independent?

## Subtopic: Unfair Trade and the Terms of Trade

Trade can drive development, but the way world trade is organised often disadvantages poorer countries. Many developing countries, especially in Africa, still depend on exporting primary products: unprocessed raw materials such as cocoa, coffee, tea, cotton, copper, oil or iron ore. For example, Zambia earns most of its export income from copper, and Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana together produce about 60 per cent of the world's cocoa.

There are several problems with this dependence. First, the prices of primary products are volatile: they can rise or fall sharply because of weather, changes in demand or speculation, making it hard for governments to plan budgets and for farmers to plan their lives. Second, over the long term, the prices of many primary products have tended to rise more slowly than the prices of manufactured goods. Economists describe this using the terms of trade, which compare the prices a country receives for its exports with the prices it pays for its imports.

Consider an example. Suppose a tractor costs 60 000 dollars. If coffee sells for 3 000 dollars per tonne, a coffee-exporting country must sell 20 tonnes to buy one tractor. If the coffee price falls to 2 000 dollars per tonne while the tractor price stays the same, it must now sell 30 tonnes for the same tractor. The country's terms of trade have worsened: it must export more just to import the same amount.

Third, most of the value of a product is added after the raw material leaves the producing country, through processing, manufacturing, branding and retail. A cocoa farmer in Ghana typically receives only a small share of the price of a chocolate bar, often estimated at around 6 per cent, while the chocolate makers, retailers and governments of rich countries capture most of the value. Processing the raw material at home would create jobs and income, but this is difficult. Rich countries have often charged higher import taxes, called tariffs, on processed goods than on raw materials, a practice called tariff escalation. And rich countries, such as those of the European Union and the United States, have paid large subsidies to their own farmers, allowing them to sell products such as cotton, sugar and maize cheaply on world markets and undercut farmers in poorer countries.

Responses to unfair trade include the Fairtrade movement, which since the late 1980s has guaranteed farmers a minimum price and a social premium for community projects; regional trade agreements such as the African Continental Free Trade Area, which began trading in 2021 and aims to increase trade among African countries; and efforts to process more raw materials locally, such as chocolate factories in Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire.

So the next question is: what role do technology and industrialisation play?

## Subtopic: Technology and Industrialisation

Technology refers to the tools, machines, methods and knowledge used to produce goods and services. Industrialisation is the process by which an economy shifts from mainly farming and handicrafts to manufacturing in factories using machines and energy.

The Industrial Revolution began in Britain from about the 1760s, with inventions such as the spinning jenny, the steam engine and later railways. Machines powered by coal greatly increased productivity, meaning the amount produced per worker. A single worker operating a spinning machine could produce many times more yarn than a hand-spinner. Industrialisation spread to Western Europe and North America in the nineteenth century, to Japan from the 1870s, and later to the Soviet Union. These early-industrialising countries gained enormous advantages: higher productivity, higher incomes, more taxes to spend on schools and health, better weapons that helped them build empires, and control of new technologies.

Countries that industrialised later often found it harder to catch up, because they faced competition from established industries, lacked capital and skills, and were sometimes prevented by colonial rule from developing their own factories. India, for example, had a large textile industry before British rule, but British policies and competition from cheap machine-made cloth from Lancashire contributed to its decline in the nineteenth century.

Some countries have industrialised rapidly in recent decades. South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore and Hong Kong, sometimes called the Asian Tigers, moved from poverty in the 1950s to high incomes by the 1990s, through investment in education, government support for export industries, high savings and access to world markets. China's industrialisation since the late 1970s has lifted hundreds of millions of people out of extreme poverty, though with serious environmental costs.

Today a key part of the technology gap is the digital divide: the difference between those who have access to computers, the internet and digital skills and those who do not. In 2023 about two thirds of the world's people used the internet, but in many low-income countries the figure was well below a third. In South Africa, the cost of data, unreliable electricity and the lack of devices in poor households, especially in rural areas, limit access. As economies depend more on information and digital services, the digital divide risks widening development gaps unless it is addressed.

So the final question is: how do these factors appear in South Africa's own story?

## Subtopic: South Africa's Development Path and the Error Museum

South Africa illustrates all three factors. Colonialism, beginning with the Dutch East India Company's settlement at the Cape in 1652 and continuing under British rule, dispossessed Khoikhoi, San and later African communities of land and livestock. The discovery of diamonds at Kimberley in 1867 and gold on the Witwatersrand in 1886 transformed the economy. Mining attracted foreign capital and built railways, ports and cities, but it relied on a migrant labour system in which black men worked on the mines for low wages, living in compounds far from their families, while their families remained in rural reserves. Laws such as the 1913 Natives Land Act ensured a cheap labour supply by limiting African land.

Industrialisation followed mining. The state founded the Iron and Steel Corporation, Iscor, in 1928, and Sasol in 1950 to make fuel from coal. Manufacturing grew during and after World War II. By the late twentieth century South Africa had the most industrialised economy in Africa. But under apartheid the benefits were concentrated among white South Africans, and black South Africans were restricted to unskilled work by job reservation and Bantu Education.

In trade, South Africa still depends heavily on exporting minerals, such as platinum group metals, gold, coal and iron ore, alongside manufactured goods such as vehicles. When world mineral prices fall, the economy suffers. Since 1994, South Africa's economy has shifted further towards services, such as finance, retail, tourism and telecommunications, but unemployment remains very high, partly because many jobs require skills that the education system has not provided to enough people, and partly because mining and farming have mechanised, reducing the number of workers they need.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Poor countries are poor because their people do not work hard." Differences in development have historical, economic and political causes, including colonialism, trade rules and access to technology, not differences in effort or ability.

Exhibit two: "Colonialism ended long ago, so it has no effect today." Borders, transport networks, export dependence, land ownership and education systems shaped by colonialism still affect many countries.

Exhibit three: "Exporting more raw materials always makes a country richer." If prices fall, a country may export more and earn less; processing and diversification are usually needed for development.

Exhibit four: "Industrialisation has only benefits." It raises productivity and incomes but can cause pollution, unsafe working conditions and, if unequally shared, deep inequality, as South Africa's history shows.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a race with an unfair start, beans that become chocolate somewhere else, and a machine that does the work of a hundred hands.

## Subtopic: A Race With an Unfair Start

Imagine a running race. Some runners start at the line. Others have their shoes taken away, are made to carry heavy bags for the leaders, and only allowed to start running much later. Even if they run fast, they are far behind.

That is a bit like what colonialism did. In the 1880s, European countries met in Berlin and divided Africa between themselves, without asking a single African. By 1914, only Ethiopia and Liberia were free. The colonisers took land, minerals and crops. They built railways, but mostly from mines to the sea, to carry riches away, not to connect African towns. They built few schools and universities, so when countries became independent, there were very few trained doctors, engineers and teachers.

In some places the cruelty was terrible. In the Congo, under King Leopold of Belgium, people were forced to collect rubber, and millions died.

So when colonies became free, they were already far behind in the race. Why did it stay so hard to catch up?

## Subtopic: Beans That Become Chocolate Somewhere Else

Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire grow about 60 per cent of the world's cocoa, the bean that chocolate is made from. But the big chocolate factories are mostly in Europe and America. A cocoa farmer may get only a few cents from a chocolate bar that sells for many rand. Most of the money is made by processing, packaging, advertising and selling the chocolate, and that happens somewhere else.

Here is another problem. Imagine a coffee-growing country wants to buy a tractor that costs 60 000 dollars. When coffee sells for 3 000 dollars a tonne, it must sell 20 tonnes to buy the tractor. But if the coffee price drops to 2 000 dollars a tonne, it has to sell 30 tonnes for the same tractor. The country works harder and gets less.

Rich countries also sometimes put extra taxes on processed goods from poor countries, and pay their own farmers to grow cheap crops. Fairtrade tries to fix part of this by promising farmers a fair minimum price.

So what helped some countries get rich?

## Subtopic: A Machine That Does the Work of a Hundred Hands

Around 250 years ago in Britain, people invented machines powered by steam and coal that could spin cloth, pump water and pull trains. One worker with a machine could do the work of many people by hand. This was the Industrial Revolution. The countries that had factories first became rich and powerful.

Countries without factories were left selling raw materials. But some caught up fast. South Korea was very poor in the 1950s and is now rich, because it invested in schools and factories.

South Africa's story began with diamonds at Kimberley in 1867 and gold in Johannesburg in 1886. Mines built cities and railways, but black miners were paid little and lived far from their families. Factories followed, like Iscor for steel and Sasol for fuel. Today, a new gap is the digital divide: people who can get online and those who cannot.

Here is the line to remember: development gaps come from history, trade and technology, not from some people trying less hard.
