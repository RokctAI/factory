### subtopic_1 — Colonialism and Its Legacy
**Assistant:** How did colonial railways affect development in Africa?

**Tutor:** Colonial railways were built to serve the economic needs of the colonial powers, and this shaped African development in ways that are still visible today. Most lines ran inland from a port to a mine, plantation or administrative centre, so that copper, cotton, cocoa or other raw materials could be exported as cheaply as possible. In Zambia and the Democratic Republic of the Congo, railways connected the copper mines to ports far away in Angola, Mozambique or South Africa. In Ghana and Nigeria, lines linked the cocoa and mineral areas to the coast. Very few lines connected neighbouring colonies, and different colonial powers often used different track widths, called gauges, so trains from one colony could not run on another's lines. The result was that African economies were linked outwards to Europe rather than to each other. Today, trade between African countries is much smaller than trade between countries in Europe or Asia, partly because transport links are poor and goods must often travel by road or be transferred between systems. Railways also encouraged towns to grow along their routes and ports to become dominant cities, while regions away from the lines were neglected. The African Continental Free Trade Area and projects to build new regional corridors, such as the Lobito Corridor, aim to change this pattern. When you write about colonialism's legacy, infrastructure is a clear, concrete example to use.

### subtopic_2 — Unfair Trade and the Terms of Trade
**Assistant:** Why don't cocoa-producing countries just make their own chocolate?

**Tutor:** Some do, and the number is growing, but there are real obstacles. First, building chocolate factories requires large amounts of capital, specialised machinery and technical skills, which have historically been concentrated in Europe and North America. Second, chocolate must be kept cool and transported carefully, so processing in hot countries requires reliable electricity and cold chains, which are expensive and sometimes unreliable. Third, most chocolate is eaten in Europe and North America, so manufacturers have located factories close to their customers and established brands, and consumers are loyal to familiar names that have been advertised for generations. Fourth, trade rules have discouraged processing in producing countries through tariff escalation, where rich countries charged higher import taxes on processed cocoa products than on raw beans, although some trade agreements have reduced these barriers for African countries. Fifth, a few large multinational companies dominate cocoa trading, grinding and chocolate making, which makes it hard for new firms to compete. Despite this, Côte d'Ivoire is now one of the world's largest grinders of cocoa beans into cocoa butter and powder, and Ghana has encouraged local chocolate brands. Governments in both countries have also cooperated to introduce a living income differential, an extra payment per tonne intended to raise farmers' incomes. Adding value locally creates jobs, skills and tax revenue, which is why it is central to many development strategies.

### subtopic_3 — Technology and Industrialisation
**Assistant:** What were the costs as well as the benefits of industrialisation?

**Tutor:** Industrialisation has been the main route by which countries have raised productivity and incomes, but it has also brought serious costs. Among the benefits, factories and machines allowed far more goods to be produced with less labour, which made goods cheaper and raised wages over time. Industrial growth produced taxes that paid for schools, hospitals, sanitation and pensions. It encouraged technological innovation, from railways to electricity to medicines, which improved health and life expectancy. It created cities with jobs and services. Among the costs, early industrial workers, including children, often worked very long hours in dangerous conditions for low wages, and lived in overcrowded, polluted slums; in Britain in the 1840s, life expectancy in some industrial cities was below thirty years. Industrialisation relied on burning coal and later oil, producing air pollution and the greenhouse gases that now drive climate change, a cost that falls heavily on poorer countries that contributed least to the problem. It also depended in part on raw materials and markets obtained through colonial empires. In South Africa, industrialisation built on mining relied on low-paid migrant labour and racial discrimination, producing great wealth alongside great inequality. Today countries face the challenge of industrialising in cleaner ways, using renewable energy and better labour standards. In an essay, a balanced answer recognises both sides and explains who gained and who paid.

### subtopic_4 — South Africa's Development Path and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How can the digital divide affect a country's development?

**Tutor:** The digital divide is the gap between people who have access to computers, smartphones, reliable internet and digital skills and those who do not. It affects development because more and more economic, educational and social activity takes place online. Businesses use the internet to reach customers, make payments and find suppliers; farmers use mobile phones to check market prices and weather; students use online resources, applications and learning platforms; job seekers search and apply online; and governments deliver services such as grant applications and tax returns digitally. People without access are excluded from these opportunities, so existing inequalities can grow. Between countries, low-income countries with poor electricity supply, few fibre-optic connections and expensive data fall further behind in digital industries, which are among the fastest-growing in the world. Within South Africa, the divide follows familiar patterns: wealthy urban households often have fibre and laptops, while many rural and township households rely on expensive prepaid mobile data and shared phones, and load-shedding has interrupted connectivity. During the Covid-19 school closures in 2020, learners without devices or data struggled to continue learning. Solutions include cheaper data, public Wi-Fi in community centres and libraries, expanding networks to rural areas, providing devices to schools and teaching digital skills. Mobile money services such as M-Pesa in Kenya show how technology can also help leapfrog older systems and bring banking to people who never had bank accounts.
