# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains alternative approaches to development, particularly alternatives to large-scale industrialisation. It covers the differences between top-down and bottom-up development, and why many geographers have questioned the industrial model; appropriate technology and small-scale development; microfinance, cooperatives and community savings; and alternative economic paths such as community-based tourism and ecotourism, agriculture, services and digital innovation, illustrated with South African and African examples. The anchor examples are the Grameen Bank in Bangladesh, which pioneered small loans to poor women, and the Makuleke community in South Africa, which regained its land in the Kruger National Park in 1998 and chose to earn income from conservation and tourism. The governing idea is that development can follow many paths, and that the most effective approaches often involve local people in decisions, use resources sustainably and build on local strengths.

## Subtopic: Top-Down and Bottom-Up Development

There are two broad approaches to planning development. Top-down development is planned and controlled by governments, large companies or international organisations, usually through large projects such as dams, highways, power stations, industrial zones and mines. Decisions are made by experts and officials, and benefits are expected to trickle down to ordinary people over time. Bottom-up development is planned and controlled by local communities, often with the help of non-governmental organisations, through smaller projects that respond directly to people's own priorities, such as a community water pump, a village clinic, a farming cooperative or a craft business.

Top-down projects can achieve things that small projects cannot. The Lesotho Highlands Water Project, built from the late 1980s, with the Katse Dam completed in 1998, transfers water from the Lesotho mountains to Gauteng and generates hydroelectricity for Lesotho, earning Lesotho significant royalties. But large projects can have serious problems: they are expensive and often financed by loans; they may displace communities, as thousands of people in Lesotho were affected by the loss of fields and grazing; their benefits may go mainly to cities or elites; they can damage the environment; and they offer opportunities for corruption, as shown when international companies were convicted of bribery in the Lesotho project.

Bottom-up projects tend to be cheaper, easier for communities to maintain and more closely matched to local needs, and they build people's skills and confidence. Their limitations are that they are small in scale, may depend on outside funding that ends, and cannot provide national infrastructure such as electricity grids or railways.

In practice, successful development usually combines both approaches: national infrastructure and services planned from above, with communities participating in decisions and running local projects. The idea of participation, that people affected by development should have a voice in it, is now accepted by most development organisations.

Why did people start to question the industrial model? First, as you learned in the previous lessons, latecomers struggled to compete with established industrial countries. Second, heavy industry often employs relatively few people because it uses a great deal of machinery, while many developing countries need large numbers of jobs. Third, industrialisation has caused severe pollution and contributed to climate change. Fourth, not every country has the resources, capital, markets or energy to industrialise successfully. These concerns encouraged a search for alternatives.

So the next question is: what kinds of technology suit communities with little money?

## Subtopic: Appropriate Technology and Microfinance

In 1973 the economist Ernst Friedrich Schumacher published a book called Small Is Beautiful, arguing that developing communities did not always need the most advanced and expensive technology. Instead they needed appropriate technology, also called intermediate technology: tools and methods that are affordable, use local materials and skills, can be repaired locally, use little energy and suit local conditions. Appropriate technology aims to help people be more productive without making them dependent on expensive imports, foreign experts or large amounts of capital.

Examples are found across Africa. Treadle pumps, operated by foot like an exercise machine, allow small farmers to irrigate their fields without fuel. Drip irrigation using buckets and plastic pipes saves water. Solar home systems and solar lanterns provide light and phone charging in villages far from the electricity grid. Improved cookstoves use less firewood and produce less smoke, protecting forests and the health of women and children who cook indoors. Rainwater tanks collect water from roofs. The Hippo Water Roller, a drum that can be rolled along the ground, was developed in South Africa in the 1990s to make it easier to carry water, a task usually done by women and girls.

Access to money is another barrier for poor people. Banks have often refused to lend to the very poor because they have no property to offer as security, called collateral, and because small loans are costly to administer. Microfinance offers very small loans, savings accounts and insurance to poor people, often women, so they can start or expand small businesses: buying a sewing machine, a few goats, stock for a vegetable stall or a mobile phone to sell airtime. The best-known example is the Grameen Bank, founded in Bangladesh by the economist Muhammad Yunus, who started by lending small amounts from his own pocket in 1976. The bank lent mainly to women organised in small groups, who supported each other and shared responsibility for repayment. Yunus and the Grameen Bank were awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 2006.

Microfinance has helped many families, but research has also shown limits: not all borrowers are able to start successful businesses, and in some places high interest rates and multiple loans have led to debt problems. It works best combined with savings, training and access to markets.

In South Africa, an older and very widespread form of community finance is the stokvel: a savings club whose members contribute a fixed amount regularly and receive the pooled money in turn, or use it for bulk buying of groceries at year-end, funerals or investments. Millions of South Africans belong to stokvels, which build on traditions of trust and mutual support, a form of bottom-up development rooted in the idea of ubuntu.

So the next question is: what other economic paths can communities follow?

## Subtopic: Tourism, Agriculture, Services and Digital Paths

Tourism is one important alternative. Countries with natural beauty, wildlife, beaches, mountains or cultural heritage can earn foreign currency and create many jobs, from guides and hotel workers to craft-makers and drivers. Tourism employs more people per unit of investment than heavy industry and can encourage conservation. However, mass tourism can damage environments, raise local prices, create seasonal and low-paid jobs and send profits to foreign-owned hotel chains.

Ecotourism and community-based tourism aim to overcome these problems. Ecotourism is responsible travel to natural areas that conserves the environment and improves the well-being of local people. In community-based tourism, local communities own or co-own the tourism enterprises and share in the profits. A South African example is the Makuleke community, who were forcibly removed from their land in the northern part of the Kruger National Park in 1969. In 1998, after a land claim, the land known as the Makuleke region, about 24 000 hectares, was returned to them. Instead of farming it or settling it, the community chose to keep it under conservation, in partnership with South African National Parks, and to earn income from lodges and tourism concessions, using the revenue for local development such as schools and bursaries.

Agriculture-led development is another path. Most poor people in Africa live in rural areas and depend on farming, so improving smallholder farming through better seeds, irrigation, storage, roads to markets and land rights can reduce poverty directly. Ethiopia and Rwanda have emphasised agriculture in their development plans, and farming cooperatives allow small farmers to buy inputs together and negotiate better prices.

Services and digital innovation offer a third path. India built a large information technology and business services industry from the 1990s, centred on cities such as Bengaluru. In Kenya, the mobile money service M-Pesa, launched in 2007, allowed people to send and receive money by text message without a bank account, and research has linked it to lifting households out of poverty, particularly households headed by women. This is an example of leapfrogging: skipping an older technology stage, such as bank branches or landline telephones, and moving directly to newer technology.

So the final question is: how has South Africa used these alternatives, and what are their limits?

## Subtopic: South African Alternatives and the Error Museum

South Africa has combined top-down industrial development with several alternative approaches. The Working for Water programme, started in 1995, employs people from poor communities to clear invasive alien plants such as wattle, pine and eucalyptus, which use large amounts of water and increase fire risk. It combines job creation, skills training and environmental restoration. Related programmes include Working on Fire, which trains firefighters, and Working for Wetlands. The Expanded Public Works Programme, started in 2004, creates short-term jobs in infrastructure, environmental and social projects such as home-based care.

The Renewable Energy Independent Power Producer Procurement Programme, from 2011, required wind and solar projects to create local jobs and allocate a share of ownership to community trusts, giving rural communities in the Northern Cape and Eastern Cape income from clean energy. Township tourism, food gardens, cooperatives, the informal sector and small businesses supported by funding agencies are other examples. The informal sector, including spaza shops, street vendors and minibus taxis, provides livelihoods for millions of South Africans, although informal workers often lack protection.

Alternatives have limits. Small projects may not create enough jobs for a country with very high unemployment. Tourism collapsed during the Covid-19 pandemic in 2020, showing its vulnerability. Microfinance can create debt. Community projects can be captured by local elites if decision-making is not transparent. Most development experts now argue that alternative and conventional approaches should complement each other.

When you evaluate any development project in an exam, a useful set of questions is: Who planned it and who was consulted? Who benefits and who bears the costs? Is it affordable to maintain after outside funding ends? Does it use resources sustainably? Does it build local skills and ownership? Applying these questions to a dam, a tourism lodge or a microfinance scheme lets you write a balanced paragraph that recognises both advantages and disadvantages, which is what examiners look for when they ask you to evaluate or discuss.

It also helps to remember that alternatives are not only for poor countries. Wealthy countries are increasingly interested in cooperatives, local food systems, community energy schemes and repair of goods rather than replacement, because they too face unemployment in some regions, environmental limits and the wish for more local control over the economy.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Development always means building factories." Industrialisation is one path; tourism, agriculture, services, digital innovation and community-based projects are others.

Exhibit two: "Bottom-up projects are always better than top-down projects." Each approach has strengths and weaknesses; national infrastructure needs top-down planning, while local participation makes projects work better.

Exhibit three: "Appropriate technology means old-fashioned or second-rate technology." It means technology that fits local needs, skills and budgets, and it can be very modern, such as solar systems or mobile money.

Exhibit four: "Ecotourism has no negative impacts." Poorly managed ecotourism can still disturb wildlife, raise prices and send profits elsewhere; benefits depend on local ownership and careful management.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: two ways to build a village, a small loan that changes a life, and a community that earns money by keeping its land wild.

## Subtopic: Two Ways to Build a Village

Imagine a village that needs water. One way: the government sends engineers who build a huge dam far away, with pipes to the cities. It is big and powerful, but the village is not asked, some families lose their fields under the water, and the village may still have no tap. That is top-down development: planned from the top.

The other way: the villagers meet, decide they want a borehole and a pump, raise some money, get help from an organisation, and look after the pump themselves. It is small, but it fits their needs and they know how to fix it. That is bottom-up development: planned from the ground up.

Both have their place. A country needs big things like power stations and highways. But projects work best when the people affected have a say.

Many people now ask: does every country have to become a factory country to develop? Factories use lots of machines and few workers, can pollute, and are hard to start when richer countries already make things cheaply. So people looked for other roads.

So what are some of those other roads, and who walks them?

## Subtopic: A Small Loan, a Big Change

Imagine a woman who wants to sell vegetables at the taxi rank but cannot afford to buy her first stock. A bank will not lend her money because she has no house or car to offer as security.

In Bangladesh in the 1970s, an economist named Muhammad Yunus started lending very small amounts to poor women like her. They worked in groups and helped each other repay. His Grameen Bank grew to help millions, and he won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2006. This is called microfinance.

In South Africa, many people use stokvels: groups of friends or neighbours who each put in money every month, and take turns to receive the total, or buy groceries in bulk together.

There are also clever, cheap tools called appropriate technology: a foot-powered pump for watering crops, a solar lamp for a home without electricity, a stove that uses less wood, or the Hippo Water Roller, a South African invention that lets you roll water home instead of carrying it on your head.

So what other roads are there, and where do they lead?

## Subtopic: Keeping the Land Wild

In 1969 the Makuleke people were forced off their land in the north of the Kruger National Park. In 1998 they won it back. Instead of farming it, they decided to keep it wild and work with the national parks. Tourists now stay in lodges there, and the money helps pay for the community's schools and bursaries. That is community-based tourism.

There are other roads too. In Kenya, M-Pesa lets people send money by cellphone, even without a bank account. That is called leapfrogging: jumping straight to new technology. In South Africa, Working for Water pays people to clear thirsty alien trees, creating jobs and saving water at the same time.

These roads are not perfect. Tourism stopped almost overnight during Covid-19, and small projects cannot create millions of jobs on their own.

Here is the line to remember: there is more than one road to development, and the best ones are shaped by the people who walk them.
