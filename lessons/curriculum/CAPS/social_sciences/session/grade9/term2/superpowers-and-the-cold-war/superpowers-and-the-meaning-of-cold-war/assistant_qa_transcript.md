### subtopic_1 — What Is a Superpower?
**Assistant:** Why was the Soviet Union called a superpower if its economy was weaker?

**Tutor:** Being a superpower depended on a combination of military, political, ideological and geographical power, not only economic strength. It is true that the Soviet economy was smaller than the American economy throughout the Cold War, and that the Soviet Union had suffered enormous destruction in World War II, losing about 27 million people and much of its industry and farmland in the west. However, it had other sources of power. Militarily, it had the largest army in the world in 1945, which occupied Eastern Europe and eastern Germany, and after 1949 it possessed nuclear weapons, later building an arsenal comparable to that of the United States. Geographically, it was the largest country in the world, covering about one sixth of the Earth's land surface, stretching from Europe to the Pacific, with vast natural resources such as oil, gas, coal, timber and minerals. Politically, it controlled a bloc of satellite states in Eastern Europe and led the world communist movement, giving it influence in China until the split of around 1960, as well as in Cuba, Vietnam, parts of Africa and communist parties in many countries. Ideologically, communism offered an alternative model of development that attracted many people in colonised and poor countries. Technologically, it achieved remarkable firsts, such as the first satellite in 1957 and the first person in space in 1961. The economic weakness eventually mattered, however: the cost of competing with the United States contributed to the Soviet Union's collapse in 1991.

### subtopic_2 — The Meaning of the Cold War
**Assistant:** How did mutually assured destruction keep the peace?

**Tutor:** Mutually assured destruction, or MAD, was a situation in which both superpowers had enough nuclear weapons, and enough ways to deliver them, that each could destroy the other even after being attacked first. This second-strike capability was the key. If the Soviet Union launched a surprise nuclear attack on the United States, American submarines at sea, bombers in the air and missiles in hardened underground silos would survive and retaliate, destroying the Soviet Union in return, and the same was true the other way round. Because any nuclear war would mean the destruction of the side that started it, neither side had a rational reason to begin one. In this way, the threat of total destruction acted as a deterrent, and many historians argue that it helped prevent a direct war between the superpowers in Europe, where huge armies faced each other for four decades. However, MAD had serious dangers. It depended on leaders behaving rationally and on systems working correctly. There were several close calls caused by misunderstandings or technical faults. In 1983, for example, a Soviet early-warning system falsely reported incoming American missiles, and the duty officer, Stanislav Petrov, judged it a false alarm and did not report it as an attack, possibly averting a nuclear war. MAD also encouraged an expensive arms race, as each side tried to ensure it could still retaliate. Critics argued that peace based on the threat of mass destruction was fragile and morally troubling, which is why movements for nuclear disarmament grew.

### subtopic_3 — Methods of the Cold War
**Assistant:** What is a proxy war and why did the superpowers use them?

**Tutor:** A proxy war is a conflict in which major powers support opposing sides with weapons, money, training, advisers and sometimes their own troops, while avoiding direct war with each other. The local forces act as proxies, fighting on behalf of the larger powers' interests as well as their own. During the Cold War, the superpowers used proxy wars for several reasons. First, direct war between them risked nuclear escalation and mutual destruction, so they competed where the risk was lower. Second, proxy wars allowed each side to expand its influence, protect allies or weaken the other side at a lower cost to itself. Third, many conflicts in newly independent or divided countries already existed, between rival movements, ethnic groups or political parties, and the superpowers chose sides in them. Examples include the Korean War from 1950 to 1953, the Vietnam War, the Soviet war in Afghanistan from 1979 to 1989, and the civil wars in Angola and Mozambique. The consequences for the countries involved were devastating: millions killed and displaced, economies destroyed, landmines left in the ground for decades, and governments that depended on foreign backing rather than their own people. Angola's civil war, which began in 1975 with Soviet, Cuban, American and South African involvement, continued after the Cold War ended and only finished in 2002. For Africans, the Cold War was often anything but cold.

### subtopic_4 — The Global Cold War, Africa and South Africa, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How did the apartheid government use the Cold War?

**Tutor:** The apartheid government used the Cold War to present itself to Western countries as an ally against communism and to justify repression at home and military action in the region. From 1948 the National Party government portrayed South Africa as a Christian, anti-communist bastion on a continent threatened by Soviet influence. The Suppression of Communism Act of 1950 banned the Communist Party of South Africa, but its definition of communism was so broad that it covered almost any attempt to bring about political, social or economic change, including campaigns for racial equality. It was used to ban, restrict and imprison many anti-apartheid activists who were not communists. The government argued that the ANC and other liberation movements were simply tools of Moscow, especially after the ANC, banned in 1960, accepted military training and support from the Soviet Union and its allies when Western countries would not help. In the late 1970s and 1980s, under Prime Minister and later President P. W. Botha, the government spoke of a total onslaught by communist forces and responded with a total strategy, increasing military spending, conscripting white men, and raiding ANC bases in neighbouring countries. South African forces fought in Angola against the Soviet- and Cuban-supported MPLA government. Some Western governments, such as those of Britain under Margaret Thatcher and the United States under Ronald Reagan in the early 1980s, were reluctant to impose sanctions, partly for Cold War reasons. When the Cold War ended, this argument lost its force, which helped create conditions for negotiations.
