# Part 1 — Expert

This session defines the term superpower and explains the meaning of the Cold War. It covers what made the United States and the Soviet Union superpowers after 1945; the meaning, origins and time span of the term Cold War; the main methods by which the Cold War was fought, including alliances, propaganda, espionage, the arms and space races, and proxy wars; and the global reach of the Cold War, including the non-aligned countries, Africa and South Africa. The anchor definition is that the Cold War was a state of political, ideological, economic and military rivalry between the United States and its allies and the Soviet Union and its allies, from about 1947 to 1991, which stopped short of direct, full-scale war between the superpowers themselves. The governing idea is that the threat of nuclear destruction kept the superpowers from fighting each other directly, so their rivalry was fought by every other means, often with devastating consequences for other countries.

## Subtopic: What Is a Superpower?

A superpower is a state with dominant power and influence across the whole world, far greater than that of ordinary great powers. The term was used in 1944 by the American political scientist William Fox to describe countries with great power plus great mobility of power. After 1945, two countries fitted this description.

Several characteristics define a superpower. The first is military strength: very large armed forces, the ability to deploy them anywhere in the world through navies, air forces and bases, and, from 1945 for the United States and 1949 for the Soviet Union, nuclear weapons. The second is economic strength: a large economy and industrial capacity able to produce weapons, support allies with aid and trade, and influence the world economy. The third is political and ideological influence: the ability to lead an alliance of other countries and to promote a model of society that others might follow. The fourth is global reach: interests and influence on every continent, not only in its own region. Some definitions add technological and cultural influence, such as American films, music and consumer goods, or Soviet achievements in space and science.

The United States emerged from World War II as the strongest country in the world. Its territory had not been invaded or bombed, apart from Pearl Harbour and some Pacific islands, and its economy had grown enormously during the war. In 1945 it produced about half of the world's manufactured goods, held most of the world's gold reserves, had the world's largest navy and air force and had a monopoly on the atomic bomb. It led the creation of new international institutions such as the United Nations, the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank.

The Soviet Union was a superpower in a different way. Its economy had been devastated by the war and it had lost about 27 million people, but it had the largest army in the world, occupied Eastern Europe and part of Germany, controlled an enormous territory stretching from Europe to the Pacific, and led the world communist movement. After testing its first atomic bomb in 1949 and launching the first satellite in 1957, it matched the United States in key areas of military and technological power, though its economy was always smaller.

Britain and France, the great powers of the previous century, were weakened by the war and gradually lost their empires as colonies gained independence, so they became secondary powers aligned with the United States. China became communist in 1949 under Mao Zedong and later grew into a major power, but it was not a superpower during the Cold War.

So the next question is: what does the term Cold War mean?

## Subtopic: The Meaning of the Cold War

A hot war is one in which countries fight each other directly with armed forces. A cold war, by contrast, is a state of intense hostility and rivalry between countries that does not lead to direct, full-scale fighting between them. The British writer George Orwell used the phrase in an essay in October 1945, You and the Atomic Bomb, where he predicted that the atomic bomb might create a permanent state of cold war between a few great powers that could not conquer one another. In April 1947 the American financier Bernard Baruch said in a speech, "Let us not be deceived: we are today in the midst of a cold war," and later that year the journalist Walter Lippmann published a book titled The Cold War, which made the term widely known.

The Cold War is usually dated from about 1947, when the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan divided Europe into two camps, until 1991, when the Soviet Union collapsed. Some historians date its beginning to 1945 or even 1917, when the communist revolution in Russia first created ideological hostility with the West.

Why did the Cold War stay cold between the superpowers? The main reason was nuclear weapons. Once both sides had large numbers of nuclear weapons, a direct war could have destroyed both countries and much of the world. This situation became known as mutually assured destruction, with the fitting abbreviation MAD: if one side launched a nuclear attack, the other would still be able to retaliate and destroy the attacker. The fear of nuclear war acted as a deterrent, discouraging both sides from attacking each other directly. Several times, most dangerously during the Cuban Missile Crisis of October 1962, the world came close to nuclear war, but each time leaders stepped back.

The Cold War was a conflict on several levels at once. It was ideological, a struggle between capitalism and liberal democracy on one side and communism and one-party rule on the other, with each side claiming to represent the future of humanity. It was geopolitical, a competition for territory, allies, bases and resources around the world. It was military, involving huge armies and nuclear arsenals. It was economic, with each side offering aid and trade to win friends. And it was cultural and scientific, from the Olympic Games to the race to the Moon.

The world was often described as bipolar, meaning divided around two poles of power. On one side was the West or First World, led by the United States; on the other the Eastern bloc or Second World, led by the Soviet Union; and many newly independent countries in Africa, Asia and Latin America were called the Third World, a term that originally meant those not aligned with either bloc.

So the next question is: if the superpowers did not fight each other directly, how did they fight the Cold War?

## Subtopic: Methods of the Cold War

The superpowers used many methods short of direct war.

Alliances were the first method. In April 1949 the United States, Canada and ten Western European countries founded the North Atlantic Treaty Organization, NATO, a military alliance in which an attack on one member would be treated as an attack on all. West Germany joined in 1955. In response, in May 1955, the Soviet Union and its Eastern European satellites formed the Warsaw Pact. Europe was now divided into two armed camps facing each other across the Iron Curtain. The superpowers also formed alliances in other regions and built military bases around the world.

Propaganda was the second method. Each side used newspapers, radio, films, posters, cartoons and education to present its own system as good and the other as evil. American radio stations such as Radio Free Europe broadcast into Eastern Europe, while Soviet media portrayed the United States as a land of exploitation, racism and unemployment. In the United States, fear of communist influence led to McCarthyism in the early 1950s, when Senator Joseph McCarthy accused many people, often without evidence, of being communists.

Espionage, or spying, was the third method. The American Central Intelligence Agency, the CIA, founded in 1947, and the Soviet KGB, founded in 1954, gathered intelligence, recruited spies and carried out secret operations, including supporting coups against governments they opposed. Spies such as Klaus Fuchs passed atomic secrets to the Soviet Union. In 1960 an American U-2 spy plane was shot down over the Soviet Union and its pilot, Gary Powers, captured.

The arms race and the space race, which you will study in the next lessons, were the fourth and fifth methods: competitions to build more and better weapons and to achieve firsts in space, demonstrating technological superiority.

Proxy wars were the sixth and deadliest method. A proxy war is a conflict in which the superpowers support opposing sides, with weapons, money, advisers and sometimes troops, without fighting each other directly. Examples include the Korean War from 1950 to 1953, in which American-led United Nations forces fought North Korean and Chinese forces backed by the Soviet Union; the Vietnam War, in which the United States fought communist North Vietnam and its allies until 1973; the Soviet war in Afghanistan from 1979 to 1989, in which the United States armed Afghan resistance fighters; and wars in Angola and Mozambique. Millions of people died in these conflicts.

Aid and economic competition were a seventh method. Both superpowers offered loans, grants, weapons and technical assistance to win the loyalty of newly independent countries.

So the final question is: how did the Cold War affect countries outside the two blocs, including Africa and South Africa?

## Subtopic: The Global Cold War, Africa and South Africa, and the Error Museum

Many newly independent countries did not want to choose sides. In 1955, leaders of Asian and African countries met at Bandung in Indonesia, and in 1961 the Non-Aligned Movement was founded in Belgrade, led by figures such as Jawaharlal Nehru of India, Gamal Abdel Nasser of Egypt, Sukarno of Indonesia, Kwame Nkrumah of Ghana and Josip Broz Tito of Yugoslavia. Non-aligned countries sought to stay independent of both blocs and to focus on decolonisation and development, although in practice some leaned towards one side.

Africa became an arena of Cold War competition as colonies gained independence from the late 1950s. In the Congo, independent in 1960, the superpowers became involved in a crisis that led to the overthrow and murder of the first prime minister, Patrice Lumumba, in January 1961, with Belgian involvement and American support for his opponents. The United States then backed the dictator Mobutu Sese Seko for decades because he was anti-communist. In Ethiopia, Somalia, Angola and Mozambique, the superpowers armed rival governments and movements.

Southern Africa was deeply affected. In Angola, after independence from Portugal in 1975, a civil war broke out between the MPLA government, supported by the Soviet Union and Cuban troops, and the UNITA and FNLA movements, supported at various times by the United States and South Africa. South Africa's apartheid government presented itself to the West as a bulwark against communism. It used anti-communism to justify repression at home, passing the Suppression of Communism Act in 1950, which defined communism so broadly that it was used against many opponents of apartheid, not only communists. In the 1980s it spoke of a total onslaught by communist forces to justify military spending and raids into neighbouring countries. South African forces fought in Angola during the Border War from 1966 to 1989, culminating in the battles around Cuito Cuanavale in 1987 and 1988. The ANC, banned in 1960, received military training and support from the Soviet Union and its allies, while many Western governments were slow to impose sanctions on apartheid. The end of the Cold War helped open the way to Namibia's independence in 1990 and to negotiations in South Africa, as you will study in Term 4.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "The Cold War was cold because nobody fought." The superpowers did not fight each other directly, but millions died in proxy wars in Korea, Vietnam, Afghanistan, Angola and elsewhere.

Exhibit two: "Any powerful country is a superpower." A superpower has global military reach, economic strength, political leadership of an alliance and worldwide influence; only the United States and the Soviet Union fitted this during the Cold War.

Exhibit three: "The Cold War only happened in Europe." It reached every continent, including Africa, Asia, Latin America and South Africa.

Exhibit four: "Every country took a side." Many joined the Non-Aligned Movement from 1961, trying to stay independent of both blocs.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: two heavyweight boxers who never throw a punch, fights through other people, and a battlefield in our own region.

## Subtopic: Two Heavyweights in the Ring

After World War II, two countries were far stronger than all the others. The United States was rich, had the biggest navy and air force and had the atomic bomb. The Soviet Union had the biggest army, controlled Eastern Europe and led communist countries. Countries this powerful, with influence all over the world, are called superpowers.

Now imagine two heavyweight boxers in a ring. Each one knows that a single punch from the other could knock him out cold. So they circle each other, flex their muscles, shout insults and try to frighten each other, but they never throw a full punch. That is the Cold War.

It was called cold because the two superpowers never fought a full war against each other directly. Why not? Because both had nuclear weapons. If one attacked, the other could strike back and destroy it too. This idea was called mutually assured destruction, or MAD.

The Cold War lasted from about 1947 until 1991.

So if they did not fight directly, how did they compete?

## Subtopic: Fighting Through Others

The superpowers competed in many ways. They made teams: in 1949 America and its friends formed NATO, and in 1955 the Soviet Union and its friends formed the Warsaw Pact. They used propaganda, like posters, films and radio, to say their side was good and the other was bad. They spied on each other. They raced to build more weapons and to be first in space.

The worst way was proxy wars. A proxy is someone who acts for someone else. Instead of fighting each other, the superpowers gave guns, money and advisers to opposite sides in other countries' wars. In Korea, Vietnam and Afghanistan, millions of people died in these wars. The Cold War was cold for the superpowers, but very hot for the people caught in the middle.

So did this reach Africa?

## Subtopic: A Battlefield Close to Home

Yes. As African countries became independent, both superpowers tried to win them over. In the Congo in 1961, the first prime minister, Patrice Lumumba, was overthrown and killed, with foreign powers involved.

In Angola, after 1975, the government was helped by the Soviet Union and Cuban soldiers, while its enemies were helped at times by America and by apartheid South Africa. South African soldiers fought in Angola for many years.

The apartheid government told the world it was fighting communism. It used a 1950 law against communism to ban many people who simply opposed apartheid. Meanwhile, the ANC received training and support from the Soviet Union.

Many countries, like India, Ghana and Egypt, refused to pick a side and formed the Non-Aligned Movement in 1961.

Here is the line to remember: the superpowers never fought each other directly because of nuclear fear, so they fought through alliances, propaganda, spies, races and other people's wars.
