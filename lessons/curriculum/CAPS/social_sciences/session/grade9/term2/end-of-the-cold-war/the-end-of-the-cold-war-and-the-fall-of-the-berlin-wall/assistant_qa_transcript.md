### subtopic_1 — A Superpower in Trouble
**Assistant:** Why did Gorbachev not send tanks to stop the revolutions of 1989?

**Tutor:** Gorbachev's decision not to use force was one of the most important choices of the twentieth century, and historians explain it in several ways. First, he genuinely believed in his new thinking: he thought that the use of force to keep unpopular governments in power was morally wrong and that it had damaged the Soviet Union's reputation. He had publicly told the United Nations in December 1988 that every nation should choose its own path, and going back on this would have destroyed his credibility with the West. Second, there were practical reasons. The Soviet economy was in crisis, and Gorbachev needed Western trust, trade, loans and arms reductions to rescue it. Sending tanks into Poland or East Germany would have ended the improving relationship with the United States and Western Europe and restarted an expensive arms race. Third, the Soviet Union was exhausted by its war in Afghanistan, which had shown how costly and unpopular military intervention could be. Fourth, Gorbachev seems to have hoped that reform communists, people like himself, would come to power in Eastern Europe and keep their countries friendly to Moscow, rather than expecting communism to disappear altogether. He underestimated how unpopular communism was. Finally, the speed of events meant that by the time it was clear the communist governments were collapsing, using force would have meant enormous bloodshed. Some hardliners in Moscow were furious, and their anger helped lead to the attempted coup against Gorbachev in August 1991.

### subtopic_2 — Gorbachev, Reform and a New Relationship
**Assistant:** Why do historians say Schabowski's mistake opened the Wall?

**Tutor:** The opening of the Wall on 9 November 1989 was partly an accident, and historians use it as a good example of how chance and individual actions can shape big events. By November 1989 the East German government was under enormous pressure from mass demonstrations and from thousands of its citizens escaping through Hungary and Czechoslovakia. The new leader, Egon Krenz, and his colleagues drafted new rules that would let East Germans apply for permission to travel or emigrate, with the idea that the rules would come into effect the following day and that people would still need visas. Günter Schabowski, the party spokesman, was handed the document shortly before a live press conference and had not taken part in the discussions. At the end of the press conference he read the rules out, and when an Italian journalist and others asked when they came into force, he shuffled through his notes and said that, as far as he knew, it was immediately, without delay. The news was broadcast on West German television, which most East Germans watched. Thousands went to the checkpoints, demanding to cross. The border guards had received no orders. At Bornholmer Strasse, the officer in charge, Harald Jäger, telephoned his superiors repeatedly but got no clear instruction, and as the crowd grew he feared people would be crushed or shot. At about 11.30 that night he ordered the gate opened. Historians stress that the mistake only mattered because of the deeper pressure that had built up, and because the guards chose not to shoot.

### subtopic_3 — The Revolutions of 1989 and the Night the Wall Opened
**Assistant:** Did the people of Eastern Europe or the leaders end the Cold War?

**Tutor:** This is one of the big debates about the end of the Cold War, and the best answer is that both mattered, working together. Some historians and many Americans emphasised the role of leaders, especially Ronald Reagan, arguing that his military build-up and the Strategic Defense Initiative put pressure on the Soviet Union that it could not withstand, and that his firm stance forced Soviet leaders to negotiate. Other historians emphasise Gorbachev, pointing out that the Cold War could only end peacefully because a Soviet leader chose not to use force and chose to reduce nuclear weapons; another leader could have chosen repression. Some point to Pope John Paul II, who was Polish and whose visits to Poland inspired resistance, and to Margaret Thatcher, who encouraged dialogue with Gorbachev. But many historians argue that ordinary people were the real drivers. Solidarity in Poland kept resisting for a decade. Hungarians cut the border fence. Hundreds of thousands of East Germans marched in Leipzig and Berlin, risking being shot, and thousands more voted with their feet by escaping. In Czechoslovakia students and workers filled the squares, and in the Baltic states about two million people formed a human chain in August 1989. Leaders created the conditions in which change was possible, but it was the courage of ordinary people that turned possibility into revolution. When you answer a question like this in an essay, you should weigh up several factors rather than choose only one.

### subtopic_4 — Consequences, South Africa and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How exactly did the end of the Cold War affect South Africa?

**Tutor:** The end of the Cold War was one of several factors that helped bring about the negotiated end of apartheid, and it affected both the government and the liberation movements. For the apartheid government, the Cold War had provided a powerful justification. Leaders such as P. W. Botha argued that South Africa faced a total onslaught organised by the Soviet Union, and that the ANC and SACP were part of this communist threat. This was used to justify detention without trial, states of emergency, huge military spending and wars in Angola and Namibia. Western governments, especially the United States and Britain, had often been reluctant to put strong pressure on South Africa because they saw it as an anti-communist ally. When communism collapsed in Eastern Europe in 1989, the total onslaught argument became much harder to believe, and Western countries had less reason to protect the South African government. For the ANC, the Soviet Union and East Germany had been important sources of weapons, training, money and scholarships. As the Soviet Union turned inward and sought better relations with the West, it encouraged a negotiated settlement, and the ANC's support for armed struggle became harder to sustain. Agreements in December 1988 had already led to Cuban troops leaving Angola and to Namibian independence in March 1990. On 2 February 1990 F. W. de Klerk unbanned the liberation movements. However, internal resistance, international sanctions, economic decline and pressure from ordinary South Africans were also central causes, so the end of the Cold War was an opportunity rather than the only cause.
