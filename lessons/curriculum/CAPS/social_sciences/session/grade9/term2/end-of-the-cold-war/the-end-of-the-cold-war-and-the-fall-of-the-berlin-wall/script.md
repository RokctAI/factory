# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the end of the Cold War and the fall of the Berlin Wall in 1989. It covers the problems that weakened the Soviet Union by the 1980s; the reforms of Mikhail Gorbachev and the new relationship with the United States; the revolutions of 1989 in Eastern Europe and the opening of the Berlin Wall on 9 November 1989; and the consequences, including German reunification in 1990 and the effects on South Africa. The anchor dates are March 1985, when Gorbachev became leader of the Soviet Union, and 9 November 1989, when the Berlin Wall was opened. The governing idea is that the Cold War ended not through a war between the superpowers but because the Soviet system could no longer compete, because a Soviet leader chose reform instead of force, and because ordinary people in Eastern Europe seized the chance to demand freedom.

## Subtopic: A Superpower in Trouble

By the early 1980s the Soviet Union faced deep problems, even though it still had a huge army and thousands of nuclear weapons. Its centrally planned economy, in which the state decided what factories should make and at what price, had stopped growing fast. Factories met production targets on paper but often produced poor-quality goods that people did not want, while ordinary Soviet citizens queued for food, clothing and consumer goods. Technology lagged behind the West, especially in computers and electronics. Farming was so inefficient that the Soviet Union, with some of the best farmland in the world, had to import grain.

The Cold War itself was a heavy burden. The arms race cost the Soviet Union a far larger share of its national wealth than it cost the United States, because the Soviet economy was much smaller. Western estimates suggest that military spending took perhaps 15 to 25 percent of Soviet economic output. In 1979 Soviet troops invaded Afghanistan to support a communist government, and became trapped in a long, costly war against Afghan resistance fighters, the mujahideen, who were armed by the United States and others. About 15 000 Soviet soldiers died, and the war became deeply unpopular at home.

Meanwhile the United States under President Ronald Reagan, elected in 1980, increased pressure. Reagan called the Soviet Union an evil empire, raised American military spending sharply and in 1983 announced the Strategic Defense Initiative, a plan for a space-based shield against missiles, which critics called Star Wars. The Soviet Union could not easily match this spending. In the mid-1980s, the world price of oil, the Soviet Union's main export, also fell, reducing its income.

Discontent was also growing in Eastern Europe. In Poland in 1980, workers at the Gdańsk shipyard went on strike and formed Solidarity, the first independent trade union in the communist world, which soon had about ten million members. The Polish government declared martial law in December 1981 and banned Solidarity, but could not destroy it. Eastern European economies had borrowed heavily from Western banks in the 1970s and were struggling to repay their debts, and many of their citizens could see from Western television that people in the West lived far better.

The Soviet Union was also led by old, sick men. Leonid Brezhnev died in 1982, and his two successors, Yuri Andropov and Konstantin Chernenko, both died within about a year of taking office. The system needed new leadership.

So the next question is: what did the new leader, Mikhail Gorbachev, do?

## Subtopic: Gorbachev, Reform and a New Relationship

In March 1985 Mikhail Gorbachev, aged 54, became General Secretary of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union. He was a committed communist, but he believed the system had to be reformed to survive. He introduced two key policies.

Glasnost means openness. Gorbachev allowed more freedom of speech and of the press, released many political prisoners, and allowed open discussion of problems and of the crimes of the past, such as Stalin's purges. The nuclear disaster at Chernobyl in April 1986, which the government at first tried to hide, convinced Gorbachev that secrecy was dangerous. Perestroika means restructuring. It aimed to make the economy more efficient by allowing some private enterprise, giving factories more control over their own decisions and reducing central planning. In practice, perestroika disrupted the old system without creating a working new one, and shortages grew worse.

Gorbachev also changed Soviet foreign policy, which he called new thinking. He wanted to cut military spending so that resources could go to the economy, and he believed that nuclear war could have no winner. He met Reagan at a series of summits, at Geneva in 1985 and Reykjavik in 1986, and the two leaders built personal trust. In December 1987 they signed the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty, known as the INF Treaty, the first agreement to destroy a whole category of nuclear weapons, with inspections on both sides. Soviet troops withdrew from Afghanistan, and the last left in February 1989. In June 1987 Reagan stood at the Brandenburg Gate in West Berlin and said, "Mr Gorbachev, tear down this wall!"

Most importantly for Eastern Europe, Gorbachev abandoned the Brezhnev Doctrine, under which the Soviet Union had sent tanks into Hungary in 1956 and Czechoslovakia in 1968 to keep communist governments in power. In a speech at the United Nations in December 1988, he announced that the Soviet Union would cut its armed forces by 500 000 soldiers and said that every nation should be free to choose its own path. Soviet officials later joked that the new policy was the Sinatra Doctrine, after the song "My Way": the countries of Eastern Europe could now do things their way.

Inside the Soviet Union, glasnost had unexpected effects. Once people could speak freely, they criticised the Communist Party, and nationalist movements grew in the non-Russian republics. In March 1989 Soviet citizens voted in the first partly competitive elections since the 1917 revolution, and some senior party officials were defeated. Gorbachev hoped that openness would strengthen communism by making it more honest and efficient, but it also revealed how many people had lost faith in it.

So the next question is: what happened when the people of Eastern Europe realised the tanks would not come?

## Subtopic: The Revolutions of 1989 and the Night the Wall Opened

In 1989 the communist governments of Eastern Europe fell one after another, mostly peacefully. In Poland, the independent trade union Solidarity, led by Lech Wałęsa, had been banned in 1981 but survived underground. In early 1989 the government agreed to round-table talks, and in partly free elections in June 1989 Solidarity won almost every seat it was allowed to contest. In August 1989 Tadeusz Mazowiecki became the first non-communist prime minister in Eastern Europe since the 1940s. In Hungary, reformers in the communist government began removing the fence on the border with Austria in May 1989, and on 11 September 1989 they opened the border to East Germans.

Thousands of East Germans, who were allowed to travel to Hungary on holiday, now escaped to the West through Austria, while others crowded into the West German embassy in Prague. Inside East Germany, protest grew. Every Monday evening, people gathered at the Nikolai Church in Leipzig to pray for peace and then marched through the city, chanting "We are the people". On 9 October 1989 about 70 000 people marched in Leipzig; the security forces were ready to shoot but did not, and from then on the protests grew. The hardline leader Erich Honecker was forced to resign on 18 October 1989. On 4 November 1989 a huge demonstration of about half a million people filled the Alexanderplatz in East Berlin.

The new government decided to ease travel rules to reduce the pressure. On the evening of 9 November 1989, at a live television press conference, the party spokesman Günter Schabowski announced the new rules. He had not been properly briefed, and when a journalist asked when the rules took effect, he looked at his notes and said, "immediately, without delay." Thousands of East Berliners rushed to the border crossings. At the Bornholmer Strasse checkpoint, the officer in charge, Harald Jäger, faced a growing crowd with no orders. At about 11.30 that night, to avoid bloodshed, he opened the gate. Other crossings followed. People streamed into West Berlin, strangers embraced, and Berliners climbed onto the Wall at the Brandenburg Gate and began chipping pieces off it with hammers. Nobody had planned the opening of the Wall; it happened because of a mistake, a crowd, and the decision of guards not to shoot.

In the weeks that followed, the Velvet Revolution in Czechoslovakia brought the playwright Václav Havel to power by the end of December 1989, and Bulgaria's leader was removed. Only in Romania did the revolution turn violent: hundreds of people were killed, and the dictator Nicolae Ceaușescu and his wife were executed on 25 December 1989.

So the final question is: what did the fall of the Wall lead to, and what did it mean?

## Subtopic: Consequences, South Africa and the Error Museum

In December 1989, at a summit in Malta, President George H. W. Bush and Gorbachev declared that a new era was beginning, and many historians see this meeting as the symbolic end of the Cold War. East Germany held its first free election in March 1990, and the communists were heavily defeated. In September 1990 the two German states and the four former occupying powers signed the Two Plus Four Treaty, and on 3 October 1990 Germany was reunified, with Berlin later restored as its capital. Official demolition of the Wall had begun in June 1990, though some sections were kept as memorials. In 1990 Gorbachev received the Nobel Peace Prize, although within the Soviet Union many blamed him for the loss of its empire in Eastern Europe. The Warsaw Pact was dissolved in 1991, and the Soviet Union itself collapsed in December 1991, which is the subject of the next lesson.

The end of the Cold War also affected South Africa. For decades the apartheid government had justified its policies, its huge military spending and its wars in Angola and Namibia by claiming that South Africa faced a total onslaught from communism directed by Moscow, and it portrayed the ANC, which received weapons and training from the Soviet Union, as a communist threat. When communism collapsed in Eastern Europe, this argument lost much of its force, and Western governments were less willing to tolerate apartheid as an ally against communism. At the same time, the ANC lost a major source of support and saw that a negotiated settlement was necessary. In December 1988, agreements signed in New York had linked the withdrawal of Cuban troops from Angola to independence for Namibia, which followed in March 1990. On 2 February 1990, less than three months after the Wall opened, President F. W. de Klerk announced the unbanning of the ANC, the PAC and the South African Communist Party and the coming release of Nelson Mandela. De Klerk later said that the fall of the Berlin Wall had made this decision easier. Internal resistance, sanctions and economic crisis were, of course, also major reasons for change, as the Term 4 lessons show.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "The Wall was knocked down in a planned ceremony." The Wall was opened suddenly on 9 November 1989 after a confused announcement and pressure from crowds; demolition came later.

Exhibit two: "The Cold War ended with a victory in battle." The superpowers never fought each other directly; the Cold War ended through reform, negotiation and mostly peaceful revolutions.

Exhibit three: "Germany was reunified on the night the Wall fell." Reunification came almost a year later, on 3 October 1990.

Exhibit four: "Gorbachev wanted to end communism." He wanted to reform and save communism, but his reforms released forces he could not control.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: an old car running out of fuel, a new driver who opens the windows, and a gate that swings open by mistake.

## Subtopic: An Old Car Running Out of Fuel

By the 1980s the Soviet Union was like a big, heavy old car. It looked powerful, with a huge army and many nuclear weapons, but its engine, the economy, was sputtering. Shops were often empty, people queued for bread and shoes, and factories made goods nobody wanted.

The Cold War was burning up its fuel. Building weapons cost the Soviet Union a much bigger share of its money than it cost America. A war in Afghanistan dragged on for ten years and killed thousands of young Soviet soldiers. American President Reagan spent even more on weapons. And the Soviet leaders were very old; three of them died in just over two years.

So who took the wheel?

## Subtopic: A New Driver Opens the Windows

In 1985 a younger leader, Mikhail Gorbachev, took over. He wanted to fix the car, not throw it away. He opened the windows with glasnost, which means openness: people could speak and write more freely. He tried to repair the engine with perestroika, which means restructuring the economy.

He also made friends with Reagan. They signed a treaty in 1987 to destroy a whole type of nuclear missile. Soviet soldiers came home from Afghanistan. And Gorbachev said something huge: the countries of Eastern Europe could choose their own way. The Soviet tanks would not come to stop them.

So what did the people do?

## Subtopic: A Gate Swings Open by Mistake

In 1989 people across Eastern Europe took their chance. Poland voted in a non-communist government. Hungary opened its border fence. In East Germany, crowds marched every Monday, chanting "We are the people".

On 9 November 1989, an East German official read out new travel rules on television. Asked when they started, he said, "immediately". He had made a mistake, but thousands of people rushed to the Wall. A guard at one gate, with no orders and a huge crowd in front of him, opened it. People poured through, laughing, crying and hugging strangers. Some climbed the Wall with hammers and chipped off pieces to keep as souvenirs. Families who had been separated for twenty-eight years met again that night.

In the weeks that followed, the same thing happened in other countries. In Czechoslovakia, huge crowds jingled their keys in the squares, as if to tell the communist leaders it was time to go home, and they went. In most countries hardly a shot was fired.

Less than a year later, in October 1990, Germany became one country again. And in South Africa, in February 1990, the government unbanned the ANC and announced Nelson Mandela's release.

Here is the line to remember: the Cold War ended because the Soviet Union could no longer afford it, because Gorbachev chose reform instead of force, and because ordinary people walked through the gate.
