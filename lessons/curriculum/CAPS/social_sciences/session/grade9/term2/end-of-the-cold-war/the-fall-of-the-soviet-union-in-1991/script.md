# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the fall of the Soviet Union in 1991. It covers how the Soviet Union was organised as a union of fifteen republics and why that union was fragile; the growing crisis from 1989 to 1991, including economic collapse and nationalist movements; the attempted coup of August 1991 and the role of Boris Yeltsin; and the final dissolution of the Soviet Union in December 1991 and its consequences. The anchor dates are 19 August 1991, when hardliners tried to seize power from Mikhail Gorbachev, and 25 December 1991, when Gorbachev resigned and the Soviet flag was lowered over the Kremlin. The governing idea is that the reforms meant to save the Soviet Union released economic chaos and national demands for independence that the system could not survive, and that the failed coup turned a slow decline into a sudden collapse.

## Subtopic: A Union of Fifteen Republics

The Union of Soviet Socialist Republics, the USSR, was formed in 1922, after the Russian Revolution of 1917 and the civil war that followed. By the 1940s it consisted of fifteen republics. The largest by far was Russia. The others were Ukraine and Belarus in the west; Moldova; the three Baltic republics of Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania; Georgia, Armenia and Azerbaijan in the Caucasus; and Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan in Central Asia. In 1991 the Soviet Union had nearly 290 million people, belonging to more than one hundred nationalities and speaking many languages.

On paper, each republic had its own government and even the right to leave the Union. In reality, power was held by the Communist Party in Moscow, and the republics had little real independence. Russian was the main language of government, education and the army, and many Russians were sent to live and work in the other republics. Under Stalin, whole national groups, such as the Chechens and the Crimean Tatars, had been deported, and in Ukraine millions died in the famine of 1932 to 1933, which Ukrainians call the Holodomor. The Baltic republics had been independent countries between the two world wars and were forcibly taken over by the Soviet Union in 1940 under a secret agreement with Nazi Germany. Many people in these republics never accepted Soviet rule, but while the Soviet state was strong and used its secret police, the KGB, to crush dissent, they could not openly resist.

It is important to be balanced about the Soviet record. The Soviet Union had turned a largely rural country into an industrial power, it had played the biggest part in defeating Nazi Germany at enormous cost, with about 27 million Soviet dead, and it had achieved great things in science and space, sending the first satellite and the first human into space. Education and basic health care were free, literacy became almost universal, and unemployment was officially unknown. Many Soviet citizens were proud of these achievements, and the Soviet Union also gave support to anti-colonial and liberation movements in Africa, including in South Africa. But these achievements came at a terrible cost in lives and freedom, and by the 1980s the system was no longer delivering a better life.

So the union was held together by force and by the power of the Communist Party, not by the free choice of its peoples. As long as the centre was strong, this worked. Once the centre weakened, the union was in danger.

So the next question is: how did the centre weaken between 1989 and 1991?

## Subtopic: Crisis From 1989 to 1991

Gorbachev's reforms, glasnost and perestroika, were meant to strengthen the Soviet Union, but they weakened the forces that held it together. Glasnost allowed people to speak openly for the first time in decades. In the republics, people began to talk about their own languages, histories and suffering under Soviet rule, and nationalist movements grew rapidly. On 23 August 1989, the fiftieth anniversary of the secret Nazi-Soviet pact, about two million people in Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania joined hands in a human chain, the Baltic Way, stretching more than 600 kilometres across the three republics, to demand independence. On 11 March 1990 Lithuania became the first Soviet republic to declare independence. In January 1991 Soviet troops attacked the television tower in Vilnius, the capital of Lithuania, and fourteen unarmed civilians were killed. Instead of frightening people, the violence strengthened their determination. Similar movements grew in Georgia, Armenia, Moldova and Ukraine, where people demanded the right to use their own languages and to control their own affairs. Conflicts also broke out between nationalities, for example between Armenians and Azerbaijanis over the region of Nagorno-Karabakh.

At the same time, the economy was collapsing. Perestroika weakened central planning, but there was no proper market to replace it. Factories could not get supplies, shortages of food and basic goods grew worse, prices rose and the government printed money, causing inflation. Rationing returned in many cities. Coal miners went on strike in 1989 and 1991. Many people began to blame Gorbachev and the Communist Party.

Politics also changed. In 1990 the Communist Party gave up its constitutional monopoly on power, and other parties were allowed. Boris Yeltsin, a former Communist Party leader in Moscow who had become a fierce critic of Gorbachev, became the leader of Russia within the Soviet Union, and in June 1991 he was elected President of Russia by popular vote, the first time Russians had directly chosen their leader. Yeltsin argued that Russia itself should have more power than the Soviet centre. In March 1991 Gorbachev held a referendum on keeping a reformed union; about 76 percent of those who voted said yes, but six republics, including the three Baltic states, refused to take part. Gorbachev then negotiated a new union treaty that would give the republics much more power, due to be signed on 20 August 1991.

So the next question is: why did some leaders try to stop this, and what happened?

## Subtopic: The August Coup of 1991

Hardline communists in the government, the army and the KGB believed that the new union treaty would destroy the Soviet Union. On 18 August 1991, while Gorbachev was on holiday at his country house at Foros in Crimea, they placed him under house arrest and cut his telephone lines. On 19 August they announced on television that Gorbachev was ill and that an eight-man State Committee for the State of Emergency had taken power. The group included the vice-president Gennady Yanayev, the head of the KGB Vladimir Kryuchkov and the defence minister Dmitry Yazov. Tanks rolled into Moscow.

The coup leaders made serious mistakes. They did not arrest Yeltsin. He went to the Russian parliament building in Moscow, known as the White House, and climbed onto a tank outside it to read a statement calling the coup illegal and calling for a general strike. Tens of thousands of Muscovites came out to defend the White House, building barricades from buses, trolleys and paving stones. Some soldiers and tank crews refused to obey orders and changed sides. Three young men were killed in a clash with armoured vehicles on the night of 20 to 21 August. The coup leaders, divided and unsure, could not bring themselves to order a full assault that would have caused a massacre.

Gorbachev himself had refused to cooperate. When members of the plot visited him at Foros on 18 August and demanded that he either declare a state of emergency or hand power to the vice-president, he refused and told them they would fail. He and his family, cut off from the world, listened to foreign radio broadcasts on a small radio to learn what was happening in Moscow.

On 21 August 1991 the coup collapsed and Gorbachev returned to Moscow. But he returned to a different country. Yeltsin had become the hero of the resistance, and he openly humiliated Gorbachev in the Russian parliament. The Communist Party was blamed for the coup: Gorbachev resigned as its General Secretary on 24 August, and its activities were suspended and later banned in Russia. The coup, intended to save the Soviet Union, had destroyed the Communist Party, the institution that held it together.

So the final question is: how did the Soviet Union finally end, and what did its end mean?

## Subtopic: The Union Ends, Consequences and the Error Museum

In the days after the coup, republic after republic declared independence. The independence of Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania was recognised in September 1991. On 1 December 1991 the people of Ukraine, the second-largest republic, voted in a referendum, and over 90 percent of voters chose independence. Without Ukraine, a union with Russia made little sense. On 8 December 1991 the leaders of Russia, Ukraine and Belarus met in a hunting lodge in the Belovezha Forest in Belarus and declared that the Soviet Union no longer existed. They set up a loose association called the Commonwealth of Independent States, and on 21 December, at Alma-Ata in Kazakhstan, eleven former republics joined it. Gorbachev was now a president without a country. On 25 December 1991 he resigned in a television address, and that evening the Soviet flag over the Kremlin was replaced by the Russian flag. The Soviet Union formally ceased to exist the next day, and fifteen independent countries took its place.

The consequences were enormous. The Cold War was over, and the United States was now the world's only superpower. Russia took over the Soviet Union's seat on the United Nations Security Council, and the nuclear weapons left in Ukraine, Belarus and Kazakhstan were transferred to Russia by the mid-1990s. Many people in the former Soviet Union faced great hardship in the 1990s: rapid economic changes caused unemployment, inflation and falling living standards, and a few people became very rich by buying state industries cheaply. Some conflicts between and within the new states continued, and debates about the borders and loyalties of former Soviet regions, such as Crimea, still shape world events today.

For South Africa, the collapse of the Soviet Union completed the change that the fall of the Berlin Wall had begun. The ANC and the South African Communist Party lost their main foreign supplier of weapons, training and funding, while the apartheid government could no longer claim it was defending the West against communism. Both sides had stronger reasons to negotiate, and the multiparty talks known as CODESA began in December 1991, the same month the Soviet Union ended.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "The Soviet Union and Russia were the same thing." Russia was the largest of fifteen Soviet republics; when the Union ended, Russia became one of fifteen independent countries.

Exhibit two: "The August coup was carried out by reformers who wanted democracy." It was carried out by hardline communists who wanted to stop reform and save the Union.

Exhibit three: "The Soviet Union was defeated by the United States in a war." It collapsed from within, through economic failure, nationalism and political crisis.

Exhibit four: "The Berlin Wall fell in 1991." The Wall was opened in 1989; the Soviet Union ended in December 1991.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a house held together with straps, a man on a tank, and a flag coming down on Christmas night.

## Subtopic: A House Held Together With Straps

Imagine one enormous house with fifteen rooms, each room home to a different family with its own language and history. That was the Soviet Union. Russia was the biggest room by far. The other rooms included Ukraine, Belarus, the three small Baltic countries, Georgia, Armenia, Kazakhstan and others.

The families did not choose to live together. Many had been forced in. What held the house together were strong straps: the Communist Party, the army and the secret police. As long as the straps were tight, nobody could leave.

Then Gorbachev loosened the straps. With glasnost, people could speak freely, and many families said, we want our own house. In 1989, about two million people in the Baltic countries held hands in a human chain hundreds of kilometres long to ask for freedom. At the same time the shops were empty, prices rose, and people grew angry. Some families even began to fight with each other. The landlord, Gorbachev, tried to write a new agreement that would let each family run its own room but stay in the house.

So what happened when some people tried to tighten the straps again?

## Subtopic: A Man on a Tank

In August 1991, hardline communists decided to stop the changes. They locked Gorbachev in his holiday house, told the country he was ill, and sent tanks into Moscow.

But they forgot to arrest Boris Yeltsin, the elected leader of Russia. Yeltsin went to the Russian parliament, climbed up onto a tank, and told the people: this is illegal, resist it. Thousands of people built barricades with buses and stones. Some soldiers refused to attack their own people. After three days, the plotters gave up.

Gorbachev, meanwhile, had refused to give in to the plotters. Cut off at his holiday house, he and his family listened to news on a small radio.

Gorbachev came home, but the plot had backfired. He had left one country and come back to another. People blamed the Communist Party, and Yeltsin was now the hero.

So what happened to the great house?

## Subtopic: A Flag Comes Down on Christmas Night

After the failed plot, one family after another moved out. The Baltic countries left. In December 1991 Ukraine voted to leave, with more than nine out of ten voters saying yes. Then the leaders of Russia, Ukraine and Belarus met in a forest lodge and agreed that the Soviet Union was finished.

On 25 December 1991, Gorbachev went on television and resigned. That night, the red flag with the hammer and sickle came down over the Kremlin, and the Russian flag went up. Fifteen new countries now stood where one superpower had been. The Cold War was truly over. For some people this was a day of joy and freedom; for others, especially many older Russians, it felt like losing their country, and the hard years that followed made life difficult for millions.

Far away in South Africa, this mattered too. Without the Soviet Union, the apartheid government could no longer say it was fighting communism, and the ANC lost its biggest foreign helper. Both sides had more reason to talk, and the talks called CODESA began that same December. So events in a forest lodge in Belarus and on a tank in Moscow helped to shape the road to South Africa's democracy in 1994.

Here is the line to remember: the Soviet Union was held together by force; when reforms loosened the straps and a hardline coup failed in August 1991, its fifteen republics walked away, and on 25 December 1991 it was gone.
