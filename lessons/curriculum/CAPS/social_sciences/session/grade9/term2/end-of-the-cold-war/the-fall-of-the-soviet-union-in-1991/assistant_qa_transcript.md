### subtopic_1 — A Union of Fifteen Republics
**Assistant:** Why did the Baltic republics lead the way to independence?

**Tutor:** Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania led the movement for independence for several historical and practical reasons. First, unlike most Soviet republics, they had been independent countries between 1918 and 1940, so many people remembered or had been told about independence, and they had their own flags, anthems and national traditions. Second, they had been taken over by force in 1940, after the Nazi-Soviet pact of 1939 secretly placed them in the Soviet sphere. Under glasnost, Soviet historians were finally allowed to admit that this secret agreement had existed, which strengthened the Baltic argument that their incorporation into the Soviet Union had been illegal. Third, the Baltic peoples had suffered greatly under Soviet rule, with mass deportations to Siberia in the 1940s and the settlement of many Russian-speakers in their countries, which made people fear that their languages and cultures would disappear. Fourth, they were closer to Western Europe, especially to Finland and Scandinavia, and could see the contrast in living standards. Popular movements, called Popular Fronts in Estonia and Latvia and Sąjūdis in Lithuania, were formed in 1988 and organised peacefully, using song festivals, mass rallies and the human chain of the Baltic Way in August 1989. Their protests are often called the Singing Revolution. Because Western governments had never officially recognised the Soviet takeover in 1940, the Baltic states also had international support. When Soviet troops killed fourteen civilians in Vilnius in January 1991, the world condemned the attack, and the Baltic movements became even stronger.

### subtopic_2 — Crisis From 1989 to 1991
**Assistant:** Why did perestroika make the economy worse instead of better?

**Tutor:** Perestroika is a good example of how a reform can fail if it is only half carried out. The Soviet economy was run by central planning: officials in Moscow decided what every factory should produce, where it should get its materials, and what prices should be charged. This system was inefficient, but at least everyone knew their place in it. Gorbachev's reforms, especially the Law on State Enterprises of 1987, gave factory managers more freedom to make their own decisions and allowed some small private businesses, called cooperatives. However, prices were still mostly fixed by the state, there was no proper banking system or market to connect buyers and sellers, and managers often used their new freedom to raise wages or produce whatever was most profitable rather than what was needed. The old system of instructions broke down, but no working market replaced it. Supplies stopped arriving, shelves emptied, and people hoarded goods. The republics began to keep their own products for themselves instead of sending them to the rest of the Union. Meanwhile the government ran large budget deficits, made worse by falling oil income, the cost of the Chernobyl clean-up and the Armenian earthquake of 1988, and it printed money to pay its bills, which caused inflation. Gorbachev hesitated between radical market reform and keeping the old system, and he never fully committed to either. By 1990 and 1991, many Soviet citizens felt that their lives were getting worse, which undermined support for Gorbachev and the Union.

### subtopic_3 — The August Coup of 1991
**Assistant:** Why did the August 1991 coup fail so quickly?

**Tutor:** The coup failed for several reasons, some caused by the plotters' own weaknesses and some by the courage of ordinary people. The plotters were poorly organised and divided. They did not arrest Boris Yeltsin, the elected President of Russia, who was able to reach the Russian parliament building and organise resistance. They did not cut off all communications, so news spread through foreign radio stations such as the BBC, through fax machines and through independent journalists, and Yeltsin's appeal reached millions. At their own press conference on 19 August, the coup leaders looked nervous, and the hands of the acting president, Gennady Yanayev, were seen shaking on television, which made them look weak rather than in control. Ordinary people refused to be frightened. Tens of thousands gathered around the White House, building barricades and forming human chains. Most importantly, the army and the KGB were not united. Some tank commanders turned their guns away from the White House, and elite units refused to storm it, because soldiers did not want to shoot their own people. After the deaths of three young men on the night of 20 to 21 August, the plotters lost their nerve and called off the attempt. Six years of glasnost had also changed society: people were no longer willing to return to fear and obedience. The coup's failure showed that the old methods of control no longer worked, and it destroyed the authority of the Communist Party.

### subtopic_4 — The Union Ends, Consequences and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Was the collapse of the Soviet Union a good thing?

**Tutor:** Historians and people from the former Soviet Union disagree, and a balanced answer looks at different perspectives. For many people, the collapse brought great benefits. The Baltic states, Ukraine, Georgia and others gained the independence they had long wanted, and countries in Eastern Europe were finally free from Soviet control. The end of the Cold War greatly reduced the danger of nuclear war between the superpowers, and both sides cut their nuclear arsenals in the following years. Political prisoners were freed, censorship ended in many places, and people could travel, worship and speak more freely. For South Africa and other parts of Africa, the end of superpower rivalry helped to end proxy wars and opened space for negotiated settlements. However, the collapse also brought serious suffering. In Russia and several other republics, the 1990s were years of economic crisis: savings were wiped out by inflation, unemployment rose, life expectancy fell, and state property was often sold cheaply to a few well-connected people. Wars broke out in some regions, such as Chechnya and parts of the Caucasus, and millions of Russians suddenly found themselves living as minorities in new countries. Some Russians felt humiliated by the loss of superpower status, and in 2005 Russia's president Vladimir Putin called the collapse a great geopolitical catastrophe. Many people in the Baltic states and Ukraine, by contrast, see 1991 as their liberation. So whether it was good depends partly on whose experience you look at, which is why historians use many sources.
