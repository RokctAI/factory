### subtopic_1 — Germany and Berlin Divided into Zones
**Assistant:** Why was Berlin so important to both sides?

**Tutor:** Berlin mattered for symbolic, political and strategic reasons. Symbolically, it had been the capital of Germany and of Hitler's Third Reich, so controlling it carried great prestige. For the Western Allies, West Berlin became a symbol of their commitment to defend freedom and resist communism: if they abandoned it, their promises to protect other allies in Europe would lose credibility. American leaders often said that if Berlin fell, Western Europe might follow. For the Soviet Union and East Germany, West Berlin was a thorn in their side. It was an island of capitalism and democracy about 160 kilometres inside East German territory, with bright shops, Western radio broadcasts and newspapers that showed East Germans an alternative way of life. It was also a base for Western intelligence services to spy on the Eastern bloc. Most importantly, before 1961, West Berlin was an open door through which East Germans could escape to the West, which drained East Germany of skilled workers and embarrassed the communist government. Strategically, Berlin was also where the superpowers' forces stood very close to each other, which created the risk that a small incident could escalate into war, as in the tank standoff at Checkpoint Charlie in October 1961. This is why Berlin was the scene of two of the most serious Cold War crises, the Blockade of 1948 to 1949 and the building of the Wall in 1961, and why the fall of the Wall in 1989 symbolised the end of the Cold War.

### subtopic_2 — The Berlin Blockade and Airlift, and Two German States
**Assistant:** How did the Berlin Airlift manage to supply a whole city?

**Tutor:** The airlift was one of the greatest logistical achievements in history, and it succeeded through careful organisation, determination and the cooperation of many people. When the blockade began in June 1948, few believed that a city of about two million people could be supplied by air, since experts estimated that West Berlin needed about 4 500 tonnes of supplies a day, including food and coal for heating, electricity and industry. At first, the airlift delivered far less, using small American and British transport planes. American General Lucius Clay and the airlift commander, General William Tunner, organised the operation like a conveyor belt. Larger American C-54 aircraft were brought in from around the world. Planes flew along three air corridors at fixed heights and intervals, landing at Tempelhof, Gatow and later a new airport at Tegel, built in about three months by Berlin workers, many of them women. Each aircraft had one chance to land; if it missed, it returned to base rather than circling, to avoid disrupting the timing. Unloading crews, including German workers, could empty a plane in about half an hour. Pilots and ground crews came from the United States, Britain, Australia, New Zealand, Canada and South Africa. By spring 1949 deliveries exceeded the daily target, and on the Easter Parade of 16 April 1949, nearly 1 400 flights delivered about 13 000 tonnes in one day. In total, about 2,3 million tonnes were delivered, two thirds of it coal. The success convinced Stalin that the blockade had failed.

### subtopic_3 — Why and How the Berlin Wall Was Built
**Assistant:** Why did so many East Germans want to leave?

**Tutor:** People left East Germany for a combination of economic, political and personal reasons. Economically, life in West Germany was improving much faster. With Marshall Plan aid, a new currency and a market economy, West Germany enjoyed its economic miracle in the 1950s: unemployment fell, wages rose, and shops filled with consumer goods such as cars, refrigerators and televisions. In East Germany, reparations to the Soviet Union, central planning and the collectivisation of farms and businesses caused shortages of food, housing and consumer goods, and wages were lower. Skilled professionals, such as doctors, engineers and scientists, could earn much more in the West. Politically, East Germany was a one-party state ruled by the SED. Freedom of speech, the press, religion and movement were restricted, elections offered no real choice, and the Stasi secret police watched and informed on citizens. The crushing of the workers' uprising of June 1953 by Soviet tanks showed that protest was dangerous. Farmers forced into collective farms and owners of small businesses whose property was taken had strong reasons to leave. Personally, many people had family in the West, and young people were attracted by Western music, fashion and freedom. Because Berlin was open, leaving was relatively easy until 1961. About 2,7 million people left between 1949 and 1961, about one in six East Germans. The rising flood in 1961, about 30 000 in July alone, convinced the East German leaders that they had to close the border.

### subtopic_4 — Life With the Wall, Its Meaning and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How did people escape over or under the Berlin Wall?

**Tutor:** East Germans showed great courage and ingenuity in trying to escape, though many attempts failed and some cost lives. In the first days after 13 August 1961, when the barrier was still barbed wire, some people simply climbed through or jumped over, like the soldier Conrad Schumann. In Bernauer Strasse, where the front walls of apartment buildings formed the border, people jumped from windows into the street on the western side, sometimes into nets held by West Berlin firefighters, until the windows were bricked up and the buildings later demolished. As the Wall was strengthened, escapes became more inventive. Groups dug tunnels under the Wall, often starting from cellars in West Berlin; in October 1964, Tunnel 57 allowed 57 people to escape over two nights. People hid in specially modified cars, in hidden compartments behind seats or inside dashboards, crossing at checkpoints with the help of Western drivers. Some used forged passports or disguised themselves as Western soldiers. In 1979 two families escaped from East Germany across the inner German border in a home-made hot-air balloon sewn from curtains and bedsheets. Others swam across rivers and canals or used small aircraft. Escape helpers in the West sometimes charged money, while others helped for free. The East German government punished attempts severely: people caught were imprisoned, and guards were rewarded for stopping escapes. At least 140 people died at the Berlin Wall, and hundreds more along the inner German border. The courage of escapers made the Wall a symbol of the desire for freedom.
