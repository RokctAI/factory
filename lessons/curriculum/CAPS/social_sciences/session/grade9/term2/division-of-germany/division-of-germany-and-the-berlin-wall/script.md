# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the division of Germany after World War II and the building of the Berlin Wall. It covers the division of Germany and Berlin into four occupation zones and the growing disagreements between the Allies from 1945 to 1947; the Berlin Blockade and Airlift of 1948 to 1949 and the creation of two German states in 1949; the reasons for building the Berlin Wall in August 1961 and how it was built; and life with the Wall, attempts to escape and the Wall's significance as a symbol of the Cold War. The anchor dates are 24 June 1948, when the Soviet Union began the Berlin Blockade, and 13 August 1961, when East German forces sealed the border through Berlin. The governing idea is that Germany became the place where the Cold War was most visible: a defeated country divided between two hostile systems, with its former capital split by a wall that stood as a symbol of division until 1989.

## Subtopic: Germany and Berlin Divided into Zones

As agreed at Yalta and confirmed at Potsdam in 1945, defeated Germany was divided into four occupation zones, each controlled by one of the victorious powers: the United States in the south, Britain in the north-west, France in the west, and the Soviet Union in the east. German territory east of the Oder and Neisse rivers was placed under Polish administration, and millions of Germans living there and elsewhere in Eastern Europe were expelled westwards. Berlin, the former capital, lay about 160 kilometres inside the Soviet zone, but because of its importance it was also divided into four sectors, with the Western Allies given access to their sectors along agreed road, rail and air corridors. An Allied Control Council was supposed to govern Germany as a whole.

The Allies agreed on some aims, often called the four Ds: denazification, removing Nazis from public life and putting leading Nazis on trial at Nuremberg; demilitarisation, abolishing Germany's armed forces; democratisation, rebuilding democratic political life; and decentralisation of the economy and government. But they soon disagreed about Germany's future.

The Soviet Union, having suffered terribly from German invasion, wanted Germany kept weak, demanded large reparations and took factories, machinery and goods from its zone to rebuild its own economy. It supported German communists, and in April 1946 forced the merger of the Communist Party and the Social Democratic Party in its zone into the Socialist Unity Party, the SED, which would rule East Germany. The Western Allies, especially the United States and Britain, came to believe that a prosperous, democratic Germany was essential for the recovery of Europe as a whole and as a barrier against communism. They found that feeding the population of their zones was costing them a great deal, and that Soviet reparations from the east were making the problem worse.

In 1946 the Western Allies stopped reparation deliveries from their zones to the Soviet Union. On 1 January 1947 the American and British zones were merged economically into a single area called Bizonia, and the French zone later joined to form Trizonia. Marshall Plan aid flowed into the western zones from 1948, but the Soviet zone was excluded. Germany was dividing in practice along the line of the Iron Curtain.

So the next question is: how did this division become a crisis over Berlin?

## Subtopic: The Berlin Blockade and Airlift, and Two German States

In June 1948 the Western Allies introduced a new currency, the Deutsche Mark, in their zones, to stop inflation and the black market and to encourage economic recovery. It was soon introduced into West Berlin too. Stalin saw this as a step towards creating a separate West German state and feared that a strong western currency would undermine the economy of the Soviet zone.

On 24 June 1948 the Soviet Union cut off all road, rail and canal routes between the western zones and West Berlin, and stopped supplies of electricity from the Soviet sector. About two million West Berliners were left with only enough food for about five weeks. Stalin's aim was to force the Western Allies either to abandon their plans for West Germany or to leave Berlin altogether. The Western Allies did not want to give up Berlin, which had become a symbol of their commitment to resisting communism, but they also wanted to avoid war by trying to force a way through with armed convoys.

Their solution was the Berlin Airlift, which the Americans called Operation Vittles. The air corridors had been guaranteed in writing, and shooting down unarmed transport planes would have been an act of war. American and British aircraft, with help from crews from Australia, New Zealand, Canada and South Africa, began flying in food, coal, medicine and other supplies. At its peak, an aircraft landed in West Berlin every few minutes, day and night. On a single day in April 1949, almost 1 400 flights delivered about 13 000 tonnes of cargo. Over about eleven months, the airlift made more than 270 000 flights and delivered about 2,3 million tonnes of supplies, most of it coal. One American pilot, Gail Halvorsen, became famous as the Candy Bomber for dropping sweets attached to small parachutes for Berlin's children. About 100 people died in accidents during the airlift.

The airlift succeeded. On 12 May 1949 the Soviet Union lifted the blockade, having failed to force the West out of Berlin. It was a propaganda defeat for the Soviet Union and a demonstration of Western determination, and it helped persuade Western countries to form NATO in April 1949.

The division of Germany then became formal. On 23 May 1949 the western zones became the Federal Republic of Germany, known as West Germany, a democratic, capitalist state with its capital in Bonn and Konrad Adenauer as its first chancellor. On 7 October 1949 the Soviet zone became the German Democratic Republic, known as East Germany, a communist one-party state led by the SED under Walter Ulbricht, with East Berlin as its capital. West Germany joined NATO in 1955 and East Germany the Warsaw Pact in 1956.

Notice how the CAPS syllabus refers to the division of Germany in 1946. By then, the four-zone system was in place, reparations from the western zones to the Soviet Union had been stopped, and the forced creation of the SED had shown the direction of the Soviet zone. Historians therefore see 1946 and 1947 as the years when temporary occupation zones began hardening into permanent division.

So the next question is: why was the Berlin Wall built in 1961?

## Subtopic: Why and How the Berlin Wall Was Built

During the 1950s, the two German states developed very differently. With Marshall Plan aid and a market economy, West Germany experienced an economic miracle, the Wirtschaftswunder, with rapid growth, rising wages and consumer goods. East Germany, having paid heavy reparations and under a centrally planned economy, grew more slowly, with shortages of food and consumer goods, and its citizens lived under political repression by the secret police, the Stasi, founded in 1950. In June 1953 a workers' uprising across East Germany against higher work quotas was crushed by Soviet tanks.

From 1952 the border between the two German states was closed with fences and guards, but Berlin remained a gap in the Iron Curtain. East Germans could travel into West Berlin by underground train or on foot, and from there fly to West Germany. Between 1949 and August 1961, about 2,7 million people left East Germany, about one in six of the population, many of them through Berlin. Many were young and skilled, such as doctors, engineers, teachers and technicians, a serious brain drain for East Germany. The exodus also embarrassed the communist government, because it showed that many people preferred life in the capitalist West. In July 1961 alone, about 30 000 people fled.

At the same time, the Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev was pressing the West to leave Berlin, and at a summit in Vienna in June 1961 he tried to pressure the new American president, John F. Kennedy. Kennedy responded firmly that the West would defend its rights in West Berlin, but he signalled that he would not interfere with what happened in East Berlin. Walter Ulbricht had said in June 1961 that nobody intended to build a wall, but in secret, with Khrushchev's approval, plans were made.

In the early hours of Sunday 13 August 1961, East German soldiers, police and workers sealed the border between East and West Berlin with barbed wire and fencing, tearing up streets and blocking underground railway stations. Within days, the barbed wire was replaced with concrete blocks. The East German government called it the Anti-Fascist Protection Rampart, claiming it protected the East from Western aggression, but its real purpose was to stop East Germans from leaving. In October 1961, American and Soviet tanks faced each other at Checkpoint Charlie, a crossing point in the city, for sixteen hours, before both withdrew.

So the final question is: what was life like with the Wall, and what did it mean?

## Subtopic: Life With the Wall, Its Meaning and the Error Museum

The Wall cut through streets, squares, cemeteries and even buildings, separating families and friends overnight. Over the years it was developed into a deadly border system. By the 1980s it consisted of an outer concrete wall about 3,6 metres high, with a smooth pipe along the top to prevent climbing, an inner wall, and between them a strip called the death strip, with sand to show footprints, watchtowers, floodlights, dog runs, trip wires and anti-vehicle barriers. The whole border around West Berlin was about 155 kilometres long, of which about 43 kilometres ran through the middle of the city. Guards had orders to shoot people trying to escape.

Despite this, thousands of people escaped, through tunnels dug under the Wall, by jumping from buildings in the early days, hiding in cars, crossing with forged passports, or even by hot-air balloon in 1979. In the first days, the young East German soldier Conrad Schumann leapt over the barbed wire to the West, a moment captured in a famous photograph. At least 140 people were killed at the Berlin Wall, according to research by the Berlin Wall Memorial. In August 1962 the eighteen-year-old Peter Fechter was shot while climbing the Wall and bled to death in the death strip while guards on both sides did not help him, causing outrage in the West. The last person shot dead trying to escape was Chris Gueffroy in February 1989.

The Wall became the most powerful symbol of the Cold War and of the division of Europe. In June 1963 President Kennedy visited West Berlin and declared to a huge crowd, "Ich bin ein Berliner", meaning I am a Berliner, expressing solidarity with the city. In June 1987, President Ronald Reagan stood near the Brandenburg Gate and challenged the Soviet leader: "Mr Gorbachev, tear down this wall!" For South Africans, the Wall has a familiar meaning: just as apartheid divided cities with laws and buffer zones, the Berlin Wall divided a city by force, and both divisions eventually fell. The fall of the Wall in 1989 is the subject of the next lesson.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "The Berlin Wall divided East Germany from West Germany." It surrounded West Berlin, which was inside East Germany; the border between the two German states was a separate fortified line.

Exhibit two: "The Wall was built to keep Westerners out." Its real purpose was to stop East Germans from leaving for the West.

Exhibit three: "The Berlin Airlift was a military attack." It was a peaceful operation that supplied West Berlin by air during the Soviet blockade.

Exhibit four: "Germany was divided into two zones in 1945." It was divided into four occupation zones in 1945; the two German states were created in 1949.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a cake cut into four, planes landing every few minutes, and a wall that appeared overnight.

## Subtopic: A Cake Cut Into Four

After World War II, the winners cut defeated Germany into four pieces, like a cake. America, Britain, France and the Soviet Union each got a piece to look after. The capital, Berlin, was deep inside the Soviet piece, but it was also cut into four.

The winners soon argued about what to do with Germany. The Soviet Union had been badly hurt by Germany and wanted to keep it weak and take its factories as payment. America and Britain wanted Germany to recover so that Europe could recover, and so that hungry Germans would not turn to communism.

In 1947 America and Britain joined their pieces together, and France later joined too. The Western pieces got Marshall Plan money; the Soviet piece did not. Germany was slowly splitting into two halves, west and east.

So what happened when the West introduced its own money?

## Subtopic: Planes Every Few Minutes

In June 1948 the Western Allies brought in a new currency, the Deutsche Mark, including in West Berlin. Stalin was furious. On 24 June 1948 he blocked every road, railway and canal into West Berlin. About two million people were trapped with food for only about five weeks.

The West did not want to fight a war, and it did not want to abandon Berlin. So it flew everything in. Planes landed every few minutes, day and night, carrying food, medicine and mostly coal. Some pilots, including South Africans, joined in. One American pilot dropped sweets on tiny parachutes for the children. In eleven months, planes made more than 270 000 flights.

In May 1949 Stalin gave up. That same year, Germany officially split into two countries: West Germany, a democracy with a market economy, and East Germany, a communist state.

So why was a wall built?

## Subtopic: A Wall Overnight

West Germany became rich quickly. East Germany had shortages and a frightening secret police. So people left: about 2,7 million East Germans escaped to the West between 1949 and 1961, many through Berlin, where it was easy to cross. Many were young doctors, teachers and engineers. East Germany was losing its future.

On the night of 13 August 1961, while people slept, East German soldiers rolled out barbed wire across Berlin. Within days it became a concrete wall. Families woke up to find they could not visit grandparents a few streets away.

Over the years the Wall grew deadlier, with watchtowers, guard dogs and a death strip. At least 140 people died trying to cross it. Some escaped through tunnels, in car boots, and once even in a home-made hot-air balloon.

Here is the line to remember: Germany was cut into four, then two, and in 1961 a wall split Berlin itself, becoming the biggest symbol of the Cold War.
