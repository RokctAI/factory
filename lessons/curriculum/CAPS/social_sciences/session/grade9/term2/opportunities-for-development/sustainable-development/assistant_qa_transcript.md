### subtopic_1 — What Sustainable Development Means
**Assistant:** Why does the Brundtland definition mention the needs of the present as well as the future?

**Tutor:** The Brundtland Commission deliberately included the needs of the present because it wanted to make clear that sustainable development is not about stopping development or freezing the world as it is. In 1987, more than a third of the world's population lived in extreme poverty, without enough food, clean water, sanitation, health care or energy. The report argued that the essential needs of the world's poor should be given overriding priority. It also recognised that poverty itself can damage the environment: people with no alternatives may cut down forests for fuel, overgraze land or farm steep slopes, causing erosion, simply to survive. So reducing poverty is part of protecting the environment. At the same time, the report warned that development based on unlimited consumption by the rich would exhaust resources and pollute the planet, harming future generations. The phrase about future generations introduces fairness across time: people who are not yet born cannot vote or speak for themselves, so present generations have a responsibility to leave them a world in which they can meet their own needs. Balancing these two kinds of fairness is difficult. Developing countries often argue that they have the right to use their resources and grow their economies, as rich countries did, while rich countries, which have caused most historical pollution, should make the biggest changes. This debate about common but differentiated responsibilities continues in climate negotiations today.

### subtopic_2 — Balancing Economic, Social and Environmental Factors
**Assistant:** How can a project be good for the economy but bad for sustainability?

**Tutor:** A project can be good for the economy in the short term, by creating jobs, profits and taxes, while being unsustainable because it damages the environment or harms people in ways that reduce well-being and future opportunities. Consider an example. A company proposes to mine coal near a wetland that supplies water to farms and a town. The mine would employ several hundred people and earn export income for twenty years. But mining could drain or pollute the wetland, a process called acid mine drainage, in which water flowing through exposed rock becomes acidic and carries heavy metals into rivers. Farmers downstream could lose irrigation water, the town's water treatment costs could rise, fish and birds could disappear, and dust could cause respiratory illness. When the coal runs out, the jobs end, but the pollution may continue for decades and cost the public millions to clean up. Burning the coal also adds carbon dioxide to the atmosphere, contributing to climate change. A full evaluation would compare the benefits with all these costs, including costs to people not directly involved and to future generations, which economists call externalities. It would consider alternatives, such as locating the mine elsewhere, stricter controls, rehabilitation funds, or different forms of development. South Africa has many old abandoned mines whose pollution is now a public burden, which shows why sustainability must be considered before projects begin.

### subtopic_3 — Global Agreements and the Sustainable Development Goals
**Assistant:** What lessons did Cape Town's Day Zero crisis teach?

**Tutor:** The Cape Town water crisis of 2017 and 2018 taught several lessons about sustainability, governance and collective action. First, it showed the impact of climate variability: the three-year drought from 2015 to 2017 was among the most severe on record, and scientists found that climate change had made such a drought more likely. Cities that rely mainly on rain-fed dams are vulnerable, so they need diverse water sources, such as groundwater, recycled wastewater and desalination. Second, it showed the importance of planning and communication. Some critics argued that the city and national government responded too slowly, and debates about responsibility caused confusion. When the Day Zero warning was announced, it frightened people but also motivated action. Third, it showed the power of collective action. Residents reduced their use dramatically, and the city's total consumption fell from about 1,2 billion litres a day in 2015 to roughly half that by 2018, through restrictions to 50 litres per person per day, higher water tariffs, pressure management, fixing leaks and sharing tips for saving water. Agriculture also accepted reduced allocations, which affected farm jobs. Fourth, it raised questions of fairness: wealthier households could drill boreholes or buy bottled water, while poorer households in informal settlements already used far less than 50 litres a day at communal taps. Finally, it showed that behaviour can change permanently: water use in Cape Town remained lower than before the crisis even after the dams refilled.

### subtopic_4 — Sustainable Development in South Africa and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What is a just transition in South Africa?

**Tutor:** A just transition is a shift from a high-carbon economy based on coal to a low-carbon economy based on renewable energy, done in a way that is fair to workers and communities who depend on the old industries. South Africa needs to reduce greenhouse gas emissions, because coal power stations make the country one of the world's larger emitters, and because coal pollution damages health, especially in Mpumalanga, where air quality on parts of the Highveld has been among the worst in the world. Many coal power stations are also ageing and unreliable, which has contributed to load-shedding. But coal mining and coal power provide tens of thousands of jobs, and whole towns, such as eMalahleni and Middelburg, depend on them. If coal closed suddenly, workers would lose their incomes, businesses would close and municipalities would lose revenue. A just transition aims to avoid this by planning ahead: retraining workers for jobs in renewable energy, manufacturing and other industries; investing in new economic activities in coal regions; providing social support during the change; and involving workers, unions and communities in decisions. The Komati power station, which stopped burning coal in 2022, is being repurposed with solar panels, battery storage and training facilities as a pilot. The Just Energy Transition Partnership of 2021 promised international finance, starting at 8,5 billion US dollars. Critics debate whether the money is enough and whether loans will increase debt, but the principle is that the costs of change should not fall on the poorest.
