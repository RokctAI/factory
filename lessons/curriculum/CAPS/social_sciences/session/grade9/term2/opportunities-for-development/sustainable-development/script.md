# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains sustainable development as an opportunity for development. It covers the definition and origins of sustainable development; its economic, social and environmental dimensions and the trade-offs between them; global agreements and the Sustainable Development Goals; and sustainable development in South Africa, including water, energy and community initiatives. The anchor definition comes from the Brundtland Report of 1987, Our Common Future: sustainable development is development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. The anchor case study is Cape Town's water crisis of 2017 and 2018, when residents were limited to 50 litres per person per day to avoid Day Zero, the day the taps would be turned off. The governing idea is that development and environmental protection are not enemies; development that destroys its own resource base or excludes the poor cannot last, while well-planned sustainable development creates new opportunities for jobs, health and security.

## Subtopic: What Sustainable Development Means

The idea of sustainable development grew from concerns in the 1960s and 1970s that rapid economic growth was damaging the environment and using up resources. Books such as Rachel Carson's Silent Spring of 1962, which showed how pesticides were killing birds, and the report The Limits to Growth of 1972 raised public awareness. In 1983 the United Nations set up the World Commission on Environment and Development, chaired by the former Norwegian prime minister Gro Harlem Brundtland. Its 1987 report, Our Common Future, often called the Brundtland Report, gave the definition that is still used today: development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

Two key ideas are contained in this definition. The first is needs, especially the essential needs of the world's poor, which the report said should be given overriding priority. Sustainable development is not about stopping development; billions of people still need food, water, energy, housing, health care and jobs. The second is limits: the environment's ability to provide resources and absorb waste is limited, and technology and social organisation must work within those limits.

A helpful way to think about this is the difference between living off interest and spending capital. If you have money in a savings account, you can spend the interest each year and the savings remain for the future. If you spend the savings themselves, they run out. Natural resources work in a similar way. A forest can supply timber indefinitely if trees are cut no faster than they regrow; a fish stock can supply fish if the catch is less than the number of new fish born each year; groundwater can be used if extraction does not exceed recharge from rain. When we take more than the system can replace, we are spending natural capital, and future generations will have less.

The idea of fairness between generations, called intergenerational equity, is central to sustainable development. So is fairness within the present generation, called intragenerational equity: the rich should not consume resources and pollute in ways that harm the poor, who are often most dependent on natural resources and most exposed to environmental hazards such as floods, droughts and polluted air.

Scientists measure human pressure on the planet in several ways. The ecological footprint compares the land and sea area needed to supply what people consume and absorb their waste with the area actually available. On current estimates, humanity uses resources as if it had about 1,7 Earths, which means we are drawing down natural capital. The organisation that calculates this marks Earth Overshoot Day, the date each year by which humanity has used what the Earth can regenerate in a year; in recent years it has fallen in late July or early August. Footprints are very unequal: the average person in the United States has a footprint several times larger than the average person in India or most African countries.

So the next question is: what are the dimensions of sustainable development, and why must they be balanced?

## Subtopic: Balancing Economic, Social and Environmental Factors

Sustainable development is usually described as having three dimensions, sometimes pictured as three overlapping circles, with sustainable development in the area where they all overlap.

The economic dimension requires an economy that creates jobs and incomes, raises productivity and can continue over time without collapse. An economy based on exhausting a single non-renewable resource, such as a mine that will close in twenty years, is not economically sustainable unless the income is used to build other industries and skills.

The social dimension requires that development meets people's basic needs, reduces poverty and inequality, respects human rights and cultures, and involves people in decisions. Development that enriches a few while most people remain poor and excluded is unlikely to be stable or fair.

The environmental dimension requires that development protects ecosystems, biodiversity, soil, water and the atmosphere, and uses renewable resources no faster than they regenerate and non-renewable resources efficiently while substitutes are developed.

The three dimensions often involve trade-offs, and real decisions require weighing them. Consider a proposal to open a coal mine near a rural community. Economically, it would create jobs, taxes and export earnings. Socially, it might bring work to unemployed young people but also displace families, create dust that harms health and attract crime. Environmentally, it could pollute rivers, destroy farmland and add to carbon emissions. A sustainable decision would consider all of these, look for ways to reduce harm, compare alternatives such as renewable energy or agriculture, and involve the community in the decision. South African law requires an environmental impact assessment for such projects before they are approved.

There are also many win-win opportunities, where one action improves all three dimensions together. Insulating low-cost houses keeps them warmer in winter and cooler in summer, reducing illness, saving households money on heating and reducing energy use. Restoring wetlands improves water quality, reduces flood damage and creates jobs. Solar water heaters reduce electricity bills and emissions. Recycling creates income for waste pickers while reducing landfill.

South Africa's Constitution recognises the environmental dimension in Section 24 of the Bill of Rights, which gives everyone the right to an environment that is not harmful to their health or well-being, and to have the environment protected for present and future generations through measures that secure ecologically sustainable development while promoting justifiable economic and social development. This is one of the clearest legal statements of sustainable development in any constitution.

So the next question is: how has the world tried to put sustainable development into practice?

## Subtopic: Global Agreements and the Sustainable Development Goals

The first major global step after the Brundtland Report was the United Nations Conference on Environment and Development, known as the Earth Summit, held in Rio de Janeiro in 1992. More than 170 countries attended. It produced Agenda 21, a plan of action for sustainable development; conventions on biological diversity and on climate change; and principles such as the precautionary principle, which says that where there is a threat of serious harm, lack of full scientific certainty should not be used as a reason to delay action. In 2002, the World Summit on Sustainable Development was held in Johannesburg, reviewing progress ten years after Rio.

In 2000 the United Nations adopted eight Millennium Development Goals, focused on reducing extreme poverty, hunger and child mortality, achieving universal primary education and fighting diseases by 2015. Considerable progress was made: the proportion of the world's people living in extreme poverty more than halved between 1990 and 2015. In 2015 they were replaced by seventeen Sustainable Development Goals, the SDGs, also called the Global Goals, to be achieved by 2030. Unlike the earlier goals, the SDGs apply to all countries, rich and poor, and combine economic, social and environmental aims. They include ending poverty and hunger, good health, quality education, gender equality, clean water and sanitation, affordable and clean energy, decent work, reduced inequalities, sustainable cities, responsible consumption, climate action, life below water, life on land and peace and justice.

Also in 2015, countries signed the Paris Agreement on climate change, agreeing to limit global warming to well below 2 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels and to pursue efforts to limit it to 1,5 degrees. Each country submits its own plan for reducing emissions. Climate change is closely linked to sustainable development, because it threatens food, water, health and livelihoods, especially in poorer countries that have contributed least to the problem.

Progress towards the SDGs has been mixed. The Covid-19 pandemic, conflicts and rising food and energy prices from 2020 onwards reversed some gains, and United Nations reports in the 2020s warned that most targets were off track for 2030. Critics also argue that seventeen goals and 169 targets are too many, and that some goals, such as rapid economic growth and environmental protection, conflict with each other.

So the final question is: what does sustainable development look like in South Africa?

## Subtopic: Sustainable Development in South Africa and the Error Museum

South Africa faces a particular sustainability challenge in energy. In recent years about 80 per cent of its electricity has come from burning coal, making it one of the most carbon-intensive economies in the world per unit of output, and coal power stations also cause air pollution in the Mpumalanga Highveld. At the same time, the country suffered years of load-shedding because of breakdowns at ageing power stations. Coal mining employs tens of thousands of workers, so a rapid shift away from coal could cause unemployment in Mpumalanga. The idea of a just transition means moving to cleaner energy in a way that protects workers and communities through retraining, new industries and social support. In 2021 South Africa agreed a Just Energy Transition Partnership with several wealthy countries, which pledged an initial 8,5 billion US dollars in finance. South Africa has excellent solar and wind resources, especially in the Northern Cape, and renewable energy has grown rapidly since 2011, including rooftop solar on homes and businesses.

Water is another critical issue. South Africa is a water-scarce country, with average rainfall of about 450 millimetres a year, well below the world average of about 860 millimetres. From 2015 to 2018 the Western Cape experienced a severe drought. By early 2018 Cape Town's dam levels had fallen so low that the city announced it might have to turn off household taps on a date called Day Zero. Residents were restricted to 50 litres per person per day. A family of four was therefore limited to 200 litres a day, about 6 000 litres in a 30-day month. Through strict restrictions, higher tariffs, public campaigns, reduced agricultural allocations and the return of rains in winter 2018, Day Zero was avoided. Cape Town's water use roughly halved. The crisis showed both the vulnerability of cities to climate variability and the power of collective action, and it led to new investments in groundwater, water recycling and desalination.

Other examples of sustainable development in South Africa include the Working for Water programme; community food gardens and agroecology that build soil fertility without expensive chemicals; recycling cooperatives and the estimated tens of thousands of informal waste pickers who collect most of the paper and packaging recycled in the country; green building standards; and the National Development Plan, adopted in 2012, which sets goals for 2030 including a transition to a low-carbon economy.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "Sustainable development means stopping development to protect nature." It means meeting present needs, especially of the poor, while protecting the resources future generations need.

Exhibit two: "Sustainable development is only about the environment." It has economic, social and environmental dimensions that must be balanced.

Exhibit three: "The SDGs apply only to poor countries." Unlike the earlier Millennium Development Goals, the SDGs apply to all countries, including rich ones whose consumption has the largest footprint.

Exhibit four: "Individual actions make no difference." Cape Town's residents roughly halved the city's water use through collective individual action, helping to avoid Day Zero.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a savings account, a three-strand rope and a city that nearly ran out of water.

## Subtopic: A Savings Account for the Future

Imagine your grandmother leaves you a savings account. Each year it earns a little interest. If you spend only the interest, the money in the account stays there for your own children. If you spend the savings themselves, one day the account is empty.

Nature works like that. A forest can give us wood forever if we cut trees slowly enough for new ones to grow. The sea can give us fish forever if we leave enough fish to breed. But if we take too much, too fast, there will be nothing left for the people who come after us.

In 1987 a United Nations report gave a famous definition: sustainable development is development that meets the needs of people today without spoiling the chances of future generations to meet their needs. Notice that it says needs today too. Billions of people still need food, water, homes and jobs. Sustainable development does not say stop; it says develop wisely.

Right now, scientists say humanity is using nature as if we had about 1,7 Earths. We only have one.

So how do we develop wisely?

## Subtopic: A Three-Strand Rope

Picture a rope made of three strands twisted together. One strand is the economy: jobs and money. One is society: fairness, health, education and rights. One is the environment: clean air, water, soil and living things. If one strand snaps, the rope is weak.

Sometimes the strands pull against each other. A new coal mine might bring jobs, but also dust, pollution and lost farmland. A good decision looks at all three strands and asks the community what they think.

Sometimes one action strengthens all three. A solar water heater saves a family money, uses less electricity and cuts pollution.

In 2015 the countries of the world agreed on 17 Sustainable Development Goals to reach by 2030, like ending poverty and hunger, clean water for all, quality education, clean energy and climate action. They apply to every country, rich and poor.

So what does this look like at home?

## Subtopic: The City That Nearly Ran Dry

In 2018 Cape Town nearly became the first big city in the world to run out of water. After three dry years, the dams were almost empty. The city warned of Day Zero, the day the taps would be switched off.

Everyone was asked to use only 50 litres a day. That is about one short shower plus a few flushes and some drinking water. A family of four had only 200 litres a day. People reused bath water to flush toilets, collected rain and stopped watering gardens. Together they cut the city's water use roughly in half, and Day Zero never came.

South Africa has other challenges too. Most of our electricity comes from coal, which pollutes the air and warms the planet. But we also have lots of sun and wind, and solar panels and wind farms are spreading fast. The goal is a just transition: cleaner energy without leaving coal workers behind.

Here is the line to remember: sustainable development means living on nature's interest, not its savings, while making sure everyone today has what they need.
