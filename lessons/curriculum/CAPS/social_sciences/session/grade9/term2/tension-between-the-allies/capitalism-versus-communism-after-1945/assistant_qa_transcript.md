### subtopic_1 — Two Competing Ideologies: Capitalism and Communism
**Assistant:** Why did Stalin want control over Eastern Europe?

**Tutor:** Historians identify several motives, and they disagree about which mattered most. The first was security. The Soviet Union had been invaded from the west in 1812, 1914 and 1941, each time through the open plains of Poland and Eastern Europe. In World War II it lost about 27 million people, more than any other country, and much of its western territory, including cities, farms and factories, was destroyed. Stalin was determined to ensure that the countries on the Soviet Union's western border would never again be a route for invasion, which meant governments friendly to Moscow. The second motive was ideological. As a communist, Stalin believed that the spread of communism was part of history's progress, and creating communist states expanded the socialist camp. The third was economic. The Soviet Union needed resources to rebuild, and it took reparations from Eastern Germany and other areas, including dismantling factories and shipping them east. The fourth was Stalin's personal suspicion and desire for control, which led him to distrust even communist leaders who were not completely obedient, as his break with Tito in 1948 showed. Western leaders, by contrast, saw Soviet actions as expansionist and as a breach of the Yalta promise of free elections. When writing an essay, show that the same actions could be interpreted as defensive or aggressive depending on perspective, and use evidence such as the Polish elections of 1947 and the Czech coup of 1948.

### subtopic_2 — Wartime Allies and the Conferences of 1945
**Assistant:** What was the significance of Churchill's Iron Curtain speech?

**Tutor:** Churchill's speech at Fulton, Missouri, on 5 March 1946 was significant because it publicly named and dramatised the division of Europe, shaped Western opinion and signalled the end of the wartime alliance in public language. Churchill was no longer prime minister, having lost the 1945 election, but he remained one of the most respected leaders of the war, and President Truman sat on the platform, which gave the speech added weight, although Truman did not officially endorse it at the time. Churchill described how an iron curtain had descended from Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic, placing the capitals of central and Eastern Europe under increasing control from Moscow. He warned that communist parties were seeking totalitarian control and that the Soviet Union desired the fruits of war and the indefinite expansion of its power and doctrines. He called for a special relationship between Britain and the United States to stand up to it. The speech had a mixed reception: some Americans thought it too aggressive, and many still hoped for cooperation with the Soviet Union. Stalin responded furiously in the Soviet newspaper Pravda, comparing Churchill to Hitler and accusing him of calling for war. In source-based questions, this speech is a primary source with a clear perspective: it was intended to persuade an American audience to take a firm stand against the Soviet Union, so you should consider its purpose and audience when assessing its reliability.

### subtopic_3 — Soviet Control of Eastern Europe and the Iron Curtain
**Assistant:** Why did the United States offer the Marshall Plan?

**Tutor:** The United States had both humanitarian and strategic reasons. After the war, much of Western Europe was in ruins. Cities had been bombed, factories, railways and bridges destroyed, and millions of people were homeless or displaced. Food, fuel and coal were scarce, and the extremely cold winter of 1946 to 1947 caused severe hardship. European countries lacked the dollars needed to buy food, raw materials and machinery from the United States. American leaders genuinely wanted to relieve suffering, but they also had strategic and economic motives. They feared that poverty, unemployment and despair would increase support for communist parties, which were already strong in France and Italy, and that communists might come to power through elections or unrest. A prosperous Western Europe would be a stronger barrier against Soviet influence. There were also economic motives: a recovered Europe would be a market for American goods, supporting American jobs and preventing a return to depression. George Marshall announced the plan in a speech at Harvard University in June 1947, presenting it as directed not against any country but against hunger, poverty, desperation and chaos. Between 1948 and 1952 about 13 billion dollars was provided to sixteen countries, including Britain, France, Italy and West Germany. Recovery was rapid, though historians debate how much was due to the plan itself. The Soviet Union saw it as dollar imperialism and prevented Eastern European countries from joining.

### subtopic_4 — Containment, the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan, and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Who was to blame for the breakdown of the wartime alliance?

**Tutor:** This is one of the great historical debates, and historians have taken different positions over time, which is why it is useful for practising essay writing. The traditional or orthodox view, common in the West in the 1950s, blamed the Soviet Union. It argued that Stalin broke the Yalta promises, imposed communist governments on Eastern Europe through force and fraud, and aimed to spread communism further, so that the United States responded defensively with containment. The revisionist view, which grew in the 1960s during the Vietnam War, placed more responsibility on the United States. Revisionists argued that the Soviet Union, devastated by war, was mainly seeking security, while the United States used its economic power, the Marshall Plan and its monopoly of the atomic bomb to expand its influence and markets, and that Truman's hard line made Stalin more suspicious. A third view, often called post-revisionist, argues that both sides contributed through mutual fear, misunderstanding and conflicting interests: each side's defensive actions looked aggressive to the other, creating a spiral of mistrust. After the Soviet archives partly opened in the 1990s, some historians argued that Stalin's ideology and personality played a larger role than revisionists had thought. In an essay, you should take a clear line of argument but acknowledge other views, support your points with evidence such as Yalta, Potsdam, the Iron Curtain speech, the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan, and reach a reasoned conclusion.
