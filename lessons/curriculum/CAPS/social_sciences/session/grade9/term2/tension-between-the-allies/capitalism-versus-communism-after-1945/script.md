# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the growing tension between the Allies after World War II in Europe. It covers the two competing ideologies of capitalism and communism; the wartime alliance and the conferences at Yalta and Potsdam in 1945, where disagreements emerged; the spread of Soviet control over Eastern Europe and the Western response, captured in Churchill's phrase the Iron Curtain; and the American policies of containment, the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan, together with the Soviet reaction. The anchor date is 5 March 1946, when Winston Churchill declared at Fulton, Missouri, that "from Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic, an iron curtain has descended across the Continent". The governing idea is that the wartime alliance had been a marriage of convenience against a common enemy, and that once Germany was defeated, deep differences in ideology, security needs and mutual suspicion turned the allies into rivals.

## Subtopic: Two Competing Ideologies: Capitalism and Communism

An ideology is a set of beliefs about how society, the economy and politics should be organised. After 1945 the world's two most powerful countries, the United States and the Soviet Union, followed opposing ideologies.

Capitalism is an economic system in which the means of production, such as land, factories, mines and banks, are mainly owned privately by individuals and companies, who produce goods and services to make a profit. Prices, wages and production are largely determined by competition in a free market, where buyers and sellers make their own choices. In the United States, capitalism was linked to liberal democracy: multi-party elections, freedom of speech, the press and religion, and protection of individual rights. Supporters argued that capitalism encouraged hard work, innovation and efficiency and that democracy protected freedom. Critics pointed out that capitalism also produced great inequality, periods of mass unemployment such as the Great Depression of the 1930s, and exploitation of workers.

Communism is based on the ideas of Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, who wrote The Communist Manifesto in 1848. Marx argued that history was a struggle between social classes, and that under capitalism the owners of factories, the bourgeoisie, exploited the workers, the proletariat. He predicted that workers would overthrow capitalism and create a classless society in which property was owned in common. In practice, in the Soviet Union, which emerged from the Russian Revolution of 1917 led by Vladimir Lenin and the Bolsheviks, the state owned and controlled land, factories and businesses. The economy was centrally planned: government planners set production targets in five-year plans and decided prices and wages. Supporters claimed this would end unemployment and inequality, provide free education and health care, and industrialise rapidly, which the Soviet Union did in the 1930s. In practice, the Communist Party held all power in a one-party state. Under Joseph Stalin, who led the Soviet Union from the late 1920s until his death in 1953, millions of people were imprisoned in labour camps known as the Gulag or executed, and millions died in famines caused partly by forced collectivisation of farms. Freedom of speech, religion and the press were severely restricted.

Each side saw the other as a threat. American leaders feared that communism aimed to spread worldwide revolution and destroy freedom and private property. Soviet leaders believed capitalism was doomed but aggressive, and that capitalist countries wanted to destroy the world's first communist state. These beliefs shaped how each side interpreted the other's actions after 1945.

So the next question is: if they were so different, how did they become allies, and why did trust break down?

## Subtopic: Wartime Allies and the Conferences of 1945

The alliance between the United States, Britain and the Soviet Union, sometimes called the Grand Alliance, was formed out of necessity. Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941, and Germany declared war on the United States in December 1941. The three powers now shared one overriding aim: defeating Nazi Germany. Churchill, a lifelong anti-communist, explained his decision to support Stalin by saying that if Hitler invaded hell, he would at least make a favourable reference to the devil in the House of Commons.

Mistrust existed from the start. Soviet leaders remembered that Britain, the United States and other countries had sent troops to support the anti-communist side in the Russian Civil War from 1918 to 1920, and that Britain and France had appeased Hitler at Munich in 1938 without consulting the Soviet Union. The West remembered that Stalin had signed the Nazi-Soviet Pact with Hitler in August 1939 and joined in dividing Poland. During the war, Stalin repeatedly demanded that the Western Allies open a second front in Western Europe to relieve pressure on the Red Army, which was bearing the brunt of German forces; he suspected the delay until D-Day in June 1944 was deliberate, so that the Soviet Union would be weakened. The United States and Britain also developed the atomic bomb in secret, without telling their Soviet ally officially until July 1945.

At the Yalta Conference in the Crimea in February 1945, Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin agreed that Germany would be divided into occupation zones and that Berlin, inside the Soviet zone, would also be divided; that Nazi leaders would be tried; that the Soviet Union would join the war against Japan within three months of Germany's defeat; that a United Nations organisation would be created; and that the liberated countries of Eastern Europe would hold free elections. But they disagreed about Poland: Stalin wanted a pro-Soviet government and Poland's eastern border moved westward, while the Western leaders supported the Polish government in exile in London.

By the Potsdam Conference, held near Berlin from July to August 1945, circumstances had changed. Roosevelt had died in April and was replaced by Harry Truman, who was more suspicious of Stalin. Churchill was replaced during the conference by Clement Attlee after losing the British election. The Red Army now occupied most of Eastern Europe. Truman informed Stalin of a powerful new weapon, the atomic bomb, which had just been tested. The leaders confirmed the division of Germany and agreed on reparations, but argued about the amount the Soviet Union should take from Germany and about Soviet actions in Poland. The atmosphere was far cooler than at Yalta.

So the next question is: what happened in Eastern Europe, and why did it alarm the West?

## Subtopic: Soviet Control of Eastern Europe and the Iron Curtain

The Soviet Union had suffered terribly in the war, losing about 27 million people, and much of its western territory had been devastated. Russia had been invaded from the west through Poland three times in little more than a century: by Napoleon in 1812, by Germany in World War I, and by Germany again in 1941. Stalin was determined to create a buffer zone of friendly states between the Soviet Union and Germany, so that this could never happen again. He also saw spreading communism as a historic goal.

Between 1945 and 1948, communist parties backed by the Soviet Union took control of Poland, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, Czechoslovakia and, after 1949, East Germany. Methods included rigged elections, the arrest and intimidation of opposition politicians, communist control of the police and the security services, and the presence of Soviet troops. The communist takeover in Czechoslovakia in February 1948, after which the foreign minister Jan Masaryk died in suspicious circumstances, particularly shocked the West, because Czechoslovakia had been a democracy. These countries became known as Soviet satellite states, nominally independent but controlled from Moscow. Yugoslavia, under Josip Broz Tito, was communist but broke with Stalin in 1948 and remained independent of Soviet control.

To Stalin, these actions were defensive and justified by Soviet sacrifices. To Western leaders, they broke the Yalta promise of free elections and looked like the first steps of communist expansion across Europe, at a time when communist parties were also strong in France and Italy and a civil war was raging in Greece.

On 5 March 1946, at Westminster College in Fulton, Missouri, with President Truman present, Churchill, no longer prime minister, gave the speech that named the division. He said an iron curtain had descended across the continent, behind which lay the ancient capitals of central and eastern Europe, subject to a very high and increasing measure of control from Moscow. He called for British and American cooperation to resist Soviet expansion. Stalin responded by comparing Churchill to Hitler and accusing him of warmongering. A month earlier, in February 1946, the American diplomat George Kennan had sent the so-called Long Telegram from Moscow, arguing that Soviet leaders saw the world as permanently hostile and that the United States should firmly resist Soviet expansion. These ideas shaped American policy.

So the final question is: how did the United States respond in practice, and what did the Soviet Union do in return?

## Subtopic: Containment, the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan, and the Error Museum

The American policy that emerged was called containment: preventing communism from spreading beyond where it already existed, rather than trying to overthrow it there. In early 1947 Britain announced that it could no longer afford to support the Greek government against communist guerrillas or to help Turkey resist Soviet pressure. On 12 March 1947 President Truman asked Congress for 400 million dollars in aid for Greece and Turkey, declaring that it must be the policy of the United States to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures. This became known as the Truman Doctrine. It presented the world as divided between two ways of life and committed the United States to resisting communism globally.

The Truman Doctrine was followed by an economic plan. Western Europe was in ruins, with shortages of food, fuel and housing, and the bitter winter of 1946 to 1947 made conditions worse. American leaders feared that poverty and despair would make communism attractive. In June 1947 Secretary of State George Marshall announced the European Recovery Program, known as the Marshall Plan. Between 1948 and 1952 the United States gave about 13 billion dollars in aid, mostly grants, to sixteen Western European countries, which helped rebuild industry and trade. Aid was offered to Eastern Europe too, but Stalin forbade Soviet satellites to accept it, seeing it as a way for the United States to gain economic and political influence. Truman described the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan as two halves of the same walnut.

The Soviet Union responded by creating the Cominform in 1947 to coordinate communist parties across Europe, and Comecon in 1949, an economic organisation for the Eastern bloc that was meant to be its alternative to the Marshall Plan. By 1947 to 1948, Europe was clearly divided into two camps, and the term Cold War, popularised in 1947 by the American journalist Walter Lippmann, came into common use.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "The Cold War began because the Allies had always been enemies." They were genuine allies against Germany from 1941 to 1945; the rivalry grew as wartime cooperation ended and disagreements multiplied.

Exhibit two: "Capitalism and communism are just names for political parties." They are economic and political systems, based on private ownership and markets on one side and state ownership and central planning on the other.

Exhibit three: "The Marshall Plan was military aid." It was economic aid to rebuild Europe; the Truman Doctrine included aid to Greece and Turkey against communist pressure.

Exhibit four: "Historians agree that one side alone caused the tension." Historians debate this: some blame Soviet expansion, some American economic and military power, and others see mutual fear and misunderstanding. In essays, present evidence for different views.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: two ways to share a harvest, two partners who did not trust each other, and a curtain falling across a continent.

## Subtopic: Two Ways to Share a Harvest

Imagine a village that has to decide how to run its farms. In the first plan, each family owns its own fields, grows what it chooses and sells its crops at the market for the best price it can get. Families that work hard or are lucky can become rich; others may stay poor. That is like capitalism: private ownership, free markets and profit. In the United States it came with democracy: elections with many parties, free speech and a free press.

In the second plan, all the fields belong to everyone together, and a village council decides what to plant and how to share the harvest so that nobody goes hungry. That is the idea of communism, based on the writings of Karl Marx. In the Soviet Union, though, the council was the Communist Party, there were no free elections, and people who disagreed could be sent to prison camps.

Each side thought the other's way was dangerous. Americans feared communism would take away freedom and property. Soviet leaders feared capitalist countries wanted to destroy them.

So how did two such different countries end up on the same side?

## Subtopic: Partners Who Did Not Trust Each Other

Imagine two neighbours who dislike each other. A dangerous gang starts attacking both their houses, so they team up to fight it. Once the gang is defeated, they remember why they did not like each other.

That is what happened. Hitler attacked the Soviet Union in 1941, and Germany declared war on the United States the same year. So the United States, Britain and the Soviet Union became allies. But there was old mistrust. Stalin had once made a deal with Hitler. The West had sent soldiers against the communists in Russia after 1917. Stalin felt the Western Allies took too long to invade France, leaving Soviet soldiers to do most of the dying.

In 1945 the leaders met twice, at Yalta and Potsdam. They agreed to split Germany into zones. But they argued about Poland and about how much Germany should pay. By Potsdam, the friendly American president Roosevelt had died, the new president Truman was more suspicious, and he had a new secret weapon: the atomic bomb.

So what happened next?

## Subtopic: A Curtain Across Europe

The Soviet Union lost about 27 million people in the war. Stalin wanted a wall of friendly countries between his country and Germany. So in Poland, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria and Czechoslovakia, communist governments backed by Soviet troops took power, often through unfair elections.

In March 1946 Churchill said an iron curtain had come down across Europe. On one side, the West. On the other, countries controlled from Moscow.

America decided to stop communism from spreading. This was called containment. In 1947 President Truman promised to help free peoples resist communism, starting with Greece and Turkey. Then the Marshall Plan gave Western Europe about 13 billion dollars to rebuild, so that hungry people would not turn to communism. Stalin refused to let Eastern Europe take the money.

Here is the line to remember: the allies who beat Hitler together became rivals because of different beliefs, deep fear and mistrust, and that rivalry became the Cold War.
