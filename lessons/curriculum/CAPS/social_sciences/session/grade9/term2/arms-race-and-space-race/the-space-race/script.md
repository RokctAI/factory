# Part 1 — Expert

This session defines and explains the space race between the United States and the Soviet Union. It covers the meaning of the space race and why it was part of the Cold War; the early Soviet lead, from Sputnik to the first human in space; the American response, from the creation of NASA to the Apollo Moon landings; and the consequences of the space race, including technology, cooperation and South Africa's role. The anchor dates are 4 October 1957, when the Soviet Union launched Sputnik 1, and 20 July 1969, when the American astronauts Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin landed on the Moon. The governing idea is that the space race was both a scientific adventure and a Cold War competition: it was driven by military fears and the search for prestige, but it produced achievements and technologies that changed life on Earth and eventually brought the rivals together in cooperation.

## Subtopic: What Was the Space Race and Why Did It Matter?

The space race was the competition between the United States and the Soviet Union, from 1957 to about 1975, to achieve superior spaceflight capability and to be first to reach milestones in space exploration: the first satellite, the first living creature in orbit, the first human in space, the first spacewalk and, above all, the first humans on the Moon.

The space race was closely linked to the arms race. The rockets used to launch satellites and astronauts were developed from military missiles. Many of the scientists who built them on both sides had begun their work on weapons. The German engineer Wernher von Braun, who had designed the V-2 rocket used by Nazi Germany to bomb London and Antwerp, using forced labour from concentration camps to build it, was brought to the United States after the war, where he later led the development of the Saturn V rocket that carried astronauts to the Moon. The Soviet programme was led by the brilliant engineer Sergei Korolev, whose identity was kept secret during his lifetime and who had himself been imprisoned in the Gulag under Stalin.

The space race mattered for three main reasons. The first was military: a rocket powerful enough to put a satellite into orbit could also deliver a nuclear warhead to the other side of the world, and satellites could be used to spy on the enemy. The second was propaganda and prestige: each side wanted to show the world, and especially the newly independent countries of Africa and Asia, that its system, capitalism or communism, produced the most advanced science and technology. The third was science and national pride: space exploration captured the imagination of millions and pushed the limits of human knowledge.

Space exploration was also a matter of international rules. In 1967 the Outer Space Treaty, signed by the United States, the Soviet Union and many other countries, declared that space should be used for peaceful purposes, that no country could claim the Moon or other celestial bodies, and that nuclear weapons could not be placed in orbit. This shows that even rivals could agree on limits.

So the next question is: how did the Soviet Union take the early lead?

## Subtopic: The Soviet Lead: Sputnik, Laika and Gagarin

On 4 October 1957 the Soviet Union launched Sputnik 1, the first artificial satellite, on an R-7 rocket, the same type as its new intercontinental missile. Sputnik was a polished metal sphere about 58 centimetres in diameter, weighing about 84 kilograms, with four long antennas. It orbited the Earth about every 96 minutes, which means it circled the planet about fifteen times a day, since there are 1 440 minutes in a day and 1 440 divided by 96 is 15. Its radio beeps could be picked up by amateur radio operators around the world.

The effect in the United States was shock, sometimes called the Sputnik crisis. Americans had assumed their technology was superior. Now the Soviet Union had shown it could reach space, and by implication could hit American cities with missiles. Newspapers spoke of a technological Pearl Harbour. Only a month later, on 3 November 1957, Sputnik 2 carried a dog named Laika into orbit, the first animal to orbit the Earth; she died within hours from overheating, as there was no plan to bring her back. The first American attempt to launch a satellite, Vanguard, exploded on the launch pad in December 1957, in front of television cameras, and the press mocked it as Flopnik.

The United States launched its first successful satellite, Explorer 1, on 31 January 1958, which discovered the radiation belts around the Earth named after the scientist James Van Allen. In July 1958 Congress created the National Aeronautics and Space Administration, NASA, to run a civilian space programme, and the National Defense Education Act provided money for science, mathematics and language education to train more scientists and engineers.

The Soviet Union continued to set records. In 1959 its Luna 2 probe became the first human-made object to reach the Moon, and Luna 3 photographed the far side of the Moon, which is never visible from Earth. On 12 April 1961 the Soviet cosmonaut Yuri Gagarin became the first human in space, orbiting the Earth once in the spacecraft Vostok 1 in a flight of about 108 minutes. He became a worldwide hero and toured many countries. Less than a month later, on 5 May 1961, the American astronaut Alan Shepard made a fifteen-minute suborbital flight, going up into space and coming straight down; it was not until 20 February 1962 that John Glenn became the first American to orbit the Earth. On 16 June 1963 Valentina Tereshkova became the first woman in space, and on 18 March 1965 Alexei Leonov made the first spacewalk, almost failing to get back inside when his spacesuit ballooned in the vacuum.

So the next question is: how did the United States respond and overtake the Soviet Union?

## Subtopic: The American Response and the Moon Landing

On 25 May 1961, only six weeks after Gagarin's flight and shortly after the failed Bay of Pigs invasion of Cuba, President John F. Kennedy told Congress that the United States should commit itself to achieving the goal, before the decade was out, of landing a man on the Moon and returning him safely to the Earth. In a famous speech at Rice University in Texas in September 1962 he said, "We choose to go to the Moon in this decade and do the other things, not because they are easy, but because they are hard." The Moon was chosen partly because it was a goal so difficult that the Soviet lead in rockets would not necessarily give it an advantage.

NASA's budget grew rapidly, reaching more than four per cent of the entire federal budget in the mid-1960s. About 400 000 people worked on the Apollo programme, in NASA and in thousands of companies and universities. The programme drew on the work of many people who were not well known at the time, including African American women mathematicians such as Katherine Johnson, whose calculations of flight paths were essential to John Glenn's orbit and to the Apollo missions. The Gemini missions of 1965 and 1966 practised spacewalks, long flights and docking two spacecraft in orbit.

There were tragedies on both sides. In January 1967, three American astronauts, Gus Grissom, Ed White and Roger Chaffee, died when fire swept through their Apollo 1 capsule during a ground test. In April 1967 the Soviet cosmonaut Vladimir Komarov died when the parachute of his Soyuz 1 spacecraft failed. The Soviet programme also suffered after Korolev died in 1966, and its giant N1 Moon rocket exploded in all four test launches between 1969 and 1972.

In December 1968 Apollo 8 carried three astronauts around the Moon, the first humans to leave Earth's orbit, and they took the famous photograph called Earthrise, showing the blue Earth rising above the grey lunar surface. Then, on 16 July 1969, Apollo 11 was launched on a Saturn V rocket. On 20 July 1969 the lunar module Eagle, carrying Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin, landed in the Sea of Tranquillity, while Michael Collins orbited the Moon in the command module. Armstrong stepped onto the surface and said, "That's one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind." An estimated 600 million people watched on television, about a fifth of the world's population. The astronauts planted an American flag and left a plaque reading, "We came in peace for all mankind." They returned safely on 24 July. Between 1969 and 1972, six Apollo missions landed twelve astronauts on the Moon; no human has returned since.

So the final question is: what were the consequences of the space race?

## Subtopic: Consequences, Cooperation, South Africa's Role and the Error Museum

The space race had important consequences. It demonstrated American technological power and was a major propaganda victory for the United States, though the Soviet Union continued to lead in some areas, such as long-duration flights and space stations, launching the first space station, Salyut 1, in 1971. It was very expensive: the Apollo programme cost about 25 billion dollars at the time, equivalent to well over 200 billion dollars today. Critics, including civil rights leaders, argued that this money should have been spent on fighting poverty; on the day before Apollo 11's launch, the civil rights leader Ralph Abernathy led a protest of poor families to the launch site.

The space race also produced technologies that changed everyday life. Satellites now provide weather forecasts, global communication, television broadcasting and navigation systems such as GPS; satellite images help geographers monitor droughts, deforestation and cities. Research for spaceflight contributed to advances in computers, materials and medical devices.

Rivalry gradually gave way to cooperation. In July 1975, during détente, the Apollo-Soyuz Test Project saw an American Apollo spacecraft dock with a Soviet Soyuz spacecraft in orbit, and the commanders shook hands through the open hatch, a symbolic end to the space race. After the Cold War, Russia, the United States, Europe, Japan and Canada built the International Space Station, whose first part was launched in 1998 and which has been continuously occupied since November 2000.

South Africa played a part in the space race. In 1961 NASA built a tracking station at Hartebeesthoek, near Johannesburg, because its position in the southern hemisphere allowed it to communicate with spacecraft when they were out of contact with stations in the United States. It helped track early satellites and Moon and planetary missions. In 1975, during growing international opposition to apartheid, NASA closed its involvement, and the station was converted into the Hartebeesthoek Radio Astronomy Observatory. Today South Africa hosts the MeerKAT radio telescope in the Northern Cape, part of the international Square Kilometre Array project, and the South African National Space Agency, founded in 2010. In 2002 Mark Shuttleworth became the first South African, and first African, in space, visiting the International Space Station on a Russian Soyuz.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "The United States was ahead in space from the start." The Soviet Union achieved most early firsts: the first satellite, animal in orbit, human in space, woman in space and spacewalk.

Exhibit two: "The space race was only about science." It was closely tied to military missiles and to Cold War propaganda and prestige.

Exhibit three: "Yuri Gagarin landed on the Moon." Gagarin was the first human in space in 1961; Armstrong and Aldrin were the first on the Moon in 1969.

Exhibit four: "The space race had no effect on ordinary life." Satellites for weather, communication and navigation, and many technologies developed for spaceflight, are used every day.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a beeping ball in the sky, a race of firsts, and footprints in grey dust.

## Subtopic: A Beeping Ball in the Sky

On 4 October 1957, the Soviet Union launched a shiny metal ball, about the size of a beach ball, into space. It was called Sputnik, and it was the first satellite ever. It went round the Earth about every 96 minutes, so about 15 times a day, sending out beep, beep, beep on the radio. People all over the world tuned in to hear it.

Americans were shocked. They had thought they were ahead in technology. And they worried: if the Soviet Union had a rocket strong enough to throw a ball into space, that same rocket could carry a nuclear bomb to America. That is why the space race was part of the Cold War. Each side also wanted the world to think its way of life was the best.

A month later, the Soviets sent a dog called Laika into orbit. America's first try at a satellite blew up on the launch pad. In 1958 America finally launched its own satellite and created NASA, its space agency.

So who would put a person into space first?

## Subtopic: A Race of Firsts

The Soviet Union won that one too. On 12 April 1961, Yuri Gagarin became the first human in space, circling the Earth once in about 108 minutes. In 1963 Valentina Tereshkova became the first woman in space, and in 1965 Alexei Leonov became the first person to float outside a spacecraft on a spacewalk.

America was behind, so President Kennedy set a huge goal in 1961: land a man on the Moon and bring him home safely before 1970. He said, "We choose to go to the Moon, not because it is easy, but because it is hard."

Hundreds of thousands of people worked on the plan, including brilliant mathematicians like Katherine Johnson, who calculated the paths of the spacecraft. There were sad moments too: astronauts and cosmonauts died in accidents on both sides.

So did America make it?

## Subtopic: Footprints in Grey Dust

Yes. On 20 July 1969, Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin landed on the Moon in Apollo 11. Armstrong said, "That's one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind." About 600 million people watched on television. Between 1969 and 1972, twelve astronauts walked on the Moon.

Then the rivals became partners. In 1975 an American and a Soviet spacecraft joined together in orbit, and the crews shook hands. Later, many countries built the International Space Station together.

South Africa helped too! NASA built a tracking station at Hartebeesthoek, near Johannesburg, to talk to spacecraft. Today South Africa has the MeerKAT telescope in the Northern Cape, and in 2002 Mark Shuttleworth became the first African in space.

And the space race changed your life: satellites give us weather forecasts, TV, phone signals and maps on your phone.

Here is the line to remember: the Soviets were first into space, the Americans were first on the Moon, and in the end the rivals worked together.
