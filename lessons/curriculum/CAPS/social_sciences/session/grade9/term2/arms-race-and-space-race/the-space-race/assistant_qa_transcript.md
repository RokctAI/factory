### subtopic_1 — What Was the Space Race and Why Did It Matter?
**Assistant:** Why did Sputnik frighten Americans so much?

**Tutor:** Sputnik frightened Americans because of what it revealed rather than what it could do itself. The satellite itself was harmless: an 84-kilogram ball with a radio transmitter. But it showed several alarming things. First, it showed that the Soviet Union had a rocket powerful enough to put an object into orbit, and therefore powerful enough to send a nuclear warhead across the world to American cities. In August 1957 the Soviet Union had announced the test of its first intercontinental ballistic missile, and Sputnik, launched on the same type of rocket, made that claim visible to everyone, as it passed over the United States several times a day. Second, it shattered Americans' confidence that their science and technology were the best in the world. Many had assumed the Soviet Union was a backward country that could only copy the West. Now it had beaten the United States to a historic first. Third, it raised fears about education: politicians and commentators argued that Soviet schools were producing more scientists and engineers. Fourth, it was a propaganda victory for communism in the eyes of the world, especially in newly independent countries deciding which model of development to follow. The American response was dramatic: NASA was created in 1958, the National Defense Education Act funded science and mathematics education, and spending on missiles and research increased. The Sputnik crisis also fed into the missile gap debate in the 1960 presidential election.

### subtopic_2 — The Soviet Lead: Sputnik, Laika and Gagarin
**Assistant:** Why did President Kennedy choose the Moon as the goal?

**Tutor:** Kennedy chose the Moon as a goal for political, strategic and practical reasons. In April 1961 his government suffered two humiliations within a week: Yuri Gagarin's flight on 12 April, which made the Soviet Union the first to put a human in space, and the failed Bay of Pigs invasion of Cuba between 17 and 20 April. Kennedy needed a bold initiative to restore American prestige. He asked Vice-President Lyndon Johnson to find a space programme that promised dramatic results in which the United States could win. NASA and experts such as Wernher von Braun advised that the Soviet Union had a lead in rockets for near-Earth missions, such as orbiting a space station, which the United States could not quickly overcome. But landing a person on the Moon and returning them would require a completely new, much larger rocket and many new technologies. Because both countries would have to start almost from scratch, the United States, with its larger economy and industrial base, had a good chance of getting there first. The Moon was also a clear, dramatic goal that everyone could understand and that would capture the world's imagination. On 25 May 1961, Kennedy asked Congress to commit to landing a man on the Moon before the end of the decade. Kennedy himself privately admitted that he was less interested in space for its own sake than in beating the Soviet Union, which shows how closely the Moon programme was tied to the Cold War.

### subtopic_3 — The American Response and the Moon Landing
**Assistant:** Was the Apollo programme worth its enormous cost?

**Tutor:** This was debated at the time and still is, and it makes a good question for practising balanced argument. Apollo cost about 25 billion dollars between 1960 and 1973, equivalent to well over 200 billion dollars today, and at its peak NASA's budget was more than four per cent of all American government spending. Critics argued that the money should have been spent solving problems on Earth. In the late 1960s, the United States faced poverty, racial inequality, rioting in cities and the Vietnam War. Civil rights leaders, such as Ralph Abernathy, protested at the Apollo 11 launch, and the poet and musician Gil Scott-Heron later wrote about poverty while men went to the Moon. Supporters argued that the programme had many benefits. It achieved its Cold War aim of demonstrating American technological leadership. It employed about 400 000 people and stimulated industries such as electronics and computing; the need for small, light computers for spacecraft encouraged the development of integrated circuits. It produced scientific knowledge about the Moon's origin from the rocks brought back. It inspired a generation of young people to study science and engineering. And images such as Earthrise helped inspire the environmental movement by showing the Earth as a small, fragile planet. A balanced conclusion might be that Apollo had real benefits but also opportunity costs, meaning the things that could have been done with the same money, and that how you judge it depends on the values you hold.

### subtopic_4 — Consequences, Cooperation, South Africa's Role and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What was South Africa's role in the space race?

**Tutor:** South Africa played a small but significant role because of its geography. Spacecraft orbiting the Earth or travelling into deep space need to be in constant radio contact with ground stations, so space agencies need tracking stations spread around the globe. A station in the southern hemisphere, far from the United States, was valuable for covering parts of orbits and deep-space trajectories that American stations could not reach. In 1960 and 1961 NASA built a tracking station at Hartebeesthoek, in a valley west of Johannesburg chosen because the surrounding hills shielded it from radio interference. Run in cooperation with South African scientists, it tracked early satellites and the Ranger and Mariner missions, including the first close-up images of Mars sent by Mariner 4 in 1965, and supported other space missions. However, the relationship became politically difficult. The station was operating in a country whose apartheid laws discriminated against black South Africans, and American civil rights leaders and members of Congress objected, especially as NASA itself was trying to employ more African Americans. In 1974 and 1975 NASA withdrew, and the station was handed to South Africa's Council for Scientific and Industrial Research, which converted the large dish into a radio telescope, now the Hartebeesthoek Radio Astronomy Observatory. Since 1994 South Africa has built on this heritage with the MeerKAT telescope, the Square Kilometre Array partnership and the South African National Space Agency, founded in 2010.
