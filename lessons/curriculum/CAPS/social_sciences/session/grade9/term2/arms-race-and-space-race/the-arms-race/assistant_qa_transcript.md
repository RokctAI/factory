### subtopic_1 — What Was the Arms Race?
**Assistant:** Why did each superpower keep building weapons if it already had enough to destroy the other?

**Tutor:** This question goes to the heart of the arms race, and the answer lies in fear, uncertainty and the logic of deterrence. Both sides believed that deterrence only worked if they could still destroy the enemy after absorbing a surprise first strike. Each side therefore worried that the other might develop a way to destroy most of its weapons in a first strike, by building more accurate missiles, more warheads or defences against missiles. If that happened, the enemy might be tempted to attack. So each side kept building, to make sure enough weapons would survive any attack. Intelligence was uncertain, and each side often overestimated the other's strength, as in the missile gap scare of the late 1950s, when Americans believed the Soviet Union had more missiles than it actually did. New technologies, such as MIRVs, which put several warheads on one missile, created fresh fears and new rounds of building. Domestic factors also mattered. In the United States, President Eisenhower warned in his farewell speech in 1961 against the influence of the military-industrial complex, the alliance of armed forces and weapons manufacturers that benefited from high military spending. Politicians did not want to appear weak on defence. In the Soviet Union, military leaders had great influence in the Communist Party. This combination of fear and interest produced arsenals far beyond what was needed to deter, until the costs and dangers became impossible to ignore in the 1980s.

### subtopic_2 — Stages of the Race: Bombers, Missiles and Submarines
**Assistant:** How did the Cuban Missile Crisis end without war?

**Tutor:** The crisis ended through a combination of firmness, restraint, secret diplomacy and some luck. When President Kennedy learned on 16 October 1962 that Soviet nuclear missiles were being installed in Cuba, he set up a small group of advisers, known as ExComm, to consider options. Military leaders favoured air strikes and an invasion, but Kennedy chose a naval quarantine, announced on 22 October, which gave the Soviet Union time to back down without immediate bloodshed. Khrushchev ordered Soviet ships carrying missiles to turn back before reaching the quarantine line, though work continued on the missiles already in Cuba. On 26 October Khrushchev sent a letter offering to remove the missiles if the United States promised not to invade Cuba. On 27 October, the most dangerous day, a second, tougher letter demanded the removal of American missiles from Turkey too; an American U-2 was shot down over Cuba; and American ships dropped practice depth charges on a Soviet submarine whose officers, unknown to the Americans, had a nuclear torpedo and debated using it, until one officer, Vasili Arkhipov, refused to agree. Kennedy publicly accepted the first letter's terms, while his brother Robert Kennedy privately told the Soviet ambassador that the missiles in Turkey would be removed later, provided this remained secret. On 28 October Khrushchev announced that the missiles would be withdrawn. Both leaders had found a way to step back while saving face.

### subtopic_3 — Deterrence, Fear and Cost
**Assistant:** Why was the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty so important?

**Tutor:** The INF Treaty, signed in Washington on 8 December 1987 by President Ronald Reagan and the Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev, was important because it was the first agreement to eliminate an entire category of nuclear weapons, rather than merely limit their growth. Earlier treaties, such as SALT I in 1972, had capped the number of certain weapons but allowed arsenals to remain at very high levels. The INF Treaty banned all land-based missiles with ranges between 500 and 5 500 kilometres. These missiles were particularly dangerous because, deployed in Europe, they could reach their targets within minutes, leaving almost no time for leaders to decide whether a warning was real. In the late 1970s and early 1980s, the Soviet Union had deployed SS-20 missiles aimed at Western Europe, and NATO responded by deploying American Pershing II and cruise missiles, provoking massive peace protests across Europe. Under the treaty, the two sides destroyed a total of 2 692 missiles by 1991. It also introduced detailed on-site inspections, in which each side's inspectors could visit the other's bases to verify that weapons had been destroyed, building trust that had been absent for decades. The treaty reflected Gorbachev's new thinking, his wish to reduce military spending to save the Soviet economy, and Reagan's personal desire to reduce nuclear weapons. It helped end the Cold War. The United States withdrew from it in 2019, accusing Russia of violating it, which shows that arms control requires continuing commitment.

### subtopic_4 — The Cuban Missile Crisis, Arms Control and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What were the effects of nuclear testing on ordinary people?

**Tutor:** Nuclear testing affected ordinary people in many parts of the world, often those with the least power to object. Between 1945 and the mid-1990s, more than 2 000 nuclear tests were carried out, over 500 of them in the atmosphere before the Partial Test Ban Treaty of 1963, after which most tests by the superpowers took place underground. Atmospheric tests released radioactive fallout that spread across the globe in the upper atmosphere and fell with rain onto land and crops. Scientists in the United States collected children's baby teeth in the late 1950s and early 1960s and found rising levels of strontium-90, a radioactive substance that behaves like calcium and is absorbed into bones, which helped build public support for the test ban. People living near test sites suffered most. In 1954 the American Castle Bravo test at Bikini Atoll in the Marshall Islands was much larger than expected, and fallout fell on inhabited islands and on a Japanese fishing boat, the Lucky Dragon, whose crew became sick; one crew member died. Marshall Islanders were displaced from their homes, and some islands remain contaminated. At the Soviet test site at Semipalatinsk in Kazakhstan, more than 450 tests were carried out between 1949 and 1989, and nearby residents suffered high rates of cancer and birth defects. Britain tested in Australia, affecting Aboriginal communities, and France tested in Algeria and French Polynesia. These impacts show that the arms race had real victims even without a nuclear war.
