# Part 1 — Expert

This session defines and explains the nuclear arms race between the United States and the Soviet Union. It covers the meaning of the arms race and its main stages, from atomic bombs to hydrogen bombs and missiles; the theory of deterrence, the effects on daily life and the costs of the race; the Cuban Missile Crisis of October 1962 as the most dangerous moment of the Cold War; and the attempts to control the arms race through treaties, from the Partial Test Ban Treaty of 1963 to the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty of 1987. The anchor figure is the size of the arsenals: the number of nuclear weapons in the world peaked at about 70 000 around 1986, most of them held by the two superpowers. The governing idea is that each side built weapons because it feared the other side's weapons, creating a spiral of insecurity that made both sides less safe, until leaders recognised the danger and began to negotiate.

## Subtopic: What Was the Arms Race?

An arms race is a competition between two or more countries to build up more and better weapons than their rivals. During the Cold War, the United States and the Soviet Union competed in conventional weapons such as tanks, aircraft and warships, but the most important and dangerous competition was in nuclear weapons and the means of delivering them.

The race began with the end of the American monopoly. The United States used atomic bombs in August 1945 and for four years was the only nuclear power. On 29 August 1949 the Soviet Union tested its first atomic bomb, much earlier than American experts had expected, helped by espionage from inside the Manhattan Project. The shock led President Truman in 1950 to approve the development of a far more powerful weapon, the hydrogen bomb or thermonuclear bomb, which uses an atomic explosion to trigger the fusion of hydrogen atoms, as happens in the Sun.

On 1 November 1952 the United States tested its first hydrogen device, code-named Ivy Mike, on an island in the Pacific. Its explosion was equivalent to about 10,4 million tonnes of TNT, or 10,4 megatons, roughly 700 times the power of the Hiroshima bomb, and it vaporised the island. The Soviet Union tested its own thermonuclear weapon in August 1953, and a fully developed hydrogen bomb in 1955. In October 1961 the Soviet Union exploded the largest weapon ever tested, the Tsar Bomba, with a force of about 50 megatons, more than 3 000 times the Hiroshima bomb.

Other countries joined the nuclear club: Britain in 1952, France in 1960 and China in 1964, followed later by Israel, which has never officially confirmed its weapons, India, Pakistan and North Korea. South Africa secretly built six bombs in the 1970s and 1980s before dismantling them.

The power of these weapons is measured in kilotons and megatons. One kiloton is the explosive force of 1 000 tonnes of TNT, and one megaton is 1 000 kilotons, or a million tonnes of TNT. The Hiroshima bomb was about 15 kilotons. To compare, divide one yield by the other in the same units: Ivy Mike's 10,4 megatons is 10 400 kilotons, and 10 400 divided by 15 is about 693, which is why it is described as roughly 700 times the Hiroshima bomb. The Tsar Bomba's 50 megatons is 50 000 kilotons, and 50 000 divided by 15 is about 3 333. These calculations show how quickly destructive power grew in only sixteen years after 1945.

So the next question is: how did the race develop, and how did each side try to stay ahead?

## Subtopic: Stages of the Race: Bombers, Missiles and Submarines

Building bombs was only half the race; the other half was delivery, the means of getting a weapon to its target. In the 1950s nuclear bombs were carried by long-range bombers. The United States built large bomber fleets, such as the B-52, which entered service in 1955, and kept some bombers in the air at all times, ready to strike.

The next stage was missiles. On 21 August 1957 the Soviet Union successfully tested the world's first intercontinental ballistic missile, or ICBM, the R-7, which could carry a nuclear warhead thousands of kilometres from the Soviet Union to the United States in about thirty minutes, far faster than any bomber, and which could not be shot down. In October 1957 a version of the same rocket launched Sputnik, the first artificial satellite, which you will study in the next lesson. Americans feared there was a missile gap in the Soviet Union's favour, which became an issue in the 1960 presidential election. In fact, by the early 1960s the United States had far more missiles and warheads than the Soviet Union, but the fear drove a massive American building programme.

The third stage was submarines. From 1960 the United States deployed submarines carrying Polaris missiles that could be launched from under the sea. Submarines were very hard to find and destroy, so they guaranteed that a country could retaliate even after a surprise attack. Together, bombers, land-based missiles and submarine-launched missiles formed the nuclear triad.

Later technologies made the race even more complicated. In the late 1960s and 1970s, both sides developed multiple independently targetable re-entry vehicles, or MIRVs, which allowed a single missile to carry several warheads, each aimed at a different target. This multiplied the number of warheads rapidly. In 1983 President Ronald Reagan announced the Strategic Defense Initiative, nicknamed Star Wars by critics, a plan to develop space-based systems to shoot down incoming missiles; the Soviet Union feared it could upset the balance of deterrence.

As a result of this competition, arsenals grew enormously. The American stockpile peaked at about 31 000 warheads in 1967, and the Soviet stockpile at about 40 000 in 1986. At the peak around 1986, the world had about 70 000 nuclear warheads, enough to destroy human civilisation many times over.

So the next question is: what did it feel like to live through the arms race, and what did it cost?

## Subtopic: Deterrence, Fear and Cost

The logic behind the arms race was deterrence: the belief that having enough weapons to destroy an enemy, even after being attacked first, would discourage the enemy from attacking. As you learned in the previous lesson, when both sides achieved this, the result was mutually assured destruction. Each side feared that if the other gained a lead, it might be tempted to strike first, so each side kept building. This is sometimes called the security dilemma: actions taken by one country to make itself safer make its rival feel less safe, so the rival responds, and both end up less secure.

The arms race shaped daily life. In the United States in the 1950s, schoolchildren practised duck and cover drills, hiding under their desks in case of a nuclear attack, following a 1951 civil defence film. Some families built fallout shelters in their gardens and stocked them with food. Popular films and novels imagined nuclear war. In the Soviet Union and Europe, too, people lived with fear of annihilation. Nuclear tests in the atmosphere spread radioactive fallout around the world; scientists found radioactive strontium-90 in children's teeth. Tests also harmed people living near test sites, such as Marshall Islanders in the Pacific, Indigenous communities in Australia and residents near the Soviet test site at Semipalatinsk in Kazakhstan.

The arms race was enormously expensive. Both superpowers spent large shares of their national budgets on weapons. Historians estimate that the United States spent several trillion dollars on nuclear weapons alone during the Cold War. For the Soviet Union, with a smaller economy, military spending took an even larger share of national output, possibly around 15 per cent or more in the 1980s, leaving less for consumer goods, housing and food. This burden contributed to the economic problems that helped bring about the Soviet Union's collapse in 1991. Money spent on weapons was money not spent on health, education or development, a point made strongly by many leaders of developing countries.

Public opposition grew. The Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament was founded in Britain in 1958, and in the early 1980s huge anti-nuclear demonstrations took place in Europe and the United States, with about a million people marching in New York in June 1982.

The arms race also involved Southern Africa indirectly. South Africa and Namibia supplied uranium to Western nuclear programmes from the 1950s, and the Rössing uranium mine in Namibia, opened in 1976, became one of the largest in the world, while apartheid South Africa developed its own secret weapons programme.

So the next question is: how close did the world come to nuclear war?

## Subtopic: The Cuban Missile Crisis, Arms Control and the Error Museum

The most dangerous moment of the arms race came in October 1962. In 1959 a revolution led by Fidel Castro had overthrown Cuba's American-backed dictator, and Castro's government moved closer to the Soviet Union. In April 1961 a CIA-backed invasion by Cuban exiles at the Bay of Pigs failed. The Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev, wanting to protect Cuba and to balance American missiles stationed in Turkey and Italy near the Soviet border, secretly began installing nuclear missiles in Cuba, only about 150 kilometres from Florida.

On 14 October 1962 an American U-2 spy plane photographed the missile sites, and President John F. Kennedy was informed on 16 October. For thirteen days the world stood on the edge of nuclear war. Kennedy rejected an immediate air strike and announced a naval quarantine, or blockade, of Cuba on 22 October to stop more missiles arriving. Soviet ships approached the blockade line and turned back. On 27 October an American U-2 was shot down over Cuba, killing its pilot, and a Soviet submarine was nearly provoked into using a nuclear torpedo. On 28 October Khrushchev agreed to remove the missiles in return for an American promise not to invade Cuba, and Kennedy secretly agreed to remove American missiles from Turkey.

The crisis frightened both sides into the first steps of arms control. In 1963 a hotline was set up between Washington and Moscow so leaders could communicate directly, and the Partial Test Ban Treaty banned nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in space and under water. The Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty, signed in 1968, aimed to stop the spread of nuclear weapons to more countries. During the period of détente, or relaxation of tension, in the 1970s, the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks produced SALT I and the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty in 1972, which limited the growth of arsenals. Most importantly, in 1987 Reagan and the Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev signed the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty, which for the first time eliminated a whole category of nuclear weapons, and in 1991 the Strategic Arms Reduction Treaty, START, began real cuts. By the 2020s the world's stockpile had fallen to about 12 000 warheads, though this is still enough to cause catastrophic destruction.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "The arms race was only about having more bombs." It was also about delivery systems, speed, accuracy and the ability to survive a first strike: bombers, missiles, submarines and MIRVs.

Exhibit two: "The Cuban Missile Crisis was a war between the United States and Cuba." It was a confrontation between the superpowers over Soviet missiles in Cuba, resolved by negotiation without war.

Exhibit three: "Arms control ended nuclear weapons." Treaties reduced and limited weapons, but about 12 000 remain in the world.

Exhibit four: "The arms race made both sides completely safe." Deterrence may have prevented direct war, but accidents, false alarms and crises such as 1962 showed how dangerous it was.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: two neighbours buying bigger and bigger dogs, a school drill under the desk, and thirteen days in October.

## Subtopic: Bigger and Bigger Dogs

Imagine two neighbours who do not trust each other. One buys a big guard dog. The other feels scared, so he buys two bigger dogs. The first neighbour feels scared now, so he buys three even bigger dogs. Soon both yards are full of fierce dogs, both neighbours are spending all their money on dog food, and neither feels any safer. That is an arms race.

In 1945 only America had the atomic bomb. In 1949 the Soviet Union tested one too. Then America built the hydrogen bomb in 1952, about 700 times more powerful than the Hiroshima bomb. The Soviet Union built one in 1953. In 1961 the Soviet Union tested the biggest bomb ever, the Tsar Bomba, more than 3 000 times as powerful as the Hiroshima bomb.

Then came faster ways to deliver them: giant bombers, then rockets that could cross the world in about half an hour, then submarines hiding under the sea. By around 1986 there were about 70 000 nuclear weapons in the world.

So what was it like to live with all those bombs?

## Subtopic: Under the Desk

In America in the 1950s, children practised a drill called duck and cover: when the teacher shouted, they hid under their desks with their hands over their heads, in case of a nuclear attack. Some families built underground shelters in their gardens, full of tinned food and water.

The idea behind all these weapons was deterrence: if you have enough bombs to destroy your enemy even after they attack you, they will not dare to attack. Both sides believed this. But it meant each side kept building more, in case the other got ahead.

It cost a fortune. Money that could have built schools, hospitals and houses went into weapons instead. Testing bombs in the open air also spread radioactive dust around the world. Many people protested and marched for the bombs to be banned.

So did the world ever come close to using them?

## Subtopic: Thirteen Days in October

Yes. In October 1962, an American spy plane photographed Soviet nuclear missiles being set up in Cuba, an island very close to the United States. President Kennedy put a ring of navy ships around Cuba to stop more missiles arriving. For thirteen days the whole world held its breath.

Finally, on 28 October, the Soviet leader Khrushchev agreed to take the missiles away if America promised not to invade Cuba. America also secretly agreed to remove its own missiles from Turkey.

The scare made leaders start talking. They set up a hotline so they could call each other directly. They agreed to stop testing bombs in the air in 1963. In 1987 Reagan and Gorbachev agreed to destroy a whole type of missile. Today there are far fewer nuclear weapons, about 12 000, but still enough to be very dangerous.

Here is the line to remember: each side built weapons because it feared the other, which made everyone less safe, until a near-disaster in 1962 pushed leaders to start talking.
