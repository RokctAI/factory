# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains how World War II ended in the Pacific and how the atomic bombs began the nuclear age. It covers the situation in the Pacific in 1945, including the island-hopping campaign, the battles of Iwo Jima and Okinawa and the firebombing of Japanese cities; the Manhattan Project and the development of the atomic bomb; the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki and their human consequences; and Japan's surrender and the beginning of the nuclear age. The anchor dates are 6 August 1945, the bombing of Hiroshima; 9 August 1945, the bombing of Nagasaki and the Soviet invasion of Manchuria; 15 August 1945, when Emperor Hirohito announced Japan's surrender by radio; and 2 September 1945, when Japan formally surrendered aboard the USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay, ending World War II. The governing idea is that the end of the war came through a combination of military defeat, blockade, conventional bombing, the atomic bombs and the Soviet entry into the war, and that the atomic bombs introduced a weapon so destructive that it changed international relations permanently.

## Subtopic: The Pacific War in 1945

After the Battle of Midway in June 1942, the United States and its allies took the offensive in the Pacific. They followed a strategy called island hopping: instead of capturing every Japanese-held island, they seized key islands that could serve as air and naval bases and bypassed others, leaving their garrisons isolated and cut off from supplies. American forces under Admiral Chester Nimitz advanced across the central Pacific, while forces under General Douglas MacArthur advanced through New Guinea to the Philippines, which were recaptured from late 1944. British, Indian and African troops fought the Japanese in Burma, and Australian forces fought in New Guinea and Borneo.

Japanese soldiers usually fought to the death rather than surrender, and the closer the Americans came to Japan, the higher the casualties. On Iwo Jima, a small volcanic island captured between February and March 1945, almost all of the roughly 21 000 Japanese defenders died, and about 6 800 Americans were killed. On Okinawa, from April to June 1945, about 12 500 Americans were killed, together with about 110 000 Japanese soldiers, and estimates of Okinawan civilian deaths range from about 40 000 to 150 000, many of whom died caught in the fighting or by suicide encouraged or forced by the Japanese military. Japanese pilots carried out kamikaze attacks, deliberately crashing aircraft packed with explosives into Allied ships; about 1 900 kamikaze aircraft were used during the Okinawa campaign alone.

From late 1944, American B-29 bombers based in the Mariana Islands began bombing Japanese cities. In March 1945, General Curtis LeMay switched to low-level night attacks with incendiary bombs designed to set fire to cities built largely of wood. In the firebombing of Tokyo on the night of 9 to 10 March 1945, about 100 000 people were killed, and about a quarter of the city was destroyed, more deaths than in any single air raid of the war, including the atomic bombings in their immediate effects. By August 1945 more than sixty Japanese cities had been heavily bombed. An American naval blockade, together with submarines and mines, had cut off Japan's imports of food, oil and raw materials, causing severe shortages.

Despite this, Japan's military leaders refused to accept unconditional surrender. They hoped to make an Allied invasion so costly that the Allies would agree to better terms, especially the preservation of the emperor's position. Japan still had about two million soldiers in the home islands and thousands of aircraft prepared for kamikaze attacks, and civilians were being trained to resist with bamboo spears. American planners prepared Operation Downfall, an invasion of Kyushu planned for November 1945 and of Honshu for 1946.

American planners produced casualty estimates for Operation Downfall that varied widely, from tens of thousands to several hundred thousand American dead, with far larger numbers of Japanese military and civilian deaths expected. These estimates later became central to the debate about whether the atomic bombs were justified, which is the subject of the next lesson.

So the next question is: what was the new weapon the United States had been building?

## Subtopic: The Manhattan Project and the First Atomic Bomb

In 1938 German scientists discovered nuclear fission: when the nucleus of a uranium atom is struck by a neutron, it can split, releasing a great deal of energy and more neutrons, which can split further atoms in a chain reaction. Scientists realised this could be used to make a bomb of unprecedented power. In August 1939, the physicist Albert Einstein, who had fled Nazi Germany, signed a letter to President Roosevelt warning that Germany might develop such a weapon. Many of the scientists who worked on the bomb were refugees from Nazi persecution, and the fear that Hitler might get the bomb first drove the effort.

In 1942 the United States launched the Manhattan Project, a top-secret programme directed by General Leslie Groves, with the physicist J. Robert Oppenheimer as scientific director at the Los Alamos laboratory in New Mexico. British and Canadian scientists also took part. At its height the project employed about 130 000 people at sites across the United States, most of whom did not know what they were building, and it cost about two billion dollars. Two types of bomb were developed: one used uranium-235 and the other plutonium-239.

On 16 July 1945, in the desert near Alamogordo, New Mexico, the first atomic bomb was tested in what was code-named the Trinity test. The explosion was equivalent to about 20 000 tonnes of TNT, produced a flash brighter than many suns and created a mushroom cloud rising about 12 kilometres. Oppenheimer later recalled thinking of a line from the Hindu scripture the Bhagavad Gita: "Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds."

Germany had surrendered in May 1945, so the bomb would now be used against Japan. President Truman, who had learned of the project only after becoming president in April, was told of the test while at the Potsdam Conference. On 26 July 1945 the United States, Britain and China issued the Potsdam Declaration, calling on Japan to surrender unconditionally or face "prompt and utter destruction". The declaration did not mention the atomic bomb or guarantee the emperor's position. Japan's government did not accept it.

So the next question is: what happened at Hiroshima and Nagasaki?

## Subtopic: Hiroshima and Nagasaki

At 8:15 a.m. on 6 August 1945, a B-29 bomber named the Enola Gay dropped a uranium bomb, code-named Little Boy, on Hiroshima, a city of about 350 000 people that contained military headquarters and industries. The bomb exploded about 600 metres above the city with a force of about 15 000 tonnes of TNT. A fireball with a temperature of several thousand degrees burned people and buildings within more than a kilometre, a blast wave flattened most structures within about two kilometres, and fires swept the city. About 70 000 to 80 000 people died immediately or on that day, and by the end of 1945 the death toll had reached about 140 000, including thousands of Korean forced labourers and some Allied prisoners of war. Many died later from burns, injuries and radiation sickness, which caused hair loss, bleeding, vomiting and death in the following weeks. Survivors, known in Japanese as hibakusha, suffered higher rates of cancers such as leukaemia for decades.

Truman announced the bombing, describing it as a harnessing of the basic power of the universe and warning Japan to expect a rain of ruin from the air. Japan's leaders were divided. On 8 August, the Soviet Union declared war on Japan, as Stalin had promised at Yalta, and early on 9 August a Soviet army of more than a million soldiers invaded Japanese-occupied Manchuria, quickly defeating the Japanese forces there. This ended Japan's hope that the Soviet Union might act as a mediator for peace.

At 11:02 a.m. on 9 August 1945, a plutonium bomb, code-named Fat Man, was dropped on Nagasaki from a B-29 named Bockscar. The original target, Kokura, had been obscured by cloud and smoke. Nagasaki's hilly terrain limited the damage somewhat, but about 40 000 people died immediately and about 74 000 by the end of 1945.

On the night of 9 to 10 August, Japan's Supreme War Council was deadlocked over whether to surrender, and Emperor Hirohito intervened, saying the time had come to bear the unbearable. After further exchanges with the Allies about the emperor's position, and an attempted coup by army officers who wanted to continue the war, the emperor's recorded radio broadcast announcing the decision to surrender was played on 15 August 1945. It was the first time most Japanese people had heard his voice. He referred to a new and most cruel bomb. On 2 September 1945, Japanese representatives signed the formal surrender aboard the battleship USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay, in a ceremony led by General MacArthur. World War II was over.

So the final question is: what did the atomic bomb mean for the world after 1945?

## Subtopic: The Beginning of the Nuclear Age and the Error Museum

The atomic bombs changed international relations permanently. For the first time, a single aircraft with a single bomb could destroy a city. For four years the United States had a monopoly on atomic weapons, which American leaders believed gave them an advantage in dealing with the Soviet Union, while Stalin regarded it as a form of pressure. Soviet scientists, helped by intelligence obtained from spies inside the Manhattan Project such as Klaus Fuchs, worked rapidly, and on 29 August 1949 the Soviet Union tested its first atomic bomb, ending the American monopoly. This launched the nuclear arms race, studied later this term. In 1952 the United States tested a hydrogen bomb, hundreds of times more powerful than the Hiroshima bomb, and the Soviet Union followed in 1953.

The nuclear age also produced new ideas and movements. Many of the scientists who built the bomb, including Einstein and Oppenheimer, later campaigned for international control of nuclear weapons. Survivors of Hiroshima and Nagasaki became powerful voices for peace. The Hiroshima Peace Memorial, the ruined Genbaku Dome, became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1996, and the Japanese organisation of survivors, Nihon Hidankyo, was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 2024. Nuclear energy was also developed for peaceful purposes, such as electricity generation, including at South Africa's Koeberg power station near Cape Town, which began operating in 1984.

South Africa has its own place in nuclear history: the apartheid government secretly built six atomic bombs in the 1970s and 1980s, and then dismantled them before joining the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty in 1991. It is the only country to have built nuclear weapons and voluntarily given them up.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "The atomic bombs alone ended the war." The war ended through a combination of military defeat, blockade, conventional bombing, the atomic bombs and the Soviet invasion of Manchuria; historians debate the relative importance of each.

Exhibit two: "The war ended on 6 August 1945." Hiroshima was bombed on 6 August; Japan announced surrender on 15 August and signed it on 2 September 1945.

Exhibit three: "The atomic bombings killed more people than any other raid." The Tokyo firebombing of March 1945 killed about 100 000 people in one night; Hiroshima's total, including later deaths, reached about 140 000 by the end of 1945.

Exhibit four: "Only Japanese people were killed." Victims included thousands of Korean forced labourers, Chinese and South-East Asian people, and some American prisoners of war.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: an island ladder to Japan, a secret laboratory in the desert, and a flash of light over two cities.

## Subtopic: An Island Ladder to Japan

After 1942, the Americans began to climb towards Japan across the Pacific Ocean, using islands like the rungs of a ladder. They captured some islands to use as bases and skipped others. This was called island hopping.

The closer they got to Japan, the harder the fighting became, because Japanese soldiers were taught never to surrender. On the tiny island of Iwo Jima in early 1945, almost all of about 21 000 Japanese defenders died. On Okinawa, from April to June 1945, about 12 500 Americans died, and huge numbers of Japanese soldiers and Okinawan civilians were killed. Some Japanese pilots, called kamikaze, flew their planes straight into American ships on purpose.

American bombers dropped fire bombs on Japanese cities built mostly of wood. In one night in March 1945, about 100 000 people died in Tokyo. Ships blocked food and oil from reaching Japan. But Japan's generals still would not give up. America began planning to invade Japan itself.

So what was the secret weapon being built?

## Subtopic: A Secret Laboratory in the Desert

In 1938 scientists discovered that splitting tiny atoms of uranium could release enormous energy. Fearing that Hitler would build a bomb first, the United States started a secret programme in 1942, called the Manhattan Project. About 130 000 people worked on it, and most did not even know what they were making. The lead scientist was J. Robert Oppenheimer.

On 16 July 1945, in the desert of New Mexico, they tested the first atomic bomb. The flash was brighter than the sun, and a giant mushroom cloud rose into the sky.

Germany had already surrendered, so the bomb would be used on Japan. On 26 July the Allies warned Japan to surrender or face prompt and utter destruction. Japan did not surrender.

So what happened next?

## Subtopic: A Flash Over Two Cities

At 8:15 in the morning on 6 August 1945, a plane called the Enola Gay dropped one bomb on the city of Hiroshima. In a second, a fireball and a blast destroyed most of the city. By the end of the year, about 140 000 people had died, many slowly from burns and radiation sickness.

On 8 August the Soviet Union declared war on Japan, and its huge army attacked in Manchuria. On 9 August a second atomic bomb hit Nagasaki, and about 74 000 people had died there by the end of the year.

On 15 August, Japan's emperor spoke on the radio for the first time and told his people Japan would surrender. On 2 September 1945, Japan signed the surrender on an American battleship in Tokyo Bay. World War II was finally over.

But a new age had begun: the nuclear age. In 1949 the Soviet Union tested its own bomb, and a dangerous race began.

Here is the line to remember: 6 August Hiroshima, 9 August Nagasaki and the Soviet attack, 15 August the emperor's broadcast, 2 September the surrender, and a new age of nuclear fear.
