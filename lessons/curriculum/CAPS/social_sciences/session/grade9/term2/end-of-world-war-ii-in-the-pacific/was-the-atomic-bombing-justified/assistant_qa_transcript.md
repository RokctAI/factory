### subtopic_1 — How and Why the Decision Was Made
**Assistant:** Why didn't the United States demonstrate the bomb on an empty area first?

**Tutor:** The idea of a demonstration was seriously proposed and discussed in 1945, but officials rejected it for several reasons. The Franck Report of June 1945 suggested exploding the bomb on a desert or barren island before representatives of the United Nations, including Japan, to show its power and give Japan a chance to surrender. The Interim Committee and its scientific panel, which included Oppenheimer, considered this and concluded that they could see no acceptable alternative to direct military use. First, they worried that the demonstration might fail, since the bomb was a new and complex device, and a failure would make the United States look weak and encourage Japan to keep fighting. Second, they had only a very small number of bombs. In August 1945 the United States had enough material for two bombs, with more expected in the following months, so using one for a demonstration would leave fewer for use if Japan did not surrender. Third, they feared that if Japan were warned of the time and place of an attack, it might move Allied prisoners of war into the target area or try to shoot down the aircraft. Fourth, they doubted that Japan's military leaders, who had ignored massive firebombing, would be impressed enough by a demonstration over an empty area to surrender. Critics reply that these arguments were never tested, and that a demonstration combined with clearer surrender terms might have worked, saving many civilian lives.

### subtopic_2 — Arguments That the Bombings Were Justified
**Assistant:** How reliable is Stimson's claim of a million casualties?

**Tutor:** Stimson's claim is an important source, but historians treat it with caution and analyse its purpose and context. Henry Stimson was Secretary of War in 1945 and was directly involved in the decision to use the bomb. In February 1947, Harper's Magazine published his article The Decision to Use the Atomic Bomb, in which he stated that he had been informed that an invasion of Japan might be expected to cost over a million casualties to American forces alone. The article was written at a time when criticism of the bombings was growing, including from religious leaders and the journalist John Hersey's powerful account Hiroshima, published in 1946, which described the experiences of six survivors. Historians have shown that Stimson's article was carefully prepared with the help of other officials, including James Conant, to defend the decision and shape public opinion. When historians examined military planning documents from mid-1945, they found a wide range of estimates. Some planners estimated tens of thousands of American deaths for the first invasion of Kyushu, while others, especially as intelligence showed Japan strengthening its defences, gave higher figures. The phrase over a million casualties included wounded as well as dead, and it was not clearly stated in the documents Truman saw. This does not mean the danger was imaginary, because the fighting on Okinawa had been terrible, but it shows that the figure was used after the event to justify the decision. In a source analysis, you should note the author's role, the date, the purpose and the audience.

### subtopic_3 — Arguments That the Bombings Were Not Justified
**Assistant:** Was the bombing of Nagasaki harder to justify than Hiroshima?

**Tutor:** Many historians and commentators argue that Nagasaki is harder to justify, though others disagree. The main argument against Nagasaki is timing. The bomb was dropped on 9 August, only three days after Hiroshima. During those three days, Japan's leaders were still trying to understand what had happened, since communications with Hiroshima had been cut and confirmation that it had been a nuclear weapon took time. On the morning of 9 August, the Soviet invasion of Manchuria also began, and Japan's Supreme War Council was meeting to discuss surrender when news arrived of the second bomb. Critics argue that the Japanese government was not given a reasonable chance to respond before a second city was destroyed. Interestingly, there was no separate presidential order for the second bomb: the military order issued in July authorised the use of additional bombs as soon as they were ready, and the date was moved forward because of forecasts of bad weather. Kokura was the primary target, but clouds and smoke caused the crew to divert to Nagasaki. Defenders of the decision argue that the second bomb was needed to show that the United States had more than one such weapon and could continue, convincing Japan's leaders that resistance was hopeless; they point out that even after Nagasaki, the war council remained deadlocked. In an essay, you could treat Nagasaki as a separate point, showing your understanding that justification for one bombing does not automatically justify the other.

### subtopic_4 — Historians' Views, Writing the Essay and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How should I structure an essay on whether the bombings were justified?

**Tutor:** Start by reading the question carefully and deciding on your line of argument, the main position you will defend throughout the essay. For example, you might argue that the bombings were not justified because Japan was close to surrender and alternatives existed, or that they were justified because they ended the war and prevented a costly invasion. A third option is a nuanced position: that the first bomb can be defended but not the second, or that the decision was understandable but not morally justified. In the introduction, briefly set the context, the end of the war in the Pacific in August 1945, and state your argument clearly in one or two sentences. In the body, write about four to six paragraphs, each beginning with a topic sentence that makes one point linked to your argument. Support each point with specific evidence: dates such as 6 and 9 August 1945, figures such as about 140 000 deaths in Hiroshima by the end of 1945, names such as Truman, Stimson and Franck, and sources such as the Strategic Bombing Survey of 1946. Include at least one paragraph that presents the opposing view and explain why you find it less convincing. Use connecting words such as however, furthermore and consequently to show the flow of your argument. Finally, write a conclusion that weighs the evidence and restates your argument in light of it, without introducing new information. Check your spelling of names and dates, and make sure every paragraph answers the question.
