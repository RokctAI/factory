# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines why the United States dropped the atomic bombs on Japan and whether the decision was justified. It covers how the decision was made and the reasons American leaders gave; the arguments that the bombings were justified; the arguments that they were not justified, including alternatives that were proposed at the time; and how historians have interpreted the decision and how to write a balanced, evidence-based essay on a controversial question. The anchor sources are the Franck Report of June 1945, in which a group of Manhattan Project scientists urged a demonstration of the bomb before using it on a city, and the article by the former Secretary of War Henry Stimson published in 1947, which argued that the bombs saved over a million American casualties. The governing idea is that this is a question of interpretation, not simple fact: historians agree on what happened, but they disagree about motives, alternatives and moral judgements, and a strong answer weighs evidence on several sides before reaching a reasoned conclusion.

## Subtopic: How and Why the Decision Was Made

The decision to use the atomic bomb was not made in a single meeting. When Harry Truman became president on 12 April 1945, after Roosevelt's death, he learned about the Manhattan Project for the first time. By then, many leaders assumed the bomb would be used once it was ready, as part of the war effort, just as every other weapon had been. In May 1945 the Secretary of War, Henry Stimson, set up an Interim Committee to advise on atomic policy. On 1 June it recommended that the bomb be used against Japan as soon as possible, on a war plant surrounded by workers' homes, and without prior warning.

A Target Committee chose cities that had not yet been heavily bombed, so that the bomb's effects could be clearly measured and would make a strong impression. Hiroshima, Kokura, Niigata and Nagasaki were selected. Kyoto was originally on the list, but Stimson removed it because of its cultural and religious importance and, he said, to avoid bitterness that might push post-war Japan towards the Soviet Union.

The reasons American leaders gave at the time and afterwards were mainly military. They wanted to end the war as quickly as possible and to avoid an invasion of Japan, which they expected to be extremely costly. The battles of Iwo Jima and Okinawa had shown how fiercely Japanese forces fought, and the planned invasion, Operation Downfall, was due to begin on Kyushu in November 1945. They also wanted Japan's unconditional surrender, which had been the Allied policy since 1943. Truman wrote in his diary in July 1945 that the bomb would be used against military objectives and soldiers, not women and children, although in practice the targets were cities full of civilians.

Other motives may also have played a part. The Manhattan Project had cost about two billion dollars, and leaders may have felt pressure to justify the expense to Congress and the public. Some advisers, especially Secretary of State James Byrnes, believed that demonstrating the bomb would make the Soviet Union more cooperative in post-war Europe and Asia, and that ending the war quickly would limit Soviet gains in East Asia. And years of brutal war, including Pearl Harbour, the Bataan Death March and the treatment of prisoners, had created intense hostility towards Japan, sometimes expressed in racist terms in American propaganda.

So the next question is: what are the strongest arguments that the bombing was justified?

## Subtopic: Arguments That the Bombings Were Justified

The first and most important argument is that the bombs saved lives by ending the war without an invasion. American military planners produced varying estimates of casualties for Operation Downfall, ranging from tens of thousands to several hundred thousand American dead, and Japanese military and civilian deaths would have been far higher, perhaps in the millions, given the resistance seen on Okinawa, where many civilians died. In his 1947 article in Harper's Magazine, Stimson claimed that an invasion might have cost over a million American casualties, meaning killed and wounded, and that the bomb was the least abhorrent choice.

The second argument is that Japan showed no sign of accepting unconditional surrender before the bombs. Its military leaders were preparing for a final battle, and intercepted messages showed that while some diplomats sought peace through the Soviet Union, the government would not accept the Allies' terms. Even after Hiroshima, and after the Soviet attack, Japan's war council was deadlocked, and the emperor had to intervene. Army officers even attempted a coup to stop the surrender broadcast. Supporters of the decision argue that this shows how hard it was to make Japan surrender at all.

The third argument is that every week the war continued, people were dying across Asia: Allied prisoners of war, forced labourers, and civilians in areas occupied by Japan, including China, South-East Asia and Korea, where historians estimate that many thousands died each week from violence, famine and disease. Ending the war quickly would save these lives too. The fourth argument is about context: by 1945 all the major powers had accepted bombing cities as part of total war. The firebombing of Tokyo in March 1945 killed about as many people as the Hiroshima bomb did immediately. From this perspective, the atomic bomb was a more powerful weapon in a campaign already underway, rather than a different kind of act.

Some survivors and veterans have supported this view. Many Allied prisoners in Japanese camps believed the bomb saved their lives, because they had heard that guards were ordered to kill them if an invasion began. The South African writer Laurens van der Post, held in Java, wrote that he believed the bomb had saved prisoners from massacre.

So the next question is: what are the strongest arguments against the bombings?

## Subtopic: Arguments That the Bombings Were Not Justified

The first argument is moral. The bombs deliberately targeted cities, killing tens of thousands of civilians, including women, children and the elderly, as well as Korean forced labourers and prisoners of war. Many people argue that deliberately killing civilians is always wrong, whatever the military aim, and that the use of weapons whose radiation caused suffering for decades crossed a new moral line. Some critics also argue that racism made it easier for American leaders and the public to accept the mass killing of Japanese civilians.

The second argument is that Japan was already close to defeat and surrender. Its navy and air force were destroyed, the blockade was starving the country, and its cities were burning. Some Japanese leaders were seeking peace through the Soviet Union, and American code-breakers knew this. In 1946 the United States Strategic Bombing Survey concluded that Japan would probably have surrendered before 1 November 1945, even if the atomic bombs had not been dropped, the Soviet Union had not entered the war and no invasion had been planned. Some senior American military leaders later expressed doubts: Admiral William Leahy, Truman's chief of staff, wrote that the bomb was of no material assistance in the war against Japan, and General Dwight Eisenhower later wrote that he had felt it was unnecessary.

The third argument is that alternatives were available. The Allies could have modified the demand for unconditional surrender by assuring Japan that the emperor could remain, which several American officials recommended and which was, in practice, allowed after the surrender. They could have waited for the Soviet entry into the war, which they knew was coming in August. They could have demonstrated the bomb on an uninhabited area. In June 1945 the Franck Report, written by scientists led by James Franck, argued that a demonstration before observers from the United Nations would show the bomb's power without killing civilians and would make international control of nuclear weapons more likely; in July, the scientist Leo Szilard organised a petition signed by about seventy scientists asking Truman not to use the bomb without giving Japan a chance to surrender. Officials rejected a demonstration, fearing it might fail or that Japan would move prisoners to the site.

The fourth argument concerns Nagasaki. It was bombed only three days after Hiroshima, before Japan's leaders had time to understand what had happened and respond, so critics argue that the second bomb, at least, was unnecessary. The fifth argument, made by revisionist historians such as Gar Alperovitz, is that a main purpose of the bombs was to intimidate the Soviet Union and end the war before Soviet forces could gain influence in East Asia, an approach called atomic diplomacy, so that the bombings were the first act of the Cold War as much as the last act of World War II.

So the final question is: how do historians weigh these arguments, and how should you write about them?

## Subtopic: Historians' Views, Writing the Essay and the Error Museum

Historians' views have changed over time. The traditional view, expressed by Stimson and widely accepted in the United States in the 1950s, was that the bomb was a military necessity that saved lives. Revisionist historians from the 1960s argued that Japan was ready to surrender and that the bomb was used mainly to impress the Soviet Union. Many historians today take a middle position. They argue that American leaders genuinely believed the bomb would shorten the war and save American lives, that this was their main motive, but that they did not seriously consider alternatives, that Soviet considerations were a secondary factor, and that Japan's surrender resulted from a combination of the bombs and the Soviet entry into the war. Opinion surveys show that people in the United States have become more critical over time, while in Japan most people regard the bombings as unjustified.

In the Grade 9 History essay, you may be asked a question such as: "The atomic bombs were justified because they ended the war and saved lives. Do you agree?" A strong essay has a clear introduction that states your line of argument, for example that the bombings shortened the war but were not justified because alternatives existed and civilians were targeted. The body paragraphs present evidence for both sides, each paragraph making one point supported by specific evidence, such as dates, figures, names and quotations from sources. Use your sources critically: Stimson wrote in 1947 to defend the decision against growing criticism, and the Franck scientists were worried about a future arms race, so consider purpose, author and context. Avoid simply listing points; link them to your argument. End with a conclusion that weighs the evidence and restates your view. Remember that a well-supported argument on either side can earn full marks.

It also helps to separate three different questions that are often mixed together: was the decision understandable given what leaders knew in 1945, was it necessary to end the war, and was it morally right? A person might answer yes to the first and no to the third.

Now the error museum.

Exhibit one: "There is one correct answer that all historians agree on." This is a debate of interpretation; historians agree on the facts but disagree on motives, alternatives and moral judgement.

Exhibit two: "No one questioned the decision at the time." Scientists such as James Franck and Leo Szilard opposed using the bomb on cities without warning, and some officials urged changing the surrender terms.

Exhibit three: "The figure of a million casualties was an official wartime estimate." It came from Stimson's 1947 article; wartime estimates varied widely and were often lower, which is why historians debate it.

Exhibit four: "Japan was warned specifically about the atomic bomb." The Potsdam Declaration threatened prompt and utter destruction but did not mention a new weapon.

So, with the Expert account complete, here is the walk-to-school version for anyone who wants the story told simply.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

This part tells the same story in plain words. Think of three pictures: a set of scales, a letter from worried scientists, and a courtroom where you are the judge.

## Subtopic: Weighing the Scales

Imagine a set of scales. On one side, put the reasons people give for dropping the bombs. On the other, the reasons against.

On the "for" side: in 1945 Japan's generals refused to surrender. The fighting on Okinawa had been terrible, and America was planning to invade Japan in November. Leaders feared that an invasion would kill huge numbers of American soldiers and even more Japanese soldiers and civilians. They believed the bomb would shock Japan into giving up and end the war quickly. Prisoners of war and people in countries Japan occupied were dying every week the war went on.

On the "against" side: the bombs deliberately hit cities full of ordinary people, including children. Japan was already badly beaten, its navy gone and its people hungry. Some of its leaders were looking for a way to make peace. And there may have been other ways to end the war.

So what were those other ways?

## Subtopic: Voices That Said Wait

Not everybody agreed with dropping the bombs, even at the time. In June 1945 a group of scientists who had helped build the bomb wrote a report. They said: show Japan the bomb first by exploding it somewhere empty, in front of witnesses, instead of on a city. Another scientist, Leo Szilard, collected about seventy signatures asking the president not to use it without warning.

There were other ideas too. Japan's leaders were very worried about what would happen to their emperor. Some American officials said: promise them the emperor can stay, and they might surrender. In the end, the emperor did stay.

Others pointed out that the Soviet Union was about to join the war against Japan, which might be enough to make Japan give up. And Nagasaki was bombed only three days after Hiroshima, before Japan had time to respond.

Some historians even believe that America wanted to show the Soviet Union how powerful its new weapon was.

So who is right?

## Subtopic: You Are the Judge

In History, some questions do not have one answer that everyone agrees on. This is one of them. Historians agree on what happened. They disagree on why, and whether it was right.

Your job is to be like a fair judge. Listen to both sides. Look at the evidence: dates, numbers, and what people said. Ask who wrote each source and why. For example, a former American leader wrote in 1947 that the bombs saved a million American casualties, but he was defending a decision he helped make.

Then make up your own mind, and explain your reasons clearly. You can argue that the bombs were justified or that they were not. What matters is that you give evidence for your view and show you understand the other side.

Here is the line to remember: in a debate, the strongest answer is not the loudest one, but the one that weighs the evidence fairly and explains its judgement.
