### subtopic_1 — The Pacific War in 1945
**Assistant:** Why did Japan refuse to surrender before August 1945?

**Tutor:** By the middle of 1945 Japan had clearly lost the war in military terms. Its navy and air force were largely destroyed, its cities were being burned, and the blockade was causing hunger. Yet its leaders did not surrender, for several reasons. First, Japan's military leaders, who dominated the government, held to a code that regarded surrender as deeply shameful. They believed that if they made an Allied invasion of the home islands extremely costly, in a final decisive battle, the Allies might accept a negotiated peace on better terms. They were preparing about two million soldiers, thousands of kamikaze aircraft and armed civilians to resist. Second, the Allied demand was for unconditional surrender, and Japanese leaders feared what this would mean for the emperor, who was regarded as sacred and as the centre of the national identity. They wanted guarantees that the imperial system would survive, that Japan would not be occupied, and that they themselves would handle disarmament and any war crimes trials. Third, some Japanese leaders hoped that the Soviet Union, which was not yet at war with Japan, would act as a mediator to negotiate peace with the United States and Britain, and Japanese diplomats were making approaches to Moscow in July 1945. Fourth, the government was divided between those who wanted to seek peace and those determined to fight, and decisions required consensus. These factors help explain why it took the atomic bombs and the Soviet entry into the war to force a decision.

### subtopic_2 — The Manhattan Project and the First Atomic Bomb
**Assistant:** What did the atomic bomb do to the people of Hiroshima?

**Tutor:** The effects came in several stages, and survivors' testimonies are among the most important primary sources for understanding them. The first was the flash and heat. The bomb produced a fireball with a surface temperature of several thousand degrees, and the intense heat lasted only a few seconds but caused severe burns to exposed people more than three kilometres away; near the hypocentre, people were killed instantly, and some left only shadows on stone steps. The second was the blast: a shock wave and winds of enormous force flattened wooden buildings across most of the city, killing and trapping people under collapsed houses, and sending glass and debris flying. The third was fire: hundreds of fires merged into a firestorm that burned for hours, and many people trapped in the ruins burned to death; survivors fled to the rivers to escape the heat, and many drowned. The fourth was radiation. In the days and weeks that followed, people who seemed uninjured fell ill with radiation sickness: nausea, hair loss, bleeding gums, purple spots on the skin and fatal infections. Doctors did not understand what was happening, and most of the city's doctors and nurses had been killed or injured. Over the following decades, survivors had higher rates of leukaemia and other cancers, and many faced discrimination, as people feared radiation was contagious or would affect their children. By the end of 1945, about 140 000 people in Hiroshima had died.

### subtopic_3 — Hiroshima and Nagasaki
**Assistant:** How important was the Soviet invasion of Manchuria in ending the war?

**Tutor:** Historians increasingly recognise that the Soviet entry into the war was a major cause of Japan's surrender, alongside the atomic bombs, although they disagree about which was decisive. The Soviet Union had promised at Yalta to join the war against Japan within three months of Germany's defeat, and it kept that promise exactly: Germany surrendered on 8 May, and the Soviet Union declared war on 8 August 1945. Early on 9 August, more than a million Soviet soldiers with thousands of tanks and aircraft attacked Japanese forces in Manchuria, northern China, and quickly overwhelmed them. This had several important effects. It ended Japan's diplomatic hope that the Soviet Union would mediate peace on better terms. It showed that Japan now faced enemies on all sides, with no prospect of help. It raised the fear that the Soviet Union might occupy part of Japan itself, as it soon did with the southern Kuril Islands and southern Sakhalin, and that communism might threaten the emperor. Some Japanese leaders feared Soviet occupation more than American occupation. The historian Tsuyoshi Hasegawa has argued that the Soviet entry was more decisive than the bombs in persuading Japan's leaders to surrender. Others argue that the bombs gave the emperor and peace faction a reason to overrule the military. Most historians conclude that the combination of the bombs, the Soviet attack and the hopeless military situation forced the decision.

### subtopic_4 — The Beginning of the Nuclear Age and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why is South Africa's nuclear history unusual?

**Tutor:** South Africa is the only country in the world that developed its own nuclear weapons and then voluntarily dismantled all of them. South Africa had large uranium deposits, which were mined from the 1950s, partly to supply the American and British nuclear programmes, and it developed a nuclear research programme, including the Pelindaba research centre near Pretoria. In the 1970s, the apartheid government, increasingly isolated internationally and fearing threats from Soviet-backed forces in Angola and elsewhere in Southern Africa, began a secret programme to build nuclear weapons. By the late 1980s it had built six atomic bombs, each roughly comparable in power to the Hiroshima bomb, and was working on a seventh. The programme was kept so secret that very few people knew about it. In 1977 Soviet and American satellites detected preparations for a possible test site in the Kalahari Desert, and international pressure forced South Africa to abandon it. In 1989, as the Cold War ended, Cuban troops left Angola and Namibia moved towards independence, President F. W. de Klerk ordered the weapons to be dismantled. South Africa joined the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty in 1991, and in 1993 de Klerk publicly revealed the programme to Parliament. International inspectors confirmed the dismantling. Some historians suggest that one motive was to prevent a future ANC-led government from inheriting nuclear weapons. Today South Africa supports nuclear disarmament and is a party to the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons, and its only nuclear power station, Koeberg, generates electricity.
