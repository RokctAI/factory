# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the basic measures that geographers use to describe population change. The anchor is South Africa's census of 2022, which counted about sixty-two million people. The governing idea is that population change can be measured precisely with rates per thousand people, which allow places of very different sizes to be compared fairly, and that the gap between the birth rate and the death rate, together with migration, decides whether a population grows, stays steady or shrinks.

## Subtopic: Population and the Census

A population is the total number of people living in a particular area at a particular time, whether a village, a city, a country or the whole world. The study of population is called demography, and the people who study it are demographers.

Population information comes mainly from a census, an official count of every person in a country on a particular night, which also collects information such as age, sex, language, education, employment, housing and access to water and electricity. South Africa's censuses after democracy were held in 1996, 2001, 2011 and 2022, run by the national statistics agency, Statistics South Africa, known as Stats SA. The 2022 census counted about sixty-two million people. Between censuses, Stats SA publishes yearly mid-year population estimates.

Population information also comes from the registration of births and deaths with the Department of Home Affairs, and from surveys of samples of households.

Accurate population data matters. Governments use it to plan schools, clinics, hospitals, houses, water, electricity and roads, to divide money between provinces and municipalities, and to draw voting boundaries. Businesses use it to decide where to build shops and factories.

Counting is difficult. Some people are missed, especially in remote rural areas, informal settlements, and among homeless people and people who distrust officials. Stats SA estimated that the 2022 census missed a significant share of people, and adjusted its figures accordingly. So all population figures are estimates, and it is wise to say about sixty-two million rather than an exact number.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of population and demography, the census, Stats SA, why population data matters, and why counts are estimates.

## Subtopic: Birth Rate and Death Rate

To compare populations of different sizes fairly, demographers use rates per thousand people, rather than total numbers. A country with a hundred million people will obviously have more births than one with one million; rates show how common births and deaths are.

The crude birth rate is the number of live births in a year for every thousand people in the population. The formula is: number of births in a year, divided by the total population, multiplied by one thousand.

Worked example. A town of fifty thousand people records one thousand births in a year. One thousand divided by fifty thousand is zero point zero two; times one thousand gives twenty. The birth rate is twenty per thousand.

The crude death rate is the number of deaths in a year for every thousand people. The formula is: number of deaths in a year, divided by the total population, multiplied by one thousand.

Worked example. The same town records four hundred deaths. Four hundred divided by fifty thousand is zero point zero zero eight; times one thousand gives eight. The death rate is eight per thousand.

The word crude means that the rates are based on the whole population, without considering its age structure.

Typical values: the world birth rate is about seventeen per thousand and the death rate about eight. South Africa's birth rate is about nineteen per thousand and its death rate about nine to ten. Some countries in West and Central Africa, such as Niger, have birth rates over forty. Many European countries and Japan have birth rates below ten, and some have more deaths than births.

Birth rates are generally high where many people are poor and rural, where children work on farms and support parents in old age, where many children die young, and where women have less access to education and family planning. Birth rates fall as education, especially of girls, health care and urbanisation increase.

The questions for this section are with you now: why rates per thousand are used, the formulas, worked examples, typical values, and factors affecting birth rates.

## Subtopic: Natural Increase and Growth Rate

The natural increase is the difference between the birth rate and the death rate. If births are greater than deaths, the population grows naturally; if deaths are greater, it shrinks, called a natural decrease.

Natural increase per thousand equals birth rate minus death rate. For the town above: twenty minus eight equals twelve per thousand.

Rates per thousand are usually changed into percentages, because growth rates are normally given as percentages. To change a rate per thousand into a percentage, divide by ten. Twelve per thousand is one point two percent. So the town's natural growth rate is one point two percent a year.

Natural increase ignores migration, the movement of people into or out of an area. People moving in are immigrants, or in-migrants within a country; people moving out are emigrants, or out-migrants. The full population growth rate includes both natural increase and net migration, the difference between people arriving and people leaving. For a country such as South Africa, which receives migrants from neighbouring countries and loses some emigrants, migration makes a difference. For cities such as Johannesburg and Cape Town, migration from other provinces is a major cause of growth.

A useful idea is the doubling time, the number of years it takes a population to double at a steady growth rate. A rough rule is to divide seventy by the growth rate in percent. At two percent a year, a population doubles in about thirty-five years; at one percent, in about seventy years; at three percent, in about twenty-three years.

The world population is growing by just under one percent a year, slower than the peak of about two percent in the 1960s. South Africa's population is growing by roughly one to one and a half percent a year.

The questions for this section are with you now: natural increase, converting rates to percentages, migration and the full growth rate, and doubling time.

## Subtopic: Reading Population Figures

Population rates allow geographers to compare countries and describe their situation.

Consider three examples, using rounded recent figures. Niger has a birth rate of over forty per thousand and a death rate of about eight, so its natural increase is over thirty per thousand, more than three percent a year; its population is doubling in less than twenty-five years, which puts great pressure on schools, food and jobs. South Africa has a birth rate of about nineteen and a death rate of about nine to ten, so its natural increase is about one percent, a moderate rate. Japan has a birth rate of about seven and a death rate of about thirteen, so it has a natural decrease, and its population is shrinking and growing older.

These differences reflect each country's level of development, which later lessons explore. A rapidly growing population is young, with many children, and needs many schools and, later, many jobs. A shrinking population is old, with many retired people, and needs care for the elderly and workers to support them.

Rates also differ within a country. In South Africa, birth rates are higher in more rural provinces such as Limpopo and the Eastern Cape than in more urban provinces such as Gauteng and the Western Cape, which grow mainly by migration.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: comparing total numbers of births instead of rates. Two: forgetting to multiply by a thousand. Three: confusing per thousand with percent. Four: thinking a high birth rate always means fast growth; if the death rate is also high, growth can be slow, as it was for most of human history.

Layout for full marks in a rate calculation: write the formula, substitute the numbers, show the answer with its unit, per thousand or percent, and interpret what it means.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing Niger, South Africa and Japan, what fast and slow growth mean, differences within South Africa, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Counting Everyone

A population is all the people living in a place. To know how many, a country holds a census: it tries to count everyone on one night and asks questions about their lives.

South Africa's 2022 census counted about sixty-two million people. Stats SA runs it. The government uses the numbers to plan schools, clinics, houses and water.

Some people are always missed, so every number is an estimate.

## Subtopic: Per Thousand

To compare a big country with a small one, we count births and deaths for every thousand people.

Birth rate: births in a year, divided by the population, times a thousand. A town of fifty thousand with a thousand births has a birth rate of twenty per thousand.

Death rate: same idea with deaths. Four hundred deaths in that town gives eight per thousand.

Natural increase: births minus deaths. Twenty minus eight is twelve per thousand. Divide by ten for a percentage: one point two percent a year.

## Subtopic: Fast, Slow and Shrinking

Some countries grow fast. Niger has over forty births per thousand people, so it grows more than three percent a year and doubles in under twenty-five years. It needs lots of new schools.

South Africa grows at about one percent a year.

Japan has fewer births than deaths, so its population is shrinking and getting older.

People moving in and out, called migration, also change population. Gauteng grows a lot because people move there.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A census counts the population. Birth and death rates are counted per thousand people. Natural increase is the birth rate minus the death rate, and dividing by ten gives a percentage. Add migration for the full growth rate. Countries grow fast, slowly, or shrink, and each needs different services.
