# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines two key indicators of health and development, infant mortality and life expectancy. The anchor is a striking fact: in some countries today, about one baby in twenty dies before the age of one, while in others it is fewer than one in three hundred. The governing idea is that these two measures reflect the conditions people live in: clean water, sanitation, nutrition, health care and education, and that they can change quickly when those conditions improve or worsen.

## Subtopic: The Infant Mortality Rate

The infant mortality rate is the number of babies who die before their first birthday for every thousand live births in a year. Note that it is calculated per thousand live births, not per thousand people in the whole population, which makes it different from the crude death rate.

The formula is: number of deaths of infants under one year old in a year, divided by the number of live births in that year, multiplied by one thousand.

Worked example. In a district, there are four thousand live births in a year, and one hundred infants die before the age of one. One hundred divided by four thousand is zero point zero two five; times one thousand gives twenty-five. The infant mortality rate is twenty-five per thousand live births.

A related measure is the under-five mortality rate, the number of children who die before their fifth birthday per thousand live births. The United Nations uses it to track progress in child health.

Typical values. The world infant mortality rate has fallen from about one hundred and forty per thousand in 1950 to about twenty-eight today. In wealthy countries such as Japan, Sweden and Finland it is about two. South Africa's infant mortality rate is about twenty-four per thousand, much lower than in the early 2000s, when it was over fifty. In some countries in West and Central Africa it is still over sixty.

Infant mortality is considered one of the best single indicators of a society's health and development, because babies are especially vulnerable to poor living conditions. When water is unsafe, food is scarce, or health care is lacking, babies die first.

## Subtopic: Why Infants Die and How to Prevent It

The main causes of infant deaths differ between poorer and richer countries.

In poorer communities, many infant deaths are caused by conditions that are preventable or treatable. Problems around birth, such as premature birth, difficult deliveries and infections, cause many deaths in the first month. Diarrhoea, caused by contaminated water, poor sanitation and unsafe food, dehydrates babies quickly. Pneumonia and other respiratory infections, made worse by smoke from cooking fires indoors, are major killers. Malnutrition weakens babies and makes infections deadlier. Malaria kills many infants in tropical Africa. In South Africa, HIV passed from mother to child was a major cause of infant deaths in the early 2000s.

Effective solutions are often simple and cheap. Care during pregnancy and birth by trained health workers, in clinics or with midwives, saves many newborns. Clean water and sanitation prevent diarrhoea. Oral rehydration solution, a mixture of clean water, sugar and salt, treats dehydration and has saved millions of children's lives. Breastfeeding protects babies against infection. Immunisation protects against measles, whooping cough, pneumococcal pneumonia and rotavirus diarrhoea; South Africa's Road to Health booklet records each child's vaccinations and growth. Mosquito nets prevent malaria. Treating pregnant mothers who are HIV-positive with antiretroviral medicines prevents most transmission to babies.

Education of mothers is one of the strongest factors of all: children of mothers with more schooling are much more likely to survive, because educated mothers are more likely to use clinics, recognise danger signs and know how to prevent illness.

Social grants also help. South Africa's Child Support Grant, paid to caregivers of millions of children, helps families buy food.

The questions for this section are with you now: causes of infant deaths, low-cost solutions, immunisation, the role of mothers' education, and social grants.

## Subtopic: Life Expectancy

Life expectancy at birth is the average number of years a newborn baby can be expected to live, if the death rates of that time stay the same throughout its life. It is an average for a whole population, not a prediction for any individual.

Life expectancy is strongly affected by infant and child mortality. If many children die very young, the average is pulled down a great deal, even if adults who survive childhood often live to sixty or seventy. That is why, in the past, life expectancy of thirty or forty years did not mean that most adults died at thirty or forty; it meant that very many children died.

World life expectancy has risen dramatically: from about thirty years in 1900, to about forty-six in 1950, to about seventy-three today. Countries with the highest life expectancy, such as Japan, Switzerland and Australia, reach about eighty-three to eighty-five years. The lowest, in some countries in Africa, are around fifty-five to sixty.

Women generally live longer than men, by about five years worldwide. Reasons include biological differences and the fact that men are more likely to smoke, drink heavily, work in dangerous jobs, be involved in violence and road accidents, and visit doctors less often.

South Africa's life expectancy tells a dramatic story. It was about sixty-two years in the early 1990s. Then the HIV and AIDS epidemic caused it to fall to about fifty-three by around 2005. After the government began providing free antiretroviral treatment in 2004, and greatly expanded it, life expectancy recovered to about sixty-five years before COVID-19, dipped during the pandemic, and has since recovered again, with women living to about sixty-six or sixty-seven and men to about sixty-one or sixty-two.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of life expectancy, why child deaths affect it so much, world trends, differences between women and men, and South Africa's story.

## Subtopic: Using the Indicators

Infant mortality and life expectancy are used together with birth and death rates to compare countries and to measure progress.

Countries are often grouped by their level of development. In more economically developed countries, birth rates and death rates are low, infant mortality is very low and life expectancy is high. In less economically developed countries, birth rates are higher, infant mortality is higher and life expectancy lower. But these are broad patterns, with many exceptions, and countries change. South Africa, with large differences between rich and poor, sits in the middle on most measures.

The indicators also show differences within countries. In South Africa, infant mortality has historically been higher in poorer rural provinces such as the Eastern Cape, Limpopo and KwaZulu-Natal than in the Western Cape and Gauteng, and higher in households without piped water and flush toilets. Life expectancy has been lower in the poorest communities. These differences reflect inequalities in income, services and health care, many of them rooted in apartheid.

Governments and international organisations set goals using these indicators. The United Nations Sustainable Development Goals, adopted in 2015, include ending preventable deaths of newborns and children under five by 2030 and ensuring healthy lives for all. Progress is measured with exactly these numbers.

When reading population data, check the date and the source, because figures change and different organisations make different estimates.

Three: thinking low life expectancy in the past meant there were no old people. Four: comparing figures from different years as if they were the same year.

## Subtopic: Babies Under One

The infant mortality rate counts how many babies die before their first birthday for every thousand babies born alive.

If four thousand babies are born and a hundred die before turning one, the rate is twenty-five per thousand.

In 1950, about a hundred and forty babies out of every thousand in the world died before age one. Today it is about twenty-eight. In South Africa it is about twenty-four. In Japan, about two.

## Subtopic: Keeping Babies Alive

Most baby deaths in poor places can be prevented. Babies die from problems at birth, from diarrhoea caused by dirty water, from chest infections, from hunger and from malaria.

Simple things save lives: a trained nurse or midwife at the birth, clean water and toilets, breastfeeding, vaccinations, mosquito nets, and a drink of clean water mixed with sugar and salt for a baby with diarrhoea. Medicine stops mothers with HIV passing it to their babies.

And when mothers have more education, their babies are much more likely to survive.

## Subtopic: How Long We Live

Life expectancy is how long, on average, a baby born today can expect to live. Around the world it has gone from about thirty years in 1900 to about seventy-three today. Women usually live a few years longer than men.

In South Africa, life expectancy fell to about fifty-three in the early 2000s because of AIDS. Free HIV medicine helped it climb back to the mid-sixties.

Poorer places usually have more baby deaths and shorter lives. These numbers help governments see where help is needed.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Infant mortality is deaths before age one per thousand live births, and it shows how healthy living conditions are. Most infant deaths are preventable with clean water, health care, vaccinations and education. Life expectancy is the average lifespan of a newborn; it has risen worldwide, and in South Africa it fell with AIDS and recovered with treatment.
