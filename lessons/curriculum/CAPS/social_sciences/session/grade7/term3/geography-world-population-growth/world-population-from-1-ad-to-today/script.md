# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the pattern of world population growth over the last two thousand years and develops the skill of interpreting a line graph. The anchor is the famous J-shaped curve of world population, flat for most of its length and then rising almost straight up. The governing idea is that the shape of the curve reflects the balance between births and deaths, and that the dramatic rise of recent centuries came mainly from falling death rates, not rising birth rates, followed more recently by falling birth rates that are now slowing growth.

## Subtopic: Reading a Line Graph

A line graph shows how something changes over time. Before interpreting any graph, read its parts.

The title says what the graph shows, for example, World population growth, 1 AD to 2022.

The horizontal axis, the x-axis, usually shows time, here years from 1 AD to the present. Check whether the intervals are even. On many population graphs, the early centuries are squeezed together, or the scale changes, so look carefully at the labels.

The vertical axis, the y-axis, shows the quantity, here the world population, usually in billions. One billion is one thousand million.

The line connects the data points. Where the line is flat or almost flat, the population was changing very little. Where the line slopes gently upwards, the population was growing slowly. Where it rises steeply, it was growing fast. A line that slopes downwards shows a decrease.

To read a value, find the year on the x-axis, go up to the line, then across to the y-axis and read the population. To find when the population reached a certain size, do the reverse.

When describing a graph, follow three steps: give the general trend, mention any important changes in the trend and when they happened, and quote figures from the graph, with units, as evidence. For example: The world population grew very slowly from about three hundred million in 1 AD to about one billion in 1800, then rose increasingly rapidly to about eight billion in 2022.

When explaining a graph, give reasons for the trends, using the word because.

The questions for this section are with you now: the parts of a line graph, reading values, the meaning of the slope, and how to describe and explain a graph.

## Subtopic: The Long, Slow Stage

For most of the period from 1 AD to about 1750, world population grew very slowly. In 1 AD, the world had an estimated two hundred and fifty to three hundred million people, fewer than the population of the United States today. By the year 1000 it had reached perhaps three hundred million, and by about 1500 perhaps four hundred and fifty to five hundred million. On a graph covering two thousand years, this part of the line is almost flat.

The reason for the slow growth was that both birth rates and death rates were high. Most families had many children, but many children died young, and adults died from infectious diseases, famines, wars and accidents. Life expectancy was only about twenty-five to thirty-five years, mainly because of high child mortality. Births and deaths were nearly balanced, so the natural increase was very small.

Sometimes the population fell. The most dramatic example was the Black Death, an epidemic of plague that swept across Asia, the Middle East, North Africa and Europe from 1347 to about 1351, killing perhaps a third to a half of Europe's population. In the Americas, after Europeans arrived in 1492, diseases such as smallpox, together with violence and forced labour, killed a very large proportion of the indigenous population. In Africa, the slave trades removed millions of young people. Famines and wars also caused falls.

These figures are estimates, made by historians and demographers from tax records, archaeological evidence, land use and other clues, because there were no censuses for most of the world. Different researchers give somewhat different numbers, but they all agree on the pattern of slow growth.

The questions for this section are with you now: world population in 1 AD and 1500, why growth was slow, falls in population such as the Black Death, and why the early figures are estimates.

## Subtopic: The Population Explosion

From about 1750 the line on the graph begins to curve upwards, and from about 1900 it rises steeply. The world reached one billion people around 1804, two billion in 1927, three billion in 1960, four billion in 1974, five billion in 1987, six billion in 1999, seven billion in 2011, and eight billion on 15 November 2022, a date chosen by the United Nations. Notice how the time needed to add each billion shrank: over a hundred years from one to two billion, then thirty-three years, then fourteen, thirteen, twelve and twelve again.

The growth rate peaked in the late 1960s at a little over two percent a year, when people spoke of a population explosion.

The main cause of this rapid growth was a fall in death rates while birth rates stayed high for a long time. Death rates fell because of a series of developments, studied in more detail later this term. The Agricultural Revolution in Europe, from the eighteenth century, produced more food. Better transport moved food to where it was needed. Improved sanitation, clean water and sewage systems in the nineteenth century reduced diseases such as cholera and typhoid. Vaccines, starting with smallpox vaccination in 1796, and later antibiotics, such as penicillin, widely used from the 1940s, controlled infectious diseases. Canned food and refrigeration preserved food safely. These changes spread first in Europe and North America, and after 1945 rapidly across Asia, Latin America and Africa.

Because birth rates fell later than death rates, the gap between them was large, and the population grew rapidly. This pattern of falling death rates followed later by falling birth rates is called the demographic transition.

The questions for this section are with you now: the dates of each billion, the shrinking time between billions, the peak growth rate, and why death rates fell.

## Subtopic: Slowing Growth and the Future

Since the late 1960s, the growth rate of the world's population has fallen, to just under one percent a year today, because birth rates have fallen in most of the world. The global fertility rate, the average number of children per woman, fell from about five in 1950 to about two point three today. On a graph, the line is still rising, because the population is so large that even a lower percentage adds tens of millions of people each year, but the curve is beginning to bend and become less steep.

Birth rates have fallen because of education, especially for girls, access to family planning, more people living in cities, better child survival, which means families no longer need many children to ensure some survive, pensions and changing ideas about family size.

The United Nations projects that the world population will continue to grow, but more slowly, reaching about nine point seven billion around 2050 and perhaps peaking at about ten point three billion in the 2080s before levelling off or slowly declining. Projections are not certain; they depend on assumptions about future birth and death rates.

Most future growth will happen in Africa, especially sub-Saharan Africa, where birth rates are still relatively high and the population is very young. By 2050 about a quarter of the world's people may live in Africa. Meanwhile, many countries in Europe and East Asia, such as Japan, Italy and China, are already shrinking or will soon begin to shrink, with ageing populations.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the population explosion was caused by people having more children than before. Two: describing a graph without quoting figures and units. Three: ignoring the scale on the x-axis. Four: treating projections as certain facts.

The questions for this section are with you now: the slowing growth rate, falling fertility, United Nations projections, where future growth will happen, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Reading the Line

A line graph shows how something changes over time. Years go along the bottom. The number of people goes up the side, in billions. A billion is a thousand million.

Where the line is flat, the population hardly changed. Where it climbs steeply, it grew fast.

To describe the graph, say what happened, when it changed, and give numbers with units: about one billion in 1800, about eight billion in 2022.

## Subtopic: Slow, Then Fast

In the year 1 AD there were only about two hundred and fifty to three hundred million people in the whole world. For seventeen hundred years numbers grew very slowly, because although families had many children, many died young, and diseases, famines and wars killed many people. The Black Death in the 1300s killed perhaps a third or more of Europe's people.

Then, from about 1750, things changed. More food, clean water, toilets and sewers, vaccines and medicines meant far fewer people died, while families still had many children. So the population shot up: one billion around 1804, two billion in 1927, and eight billion in 2022.

## Subtopic: Slowing Down

Now families are having fewer children, because of education, especially for girls, family planning, living in cities, and because most children now survive. So growth is slowing, to just under one percent a year.

Experts think the world will reach about nine point seven billion people by 2050 and stop growing later this century. Most new growth will be in Africa, while countries like Japan and Italy are already shrinking.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A line graph of world population is almost flat from 1 AD to about 1750, because births and deaths were both high, then rises steeply as death rates fell. The world reached one billion around 1804 and eight billion in 2022. Birth rates are now falling, so growth is slowing, and most future growth will be in Africa.
