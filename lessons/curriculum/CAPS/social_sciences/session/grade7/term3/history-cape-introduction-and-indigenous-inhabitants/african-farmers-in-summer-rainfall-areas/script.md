# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines where African farmers settled in southern Africa and why. The anchor is a rainfall map of South Africa, with the five hundred millimetre line running roughly from north to south through the middle of the country. The governing idea is that geography shaped history: African farmers, whose main crops of sorghum and millet needed summer rain, settled east of this line, while the dry west and the winter rainfall south-western Cape remained the land of San hunter-gatherers and Khoikhoi herders.

## Subtopic: The Arrival of African Farmers

About two thousand years ago, communities of farmers who spoke Bantu languages began moving into southern Africa from the north, as part of a long, gradual spread of farming and iron-working across central, eastern and southern Africa over many centuries. Historians and archaeologists call these communities African farmers or Iron Age farmers, because they made tools and weapons from iron.

By about 250 to 300 AD, farming communities were living in what is now Limpopo, Mpumalanga and KwaZulu-Natal. Over the following centuries, they spread across the eastern half of South Africa. Archaeological sites, such as those in the Lydenburg area, where famous clay heads were found, and Mapungubwe in the Limpopo valley, which flourished between about 1220 and 1290, show their skills and growing wealth.

These farmers were the ancestors of many of today's South Africans, including Nguni-speaking peoples, such as the Xhosa, Zulu, Swazi and Ndebele, and Sotho-Tswana peoples, such as the Basotho, Batswana and Bapedi, and others such as the Venda and Tsonga.

African farmers lived in settled villages, which they could remain in for years, unlike mobile hunter-gatherers. They grew crops, kept cattle, sheep and goats, made pottery, and smelted iron and copper. They traded with each other and, through ports on the east coast, with the Indian Ocean world.

They also interacted with the San and Khoikhoi, through trade, marriage and sometimes conflict. The click sounds in Xhosa and Zulu came from contact with Khoe and San languages.

## Subtopic: Rainfall and the 500 mm Line

To understand where African farmers settled, look at a rainfall map of South Africa. Rainfall decreases from east to west. The east coast and the eastern escarpment receive the most rain, often over eight hundred millimetres a year, while the west, including the Karoo and the Namaqualand coast, is dry, with less than two hundred and fifty millimetres in many areas.

Most of South Africa receives its rain in summer, from about October to March, often in thunderstorms. Only the south-western Cape, around Cape Town, receives most of its rain in winter, from cold fronts moving in from the Atlantic Ocean. The southern Cape coast has rain all year round.

The five hundred millimetre isohyet, a line on a map joining places with the same average rainfall, runs roughly north to south through the middle of the country, near the western Free State and the eastern edge of the Karoo, curving towards the Eastern Cape. East of this line, there is generally enough summer rain for dryland crop farming, which means growing crops using rain alone, without irrigation. West of it, it is generally too dry for reliable crop farming without irrigation, though grazing is possible.

So African farmers settled mainly east of the five hundred millimetre line, in the summer rainfall areas. In the south, the westernmost African farming communities were the Xhosa, who lived in what is now the Eastern Cape, roughly as far west as the area between the Kei and the Fish rivers by the eighteenth century, though some lived further west among Khoikhoi.

## Subtopic: Sorghum and Millet

The main crops of African farmers were sorghum and millet, grains that originally came from Africa. Sorghum, known as amabele in isiZulu and isiXhosa, and mabele in Sesotho, was domesticated in Africa, probably in the region of Sudan and Ethiopia, several thousand years ago. Pearl millet was domesticated in West Africa.

These crops are well suited to the summer rainfall areas. They are planted in spring and grow during the hot, wet summer, ripening in autumn. They are fairly drought-resistant, needing less water than many crops, but they still need at least about five hundred millimetres of rain falling in the growing season. They cannot grow well in the south-western Cape, where rain falls mainly in the cold winter, and summer is hot and dry.

Sorghum and millet were ground into flour and cooked as porridge, and fermented to make sorghum beer, which was important in social and religious life. Farmers also grew beans, gourds, pumpkins and melons. Women did most of the hoeing, planting, weeding and harvesting, while men cleared land, herded cattle and worked iron. Iron hoes made cultivation easier.

Grain was stored in granaries, raised to keep out damp and animals, or in underground pits within the cattle enclosure. A good harvest meant food security through the winter.

Maize, which today is South Africa's staple food, came from the Americas. It was brought by Portuguese traders to East and West Africa in the sixteenth century, and only became widely grown in southern Africa from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. So the farmers of earlier centuries relied on sorghum and millet.

## Subtopic: Cattle, Society and Meeting the Colony

Cattle were central to African farming societies. They provided milk, often eaten as amasi, sour milk, and meat, hides and dung for building and fuel. More importantly, cattle were a measure of wealth and status, and were used in lobola, the transfer of cattle from the groom's family to the bride's family, which created bonds between families. Cattle were also important in ceremonies honouring the ancestors.

A typical homestead was arranged in a circle, with houses around a central cattle enclosure, the kraal, where men gathered. Homesteads were grouped into chiefdoms, led by a chief who allocated land to families, settled disputes with advisers and led in war. Land was held by the community, not bought and sold by individuals. This understanding of land would clash with European ideas of private ownership.

Grazing land and water were vital. Cattle needed good summer grass, which grew in the summer rainfall areas, and herders sometimes moved cattle seasonally between sweetveld and sourveld grazing.

By the eighteenth century, the Xhosa chiefdoms in the Eastern Cape were the African farmers closest to the expanding Cape Colony. When trekboers, Dutch-speaking stock farmers, moved east in the 1770s, they met the Xhosa in the Zuurveld, the area between the Bushmans and Fish rivers. Both groups were cattle farmers who needed the same grazing land and water, which led to trade, but also to competition and the first frontier wars from 1779, studied next term.

The east of South Africa gets more rain than the west, and most of it falls in summer. Only the area around Cape Town gets its rain in winter. The farmers' crops needed at least about five hundred millimetres of summer rain a year. So they settled in the wetter east, while the dry west and the winter-rain Cape were home to the San and Khoikhoi.
