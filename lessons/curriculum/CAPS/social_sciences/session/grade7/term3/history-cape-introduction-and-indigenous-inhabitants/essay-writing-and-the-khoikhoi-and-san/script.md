# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the colonisation of the Cape and two foundations for the term: the structure of a history essay, and the indigenous people of the Cape before and during the arrival of Europeans. The anchor is the Cape Peninsula in the early 1600s, where Khoikhoi herders grazed cattle and sheep and San people hunted and gathered in the mountains. The governing idea is that the Cape was not empty when Europeans arrived; it had been home to people for thousands of years, and the story of colonisation must be told from their point of view as well.

## Subtopic: Introducing Colonisation

Colonisation is the process in which people from one country settle in another territory and take control of its land, government and resources, usually without the consent of the people who already live there. The territory under control is called a colony, and the people who settle are colonists or settlers.

From the fifteenth century, European countries, including Portugal, Spain, the Netherlands, Britain and France, sailed across the oceans to trade and conquer. Their ships went around the southern tip of Africa on the sea route to the riches of India and the East Indies, now Indonesia, especially spices such as pepper, cloves and nutmeg.

In 1652 the Dutch East India Company, known by its Dutch initials as the VOC, set up a refreshment station at the Cape. What began as a small post to supply ships grew into a colony, as settlers spread inland, slaves were imported, and the indigenous people lost their land and independence. This term we follow that story through the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

Historians use many sources: the diary, or journal, kept by Jan van Riebeeck, the first commander; VOC records, court cases, letters, travellers' accounts and maps; archaeological evidence; and oral traditions. Because most written sources were produced by colonists, historians must read them critically, asking whose point of view they show, and look for the voices of the Khoikhoi, San and enslaved people, which are often missing or distorted.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of colonisation, why European ships passed the Cape, the VOC in 1652, and why sources must be read critically.

## Subtopic: How to Write a History Essay

A history essay answers a question with a clear argument, supported by evidence, organised into paragraphs. At Grade 7 level, an essay usually has three parts.

The introduction is one paragraph. It answers the question directly in one or two sentences, stating your main argument, sometimes called your line of argument, and briefly says which main points you will discuss. For example, for the question Why did the VOC settle at the Cape in 1652?, an introduction might say: The VOC settled at the Cape in 1652 to supply its ships with fresh food and water on the long voyage to the East, because the Cape had a good harbour, a mild climate and Khoikhoi herders with cattle to trade.

The body has several paragraphs, usually three or more. Each paragraph deals with one main point. It begins with a topic sentence that states the point, then gives evidence: facts, dates, names, examples and quotations from sources. It ends by linking the point back to the question. Use linking words such as firstly, furthermore, however, as a result and therefore.

The conclusion is one paragraph that sums up your argument and answers the question again, without adding new information.

Good essays are written in the past tense, in your own words, in formal language, and in chronological order where suitable. Plan before you write: underline key words in the question, brainstorm facts, organise them into paragraphs, then write and check.

## Subtopic: The San

Revise from Grade 5. The San are the descendants of the earliest people of southern Africa. Their ancestors lived here for tens of thousands of years. They were hunter-gatherers, living in small, mobile groups of related families, moving with the seasons to find food and water. Colonists often called them Bushmen, a term many find offensive, and the Khoikhoi called them San, which some say means foragers. Today many prefer the names of particular groups, such as the !Xam, ju/'hoansi or ‡Khomani.

Men hunted antelope and other animals with bows and poisoned arrows, and set traps. Women gathered most of the food: roots, bulbs, berries, nuts, insects, honey and eggs, using digging sticks weighted with bored stones. They knew the land, plants and animals intimately, and shared food within the group.

The San had rich spiritual beliefs, connected to the eland and the rain. Healers entered a trance through dance to heal and to make rain. Their rock paintings and engravings, found across southern Africa, in places such as the Cederberg and the Drakensberg, record animals, people, ceremonies and spiritual experiences, and some are thousands of years old.

In the seventeenth century, San groups lived in the mountains of the Western Cape, the Karoo and further inland. As colonists and their livestock moved into hunting grounds, the San lost access to game and water, and resisted. In the eighteenth century, many were killed by colonial commandos, and children were captured and forced to work on farms.

## Subtopic: The Khoikhoi

The Khoikhoi, which means people of people or real people, were herders who kept fat-tailed sheep and long-horned cattle. They had acquired livestock about two thousand years ago. They lived in larger groups than the San, organised into clans and chiefdoms under leaders. Among the Khoikhoi groups near the Cape Peninsula in the 1600s were the Goringhaiqua, the Gorachouqua and the Goringhaicona, a small group of strandlopers who lived along the shore and whose leader was Autshumato, known to the Dutch as Harry, who had learnt some English and acted as interpreter. Further inland were larger groups such as the Cochoqua and the Chainouqua.

Cattle were the Khoikhoi's wealth. They provided milk, which was a main food, meat on special occasions, and hides for clothing and bags. Herders moved with their animals between seasonal grazing areas, for example to the Cape Peninsula in summer. They lived in portable domed huts made of reed mats over a frame of bent poles, which could be packed onto oxen and moved.

The Khoikhoi had traded with passing European ships for decades before 1652, exchanging cattle and sheep for copper, iron, beads and tobacco. At first trade was welcome, but conflicts arose when sailors took cattle by force or demanded more than the Khoikhoi wanted to sell, since cattle were not just goods but the basis of their wealth and status. In 1510, Khoikhoi warriors killed the Portuguese viceroy Francisco de Almeida and many of his men in a clash at Table Bay after a dispute over cattle.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: saying the Cape was empty before 1652. Two: confusing the hunter-gatherer San with the herding Khoikhoi. Three: writing an essay without a clear argument in the introduction.
