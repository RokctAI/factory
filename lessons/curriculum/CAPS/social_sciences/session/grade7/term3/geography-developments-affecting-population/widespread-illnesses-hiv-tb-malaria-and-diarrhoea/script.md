# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines four widespread illnesses and their effect on population. The anchor is South Africa's own experience: life expectancy fell sharply in the early 2000s because of HIV and AIDS, and then rose again as treatment became available. The governing idea is that illnesses affect populations mainly by raising death rates and lowering life expectancy, that they hit the poor hardest, and that most of these deaths can be prevented with knowledge, clean water, medicine and good health services.

## Subtopic: HIV and AIDS

HIV stands for human immunodeficiency virus. It attacks the immune system, the body's defence against infection, by destroying certain white blood cells. Without treatment, after several years the immune system becomes so weak that the person develops AIDS, acquired immune deficiency syndrome, and falls ill with infections that a healthy body could fight off, such as tuberculosis and pneumonia.

HIV is spread through certain body fluids: through unprotected sex, from mother to baby during pregnancy, birth or breastfeeding, and through sharing needles or contact with infected blood. It is not spread by hugging, shaking hands, sharing cups or toilets, or by mosquitoes. Knowing this helps fight stigma, the unfair shame and discrimination people living with HIV sometimes face.

There is no cure yet, but antiretroviral treatment, ARVs, keeps the virus under control. A person who takes ARVs every day can live a long, healthy life, and when the virus is suppressed it is not passed on sexually. Treatment of pregnant mothers has made mother-to-child transmission rare in South Africa.

South Africa has the largest number of people living with HIV in the world, about eight million. In the late 1990s and early 2000s, before treatment was widely available, AIDS caused hundreds of thousands of deaths a year, and life expectancy fell from about sixty-two years in 1990 to around fifty-three in the mid-2000s. Many children lost parents and were raised by grandparents or in child-headed households. From 2004 the government rolled out the world's largest ARV programme, and life expectancy recovered to over sixty-five by 2019.

## Subtopic: Tuberculosis

Tuberculosis, TB, is caused by a bacterium that usually attacks the lungs. It spreads through the air when a person with active TB of the lungs coughs, sneezes or talks, and others breathe in the germs. Crowded, poorly ventilated homes, workplaces, mines and prisons help it spread.

Many people carry the TB bacterium without becoming ill; their immune system keeps it in check. But if the immune system is weakened, for example by HIV, poor nutrition, diabetes or smoking, the infection can become active. The signs include a cough lasting more than two weeks, coughing up blood, fever, night sweats and weight loss.

TB can be cured. Treatment involves taking a combination of antibiotics every day for at least six months. Stopping early is dangerous, because the bacteria may survive and become drug-resistant, which is much harder and more expensive to treat. Clinics in South Africa test and treat TB free of charge.

TB is the leading cause of death from a single infectious agent in the world, and one of the leading natural causes of death in South Africa. HIV and TB are closely linked: people living with HIV are many times more likely to develop active TB, and TB is the most common cause of death among people with HIV. Historically, TB was common among mineworkers, who worked in dusty conditions and lived in crowded hostels.

Prevention includes good ventilation and opening windows, covering coughs, early testing and completing treatment, good nutrition, and the BCG vaccine given to babies at birth, which protects young children against severe forms of TB.

## Subtopic: Malaria

Malaria is caused by a tiny parasite, Plasmodium, that is carried by female Anopheles mosquitoes. When an infected mosquito bites, the parasite enters the blood, travels to the liver, multiplies, and then attacks red blood cells. Symptoms include high fever, chills, headache, sweating and body aches, often like flu at first. Without quick treatment, malaria can cause severe illness and death, especially in young children and pregnant women.

Malaria needs warm temperatures and standing water where mosquitoes breed. It is therefore common in tropical areas. Africa carries most of the world's burden: about ninety-five percent of the more than six hundred thousand malaria deaths each year happen in Africa, most of them children under five. In South Africa, malaria occurs mainly in low-lying parts of the north-east: north-eastern KwaZulu-Natal, Mpumalanga, including parts of the Kruger National Park, and Limpopo, mainly in the rainy summer months.

Malaria can be prevented and treated. Prevention includes sleeping under insecticide-treated mosquito nets, spraying the inside walls of homes with insecticide, removing standing water where mosquitoes breed, using repellent and wearing long sleeves in the evening, and taking anti-malaria tablets when travelling to malaria areas. In recent years, the first malaria vaccines for children have begun to be used in parts of Africa. Malaria is cured with medicines, if it is diagnosed early with a quick blood test.

Malaria raises death rates, especially of young children, lowers life expectancy, and keeps families poor because sick people cannot work or go to school. South Africa has reduced malaria cases greatly through spraying and treatment, and works with Mozambique and Eswatini to control it across borders.

## Subtopic: Diarrhoea and the Overall Effect

Diarrhoea means passing loose, watery stools three or more times a day. It is usually caused by germs, viruses, bacteria or parasites, in contaminated water or food, or passed from dirty hands. Cholera and rotavirus are examples. The danger is dehydration, the loss of water and salts from the body, which can kill a small child within a day or two.

Diarrhoea is one of the leading causes of death of children under five worldwide, causing several hundred thousand deaths a year, almost all in poor communities without safe water and sanitation. In South Africa, diarrhoea deaths among children rise in the hot summer months.

Most diarrhoea can be prevented: safe drinking water, boiled or treated if necessary; proper toilets and sanitation; washing hands with soap, especially after using the toilet and before preparing food; safe food storage; breastfeeding babies; and the rotavirus vaccine, which South Africa gives to babies. Treatment is simple and cheap: oral rehydration solution, ORS, which can be made from clean water, sugar and salt, replaces lost fluids, and zinc tablets help children recover.

Looking at all four illnesses together, they affect population in several ways. They raise death rates, especially infant and child mortality. They lower life expectancy. They reduce population growth, and in the worst years of AIDS some southern African countries grew very slowly. They also have economic and social effects: sick workers, orphaned children, high health costs and lost schooling. They hit poor communities hardest, because of crowded housing, poor nutrition, lack of clean water and far-away clinics.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, in plain words, same facts: HIV and TB, malaria, and diarrhoea.

## Subtopic: HIV and TB

HIV is a virus that weakens the body's defences. It spreads through unprotected sex, from mother to baby, and through blood. You cannot get it from hugging or sharing a cup. There is no cure, but ARV pills taken every day keep people healthy. In the early 2000s many South Africans died of AIDS and life expectancy fell, but it recovered when ARVs became available.

TB is a lung disease spread through the air by coughing. People with HIV catch it more easily. TB can be cured, but you must take your pills every day for six months.

## Subtopic: Malaria

Malaria is spread by the bite of certain mosquitoes, which carry a tiny parasite. It causes a high fever and can kill, especially young children. Most malaria deaths happen in Africa. In South Africa it is found in the hot, low parts of the north-east, like north-eastern KwaZulu-Natal, Mpumalanga and Limpopo.

To prevent malaria: sleep under a treated mosquito net, spray homes, get rid of standing water and cover up in the evenings. It can be cured with medicine if it is caught early.

## Subtopic: Diarrhoea

Diarrhoea comes from germs in dirty water, food, or on unwashed hands. In small children it is dangerous because they lose water quickly and can become dehydrated. Clean water, toilets and washing hands with soap prevent it. A drink of clean water with sugar and salt, called oral rehydration solution, saves lives.

Pocket summary of the lesson. HIV and AIDS, TB, malaria and diarrhoea raise death rates, especially of children, and lower life expectancy. They hit poor communities hardest. Most of these deaths can be prevented with knowledge, clean water, nets, vaccines and medicine.
