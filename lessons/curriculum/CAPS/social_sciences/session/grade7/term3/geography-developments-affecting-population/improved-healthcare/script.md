# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines how improved healthcare affects population growth. The anchor is the local clinic, where a mother can give birth safely, a baby is weighed and immunised, a child with diarrhoea receives oral rehydration, and an adult collects ARVs or TB medicine. The governing idea is that healthcare lowers death rates, especially of mothers, babies and young children, raises life expectancy, and, through family planning and education, also helps lower birth rates.

## Subtopic: What Healthcare Includes

Healthcare means the services that prevent, diagnose and treat illness and injury, and help people stay healthy.

It is often organised in levels. Primary healthcare is the first point of contact: community health workers, mobile clinics, clinics and community health centres staffed mainly by nurses. They provide immunisation, care for pregnant women and babies, family planning, treatment of common illnesses, HIV and TB testing and treatment, and health education. Secondary healthcare is provided in district and regional hospitals, where doctors can perform operations, deliver babies with complications and care for very sick patients. Tertiary healthcare is provided in large specialist hospitals, often linked to universities, such as Chris Hani Baragwanath Hospital in Soweto, one of the largest hospitals in the world, and Groote Schuur Hospital in Cape Town, where Professor Christiaan Barnard performed the world's first human heart transplant in 1967.

In 1978, at a conference in Alma-Ata, in what is now Kazakhstan, the countries of the world agreed that primary healthcare is the key to health for all. Most of the fall in death rates around the world has come from prevention and basic treatment rather than from high-technology hospitals.

Healthcare also includes public health: the work of governments to protect whole populations through clean water, sanitation, vaccination campaigns, food safety rules, disease surveillance, and campaigns against smoking or for road safety.

## Subtopic: Mothers, Babies and Young Children

Healthcare has its biggest effect on population by saving the lives of mothers, babies and young children.

Antenatal care, care during pregnancy, checks the health of mother and baby, provides vitamins and iron, tests for HIV and other conditions, and identifies risky pregnancies early. Giving birth with a trained midwife or nurse, in a clinic or hospital, means that complications such as heavy bleeding or a baby in the wrong position can be treated in time. Maternal mortality, the death of women from causes related to pregnancy and childbirth, has fallen sharply worldwide, but it remains much higher in poor countries than in wealthy ones.

After birth, the Road to Health booklet, given to every baby in South Africa, records the child's weight, growth, immunisations and development. Regular weighing helps nurses spot malnutrition early.

Immunisation protects children against diseases that once killed many, such as measles, polio, diphtheria, whooping cough and tetanus. South Africa's Expanded Programme on Immunisation provides free vaccines, including BCG against TB at birth, rotavirus against severe diarrhoea, pneumococcal vaccine against pneumonia, and the HPV vaccine, which is offered to Grade 5 girls to protect against cervical cancer later in life.

Other simple measures save many children's lives: promoting breastfeeding, oral rehydration solution for diarrhoea, vitamin A supplements, deworming, mosquito nets in malaria areas, and treating pneumonia quickly.

Preventing mother-to-child transmission of HIV has been one of South Africa's great healthcare successes, reducing the rate of transmission to babies from about a quarter or more without treatment to below one percent of births in recent years.

## Subtopic: Family Planning and Healthcare's Effect on Births

Healthcare affects birth rates as well as death rates.

Family planning means that individuals and couples decide whether and when to have children and how many to have, and have access to information and contraception, methods of preventing pregnancy. In South Africa, contraception and family planning advice are available free at public clinics. Health workers also provide information about reproductive health and responsible decision-making.

When family planning is available, and especially when combined with better education for girls and women, birth rates usually fall. Women can complete their education and work, and families can plan for the number of children they can care for.

Improved healthcare also lowers birth rates indirectly. When parents are confident that their children will survive, because infant and child mortality have fallen, they tend to choose to have fewer children. This is part of the demographic transition: healthcare first lowers death rates, then, after a delay, birth rates also fall.

South Africa's fertility rate, the average number of children per woman, fell from about six in the 1960s to about two point three today, one of the lowest in sub-Saharan Africa, partly because of a widely available family planning service, urbanisation and education.

Teenage pregnancy remains a concern in South Africa. Youth-friendly clinic services, school health programmes and education aim to help young people stay in school and make informed decisions.

## Subtopic: Healthcare in South Africa: Progress and Challenges

Under apartheid, healthcare was divided by race, and services for Black South Africans, especially in the homelands and rural areas, were badly underfunded.

After 1994, the government focused on primary healthcare. Free healthcare was introduced for pregnant women and children under six in 1994, and free primary healthcare for everyone at public clinics in 1996. Hundreds of new clinics were built. The ARV programme from 2004 became the largest in the world. Infant and child mortality fell, and life expectancy rose from about fifty-three in the mid-2000s to over sixty-five by 2019.

Major challenges remain. About eighty percent of South Africans depend on the public health system, while most doctors work in the private sector, which serves mainly those with medical aid. Public clinics and hospitals often face long queues, staff shortages, medicine stock-outs and ageing buildings. Rural areas have far fewer doctors per person than cities. The government plans National Health Insurance to provide more equal access to care for all, although how it will be funded and run is still debated.

Lifestyle diseases, such as diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease and some cancers, are growing, linked to unhealthy diets, smoking, alcohol and lack of exercise. Violence and road accidents also cause many deaths. Healthcare must therefore deal with both infectious diseases and these newer challenges.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking healthcare affects only death rates and not birth rates. Two: believing only big hospitals save lives. Three: thinking vaccines are only for babies.

## Subtopic: Clinics and Hospitals

Healthcare is everything that keeps people healthy and treats them when they are ill. The local clinic is the first stop: nurses give vaccines, check pregnant mothers and babies, give medicine for HIV and TB, and teach people about health. Hospitals handle operations and very sick patients. Big special hospitals, like Groote Schuur in Cape Town, do complicated operations.

## Subtopic: Mothers and Babies

Check-ups during pregnancy, and giving birth with a nurse or midwife, keep mothers and babies safe. Every baby gets a Road to Health booklet that tracks their weight and vaccines. Free vaccines protect children against measles, polio, TB and severe diarrhoea. Clinics also give free family planning, so people can decide when to have children. When parents know their children will survive, they usually have fewer.

## Subtopic: Healthcare in South Africa

Under apartheid, healthcare was unequal. After 1994, free care was given to pregnant women and young children, and in 1996 clinic care became free for everyone. Many new clinics were built, and the ARV programme helped life expectancy rise again. But there are still long queues and not enough staff, especially in rural areas, and diseases like diabetes are growing.
