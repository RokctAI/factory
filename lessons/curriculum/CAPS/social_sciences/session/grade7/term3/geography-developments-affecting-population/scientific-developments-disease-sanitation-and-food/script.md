# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines scientific developments that lowered death rates. The anchor is John Snow's discovery in London in 1854 that cholera came from a contaminated water pump, a discovery that led cities to build clean water and sewage systems. The governing idea is that most of the fall in death rates came not from expensive hospitals but from simple, large-scale improvements in hygiene, clean water, vaccines and safe food, which together allowed far more people to survive childhood.

## Subtopic: Controlling Disease and Infection

For most of history, people did not know what caused infectious diseases. Many believed in miasma, bad air from rotting matter.

Vaccination was the first great breakthrough. In 1796, the English doctor Edward Jenner showed that infecting a boy with cowpox, a mild disease, protected him against smallpox, a deadly one. The word vaccine comes from the Latin word for cow. Long before this, people in parts of Africa, India and China had practised variolation, deliberately giving people a small dose of smallpox to protect them. Smallpox vaccination spread around the world, and in 1980 the World Health Organization declared smallpox the first human disease to be eradicated, completely wiped out.

In the 1860s, the French scientist Louis Pasteur showed that many diseases are caused by germs, tiny living organisms. This is called the germ theory of disease. Pasteur also developed pasteurisation, heating liquids such as milk to kill germs. Robert Koch, a German doctor, identified the germs that cause tuberculosis in 1882 and cholera in 1883.

Germ theory led to antiseptics. The British surgeon Joseph Lister began using carbolic acid to clean wounds and instruments in 1867, and deaths after surgery fell sharply. Doctors learnt to wash their hands between patients.

In 1928 Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin, the first antibiotic, a medicine that kills bacteria. It was mass-produced from the 1940s and saved millions of lives from infected wounds, pneumonia and other infections.

Vaccines against diseases such as polio, measles, diphtheria and whooping cough followed in the twentieth century.

The questions for this section are with you now: Jenner and vaccination, Pasteur and germ theory, Lister and antiseptics, and Fleming and antibiotics.

## Subtopic: Improved Sanitation and Clean Water

Sanitation means the safe disposal of human waste, sewage and rubbish, together with access to clean water.

In the nineteenth century, industrial cities in Europe grew rapidly. Workers lived in crowded slums where sewage ran in open drains and leaked into the wells and rivers used for drinking water. Diseases such as cholera, typhoid and dysentery killed huge numbers of people.

In 1854, during a cholera outbreak in Soho, London, the doctor John Snow marked the homes of people who had died on a map. He noticed that most deaths clustered around one water pump on Broad Street. He persuaded officials to remove the pump's handle, and new cases fell. Snow's map was an early example of using geography to solve a health problem, and it showed that cholera spread through contaminated water, not bad air.

In 1858, during a hot summer, the River Thames smelt so bad that it was called the Great Stink. Parliament then funded the engineer Joseph Bazalgette to build a vast system of underground sewers that carried London's waste away from the city's water supply. Cities around the world followed, building piped clean water supplies, sewers, water treatment works with filtering and later chlorine, and rubbish collection.

These improvements cut deaths from water-borne diseases dramatically, and were one of the main reasons death rates fell in the late nineteenth century, even before antibiotics existed.

In South Africa, providing clean water and sanitation to all remains a challenge. Many rural areas and informal settlements still lack piped water and proper toilets. The Constitution recognises the right to sufficient water, and access has improved greatly since 1994.

The questions for this section are with you now: what sanitation means, John Snow's map, the Great Stink and sewers, and why sanitation lowered death rates.

## Subtopic: Canned Food and Refrigeration

For most of history, food spoiled quickly. People preserved it by drying, salting, smoking, pickling and fermenting, as with biltong, dried fish or sour porridge, but supplies often ran out, especially in winter or during droughts and long sea voyages.

In 1810 the French confectioner Nicolas Appert published a method of preserving food by sealing it in glass jars and heating them. He had been encouraged by a prize offered by the French government, which wanted to feed its armies. In the same year, Peter Durand in England patented the use of tin cans. Factories began canning food, and later Pasteur's work explained why it worked: heating killed the germs, and the seal stopped new germs getting in. Canned food could be stored for months or years and transported long distances.

Refrigeration came later. Mechanical refrigeration was developed in the nineteenth century, and by the 1870s and 1880s refrigerated ships were carrying frozen meat from Argentina, Australia and New Zealand to Europe. In the twentieth century, electric refrigerators became common in homes in wealthier countries. Cold slows the growth of bacteria, so food lasts longer and is safer. Refrigeration also keeps vaccines and medicines effective, an important part of the cold chain in health care today.

Canned and refrigerated food improved nutrition and food security. Fresh food could reach cities from far away, surplus harvests could be stored, sailors and soldiers avoided diseases like scurvy, and fewer people became ill from spoiled food. Better-nourished people resist infections better, so these developments also lowered death rates.

The questions for this section are with you now: traditional preservation, Appert and canning, refrigeration and the cold chain, and how these improved health.

## Subtopic: The Combined Effect on Population

Together, these developments explain much of the fall in death rates that caused the population explosion. In Europe and North America the main falls came in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, first from better food supply and sanitation, then from vaccines and, after 1940, antibiotics.

After the Second World War, these advances spread rapidly to Asia, Latin America and Africa. Mass vaccination campaigns, antibiotics, insecticides against malaria mosquitoes, and clean water projects cut death rates in a few decades, a change that had taken a century in Europe. Because birth rates remained high for some time, populations in these regions grew very fast.

Infant and child mortality fell most, because young children are most vulnerable to infections, diarrhoea and malnutrition. As a result, life expectancy rose sharply, from about thirty-two years worldwide in 1900 to about seventy-three today.

However, not everyone benefited equally. Poor communities still lack clean water, sanitation, refrigerators and vaccines, and they still suffer preventable deaths. New challenges have also appeared, such as antibiotic resistance, when bacteria evolve so that antibiotics no longer work, often because antibiotics are overused or treatment is not completed.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: believing the population explosion was caused mainly by hospitals and surgery, rather than by clean water, food and vaccines. Two: confusing vaccines, which prevent disease, with antibiotics, which treat bacterial infections. Three: thinking antibiotics kill viruses such as flu.

The questions for this section are with you now: the combined effect on death rates and life expectancy, the spread after 1945, unequal benefits and antibiotic resistance, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Fighting Germs

In 1796, Edward Jenner made the first vaccine, against smallpox. Later, Louis Pasteur showed that tiny germs cause many diseases. Joseph Lister started cleaning wounds and tools, so fewer patients died after operations. In 1928, Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin, a medicine that kills bacteria.

Vaccines stop you getting sick. Antibiotics treat infections caused by bacteria, but not viruses.

## Subtopic: Clean Water and Toilets

In crowded cities long ago, sewage got into drinking water and people died of cholera. In 1854, John Snow drew a map of cholera deaths in London and found they all clustered around one water pump. When the pump was closed, the deaths dropped. Cities then built sewers and clean water supplies, and far fewer people died.

## Subtopic: Keeping Food Safe

In 1810 Nicolas Appert found that food sealed in jars and heated would keep for a long time. Soon food was put in tin cans. Later, fridges and freezers kept food cold, which slows germs down. People could store and transport food, so they ate better and got sick less. Fridges also keep vaccines safe.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Vaccines, germ theory, antibiotics, clean water, sewers, canned food and refrigeration all helped fewer people die, especially children. Death rates fell while birth rates stayed high, so populations grew fast.
