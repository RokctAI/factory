# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the Spanish flu pandemic and compares it with COVID-19. The anchor is Black October 1918, when the flu swept through South Africa along the railway lines and killed hundreds of thousands of people in a few weeks. The governing idea is that pandemics can cause sudden, sharp rises in death rates, that how a disease spreads depends on how people travel and live, and that science and public health measures greatly changed how the world responded a century later.

## Subtopic: Epidemics and Pandemics

Diseases that spread from person to person are called infectious or communicable diseases. When a disease affects many more people than usual in a particular area at the same time, it is called an epidemic. When an epidemic spreads across many countries and continents, affecting very large numbers of people, it is called a pandemic. A disease that is always present in an area at a steady level, such as malaria in parts of Africa, is called endemic.

Many pandemics are caused by new viruses, especially influenza viruses and coronaviruses, to which people have no immunity. A virus can spread rapidly when people are crowded together and when they travel, carrying the infection to new places before they even feel ill.

Pandemics of the past include the Black Death in the fourteenth century, cholera pandemics in the nineteenth century, the Spanish flu of 1918 to 1920, the Asian flu of 1957 and the Hong Kong flu of 1968, and, more recently, the HIV pandemic and COVID-19.

Pandemics affect populations by causing sudden spikes in death rates, sometimes falls in birth rates, because people delay having children, and drops in life expectancy for the years they occur. On a population graph, a pandemic may show as a dip or a flattening of the line.

They also disrupt economies, as workers fall ill, businesses close and trade slows, and they change societies, sometimes leading to new public health systems.

## Subtopic: The Spanish Flu, 1918 to 1920

The Spanish flu was a pandemic of influenza, caused by a strain of the H1N1 influenza virus. It came in waves from early 1918 to 1920, as the First World War was ending. It is called the Spanish flu not because it started in Spain, but because Spain, which was neutral in the war, reported freely on the illness in its newspapers, while the countries at war censored such news. Where it began is still debated.

The pandemic spread around the world with soldiers returning from the war, along shipping routes and railways. About five hundred million people, roughly a third of the world's population at the time, were infected, and estimates of deaths range from about twenty million to fifty million or more, more than were killed in the First World War itself.

Unusually, the Spanish flu killed many healthy young adults aged twenty to forty, not only the very young and very old. Many died from pneumonia caused by bacteria that infected the weakened lungs; there were no antibiotics to treat this.

There was no vaccine and no effective medicine. Doctors did not yet know that influenza was caused by a virus; viruses could not even be seen under the microscopes of the time. Authorities closed schools, churches, theatres and public places in some cities, encouraged people to wear masks, and isolated the sick. Cities that acted early and kept measures in place generally had fewer deaths.

After the pandemic, many countries created or strengthened public health departments to deal with future epidemics.

## Subtopic: Black October in South Africa

The Spanish flu reached South Africa in September 1918 on troopships carrying soldiers of the South African Native Labour Contingent, returning from war service in Europe, which docked in Durban and Cape Town. Although some sick soldiers were held in quarantine, the deadly second wave escaped.

From the ports, the disease spread rapidly along the railway lines to Kimberley, the Witwatersrand, the Free State and the Eastern Cape. Migrant mineworkers who fell ill in Kimberley and on the Rand travelled home to rural areas, carrying the virus to villages across the country and into neighbouring territories.

October 1918 became known as Black October. Within about six weeks, an estimated three hundred thousand people in South Africa died, about six percent of the population. This made South Africa one of the five worst-hit countries in the world. Kimberley was particularly badly affected; in the mine compounds, a very large number of workers died. In Cape Town, coffins ran out and people were buried in mass graves. Whole families fell sick together, and there was no one to cook or fetch water. Many children were orphaned.

Volunteers set up soup kitchens and makeshift hospitals. The government had no department of health at that time. One result of the pandemic was the Public Health Act of 1919, which created South Africa's first national Department of Public Health.

However, the pandemic was also used to justify segregation. Some officials blamed overcrowded conditions in Black urban areas for the spread, and used this to push for separate townships, such as Ndabeni in Cape Town, rather than improving living conditions for all.

## Subtopic: Comparing with COVID-19

This comparison is for understanding and is not examinable.

COVID-19 is caused by a coronavirus, SARS-CoV-2, first identified in late 2019. The World Health Organization declared it a pandemic in March 2020. It spread across the world within weeks, carried mainly by air travel, much faster than the Spanish flu spread by ship and train.

Similarities with the Spanish flu: both were caused by new viruses spread through the air by breathing, coughing and sneezing; both came in waves; both overwhelmed hospitals; and similar measures were used: masks, closing public places, isolating the sick and keeping people apart.

Differences: scientists identified the COVID-19 virus and read its genetic code within weeks. Tests could show who was infected. Hospitals could give oxygen and intensive care, and antibiotics treated secondary infections. Most importantly, vaccines were developed and approved within about a year, which had never been done so quickly before. COVID-19 was most dangerous for older people and those with conditions such as diabetes, whereas the Spanish flu killed many young adults. The officially recorded COVID-19 deaths worldwide were about seven million, though the true number is believed to be considerably higher; this was a smaller share of the world's population than the Spanish flu.

South Africa had one of the strictest lockdowns in the world from March 2020, and its scientists played a leading role in identifying new variants, such as Beta and Omicron.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: believing the Spanish flu started in Spain. Two: thinking the Spanish flu was treated with vaccines. Three: confusing an epidemic with a pandemic.

## Subtopic: What Is a Pandemic?

An epidemic is when a disease spreads to many people in one area. A pandemic is when it spreads across the whole world. New viruses spread quickly because nobody's body has defences against them yet, and travellers carry them to new places.

Pandemics make death rates jump suddenly.

## Subtopic: The Spanish Flu

The Spanish flu was a flu pandemic from 1918 to 1920, at the end of the First World War. It got its name because Spanish newspapers wrote about it, not because it started there. It killed about twenty to fifty million people or more worldwide, many of them young adults. There was no vaccine.

It reached South Africa on ships bringing soldiers home in September 1918 and spread along the railways. In Black October, about three hundred thousand South Africans died in about six weeks. Afterwards, in 1919, the country created its first Department of Public Health.

## Subtopic: COVID-19 Compared

This comparison is not examinable. COVID-19 began in 2019. Like the Spanish flu, it was a new virus spread through the air and came in waves, and people wore masks and stayed apart. But this time scientists quickly identified the virus, made tests, and produced vaccines in about a year. Aeroplanes spread COVID-19 around the world much faster than ships and trains spread the flu.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A pandemic is a disease that spreads around the world. The Spanish flu of 1918 to 1920 killed tens of millions, including about three hundred thousand South Africans in Black October. COVID-19 spread faster, but science gave us tests and vaccines quickly.
