# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines how economic status and conflict affect population growth. The anchor is a comparison between a wealthy, peaceful country such as Japan and a poor or war-affected country, such as Niger or South Sudan, with very different birth rates, death rates and life expectancy. The governing idea is that wealth and peace usually bring lower death rates and, over time, lower birth rates, while poverty and war raise death rates, break up families and force people to flee their homes.

## Subtopic: Economic Status and Development

Economic status means how wealthy a country, community or family is. Geographers often measure a country's wealth by its gross domestic product per person, the value of all goods and services produced in a year divided by the number of people. Countries are sometimes grouped as developed or higher-income countries, such as Japan, Germany and the United States, and developing or lower-income countries, such as Niger, Malawi and Mozambique. Many countries, including South Africa, Brazil and India, are in between, sometimes called middle-income or emerging economies.

These labels are broad and can hide big differences within a country. South Africa is one of the most unequal countries in the world, with very wealthy suburbs and very poor informal settlements and rural villages, sometimes only a few kilometres apart.

Development is about more than money. The Human Development Index, used by the United Nations, combines three measures: income, education, measured by years of schooling, and health, measured by life expectancy. Population figures are therefore closely linked to development.

In general, wealthier countries have low birth rates, low death rates, low infant mortality and high life expectancy, often over eighty years. Their populations grow slowly or are even shrinking and ageing. Poorer countries generally have higher birth rates, higher infant mortality and lower life expectancy, often in the low sixties, and young, fast-growing populations.

The questions for this section are with you now: what economic status means, developed and developing countries, the Human Development Index, and the general link between wealth and population.

## Subtopic: How Wealth and Poverty Affect Births and Deaths

Why does economic status affect population so strongly?

Death rates are lower in wealthier places because people can afford nutritious food, live in better housing with clean water and sanitation, and reach good clinics and hospitals. Governments with more money can fund vaccination, health care and safe infrastructure. In poor places, malnutrition weakens the body, unsafe water spreads disease, and clinics may be far away and short of staff and medicine, so more people die young, especially babies and young children.

Birth rates are often higher in poorer places for several reasons. Where many children die, parents may have more children to ensure some survive. On farms, children help with work, fetching water and herding animals. Without pensions, parents rely on their children to care for them in old age. Girls may leave school early and marry young, and family planning may be hard to reach. In wealthier places, children are expensive to raise and educate, more women complete school and work outside the home, people marry later, and contraception is widely available, so families tend to be smaller.

Economic change can therefore change population. As a country develops, death rates fall first, then birth rates fall, which is the demographic transition studied earlier this term.

Economic crises can also affect population. When people lose jobs and income, couples may delay having children, and health and nutrition may suffer. When economies grow, migrants may move in to find work, as people from neighbouring countries have moved to South Africa's cities.

The questions for this section are with you now: why poverty raises death rates, why birth rates are often higher in poorer places, and how economic change affects population.

## Subtopic: Conflict and War

Conflicts and wars affect population in direct and indirect ways.

Direct effects are the deaths of soldiers and civilians in fighting and bombing. The two World Wars of the twentieth century killed tens of millions of people. The Second World War, from 1939 to 1945, killed an estimated seventy to eighty-five million, including civilians and the six million Jews murdered in the Holocaust. In the Rwandan genocide of 1994, about eight hundred thousand people, mainly Tutsi, were killed in about a hundred days.

Indirect effects are often even larger. War destroys farms, roads, water supplies, clinics and hospitals. Crops cannot be planted or harvested, and food cannot be transported, so famine follows. Diseases such as cholera and measles spread in crowded camps without clean water. Health workers flee. In the long civil war in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, most of the millions of deaths came from disease and hunger rather than violence.

War also changes birth rates. When men are away fighting, fewer babies are born, and birth rates may jump after the war ends, as happened in many countries after 1945, the so-called baby boom.

War can also change the structure of a population. Because more young men are often killed, there may be many more women than men in some age groups for decades afterwards, as happened in Russia after the Second World War. A population pyramid can show these scars.

The questions for this section are with you now: direct and indirect effects of war, examples of major conflicts, and how war changes birth rates and population structure.

## Subtopic: Refugees and Displaced People

War and conflict force many people to flee their homes. A refugee is a person who has fled across an international border because of war, violence or persecution and cannot safely return home. An internally displaced person has fled but remains inside their own country. At the end of 2023, the United Nations reported more than one hundred and ten million people forcibly displaced worldwide, the highest number ever recorded.

Major causes in recent years include wars in Syria, Ukraine, Sudan, South Sudan, Afghanistan, Myanmar and the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo. Most refugees do not travel far; they shelter in neighbouring countries, many of which are themselves developing countries. Uganda, for example, hosts well over a million refugees, mainly from South Sudan and the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

Refugee movements change the populations of both the places people leave and the places they arrive. The country of origin loses people, often young adults, skilled workers and families with children. Host areas suddenly grow, and must provide shelter, water, food, schooling and health care, often in large camps. Refugees bring skills, culture and energy, and many contribute to their new communities, but sudden arrivals can also put pressure on resources and sometimes cause tension.

South Africa has received asylum seekers and refugees from the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Somalia, Ethiopia, Zimbabwe and elsewhere. South Africa's own history includes exiles, people who fled apartheid and lived in neighbouring countries and abroad until the 1990s.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking war affects population only through battle deaths. Two: confusing refugees with internally displaced people. Three: believing that every poor country has the same population pattern.

The questions for this section are with you now: refugees and displaced people, where they go, their effects on population, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Rich and Poor

In wealthier countries, people usually have good food, clean water, good hospitals and schools. So fewer people die young, and people live long, often over eighty years. Families are smaller, because children are expensive and more women study and work.

In poorer countries, more babies and children die, from hunger, dirty water and lack of clinics. Families are often bigger, because children help at home and look after their parents when they are old.

Try this part's questions on rich and poor countries.

## Subtopic: Wars

Wars kill soldiers and ordinary people. But often even more people die because farms, roads, clinics and water supplies are destroyed, causing hunger and disease. During a war fewer babies are born; after a war births may jump, like the baby boom after 1945. Because many young men die, a country may have far more women than men for years.

Try this part's questions on wars.

## Subtopic: People on the Move

War forces people to run for safety. If they cross into another country, they are refugees. If they stay in their own country, they are internally displaced. Today over a hundred and ten million people have been forced from their homes, the most ever. Most go to a neighbouring country, which then needs to find water, food, shelter and schools for them.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Wealth usually brings lower death rates, longer lives and smaller families; poverty brings more child deaths and bigger families. Wars kill people directly and, even more, through hunger and disease, and they force millions to flee as refugees.
