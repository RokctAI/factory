# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the Dutch slave trade at the Cape. The anchor is the arrival in 1658 of two ships carrying enslaved people: the Amersfoort, with captives taken from a Portuguese slave ship off the coast of Angola, and the Hassalt, with captives from Guinea, in West Africa. The governing idea is that slavery was central to the building of the Cape Colony, that enslaved people came from an exceptionally wide range of places across the Indian Ocean world, and that their forced labour built the farms, houses and wealth of the colony.

## Subtopic: Why the Cape Turned to Slavery

Slavery is a system in which people are owned as property by others, forced to work without pay, and can be bought, sold and inherited, with their children also enslaved.

The VOC needed workers at the Cape to build the fort, work in the Company's Garden, cut wood, herd cattle and later to work on farms. Labour was scarce. The Company did not want to pay the high cost of bringing more European workers, who expected wages, and Company employees were few.

The VOC's instructions forbade the enslavement of the Khoikhoi, partly because the Company depended on them for trade and wanted to avoid war. The Khoikhoi were also unwilling to work for the Dutch on a permanent basis, because they had their own herds and way of life.

After 1657, when the first free burghers began farming, they also needed workers. Van Riebeeck had repeatedly asked the Company to send slaves. Slavery was already widely practised by the VOC in its Asian territories, where it bought and sold people across the Indian Ocean, so the Company saw it as a normal solution.

The first group of enslaved people arrived in 1658. As farming expanded, especially wine and wheat farming in the south-western Cape in the eighteenth century, the demand for enslaved labour grew. By 1795 there were about sixteen thousand eight hundred enslaved people and about fifteen thousand free burghers in the colony.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of slavery, why the VOC and colonists wanted enslaved labour, why the Khoikhoi were not enslaved by law, and the growth of slavery.

## Subtopic: Where Enslaved People Came From

Enslaved people at the Cape came from four main regions around the Indian Ocean and beyond.

From Africa: the first enslaved people came from West Africa and Angola in 1658, but these regions were mainly controlled by other slave traders. Later, many were brought from Mozambique and the East African coast, and from Madagascar, which was one of the most important sources. Malagasy slaves came from the island's west coast, where local rulers sold captives taken in wars.

From India: enslaved people came from the Coromandel Coast in the south-east, from Bengal and from Malabar in the south-west, all regions where the VOC traded, especially during times of famine and war, when people sometimes sold themselves or their children to survive.

From South-East Asia: many came from the Indonesian islands, including Java, Bali, Sulawesi and Timor, and from other parts of the Malay world. These people were often called Malays at the Cape, although most did not come from Malaysia.

From Sri Lanka, then called Ceylon.

Overall, historians estimate that roughly a quarter came from Africa, a quarter from Madagascar, a quarter from India and Sri Lanka, and a quarter from Indonesia, though the balance changed over time. In the later eighteenth century, more came from Madagascar and Mozambique. By the end of the eighteenth century, a growing number of enslaved people at the Cape were born there, known as Cape-born or creole slaves.

This mixture made the Cape slave population one of the most diverse in the world, with many languages, religions and cultures.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four main regions enslaved people came from, why people were enslaved there, and the diversity of the slave population.

## Subtopic: How Enslaved People Were Brought

Enslaved people were brought to the Cape in several ways.

Some were captured. People were taken prisoner in wars, raids and kidnappings in Madagascar, East Africa and South-East Asia, then sold to traders. Others were enslaved as punishment for crimes or debts, or sold by relatives during famines.

The VOC organised slaving voyages, especially to Madagascar and Mozambique, sending ships to buy captives from local rulers and traders in exchange for goods such as guns, gunpowder, cloth, beads, copper and alcohol. Captives were held in chains on the coast and then packed into ships.

Others arrived on VOC ships returning from Asia, where officials and sailors bought enslaved people in Batavia, India or Sri Lanka and brought them to the Cape to sell for profit or to keep as servants. Some were transferred from the VOC's Asian territories.

Foreign ships, including Portuguese, French and English vessels, also sold slaves at the Cape.

Conditions on the voyages were terrible. People were crowded below deck, chained, with little food or water. Many died of disease, hunger and despair; on some voyages from Madagascar a fifth or more of the captives died.

On arrival, enslaved people were sold at auctions or privately, examined like livestock, and given new names by their owners. These were often classical names, such as Cupido, Hannibal or Titus, Biblical names, or the names of the months, such as September or Januarie, sometimes with the place they came from, such as van Bengalen or van Madagascar. Many South African surnames today, such as September, Februarie, Van Java and Van Malabar, preserve these histories.

The questions for this section are with you now: how people were enslaved, VOC slaving voyages, conditions on the ships, and sales and naming at the Cape.

## Subtopic: The Slave Lodge and the End of the Trade

The VOC itself was the largest single slave owner at the Cape. The Company's slaves lived in the Slave Lodge, a large building next to the Company's Garden in Cape Town, built in 1679. At times it held around six hundred to a thousand people: men, women and children, crowded together in dark, damp rooms with little ventilation. The Lodge was also used as a brothel for soldiers and sailors, and many children born there had European fathers. Company slaves worked on public works, in the garden, at the harbour, building roads and fortifications, and in the hospital.

Most enslaved people at the Cape, however, were owned privately by free burghers, mainly by wine and wheat farmers in the districts of Stellenbosch, Drakenstein, around Paarl, and the Cape, and by households in Cape Town. Most owners had only a few slaves, but some wealthy farmers owned dozens.

Britain took control of the Cape in 1795 and again in 1806. Britain abolished the slave trade, the buying and selling of new slaves from outside the empire, in 1807, and the import of new slaves to the Cape ended in 1808. But slavery itself, the ownership of people already enslaved and their children, continued until 1834, when slavery was abolished in the British Empire, followed by four years of apprenticeship until 1838.

Today the Slave Lodge is a museum in Cape Town, part of Iziko Museums, which tells the story of slavery at the Cape. Each year on 1 December, Emancipation Day is remembered.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking all enslaved people at the Cape came from Malaysia. Two: confusing the end of the slave trade in 1808 with the end of slavery in 1834. Three: believing slavery at the Cape was small and unimportant.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Slave Lodge, private slave owners, the end of the slave trade and of slavery, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, in plain words, same facts: why, from where, and how.

## Subtopic: Why Slaves?

The VOC and the farmers at the Cape needed workers, but paying European workers was expensive, and the Company's rules said the Khoikhoi must not be enslaved. So, from 1658, they brought enslaved people to the Cape. Slaves were owned like property and forced to work without pay. By 1795 there were more slaves than free colonists.

## Subtopic: From Where?

Enslaved people came from all around the Indian Ocean: from Mozambique and East Africa, from the big island of Madagascar, from India and Sri Lanka, and from Indonesian islands like Java and Bali. They spoke many languages and had many religions.

## Subtopic: How?

People were captured in wars and raids, or sold during famines. VOC ships bought captives in Madagascar and Mozambique, in exchange for guns, cloth and beads. The voyages were crowded and many people died. At the Cape, they were sold at auctions and given new names, like September or Van Java, names many South Africans still carry. The VOC kept its own slaves in the Slave Lodge, now a museum. Slave imports ended in 1808, and slavery ended in 1834.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Slaves were brought to the Cape from 1658 because workers were needed. They came from Africa, Madagascar, India, Sri Lanka and Indonesia, captured or bought and shipped in terrible conditions. Their forced labour built the colony.
