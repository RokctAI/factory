### subtopic_3 — Slaves, Servants and Labour
**Assistant:** Did any Khoikhoi or San gain anything from working for trekboers?

**Tutor:** For some, working on a trekboer farm was a way to survive after they had lost their land and herds, and a few were able to build up small herds of their own, since some farmers paid servants with livestock or allowed them to graze their animals on the farm. Some Khoikhoi became skilled wagon drivers, hunters and guides, valued for their expertise, and some learnt to use guns and horses, which later helped groups such as the Kora and Griqua become powerful on the northern frontier. Some found protection in a household during dangerous times. But the overall relationship was deeply unequal. Workers often received no wages, could be beaten, and were prevented from leaving through debts or by farmers keeping their children. Their testimony counted for less in courts. When missionaries arrived at the end of the eighteenth century, many Khoikhoi left farms to live on mission stations, which suggests how much they wanted to escape farm labour. Historians emphasise that people made the best choices they could within a system that left them very few options.

### subtopic_4 — Conflict and Stories
**Assistant:** What is the difference between trekboers and Voortrekkers?

**Tutor:** The two are often confused, but they belong to different periods and had different motives. Trekboers were eighteenth-century stock farmers who gradually moved inland from the Cape over many decades, family by family, in search of grazing land, while remaining, at least officially, within or at the edges of the Cape Colony under the VOC and later the British. Their movement was slow, spread out and mostly unplanned. Voortrekkers were Dutch-speaking colonists, many of them descendants of trekboers, who left the Eastern Cape in organised groups between about 1835 and the 1840s, in what became known as the Great Trek. They left British rule deliberately, for reasons including resentment of British policies, the abolition of slavery, insecurity on the eastern frontier and a desire for land and self-government. They moved into the interior, across the Orange and Vaal rivers, and into Natal, where they came into conflict with the Ndebele, the Zulu and others, and founded the Boer republics. You will study the Great Trek in Grade 8. So: trekboers, eighteenth century, gradual; Voortrekkers, 1830s, organised.

### subtopic_4 — Conflict and Stories
**Assistant:** Why do historians call the attacks on the San genocide?

**Tutor:** Genocide is a term for actions carried out with the intention of destroying, in whole or in part, a national, ethnic, racial or religious group. Historians who study the northern and north-eastern frontiers of the Cape Colony in the late eighteenth century have found evidence that colonial commandos aimed not just to recover stolen livestock or punish particular raiders, but to wipe out San communities in the areas settlers wanted. Commando records report killing large numbers of San, including women and the elderly, and capturing children to be raised as farm labourers, which also destroyed San culture and language. In 1774, the colonial government authorised a general commando against the San in the north-east, and records suggest hundreds were killed and many children taken in that campaign alone. Some officials at the time expressed concern, and in the 1790s the authorities experimented with giving the San livestock to encourage peace, but violence continued. Using the word genocide is a serious historical judgement, and historians support it with evidence. Recognising this history is part of respecting the San and their descendants, who are still working to reclaim their heritage and land today.
