# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines life as an enslaved person at the Cape and the lasting legacy of slavery. The anchor is a wine farm in the Drakenstein valley, where enslaved men pruned vines and trod grapes, and enslaved women cooked, cleaned and cared for the owner's children. The governing idea is that enslaved people endured harsh control and violence, yet created families, faith, language and culture, and that Islam and Afrikaans are two of their most important contributions to South Africa.

## Subtopic: Work and Daily Life

Most enslaved people at the Cape worked on farms in the south-western Cape, especially on wine and wheat farms in the districts of Stellenbosch, Drakenstein and the Cape, or in households and workshops in Cape Town.

On farms, enslaved men ploughed, sowed and harvested wheat, pruned and tended vines, picked and pressed grapes, made wine and brandy, herded livestock, built walls and dams, and drove wagons. Work went on from sunrise to sunset, and at harvest time even longer. Enslaved women worked in the fields too, and in the farmhouse, cooking, cleaning, washing, sewing, and caring for the owner's children as nursemaids.

In Cape Town, enslaved people worked as domestic servants, cooks, washerwomen at the streams on the slopes of Table Mountain, fishermen, water carriers, porters at the harbour and skilled craftsmen: carpenters, masons, tailors, coopers who made barrels, and silversmiths. Some owners hired out skilled slaves to others and kept their earnings. Some enslaved people sold goods in the streets for their owners, and a few were allowed to keep part of their earnings and save to buy their freedom.

Food was basic: bread or rice, vegetables, sometimes fish or meat, and on wine farms a daily ration of wine, which some historians link to the later damaging tot system. Housing was often a room in an outbuilding, a loft or a corner of the kitchen. Clothing was simple, and enslaved people were not allowed to wear shoes, which became a visible sign of slavery.

Some enslaved people developed close relationships with owners' families, especially women who raised the owners' children, but the relationship always rested on unequal power and the threat of force.

## Subtopic: Control, Punishment and Family

Slave owners controlled enslaved people through laws, punishment and fear.

Enslaved people could not move freely; after dark in Cape Town they had to carry a lantern and a pass. They could not legally marry until the early nineteenth century, so their families had no protection in law. Owners could sell husbands, wives and children separately, breaking up families, although selling young children away from their mothers was eventually restricted. Enslaved people could not own property or give evidence against their owners in most cases.

Punishment was harsh. Owners were allowed to whip enslaved people for disobedience, laziness or running away, and some were put in chains. Serious offences were tried by the VOC courts, and punishments for slaves who rebelled or harmed owners were brutal and public, carried out in front of the Castle to frighten others. Some owners were cruel; a few were taken to court for extreme violence, but they were rarely punished severely.

Yet enslaved people created families and communities. Couples formed relationships, even without legal marriage, and raised children. Mothers passed on stories, songs and beliefs. Enslaved people gathered at night, on Sundays and on special occasions, shared food and music, and helped one another. In Cape Town, they met at the washing places on the mountain, at the harbour and in the streets, forming networks across households.

Some enslaved people gained their freedom through manumission, when an owner formally freed them, sometimes in a will, or when an enslaved person bought their own freedom. Manumission was more common in Cape Town than on farms, and more common for women and children, especially those with European fathers. Freed slaves were called free blacks, and some became property owners in Cape Town.

The questions for this section are with you now: laws controlling enslaved people, punishment, families and communities, and manumission.

## Subtopic: Legacy: Islam at the Cape

Islam came to the Cape mainly through two groups: enslaved people from Indonesia and India, and political exiles and convicts sent by the VOC from its Asian territories, including religious leaders who had resisted Dutch rule.

The most famous exile was Sheikh Yusuf of Makassar, a scholar and resistance leader from Sulawesi who had fought the Dutch in Java. He was exiled to the Cape in 1694 with his family and followers, and housed on a farm at Zandvliet, near the mouth of the Eerste River, in what is now Macassar. He died there in 1699, and his kramat, or shrine, is a place of pilgrimage today. He is regarded by many as the founder of Islam in South Africa.

Another important figure was Imam Abdullah Kadi Abdus Salaam, known as Tuan Guru, a prince and scholar from Tidore in Indonesia, who was imprisoned on Robben Island from 1780 to 1793. There he wrote copies of the Qur'an from memory. After his release, he helped establish the first madrasah, a Muslim school, in 1793, and the first mosque, the Auwal Mosque in Bo-Kaap, Cape Town, built in 1794.

The VOC did not allow Muslims to worship in public, so for a long time Islam was practised privately. Islam spread among enslaved people because it offered community, dignity, education and literacy, and because Muslim slave owners were expected not to sell Muslim slaves and to free them, and Christian slave owners were reluctant to baptise slaves, since the Church taught that baptised slaves should be freed. By 1840, Muslims made up a large part of Cape Town's population.

Today Cape Muslims, many of whom descend from enslaved people and exiles, are an important community, and the kramats around the Cape Peninsula form a so-called circle of tombs.

The questions for this section are with you now: how Islam came to the Cape, Sheikh Yusuf, Tuan Guru and the first mosque, and why Islam spread among enslaved people.

## Subtopic: Legacy: Afrikaans and Culture

Afrikaans developed at the Cape from the seventeenth-century Dutch spoken by the settlers, as it was used and changed by many different people: enslaved people from Africa and Asia, Khoikhoi, free blacks, and Europeans from the Netherlands, Germany and France. Enslaved people needed a common language to communicate with owners and with each other, and they spoke a simplified, changing form of Dutch, mixed with words from Malay, Portuguese creole, which was a trade language in the Indian Ocean, Khoekhoegowab and other languages.

Words in Afrikaans that came from Malay include baie, meaning very or many, piering, saucer, and pisang, banana. Words from Khoekhoegowab include kwagga, quagga, and dagga. Simplified grammar, such as using one form of the verb for all persons, also developed.

Remarkably, some of the earliest written Afrikaans was produced by Cape Muslims. In the nineteenth century, Muslim teachers wrote religious texts in Afrikaans using Arabic script, a form called Arabic-Afrikaans, years before Afrikaans was written in the Latin alphabet in books.

Later, Afrikaans was claimed by Afrikaner nationalists as a white language, and during apartheid its mixed origins were often ignored. Today historians stress that Afrikaans is a creole language, a language that grew from contact between many peoples, and that the majority of Afrikaans speakers today are not white.

Other legacies include Cape cuisine, such as bobotie, bredie, sosaties and koeksisters; Cape Dutch architecture built by skilled enslaved craftsmen; music traditions, including the Cape Minstrels, the Kaapse Klopse, who parade at New Year, linked to the Tweede Nuwe Jaar holiday; and the surnames and family histories of many South Africans.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking Afrikaans came only from the Dutch settlers. Two: thinking enslaved people had no culture or community. Three: believing Islam arrived at the Cape only in the twentieth century.

The questions for this section are with you now: how Afrikaans developed, words from other languages, Arabic-Afrikaans, other cultural legacies, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Daily Life

Most enslaved people worked on wine and wheat farms or in houses in Cape Town. They worked from sunrise to sunset. They could not wear shoes, could not legally marry, and could be whipped or sold away from their families. Still, they built families and communities, shared music and stories, and some were freed or bought their own freedom.
