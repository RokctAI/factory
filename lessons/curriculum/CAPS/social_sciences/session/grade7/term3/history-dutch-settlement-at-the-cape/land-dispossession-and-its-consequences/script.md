# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the dispossession of the Khoikhoi and San at the Cape and its consequences. The anchor is the Khoikhoi leaders' protest at the peace talks of 1660, asking by what right the Dutch had taken their land, and the reply that the land had been lost in war. The governing idea is that dispossession happened step by step, through settlement, war, unequal trade, disease and colonial law, and that it destroyed the independence of the Khoikhoi and San, turning many into labourers on land that had been theirs.

## Subtopic: What Is Land Dispossession?

To dispossess means to take land or property away from people, usually by force, unfair laws or deception. Land dispossession is the loss of land by its original inhabitants to others who take control of it.

At the Cape, dispossession happened over about one hundred and fifty years, from 1652 to the early 1800s, as the colony expanded from Table Bay to the Fish River in the east and the Orange River in the north.

A key reason for conflict was that the colonists and the indigenous people understood land in very different ways.

For the Khoikhoi, land was held in common by the group. Clans had recognised rights to use certain grazing areas and water sources at certain seasons, moving their herds between them. Land could not be sold by individuals. Allowing others to graze temporarily did not mean giving the land away.

For the San, land meant hunting and gathering territories, with rights to waterholes, plants and game, shared within the band and recognised by neighbouring bands.

For the colonists, influenced by European ideas, land could be owned as private property by individuals, marked out with boundaries, fenced, registered and sold. The VOC claimed the authority to grant land to settlers, regardless of whether others were using it. Colonists often regarded land that was not ploughed or permanently occupied as empty or unused, which justified, in their eyes, taking it.

These different ideas meant that a Khoikhoi leader who allowed settlers to graze nearby might think he was sharing land temporarily, while the settlers thought they had gained it permanently.

## Subtopic: How the Land Was Taken

Dispossession happened in several connected ways.

Settlement: from 1657, the VOC granted land along the Liesbeek River to free burghers, and later to settlers at Stellenbosch, Drakenstein and beyond, on Khoikhoi grazing land, without consent.

War: the Khoikhoi resisted in the First Khoikhoi-Dutch War of 1659 to 1660, and the Second Khoikhoi-Dutch War of 1673 to 1677 against the Cochoqua, led by Gonnema. The Dutch, using guns, horses and alliances with rival Khoikhoi groups, raided Khoikhoi herds, capturing thousands of cattle and sheep. After each war, the Company claimed more land by right of conquest.

Unequal trade and raiding: the Company and burghers obtained huge numbers of Khoikhoi cattle and sheep through trade that favoured them, and through raids. Without cattle, Khoikhoi lost their wealth, food and status, and could no longer live as independent herders.

Disease: the smallpox epidemics of 1713, 1755 and 1767 killed a large proportion of the Khoikhoi.

Trekboer expansion: from the early 1700s, the loan farm system allowed trekboers to occupy vast areas of the interior, taking grazing land and waterholes.

Commandos: in the late eighteenth century, commandos killed thousands of San and captured their children, clearing land for settlers.

Law: colonial law did not recognise Khoikhoi or San ownership of land. Land was granted, registered and sold by the colonial government. Indigenous people could not easily obtain title to land, and under British rule laws like the Caledon Code of 1809 tied Khoikhoi to farms.

## Subtopic: Consequences for the Khoikhoi and San

The consequences of dispossession were devastating.

Loss of independence: by the end of the eighteenth century, the independent Khoikhoi chiefdoms of the south-western Cape had broken up. The Khoikhoi lost political power and the right to govern themselves.

Loss of livelihood: without land and cattle, Khoikhoi could no longer live as herders. Most became labourers on colonial farms as herders, wagon drivers and servants, often for food and shelter rather than wages. Others became soldiers in the colonial Pandour Corps, later the Cape Regiment, or moved to mission stations.

Unfree labour: although legally free, many Khoikhoi were tied to farms by debt, contracts and the indenture of their children. Laws such as the Caledon Code of 1809, under British rule, required them to have a fixed address and carry passes, restricting their movement.

Loss of population: war, disease, especially smallpox, and violence greatly reduced the Khoikhoi and San populations.

Destruction of San communities: in many parts of the interior, San communities were destroyed by commandos. Children captured and raised on farms lost their language and culture. The !Xam language of the Northern Cape had died out by the early twentieth century.

Loss of language and culture: over generations, many Khoikhoi adopted Dutch, which became Afrikaans, and Christianity. Many Khoikhoi people merged with freed slaves and others in the community later classified as Coloured. Khoikhoi identity was hidden or denied for a long time.

Movement beyond the colony: some groups moved north and became the Kora and Griqua on the Orange River, or the Nama in Namaqualand, keeping more independence, as we study next term.

## Subtopic: Land Then and Now

Land dispossession did not end in the eighteenth century. It continued in the nineteenth century on the eastern and northern frontiers, and in the twentieth century through laws such as the Natives Land Act of 1913, which limited Black land ownership to about seven percent of the country, later about thirteen percent, and through forced removals under apartheid.

After 1994, the democratic government introduced land reform, including restitution, returning land or giving compensation to people dispossessed by racially discriminatory laws or practices after 19 June 1913, the date of the Natives Land Act. This date means that most dispossession of the Khoikhoi and San, which happened earlier, was not covered by the restitution process. Khoi and San groups have argued for this to be addressed, and the government has discussed possible exceptions.

Some important steps have been taken. The ‡Khomani San of the Kalahari won a land claim in 1999 that returned land in and near the Kgalagadi Transfrontier Park. The Traditional and Khoi-San Leadership Act of 2019 provides for official recognition of Khoi and San leaders and communities. Many people are reclaiming Khoi and San identities, languages and heritage.

Land remains a sensitive and debated issue in South Africa. Historians help by explaining how land was lost, so that debates can be based on evidence.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: claiming that the Khoikhoi and San simply sold their land willingly. Two: thinking dispossession happened in one single event. Three: believing that land dispossession has no consequences today.

The questions for this section are with you now: later dispossession, the 1913 cut-off for restitution, recent steps for Khoi and San communities, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Different Ideas About Land

Dispossession means taking land away from the people who lived on it. The Khoikhoi shared land as a group and moved their cattle between grazing areas. The San shared hunting grounds. The colonists believed one person could own land, fence it and sell it. So when the Khoikhoi let settlers graze nearby, the settlers thought the land was now theirs.

## Subtopic: How It Happened

The land was taken step by step: farms on Khoikhoi grazing land from 1657, wars in 1659 and 1673 to 1677, cattle taken through unfair trade and raids, smallpox epidemics, trekboers spreading inland, and commandos attacking the San. Colonial laws did not recognise that the land belonged to the Khoikhoi and San.

## Subtopic: What It Caused

The Khoikhoi lost their independence, land and cattle, and many had to work on farms for food and shelter. Many San communities were destroyed. Languages and cultures were lost. Today, land restitution only covers land lost after 1913, so most Khoikhoi and San land is not included, though some San communities, like the Khomani San, have won land back.

They lost independence, livelihood, freedom and much of their culture, and land is still debated today.
