# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the free burghers, Dutch and French Huguenot immigration, and the expansion of European settlement at the Cape. The anchor is the valley of Franschhoek, French Corner, where Huguenot families were given farms in 1688 and planted vineyards that still produce wine today. The governing idea is that the decision to release free burghers turned a refreshment station into a settler colony, and that each new wave of settlement pushed the frontier further into land used by the Khoikhoi and San.

## Subtopic: The First Free Burghers

The word burgher means citizen. Free burghers were former VOC employees who were released from their contracts and given land to farm as free citizens, rather than working for the Company for wages.

In 1657 the VOC released the first nine free burghers and gave them farms of about thirteen hectares each along the Liesbeek River, at Rondebosch and nearby, on land used for grazing by the Khoikhoi. The Company had several reasons. It hoped that private farmers would produce more food, especially wheat and vegetables, more cheaply than Company employees, so that the Company could save money. It hoped free burghers would be more motivated because they could profit from their own work. And settlers on the land would help defend the station.

But the free burghers were not truly free. They had to sell their produce to the Company at prices the Company set, and they could not trade directly with the Khoikhoi or with passing ships. They had to stay at least twenty years, and serve in the burgher militia when needed. Disputes about prices and Company control continued throughout the VOC period.

The free burghers needed labour, so they were among the first to receive enslaved people, and they used Khoikhoi workers. Their farms took land from the Khoikhoi, which, as we saw, led to the First Khoikhoi-Dutch War of 1659 to 1660.

By the 1670s and 1680s, the Company encouraged more settlers to come from the Netherlands and Germany. Many were soldiers and sailors who had finished their service. Some women were sent from orphanages in the Netherlands to marry settlers.

## Subtopic: The French Huguenots

The Huguenots were French Protestants. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, France was mainly Roman Catholic, and Protestants faced persecution. In 1598, the Edict of Nantes had given them some freedom of worship, but in 1685 King Louis XIV revoked, cancelled, the Edict, making Protestantism illegal. Huguenots were forced to convert to Catholicism, their churches were destroyed, and many were imprisoned or sent to the galleys. Hundreds of thousands fled to Protestant countries, including the Netherlands, Switzerland, England and the German states.

The VOC offered some of these refugees free passage to the Cape, as well as land, on condition that they stayed for at least five years. Between 1688 and about 1700, roughly two hundred Huguenots, including families with children, arrived at the Cape. They were a small group, but they made up a significant share of the free burgher population at the time.

Governor Simon van der Stel settled most of them in the Drakenstein valley, near Paarl, and in the valley that became known as Franschhoek. Many Huguenots had experience in farming, and some had grown grapes and made wine in France.

The VOC wanted the Huguenots to blend in with the Dutch settlers. It did not allow them their own separate church congregation for long, and required French to be replaced by Dutch in schools and church services. Within a few generations, French was no longer spoken, but French surnames such as du Toit, du Plessis, Joubert, de Villiers, Rousseau, Marais, Malan and Le Roux are common in South Africa today.

The questions for this section are with you now: who the Huguenots were, why they fled France, why they came to the Cape, and where they settled.

## Subtopic: Wine, Wheat and the Western Cape Farms

Under Governor Simon van der Stel, who ruled from 1679 to 1699, and his son Willem Adriaan van der Stel, governor from 1699 to 1707, the colony expanded. In 1679 Simon van der Stel founded Stellenbosch, named after himself, the second European town in South Africa. Settlers were given farms in the fertile valleys of Stellenbosch, Paarl, Franschhoek and later Tulbagh and the Swartland.

The south-western Cape's Mediterranean climate, with wet winters and dry, warm summers, suited crops from Europe: wheat, which grew in winter and was harvested in early summer, and grapes for wine. The Huguenots and other settlers expanded the vineyards, and wine became an important product. Simon van der Stel's own estate, Groot Constantia, produced famous sweet wines that were exported to Europe in the eighteenth century.

These farms depended on enslaved labour and Khoikhoi workers. Wealthy farmers built gracious homesteads in the Cape Dutch style, with whitewashed walls, thatched roofs and decorated gables, many of which still stand.

Tensions developed between farmers and the Company. In 1706, free burghers, led by Adam Tas, a farmer from Stellenbosch, petitioned the VOC directors in the Netherlands, complaining that Governor Willem Adriaan van der Stel and other officials used their power to run huge private farms and control the market for meat and wine, unfairly competing with the burghers. Van der Stel had Tas arrested, but in 1707 the Heeren XVII recalled the governor to the Netherlands. This episode showed a growing sense among free burghers that they had their own interests, separate from the Company.

The questions for this section are with you now: the founding of Stellenbosch, why wine and wheat suited the south-western Cape, Cape Dutch farms, and the burgher protest led by Adam Tas.

## Subtopic: Expanding Frontiers

A frontier is a zone where two or more societies meet, interact and compete, at the edge of an area of settlement. At the Cape, the frontier was not a fixed line but a broad, shifting zone where colonists, Khoikhoi, San and later Xhosa met through trade, work, marriage and conflict.

The colony's frontier expanded in waves. By 1700, settlement had spread from the Cape Peninsula to Stellenbosch, Paarl, Franschhoek and the Swartland. In the early eighteenth century, the Company allowed settlers to graze livestock on large loan farms, leenplase, further inland, for an annual fee. This encouraged stock farmers to move into the interior, beyond the mountains, to the Land of Waveren, around Tulbagh, the Breede River valley, the Roggeveld and the Karoo.

Several factors drove expansion. The population of free burghers grew, partly through large families, and new generations needed land. Good farmland near Cape Town was expensive and already taken. Raising cattle and sheep needed large areas of grazing in the dry interior. Trade with, and raiding of, Khoikhoi herds provided livestock. And Company control was weak far from Cape Town.

The expansion had devastating effects on the Khoikhoi. A smallpox epidemic in 1713, brought by a ship, killed a large proportion of the Khoikhoi in the south-western Cape, as well as many colonists and enslaved people. Combined with loss of land and cattle, this broke up most Khoikhoi chiefdoms near the Cape.

The colony's eastern boundary was set at the Gamtoos River in 1770 and at the Great Fish River in 1778. By the 1770s, colonists reached the Zuurveld, where they met the Xhosa.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking free burghers were completely free from Company control. Two: confusing the Huguenots, who were French, with the Dutch. Three: imagining a frontier as a neat border line.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of frontier, how and why settlement expanded, the 1713 smallpox epidemic, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Farms and Frontiers

Simon van der Stel founded Stellenbosch in 1679. Wheat and grapes grew well in the wet winters. Wine farms were worked by enslaved people. As families grew, settlers moved inland with cattle and sheep, onto loan farms. A frontier is a zone where different peoples meet. Smallpox in 1713 killed many Khoikhoi, and by the 1770s colonists had reached the Xhosa in the east.
