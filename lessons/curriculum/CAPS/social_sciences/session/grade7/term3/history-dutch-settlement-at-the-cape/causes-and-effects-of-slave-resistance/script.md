# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the causes and effects of slave resistance at the Cape. The anchor is the rebellion of 1808, when more than three hundred enslaved people and Khoikhoi workers marched from the Swartland towards Cape Town, demanding their freedom. The governing idea is that resistance was a constant feature of slavery, that it ranged from everyday acts to armed revolt, and that, although rebellions were crushed, resistance showed that enslaved people never accepted slavery and helped build pressure for its end.

## Subtopic: Causes of Resistance

Why did enslaved people resist? The most basic cause was slavery itself: the loss of freedom, being owned as property, and being forced to work without pay.

Harsh treatment was a major cause. Owners could whip enslaved people, and many suffered cruel punishments, long working hours, poor food and housing. Some resisted after a particular act of cruelty.

The breaking up of families, when owners sold husbands, wives or children separately, caused deep anger and grief.

Enslaved people were denied rights that free people had: they could not marry legally, own property, move freely or, in most cases, testify against owners. They were treated as inferior.

Many enslaved people had been born free in Madagascar, Mozambique, India or Indonesia and remembered their homes, families and cultures. Some hoped to return home.

New ideas of freedom also inspired resistance, especially at the end of the eighteenth century. The French Revolution of 1789 proclaimed liberty, equality and brotherhood; enslaved people in the French colony of Saint-Domingue rose up in 1791 and created the independent state of Haiti in 1804; and in 1807 Britain abolished the slave trade. News of these events reached the Cape through sailors and travellers, and some enslaved people believed freedom was coming.

## Subtopic: Everyday Resistance

Most resistance was not open rebellion, which was very dangerous, but everyday resistance: actions that challenged owners' control in quieter ways.

Enslaved people worked slowly, pretended not to understand instructions, broke tools, damaged crops, or pretended to be ill. They took food or goods from owners. These actions were a way to keep some control over their own time and bodies.

They kept their own cultures alive: their languages, stories, songs and religions, especially Islam, which gave dignity and community that owners could not control. Learning to read and write in Muslim schools was itself a form of resistance.

Some enslaved people took owners to court, complaining of cruelty. Although the courts usually favoured owners, some cases led to owners being fined or the enslaved person being sold to someone else.

Some resisted through violence against owners or overseers, usually alone and in desperation. Others, in despair, took their own lives.

Running away was one of the most common forms of resistance. Runaways, sometimes called drosters, from the Dutch word for deserters, hid in Cape Town, on ships leaving the harbour, in the mountains or in the interior. Some joined Khoikhoi or San groups, or, much later, Xhosa communities on the eastern frontier. Some tried to walk north-east, believing they could reach Mozambique or Madagascar. Runaways who were caught were punished severely, often whipped and branded, and sometimes put in chains.

## Subtopic: Hangklip and the 1808 Rebellion

Some runaways formed communities. The best known was at Cape Hangklip, the mountainous point on the eastern side of False Bay, near today's Pringle Bay. From the early eighteenth century until the 1720s and later, runaway slaves lived in caves there, hidden in rugged, remote terrain. They survived by fishing, gathering, hunting and sometimes raiding farms. Communities like these are called maroon communities, a term used in the Americas too. VOC patrols searched for them, but some groups survived for years.

The largest slave rebellion at the Cape took place in October 1808, soon after the British had ended the slave trade. It was led by Louis of Mauritius, an enslaved tailor in Cape Town, and James Hooper, an Irish sailor, and Abraham Kieviet, a Dutch-speaking settler, both of whom had sympathies with ideas of liberty. They planned to march to Cape Town, take over the Castle and Amsterdam Battery, and demand freedom for all enslaved people.

Louis dressed in a borrowed uniform, pretending to be a Spanish ship's captain, and the rebels travelled from farm to farm in the Swartland, around Malmesbury, tying up farmers, taking weapons and horses, and recruiting enslaved people and Khoikhoi workers. They did not kill anyone. The group grew to more than three hundred people. As they approached Cape Town, British troops and cavalry met them at Salt River and arrested them without a battle.

The rebels were put on trial. Sixteen were sentenced to death, though some sentences were reduced, and in the end five, including Louis, were hanged. Others were flogged, branded or imprisoned, and the farmers were given back their slaves.

## Subtopic: Effects of Resistance

What were the effects of slave resistance?

For the individuals involved, the effects were often terrible. Runaways and rebels who were caught faced whipping, branding, chains, imprisonment or execution. The punishments were public and brutal, meant to frighten others.

For the colony, resistance led to fear among slave owners and to stricter control. After rebellions, the authorities introduced tighter rules on movement, passes, the carrying of weapons and gatherings of enslaved people. Patrols searched the mountains for runaways.

But resistance also had wider effects. It showed that enslaved people never accepted slavery and were prepared to risk their lives for freedom. The 1808 rebellion in particular alarmed the British authorities and showed that slavery could be dangerous and unstable.

Together with the work of abolitionists in Britain, resistance by enslaved people across the British Empire, including major revolts in Barbados in 1816, Demerara in 1823 and Jamaica in 1831, built pressure on the British government. Partly in response, the British introduced reforms at the Cape in the 1820s, such as limiting punishments, appointing a Protector of Slaves to hear complaints, and allowing enslaved people to marry and buy their freedom. Slavery was finally abolished in 1834.

Resistance also preserved identities, faith and culture that survived slavery and are part of South African heritage today.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking enslaved people at the Cape accepted slavery without resistance. Two: believing resistance only meant armed rebellion. Three: claiming the 1808 rebellion ended slavery directly.

## Subtopic: Why Resist?

Enslaved people resisted because they had lost their freedom, were forced to work without pay, were punished cruelly, and could be sold away from their families. Many remembered being free at home. News of freedom struggles, like the Haitian Revolution, gave them hope.

## Subtopic: How They Resisted

Most resistance was quiet: working slowly, breaking tools, or keeping their own language and faith. Many ran away; runaways were called drosters. Some hid in caves at Cape Hangklip, forming a hidden community. In 1808, Louis of Mauritius led more than three hundred enslaved people and Khoikhoi workers towards Cape Town to demand freedom, but British soldiers stopped them at Salt River.

## Subtopic: What Happened?

Rebels and runaways who were caught were whipped, branded or even hanged; Louis was among five rebels executed. Owners became more afraid and made stricter rules. But resistance, here and across the British Empire, showed the world that enslaved people wanted freedom, and helped push Britain to end slavery in 1834.
