### subtopic_1 — Why the Cape Turned to Slavery
**Assistant:** Were Khoikhoi people really never enslaved?

**Tutor:** By VOC law, the Khoikhoi were free people and could not be legally bought and sold as slaves. But in practice, especially in the eighteenth century, many Khoikhoi and San people were forced into conditions close to slavery. As they lost their land and cattle, many had no choice but to work on colonial farms for food and shelter, often without wages. Children of Khoikhoi mothers and enslaved fathers, and San children captured in commando raids, were often bound to farmers under a system called inboekstelsel, or indenture, until the age of twenty-five, and many were never released. Farmers sometimes used violence to stop workers leaving. Later, the Caledon Code of 1809, under British rule, required Khoikhoi to carry passes and have a fixed place of work, which tied them to farms. Only in 1828, with Ordinance 50, were Khoikhoi formally given freedom of movement. So while the law distinguished between slaves and Khoikhoi servants, the lived experience of many Khoikhoi and San was one of forced, unfree labour. Historians therefore speak of a range of unfree labour at the Cape, not only formal slavery.

### subtopic_2 — Where Enslaved People Came From
**Assistant:** Why does it matter that enslaved people came from so many places?

**Tutor:** Because it shaped the culture of the Cape and of South Africa today. Enslaved people brought languages, religions, skills, music, food and ideas from Africa, Madagascar, India, Sri Lanka and Indonesia. Islam, for example, grew at the Cape largely through enslaved people and political exiles from Indonesia. Cape cooking, with dishes such as bobotie, bredie, sosaties, blatjang and koeksisters, shows influences from Indonesia, India and Madagascar. Enslaved people also contributed to the development of Afrikaans, which grew from Dutch as it was spoken by people from many language backgrounds, including Malay and Portuguese creole. Building and craft skills, such as carpentry and masonry, shaped the architecture of Cape Dutch houses. Because enslaved people came from different places and spoke different languages, it was harder for them to unite, which owners sometimes saw as an advantage; yet over time they created new shared communities and identities. Many descendants of enslaved people became part of the community classified under apartheid as Coloured, and today many people are rediscovering these diverse roots through family history and DNA research.

### subtopic_3 — How Enslaved People Were Brought
**Assistant:** How do historians find out about individual enslaved people?

**Tutor:** The Cape has unusually rich records, because the VOC was a bureaucratic company that wrote everything down. Historians use slave registers, which listed owners and their slaves by name, age and origin; auction and sale records; inventories of estates made when owners died, which listed slaves along with furniture and livestock; baptism records; and court records, which are especially valuable because enslaved people sometimes testified in court, in cases of theft, escape, violence or rebellion, and their words were written down. Records from the Slave Lodge and from ships also exist. These documents are kept in the Western Cape Archives in Cape Town and in the Netherlands, and many have been digitised. By piecing them together, historians have reconstructed the lives of individual men, women and children, including enslaved people who ran away or led rebellions. The records were written by owners and officials, so historians read them carefully, looking for the experiences and voices of the enslaved within them. Family historians also use them to trace ancestors.
