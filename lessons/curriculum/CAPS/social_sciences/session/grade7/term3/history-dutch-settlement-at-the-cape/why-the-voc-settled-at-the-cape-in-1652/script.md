# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines why the Dutch East India Company settled permanently at the Cape in 1652. The anchor is the long, dangerous voyage between the Netherlands and Batavia, now Jakarta, which took many months and on which many sailors died of scurvy. The governing idea is that the station was set up for trade and profit, to supply ships with fresh food and water, not originally to build a colony, but that its needs soon led to the taking of Khoikhoi land and the beginnings of colonisation.

## Subtopic: The Dutch East India Company

The Dutch East India Company, in Dutch the Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie, or VOC, was founded in 1602. In English it is often called the DEIC. It was a trading company owned by shareholders, people who invested money in it and shared its profits. It is often described as one of the first multinational companies and the first to sell shares to the public.

The government of the Netherlands gave the VOC a monopoly, the sole right, over Dutch trade between the Cape of Good Hope and the Strait of Magellan, which meant all of Asia. It also gave the Company powers usually held by governments: it could build forts, keep armies, make treaties, wage war and govern territories. It was run by a board of directors called the Heeren XVII, the Lords Seventeen.

The VOC's main aim was profit from the spice trade. Spices such as pepper, cloves, nutmeg and cinnamon were very valuable in Europe, used to flavour and preserve food and in medicines. The VOC set up its Asian headquarters at Batavia, on the island of Java, in 1619, and controlled trading posts across the Indian Ocean and East Asia, using force against local rulers and European rivals such as the Portuguese and the English. It also became deeply involved in the slave trade in the Indian Ocean world.

By the mid-1600s the Netherlands was one of the world's leading trading and seafaring nations, and Amsterdam one of the richest cities in Europe.

The questions for this section are with you now: what the VOC was, its monopoly and powers, its aim of profit from spices, and its headquarters at Batavia.

## Subtopic: Why the VOC Needed a Station

The voyage from the Netherlands to Batavia, around the Cape, was about twenty-four thousand kilometres and took six months or more, depending on winds.

Conditions on board were terrible. Ships were crowded, and fresh food ran out after a few weeks. Sailors ate salted meat, hard biscuits and dried peas, often infested with insects. Drinking water went foul in wooden barrels. Many sailors became ill with scurvy, a disease caused by a lack of vitamin C, found in fresh fruit and vegetables. Scurvy caused bleeding gums, loose teeth, weakness and eventually death. On some voyages, a large share of the crew died.

The VOC needed a halfway station where ships could stop to collect fresh water, fresh vegetables and fruit, and meat, to treat sick sailors, and to repair ships. Fresh food could prevent and cure scurvy.

The Company had already considered the Cape for some years. In 1647 a VOC ship, the Nieuwe Haarlem, was wrecked in Table Bay. Its crew survived on shore for about a year, growing vegetables, trading cattle and sheep with the Khoikhoi, and catching fish. When they returned home, two of them, Leendert Janszen and Matthys Proot, wrote a report, the Remonstrantie, in 1649, recommending a station at the Cape. They argued that the soil was fertile, the water good, the Khoikhoi willing to trade, and that a fort would protect the Company's interests.

The Heeren XVII agreed, and chose Jan van Riebeeck, a ship's surgeon and merchant who had experience in Asia, to lead the expedition.

The questions for this section are with you now: the length and dangers of the voyage, scurvy, the purpose of a halfway station, and the wreck of the Nieuwe Haarlem.

## Subtopic: Why the Cape, and the First Years

The Cape was chosen for several reasons. It lay roughly halfway between Europe and Asia on the sea route. Table Bay provided anchorage, although it was exposed to winter storms. Table Mountain's streams, such as the Fresh River, provided plenty of fresh water. The climate was mild and the soil good enough to grow European vegetables. The Khoikhoi kept large herds of cattle and sheep, so meat could be obtained by trade. And a fort at the Cape would help the Dutch control the route against rivals.

Van Riebeeck arrived on 6 April 1652 with three ships, the Drommedaris, the Reijger and the Goede Hoop, and a small group of Company employees. They built a fort of earth and wood, later replaced by the stone Castle of Good Hope, built between 1666 and 1679. They laid out the Company's Garden to grow vegetables and fruit, which still exists in central Cape Town.

The first years were hard. The first winter was wet and cold, food was short, and many people fell ill and some died. Gardens were damaged by wind and rain. The Company also found that Khoikhoi herders were often unwilling to sell as many cattle as the Dutch wanted, because cattle were the basis of their wealth.

Van Riebeeck was instructed not to make war with the Khoikhoi and not to enslave them. But he soon wrote in his journal of seizing their cattle, and the Company did not respect Khoikhoi rights to the land. The VOC employees were not meant to settle permanently; they worked for the Company and were expected to return home. This changed in 1657, as the next lessons show.

The questions for this section are with you now: why the Cape was chosen, the arrival of Van Riebeeck, the fort and the Company's Garden, and the difficulties of the first years.

## Subtopic: The Impact on the Khoikhoi

For the Khoikhoi, the arrival of the Dutch at first seemed to be a continuation of the trade with passing ships. But the station was permanent, and its demands kept growing.

Trade became unequal. The Dutch wanted ever more cattle and sheep, offering copper, beads, tobacco and alcohol, and they tried to control prices. When trade did not satisfy them, some Company employees took cattle by force.

The Company also took land. In 1657, it released some employees to farm on the land along the Liesbeek River, which was prime grazing land used by the Goringhaiqua and Gorachouqua Khoikhoi in summer. The Dutch planted a hedge of bitter almond trees to mark the boundary of the settlement, parts of which still grow in Kirstenbosch. When Khoikhoi herders returned with their cattle, they found their grazing land fenced off and ploughed.

This led to the First Khoikhoi-Dutch War of 1659 to 1660. Khoikhoi groups, led by Doman, a Khoikhoi man who had worked as an interpreter and visited Batavia, raided the farms and took cattle, especially on rainy days when the colonists' guns would not fire. The Dutch retreated into the fort. In peace talks in 1660, Khoikhoi leaders protested that the land was theirs and asked by what right the Dutch had taken it. Van Riebeeck recorded in his journal that they were told they had lost the land in war and would not get it back. This marked the beginning of the dispossession of the Khoikhoi.

Another Khoikhoi figure was Krotoa, a young Goringhaicona woman who lived in Van Riebeeck's household, learnt Dutch and Portuguese and became an important interpreter. Her life between two worlds ended tragically, and she is remembered today as a symbol of the early encounter.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: saying the VOC came to the Cape to build a colony from the start. Two: forgetting that the Khoikhoi resisted. Three: thinking scurvy was caused by bad water.

The questions for this section are with you now: unequal trade, the taking of land along the Liesbeek River, the First Khoikhoi-Dutch War, Krotoa, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: A Halfway Station

Ships took six months or more to sail to Asia. Sailors ran out of fresh food and got scurvy, a disease from not eating fruit and vegetables. The VOC needed a halfway stop. The Cape had fresh water, good soil and Khoikhoi herders with cattle to trade. So on 6 April 1652, Jan van Riebeeck arrived with three ships. They built a fort and planted the Company's Garden.

## Subtopic: The Khoikhoi Lose Land

The Dutch wanted more and more cattle, and trade became unfair. In 1657 the Company gave Khoikhoi grazing land along the Liesbeek River to Dutch farmers. The Khoikhoi fought back in the First Khoikhoi-Dutch War of 1659 to 1660, led by Doman. In the peace talks, they asked why their land had been taken, and were told they had lost it in war.
