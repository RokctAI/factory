# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the trekboers, semi-nomadic stock farmers who moved inland from the Cape in the eighteenth century. The anchor is an ox wagon creaking across the Karoo, followed by herds of sheep and cattle and a household of family members, enslaved people and Khoikhoi servants. The governing idea is that trekboer expansion was driven by the search for grazing land and independence from the Company, and that it brought both interaction and violent conflict with the Khoikhoi, San and Xhosa.

## Subtopic: Who Were the Trekboers?

The word trekboer comes from Dutch: trek means to pull or to travel, and boer means farmer. The trekboers were descendants of free burghers, mostly Dutch, German and French in origin, who became semi-nomadic stock farmers in the interior of the Cape Colony. They moved with their livestock in search of grazing and water, sometimes settling in one place for years, sometimes moving on.

Trekboers began moving beyond the mountains of the south-western Cape in the early eighteenth century, after the VOC introduced the loan farm system in 1714. A trekboer could claim a loan farm, usually measured as the land within half an hour's ride on horseback in every direction from a central point, about two thousand four hundred hectares, for a small annual fee to the Company. The farmer did not own the land, but in practice the loan farms were treated as family property.

Several factors pushed and pulled trekboers inland. Families were large, often with eight or more children, and each son wanted his own farm. Good farmland near the Cape was expensive and already taken. Stock farming needed little money to start, and cattle and sheep could walk to market, while crops needed transport. Many farmers wanted to escape Company control, taxes and fixed prices. And the interior offered grazing, game for hunting, and opportunities to obtain livestock from the Khoikhoi by trade or raiding.

Over the eighteenth century, trekboers spread north into the Roggeveld and Hantam, north-east into the Karoo and towards the Sneeuberg, and east along the coast to the Zuurveld, by the 1770s.

## Subtopic: Trekboer Lifestyle

Trekboer life was simple and hard. Families lived in ox wagons while travelling, and on their farms often in simple houses made of clay bricks or wattle and daub, with thatched roofs, earth floors and few windows. Some lived in tents or even in Khoikhoi-style mat huts at first.

Their wealth was in livestock: sheep, especially fat-tailed sheep and later merino sheep for wool, cattle, goats and horses. They hunted game for meat and skins, and grew small gardens of vegetables, wheat and tobacco near water. They made their own soap and candles from animal fat, and leather goods, such as riems, leather thongs, and veldskoene, soft shoes made of hide.

Trekboers were largely self-sufficient, but they did need some goods from the Cape: guns and gunpowder, coffee, sugar, tea, cloth, iron and tools. Once a year or less, they travelled by wagon to Cape Town or later to market towns such as Swellendam, founded in 1745, and Graaff-Reinet, founded in 1786, to sell butter, soap, hides and livestock, and to buy supplies.

Religion was important. Many families owned a Dutch Bible and read from it daily. Because churches were far away, families travelled long distances for baptisms, weddings and communion, often combining these trips with trading.

Education was limited. Many children learnt to read from the Bible, sometimes taught by travelling teachers called meesters. Isolation encouraged a strong sense of independence and family loyalty, and suspicion of government interference.

## Subtopic: Slaves, Servants and Labour

Trekboers did not travel alone. They took enslaved people with them, though fewer than the wine and wheat farmers of the south-western Cape, because slaves were expensive. Much more of their labour came from Khoikhoi and, later, San people.

As trekboers moved into the interior, Khoikhoi groups lost their grazing land, water sources and cattle. Many Khoikhoi had little choice but to work for the trekboers as herders, wagon drivers, guides, hunters and domestic servants, often in return only for food, clothing, tobacco and a place to live, or for the right to keep a few animals of their own on the farm. Their skills, knowledge of the land and of livestock were essential to trekboer success.

Although the Khoikhoi were legally free, many were treated almost like slaves. Farmers kept them through debts, by withholding wages and by keeping their children. From 1775, children born on farms to Khoikhoi mothers and enslaved fathers could be indentured, bound to work for the farmer until the age of twenty-five, under a system called inboekstelsel. Many San children captured during commando raids were also indentured.

Some trekboer households included people of many backgrounds living closely together. Some children were born of relationships between trekboers and Khoikhoi or enslaved women. Some of these people later formed communities such as the Basters and the Griqua, whom we meet next term.

Trekboers also formed commandos, armed groups of mounted farmers and their Khoikhoi servants, to recover stolen cattle and to attack San and Khoikhoi groups who resisted.

## Subtopic: Conflict and Stories

Trekboer expansion led to conflict with the people already living in the interior.

In the Roggeveld, the Sneeuberg and the north-eastern frontier, San groups, who depended on game, found their hunting grounds and waterholes taken and the game shot out. They resisted fiercely from the 1770s, raiding farms and killing livestock, sometimes driving trekboers from the land. In response, the colonial authorities authorised commandos, which killed thousands of San men, women and children and captured children for labour. Historians describe these attacks as genocidal: a deliberate attempt to destroy the San as a people in these areas. By 1800, San communities in much of the Cape interior had been destroyed.

In the east, trekboers met the Xhosa in the Zuurveld in the 1770s. Both were cattle farmers needing the same land, and the competition led to the first frontier war in 1779, beginning a century of wars, which we study next term.

Trekboers also developed a strong sense of identity, independence and resentment of Company rule. In 1795, burghers in Graaff-Reinet and Swellendam rebelled against the VOC and declared themselves independent republics, though this was short-lived.

How have trekboers been remembered? In later Afrikaner nationalist history, trekboers were celebrated as brave, God-fearing pioneers who tamed a wild land. Stories of their courage and hardship were told in books and schools. Historians today include other stories: of the Khoikhoi and San who lost their land, of the enslaved and servants who made trekboer life possible, and of the violence of the frontier. A full history includes all these voices.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking trekboers moved into empty land. Two: believing trekboers did all their work themselves. Three: confusing the trekboers of the 1700s with the Voortrekkers of the 1830s.

The questions for this section are with you now: conflict with the San, meeting the Xhosa, trekboer independence, how trekboers have been remembered, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Travelling Farmers

Trekboers were farmers who moved inland from the Cape with their wagons, cattle and sheep, looking for grazing land. From 1714, they could use big loan farms for a small fee. They moved because their families were big, land near the Cape was taken, and they wanted to be free from the Company's rules.

## Subtopic: Life on the Move

Trekboers lived in wagons or simple clay houses. Their wealth was their animals. They made their own soap, candles and shoes, and went to town once a year to buy guns, coffee and sugar. The Bible was very important to them. Their work was done mostly by Khoikhoi servants, who had lost their own land and cattle, and by some enslaved people.

## Subtopic: Conflict

As trekboers took land and water, the San fought back. Commandos of armed farmers killed thousands of San and captured their children. In the east, trekboers met the Xhosa, and the first frontier war began in 1779. Later histories called trekboers brave pioneers; today we also tell the stories of the people who lost their land.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Trekboers were stock farmers who moved inland from the 1700s, with wagons, livestock, enslaved people and Khoikhoi servants. They were independent and self-sufficient, but their expansion took Khoikhoi and San land and led to violent conflict.
