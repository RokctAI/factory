### subtopic_1 — What Is Land Dispossession?
**Assistant:** Didn't some Khoikhoi leaders sign land agreements with the Dutch?

**Tutor:** Yes, there were some agreements, and historians examine them carefully. In 1672, for example, the VOC recorded agreements in which two Khoikhoi leaders were said to have sold the land of the Cape Peninsula and Saldanha Bay area to the Company in exchange for goods such as tobacco, beads, brandy and bread, valued at a small amount. Historians question these agreements for several reasons. The goods paid were worth very little compared with the land. The Khoikhoi leaders may not have understood the European idea of permanently selling land, since in their system land could not be sold by individuals and they may have thought they were allowing the Company to use it. The documents were written in Dutch by the Company, and we do not know how they were translated. Some of the leaders involved may not have had authority over all the land concerned. And in practice the Company did not deliver all the goods promised. So these agreements were used by the Company to make its occupation appear legal, but most historians do not consider them fair or genuine sales. Similar questions arise about land treaties in many colonial settings.

### subtopic_3 — Consequences for the Khoikhoi and San
**Assistant:** Why did so many Khoikhoi move to mission stations?

**Tutor:** Mission stations, which appeared from 1738 with Genadendal and grew rapidly from the 1790s, offered Khoikhoi people something that had become very rare in the colony: a place to live on land that was not owned by a farmer. On mission stations, families could stay together, keep some livestock, grow gardens, learn to read and write, and practise trades such as carpentry and blacksmithing. Missionaries sometimes defended Khoikhoi people against abuses by farmers and officials, and some, such as Johannes van der Kemp and later John Philip of the London Missionary Society, campaigned for Khoikhoi rights, contributing to Ordinance 50 of 1828, which gave Khoikhoi freedom of movement. Of course, life on missions also had rules and expectations: residents were expected to become Christians, follow mission discipline and often abandon some traditional customs. Farmers often complained that the missions took away their labour. For many Khoikhoi, however, missions were a refuge, and some mission communities, such as Genadendal, Elim and Pniel, still exist today. We study Genadendal in the next lesson.

### subtopic_4 — Land Then and Now
**Assistant:** What does it mean that people are reclaiming Khoi and San identity?

**Tutor:** For a long time, many South Africans of Khoikhoi or San descent were not able to identify as such. Under colonialism and especially under apartheid, they were classified as Coloured, and their distinct histories, languages and cultures were ignored or looked down upon. Many families lost knowledge of their roots. Since 1994, and especially in the past twenty years, a growing movement of people has been reclaiming these identities. This includes learning and teaching Khoekhoegowab, the Khoe language still spoken by Nama people, and N|uu, a San language spoken by only a few elderly people; researching family histories; reviving music, dress and ceremonies; and seeking recognition of traditional leaders. The Traditional and Khoi-San Leadership Act of 2019 created a process for recognising Khoi and San communities and leaders. Some activists have also pressed for land, for Khoekhoegowab to become an official language, and for a fuller telling of history. In 2018 a group of Khoisan activists walked from Port Elizabeth to Pretoria to present their demands to the President. This movement shows how history continues to shape identity and justice today.
