# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines Genadendal, the first mission station in southern Africa, and the work of Wilhelm Bleek and Lucy Lloyd in recording the !Xam San language and stories. The anchor is two places of preservation: the church and school of Genadendal, where Khoikhoi people found refuge and learning, and a house in Mowbray, Cape Town, where !Xam men and women told their stories in the 1870s. The governing idea is that both projects helped preserve communities and knowledge during a time of dispossession, while also reflecting the unequal colonial world in which they took place.

## Subtopic: Georg Schmidt and the First Mission

A mission station is a settlement set up by missionaries, people sent by a church to spread Christianity, where they lived among a community, built a church, taught and often provided education, health care and work.

Georg Schmidt was a missionary of the Moravian Church, a Protestant church from Herrnhut in Germany, known for its missionary work around the world. He arrived at the Cape in 1737, and in 1738 settled in the Baviaanskloof, the Valley of Baboons, in the Riviersonderend mountains, about a hundred and forty kilometres east of Cape Town. He worked among a group of Khoikhoi people, the remnants of the Hessequa, who had already lost much of their land and cattle.

Schmidt taught the Khoikhoi to read and write in Dutch, and began a school, which is considered the first school for Khoikhoi people in southern Africa. He planted a pear tree, which became a symbol of the mission. He also taught farming skills.

In 1742 Schmidt baptised five Khoikhoi converts, including Vehettge Magdalena Tikkuie, who later kept the small congregation together by reading the Bible to them. But the baptisms caused a conflict with the Dutch Reformed Church at the Cape, which claimed that only its ministers had the right to baptise, and some colonists opposed educating Khoikhoi people. Schmidt was forced to leave the Cape in 1744.

The Moravian missionaries were not allowed to return until 1792. When they arrived, they found Magdalena, now an old woman, still holding the Dutch Bible Schmidt had given her, and a small group of believers. The mission was restarted on the same site, and in 1806 it was renamed Genadendal, Valley of Grace.

## Subtopic: Life and Significance of Genadendal

After 1792 Genadendal grew quickly. By 1806 it had over a thousand residents, and by the mid-nineteenth century it was the second-largest settlement in the Cape Colony after Cape Town. Khoikhoi people, freed slaves after 1838, and others came to live there.

Residents built houses, a church, workshops, a water mill and gardens. They farmed, and worked as carpenters, blacksmiths, cutlers, who made knives, and coopers. The Moravians were known for teaching practical skills. Residents also worked on surrounding farms at harvest time.

Education was central. The mission ran schools for children, and in 1838 it opened the first teachers' training college in South Africa, training teachers who went on to teach elsewhere. A printing press, brought in the 1850s, printed books and a newspaper.

Genadendal was important for several reasons. It offered Khoikhoi people a refuge from forced farm labour, a place where families could live together on land not owned by a farmer. It provided education and skills at a time when these were denied to most indigenous people. It helped build a community and identity. And it inspired other mission stations, such as Elim and Mamre in the Western Cape.

There were also tensions and limits. Missionaries expected residents to follow strict rules and to give up some traditional customs. Farmers complained that missions withheld labour. And the residents did not own the land; the church did, which later caused land disputes.

Today Genadendal is a living community and national heritage site. In 1995, President Nelson Mandela renamed his official residence in Cape Town Genadendal, in honour of the mission's role in education and human dignity.

The questions for this section are with you now: life at Genadendal, skills and education, its significance as a refuge, its limits, and its heritage today.

## Subtopic: Bleek, Lloyd and the !Xam

Wilhelm Bleek was a German linguist, a scientist who studies languages, who came to Natal in 1855 and later settled in Cape Town, where he became the curator of the library of Governor Sir George Grey. He was fascinated by the languages of southern Africa.

Lucy Lloyd was his sister-in-law, who lived with the Bleek family. She became a skilled linguist herself, and did much of the recording work.

The !Xam were a San people who lived in the dry Karoo and the Northern Cape, around areas such as the Strandberg and the Katkop hills. By the nineteenth century, their land had been taken by colonial farmers, and many !Xam had been killed or forced into farm labour. Some !Xam men were arrested for crimes such as stock theft, often committed because of hunger, and sent to the Breakwater Prison in Cape Town to do hard labour building the harbour.

In 1870, Bleek obtained permission from the governor for some of these prisoners to live in his home in Mowbray, Cape Town, so that he could learn their language. The first were //Kabbo and Dia!kwain; others, including family members and women, came later. Over several years, they taught Bleek and Lloyd the !Xam language and told them stories, history, beliefs, songs, and details of daily life. Bleek and Lloyd wrote it all down in notebooks, in !Xam with English translations, developing a way to write the click sounds.

Bleek died in 1875, but Lucy Lloyd continued the work for many years, later helped by Bleek's daughter Dorothea Bleek. In 1911, Lloyd published Specimens of Bushman Folklore.

The questions for this section are with you now: who Bleek and Lloyd were, who the !Xam were and how they came to Cape Town, and how the stories were recorded.

## Subtopic: The Bleek and Lloyd Archive

The result of this work is the Bleek and Lloyd Collection: more than a hundred notebooks with over twelve thousand pages of !Xam texts, plus drawings and paintings made by !Xam children and adults. It is one of the most important records of a San language and culture anywhere. In 1997, UNESCO added it to the Memory of the World Register, which recognises documents of global importance. It is held at the University of Cape Town and other institutions, and much of it is available online.

The notebooks record stories about the moon, the stars, the rain, the eland and the trickster figure /Kaggen, the mantis; accounts of daily life, hunting and gathering; personal histories, including the loss of land and violence suffered at the hands of colonists; and songs and beliefs. //Kabbo spoke of his longing to return home to his own place and people.

The archive is significant because the !Xam language had died out by the early twentieth century; without these notebooks, almost all knowledge of it would have been lost. The archive helps scholars understand San rock art, by linking images to beliefs recorded in the notebooks. It gives a voice, through translation, to people otherwise silent in colonial records.

But we should also think critically. The !Xam informants were prisoners or poor people with little power, living in a colonial household; the knowledge was recorded and owned by others. Today, descendants and scholars work to return this knowledge to communities.

The archive has inspired many South Africans. The motto on South Africa's coat of arms, adopted in 2000, is written in the !Xam language: !ke e: /xarra //ke, meaning diverse people unite.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking Genadendal was founded by the Dutch Reformed Church. Two: believing Bleek and Lloyd recorded a language that is still widely spoken. Three: ignoring the unequal circumstances in which the archive was made.

The questions for this section are with you now: the archive's contents, its significance, a critical view of how it was made, the coat of arms motto, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: The First Mission

In 1738 Georg Schmidt, a German missionary from the Moravian Church, started a mission for Khoikhoi people in a valley east of Cape Town. He taught them to read and write, the first school for Khoikhoi people. After he baptised some Khoikhoi, the Dutch church objected, and he had to leave in 1744. ## Subtopic: Valley of Grace

In 1806 the mission was named Genadendal, the Valley of Grace. It grew into a big village where Khoikhoi people, and later freed slaves, could live with their families away from forced farm work. People learnt trades like carpentry and knife-making, and in 1838 South Africa's first college for training teachers opened there. Nelson Mandela later named his Cape Town home Genadendal.

## Subtopic: Saving the !Xam Stories

In the 1870s, in Cape Town, Wilhelm Bleek and Lucy Lloyd learnt the !Xam San language from !Xam men and women, some of whom had been prisoners. They wrote down thousands of pages of stories, beliefs and history in notebooks. The !Xam language later died out, so these notebooks saved knowledge that would have been lost. South Africa's motto, diverse people unite, is written in !Xam.
