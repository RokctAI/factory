# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines natural resources, focusing on water, air and soil, and how people use and abuse them. The anchor is a single maize field in the Free State: it needs fertile soil, rain or irrigation water, clean air and sunlight, and it can be damaged by erosion, pollution and overuse. The governing idea is that natural resources are limited and connected, so damaging one, such as soil, affects others, such as rivers, and that using resources wisely, sustainably, is essential for people now and in the future.

## Subtopic: What Are Natural Resources?

Natural resources are materials and features of the natural environment that people use to meet their needs and wants. They are provided by nature, not made by people. Examples include water, air, soil, sunlight, wind, plants, animals, forests, fish, minerals such as gold, coal and iron ore, and fossil fuels such as coal, oil and natural gas.

Geographers classify natural resources in two main groups.

Renewable resources are resources that can be replaced naturally within a human lifetime, or that never run out, if they are used carefully. Examples are sunlight, wind, water, forests, fish and soil. However, renewable resources can be used up or damaged if they are overused or polluted: fish stocks can collapse if too many are caught, and soil can take hundreds of years to form.

Non-renewable resources are resources that cannot be replaced, or take millions of years to form, so once they are used up they are gone. Examples include coal, oil, natural gas, gold, platinum and diamonds. South Africa is very rich in mineral resources, and mining has been central to its economy.

Some resources, such as sunlight and wind, are sometimes called inexhaustible, because using them does not reduce the supply.

Sustainable use means using resources in a way that meets the needs of people today without damaging the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. It means using renewable resources no faster than they can be replaced, and using non-renewable resources carefully and efficiently.

## Subtopic: Water, Air and Soil

Three natural resources are essential for all life: water, air and soil.

Water: all living things need water. People need it for drinking, cooking, washing and sanitation, and for farming, industry, mining and electricity generation. About seventy-one percent of the Earth's surface is covered by water, but about ninety-seven percent of it is salty seawater. Of the three percent that is fresh water, most is frozen in ice caps and glaciers or deep underground. Less than one percent of all water is easily available fresh water in rivers, lakes and accessible groundwater. South Africa is a water-scarce country, with an average rainfall of about four hundred and fifty to five hundred millimetres a year, about half the world average.

Air: the atmosphere is a layer of gases surrounding the Earth, made up mainly of nitrogen, about seventy-eight percent, and oxygen, about twenty-one percent, with small amounts of carbon dioxide and other gases. People and animals need oxygen to breathe, plants need carbon dioxide for photosynthesis, and the atmosphere protects us from harmful radiation from the sun and keeps the Earth warm enough for life. Wind is also used to generate electricity.

Soil: soil is the thin layer of loose material on the Earth's surface, made of weathered rock particles, humus, which is decayed plant and animal matter, water, air and living organisms. Plants grow in soil, so almost all our food depends on it. Soil forms very slowly; it can take a hundred years or more to form a single centimetre of topsoil. Only about thirteen percent of South Africa's land is suitable for growing crops, so fertile soil is precious.

## Subtopic: Abuse of Water and Air

People use resources in ways that can damage them. This is called abuse or misuse of resources.

Water abuse includes pollution and waste. Water pollution happens when harmful substances enter rivers, dams, groundwater or the sea. Sources include untreated or poorly treated sewage from failing sewage works, which spreads diseases such as cholera and causes algae to grow; fertilisers and pesticides washing from farms; waste from factories; and acid mine drainage, acidic water carrying heavy metals that flows from old gold and coal mines, a serious problem on the Witwatersrand and in Mpumalanga. Litter, especially plastic, clogs rivers and ends up in the ocean. Water waste includes leaking pipes and taps, and in many South African municipalities a large share of treated water is lost through leaks before reaching consumers.

Air abuse means air pollution. Sources include burning coal in power stations, especially in Mpumalanga, where many of Eskom's coal-fired power stations are; factories; vehicle exhaust; burning coal and wood in homes for heating and cooking; burning rubbish; and dust from mines. Air pollution causes breathing problems such as asthma and bronchitis. The Mpumalanga Highveld has been named one of the most polluted areas in the world for certain air pollutants. Burning fossil fuels also releases carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas that contributes to climate change.

## Subtopic: Abuse of Soil

Soil abuse damages a resource that takes centuries to form.

Soil erosion is the removal of topsoil by water or wind faster than it can form. It is caused mainly by removing vegetation, which protects soil. Overgrazing, keeping too many animals on the land so that they eat the grass faster than it can grow back, leaves soil bare. Ploughing up and down slopes rather than along them, deforestation, and burning veld too often also expose soil. Heavy rain then washes topsoil away, forming deep channels called dongas or gullies. South Africa has serious soil erosion, especially in parts of the Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal, and eroded soil fills dams with silt, reducing their capacity.

Soil pollution and degradation also occur when too many chemical fertilisers and pesticides are used, when toxic waste or mine dumps contaminate land, and when poor irrigation brings salts to the surface, a process called salinisation. Planting the same crop year after year, monoculture, exhausts nutrients.

Desertification is the process in which land in dry areas becomes degraded and desert-like, often through overgrazing, deforestation and drought, as in parts of the Karoo.

Good practices to protect soil include contour ploughing along slopes, terracing, planting trees and grass to hold soil, rotational grazing and keeping fewer animals, crop rotation, using organic compost and mulch, and no-till farming. Many South African farmers practise conservation agriculture.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking renewable resources can never be used up. Two: believing soil can be quickly replaced. Three: assuming that because the oceans are huge, they cannot be polluted.

## Subtopic: Nature's Supplies

Natural resources are things from nature that we use, like water, air, soil, sunlight, trees, fish, coal and gold. Renewable resources, like sunlight, wind and trees, can be replaced if we use them carefully. Non-renewable resources, like coal, oil and gold, run out once they are used. Using resources sustainably means leaving enough for the future.

Air is mostly nitrogen and oxygen, which we breathe. Soil grows our food, and it takes a very long time to form, more than a hundred years for one centimetre.

## Subtopic: Damaging Our Resources

We damage water with sewage, farm chemicals, factory waste, old mine water and litter, and we waste it through leaks. We pollute air with coal power stations, cars and fires, which causes breathing problems. We damage soil when too many animals eat all the grass, and rain washes the bare soil away into dongas.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Natural resources can be renewable or non-renewable. Water, air and soil are essential for life. People abuse them through pollution, waste, overgrazing and erosion, so we must use them sustainably.
