### subtopic_2 — Water, Air and Soil
**Assistant:** If most of the Earth is covered in water, why can't we just use seawater?

**Tutor:** We can, but it is expensive and has drawbacks. Seawater contains about thirty-five grams of salt per litre, which makes it undrinkable and harmful to most crops. To use it, the salt must be removed through desalination, usually by forcing seawater at high pressure through very fine membranes, a process called reverse osmosis. Desalination plants use a lot of electricity, which in South Africa mostly comes from coal, adding to air pollution and costs. They also produce brine, very salty waste water, which must be returned to the sea carefully so that it does not harm marine life. And because the plants are on the coast, water must be pumped uphill to inland areas such as Gauteng, which is very costly. During the severe Cape Town drought of 2015 to 2018, small temporary desalination plants were built, and some coastal towns, such as Mossel Bay and Richards Bay, use desalination. Countries with very little fresh water and plenty of energy, such as Saudi Arabia and Israel, rely on it heavily. For most of South Africa, saving water, fixing leaks, reusing treated waste water and protecting rivers and groundwater are cheaper first steps.

### subtopic_3 — Abuse of Water and Air
**Assistant:** What is being done about acid mine drainage?

**Tutor:** Acid mine drainage is one of South Africa's most serious water problems, especially on the Witwatersrand, where gold mining has gone on since 1886, and in the coalfields of Mpumalanga. When mining stops, the pumps that kept the mines dry are switched off, and groundwater fills the old tunnels. The water reacts with a mineral called pyrite, producing sulphuric acid, which dissolves heavy metals such as iron, manganese and even uranium. This acidic water can rise and flow out into streams, killing aquatic life and making water unsafe for people, livestock and crops. The government has built treatment plants on the Witwatersrand that pump water out of the old mines and neutralise the acid with lime, so that it does not overflow, but the water is still very salty and further treatment is needed. Long-term solutions include more advanced treatment so that the water can be reused, requiring mining companies to set aside money for rehabilitation, preventing water from entering old mines, and holding companies responsible for the damage. It shows how mining, though important for the economy, can leave environmental problems that last for decades after the mines close.

### subtopic_4 — Abuse of Soil
**Assistant:** How long would it take to replace soil washed away in one storm?

**Tutor:** Possibly hundreds or even thousands of years. Scientists estimate that, under natural conditions, it takes roughly one hundred to several hundred years to form one centimetre of topsoil, depending on the climate, the rock, the plants and the organisms present. In dry areas like much of South Africa, soil forms especially slowly. Yet a single heavy storm on bare, overgrazed or freshly ploughed land on a slope can wash away several centimetres of topsoil in a few hours. That is why soil is often described as effectively non-renewable on a human timescale, even though it is technically a renewable resource. The lost topsoil is also the most fertile part, rich in humus and nutrients, so the land becomes less productive, farmers need more fertiliser, and yields fall. The eroded soil does not disappear; it ends up in rivers, where it makes water muddy, damages habitats, and fills dams with silt, reducing their capacity to store water. South African researchers estimate that hundreds of millions of tonnes of soil are lost each year. This is why protecting vegetation cover and using good farming practices are so important.
