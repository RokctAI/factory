### subtopic_3 — The Kuruman Mission
**Assistant:** How do you write down a language that has never been written?

**Tutor:** It is a long, careful process, and it depends on the knowledge of native speakers. First, the person learning must listen closely to how words are pronounced and identify the distinct sounds of the language. Then they choose letters, usually from an existing alphabet such as the Latin alphabet used for English and Dutch, to represent each sound, sometimes combining letters or adding marks for sounds that do not exist in their own language. They must also work out the grammar: how words change, how sentences are built, and how meaning is expressed. Missionaries such as Moffat relied heavily on Tswana speakers who taught them words, corrected their mistakes, explained meanings and helped them translate. Some of these helpers are named in records, but many are not, which is a gap in the history. Different missionaries working on the same language sometimes chose different spellings, so written Setswana in Botswana and South Africa developed slightly different conventions, which were later standardised. Writing a language down has big effects: it makes schooling, books and newspapers possible, but it can also fix one dialect as the standard. Setswana today has a rich written literature, including novels, poetry and newspapers.

### subtopic_4 — Relationships and Legacy
**Assistant:** Why was Mzilikazi friends with a missionary?

**Tutor:** Mzilikazi was a powerful and shrewd king who had founded the Ndebele kingdom after leaving Shaka's Zulu kingdom in the early 1820s. When Moffat first visited him in 1829, near today's Pretoria, Mzilikazi was interested in learning about the Europeans who were appearing on the edges of his territory: traders, hunters, missionaries and, soon, Boer trekkers. Moffat was a useful source of information and a potential link to the Cape government and to traders who could supply goods, including guns. Mzilikazi also seems to have genuinely liked and trusted Moffat as a person; Moffat treated him with respect, kept his word, and did not appear to want land. Their friendship lasted for decades, and Moffat visited him several times, including in his new kingdom in present-day Zimbabwe after the Ndebele moved north in 1838. Interestingly, Mzilikazi never became a Christian himself and limited missionary influence over his people, showing that he engaged with Moffat on his own terms. Their relationship is a good example of how African leaders used contacts with missionaries for diplomatic and practical purposes while keeping control of their own affairs.

### subtopic_2 — Robert Moffat's Early Life
**Assistant:** What happened to Jager Afrikaner's people?

**Tutor:** Jager Afrikaner led a community of Khoikhoi and people of mixed descent called the Oorlam Afrikaners, who had moved from the Cape Colony to Namaqualand and the area north of the Orange River in the late eighteenth century, after conflicts with colonial farmers. Before his conversion, his group was known for raiding and had clashed with colonial authorities and neighbouring communities. After becoming a Christian, Jager Afrikaner lived peacefully until his death in 1823. His son, Jonker Afrikaner, led the community north into central Namibia in the 1820s and 1830s, where they became a powerful force, founding the settlement that grew into Windhoek, today Namibia's capital, and dominating trade and politics in the region for several decades, often in conflict with the Herero and Nama. This shows how people displaced from the Cape Colony built new societies far to the north. The story also reminds us that the history of the Cape's frontiers stretches well beyond today's South African borders, into Namibia, Botswana, Lesotho and Zimbabwe, and that communities such as the Oorlam, Griqua and Kora shaped the history of the whole region.
