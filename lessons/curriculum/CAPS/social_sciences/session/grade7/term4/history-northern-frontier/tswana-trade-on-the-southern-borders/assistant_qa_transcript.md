### subtopic_1 — The Tswana World
**Assistant:** Why did the Tswana live in such big towns?

**Tutor:** Historians and archaeologists have suggested several reasons, which probably worked together. One is the environment: much of the Tswana region is dry, with few permanent rivers, so reliable water sources, such as springs and fountains, were precious, and it made sense for many people to settle near them. Another is defence: in times of conflict and raiding, especially in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, large towns could be defended more easily than scattered homesteads, and some towns, such as Kaditshwene and Molokwane, had stone walls. A third is political: Tswana chiefs encouraged people to live close to the chief's court, where they could take part in the kgotla, pay tribute and be called on for labour or war, which strengthened the chief's authority. Trade may also have played a role, as large towns became centres where traders and craftspeople gathered. European travellers in the early 1800s, such as John Barrow and the missionaries John Campbell and Robert Moffat, were very impressed by these towns, comparing their size with Cape Town. Archaeological research at sites like Kaditshwene and Molokwane has revealed the complex layouts of these towns.

### subtopic_3 — Trade with the Kora and Griqua
**Assistant:** Why were glass beads so popular if they had no practical use?

**Tutor:** Beads were far more than decoration. In many African societies, including the Tswana, beads had social, cultural and economic meaning. They were used to show status, wealth, age and identity; certain colours and styles were worn by particular groups or on special occasions, such as weddings and initiation. Before European glass beads arrived, people made beads from ostrich eggshell, copper, iron, bone and seeds, and some glass beads had already reached the interior through Indian Ocean trade for centuries, as finds at Mapungubwe show. When large quantities of brightly coloured European glass beads became available through the Cape trade, they became fashionable and desirable. Interestingly, fashions changed: traders sometimes found that a particular colour or size of bead that was popular one year was rejected the next, which shows that Tswana consumers had clear preferences and bargaining power. Beads could also be stored and exchanged, acting as a form of currency and savings. So it is a mistake to think that people were tricked into trading valuable goods for worthless beads; beads had real value within the society that used them, just as gold or diamonds do today.

### subtopic_4 — Changes on the Southern Tswana Borders
**Assistant:** How did the Tswana lose their land in the later 1800s?

**Tutor:** The process was gradual and involved several pressures. From the 1830s, Boer trekkers moved into the highveld, and after defeating Mzilikazi's Ndebele in 1837, they claimed large areas of land that had been occupied or used by Tswana chiefdoms, forming the South African Republic, the Transvaal. Some Tswana chiefdoms were forced to provide labour or were pushed off their land. The discovery of diamonds near Kimberley in 1867 led to British annexation of Griqualand West in 1871, which included land claimed by southern Tswana chiefs. In the 1880s, Boer volunteers set up small republics, Stellaland and Goshen, on Tswana land. Tswana leaders, such as Montshiwa of the Barolong and Mankurwane of the Batlhaping, appealed to Britain for protection. Partly to block the Boers and keep the road to the north open for British expansion, Britain declared British Bechuanaland, south of the Molopo River, a colony in 1885, and annexed it to the Cape in 1895, and declared the Bechuanaland Protectorate to the north. In British Bechuanaland, much land was given to white settlers, and Tswana reserves were created. The Protectorate later became independent Botswana in 1966.
