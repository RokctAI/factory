# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines missionaries and traders on the northern frontier, with a case study of Robert Moffat, who lived from 1795 to 1883, at Kuruman. The anchor is the Kuruman Eye, a strong spring of clear water in the dry Northern Cape, beside which Moffat built a mission with a stone church, irrigated gardens and a printing press. The governing idea is that missionaries such as Moffat brought literacy, new skills and Christianity, and served as go-betweens, but that their work was also part of a wider process of cultural change and colonial expansion that had complex and contested effects on African societies.

## Subtopic: Missionaries and Traders on the Frontier

Missionaries were people sent by churches to spread Christianity. In the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, many missionary societies were founded in Europe and America, inspired by an evangelical religious revival. The London Missionary Society, LMS, founded in 1795, was especially active in southern Africa, together with Methodists, the Glasgow Missionary Society, the Paris Evangelical Missionary Society, which worked with Moshoeshoe in Lesotho, and German, Scandinavian and American societies.

On the northern frontier, the LMS worked among the Griqua at Griquatown from 1801 and among the Batlhaping from 1816.

Missionaries did more than preach. They set up schools and taught reading and writing, often translating the Bible and other texts into African languages and creating written forms of these languages. They introduced new crops, tools, irrigation and building methods. Some offered basic medical care. They often acted as interpreters, advisers to chiefs and go-betweens with the colonial government and traders. Some, like John Philip, campaigned for the rights of indigenous people.

Traders, meanwhile, travelled north with wagons loaded with goods and returned with ivory, skins and cattle. Missions often became trading centres, with traders stopping there, and missionaries sometimes encouraged trade, believing that commerce and Christianity together would bring what they called civilisation. Hunters and explorers also used missions as bases.

Chiefs often welcomed missionaries for practical reasons: missionaries could bring access to traders and guns, give advice about dealing with the colony, act as diplomats, and provide skills such as literacy and irrigation. But conversions were often slow, and many chiefs were wary of missionaries' attempts to change customs such as polygamy, initiation, rainmaking and lobola.

The questions for this section are with you now: who missionaries were, missionary societies, the work of missionaries, traders and missions, and why chiefs welcomed or resisted missionaries.

## Subtopic: Robert Moffat's Early Life

Robert Moffat was born in 1795 in Ormiston, Scotland, into a poor family. He had little formal schooling and trained as a gardener. As a young man working in England, he became deeply religious through the Methodist movement and offered himself to the London Missionary Society. Although he had little education, he was accepted, and he arrived at the Cape in 1817.

His first posting was in Namaqualand, north of the Cape, at the kraal of Jager Afrikaner, a Khoikhoi leader known as a feared outlaw who had raided colonial farms. Moffat lived with Afrikaner's community, and Afrikaner became a Christian. In 1818, Moffat travelled with him to Cape Town, where colonists were astonished to see the former outlaw, whom the government had a price on, arrive peacefully. This made Moffat well known.

In 1819 Mary Smith, the daughter of Moffat's former employer in England, came to the Cape, and they married. Mary Moffat was a strong, determined partner in the mission's work for over fifty years, running the household, teaching and raising their children in very difficult conditions.

In 1821 the Moffats went to the LMS mission among the Batlhaping, which had been founded in 1816 near Dithakong, the capital of Kgosi Mothibi. The mission had struggled; the Batlhaping were cautious, and the missionaries' customs seemed strange. In 1823, when a large group of people displaced by conflict approached, Moffat rode to Griquatown to bring Griqua horsemen with guns, who defeated the attackers at the Battle of Dithakong. This increased Moffat's influence with Mothibi.

The questions for this section are with you now: Moffat's background, his time with Jager Afrikaner, his marriage to Mary Smith, his arrival among the Batlhaping, and his role in 1823.

## Subtopic: The Kuruman Mission

In 1824 Moffat moved the mission to a better site beside the Kuruman Eye, a powerful spring that produces millions of litres of clear water every day, even in drought, about ten kilometres from the earlier site. Here he built a mission station that became famous across southern Africa.

Moffat used his gardening skills to dig an irrigation furrow from the spring, watering gardens and fields of vegetables, fruit trees and wheat. The Batlhaping and others learnt irrigation methods, which allowed farming in this dry area.

The Moffats and their helpers built houses of stone and a large stone church, completed in 1838. It could seat several hundred people and is one of the oldest buildings north of the Orange River still standing. The church was built with local stone, timber carried by ox wagon from far away, and the labour of local people.

The mission ran a school, where children and adults learnt to read and write in Setswana, as well as practical skills.

Moffat's greatest work was translation. Setswana had not been written down before missionaries began to do so. With the help of Tswana speakers, Moffat learnt the language, developed a way of writing it, and translated the Bible. He published the Gospel of Luke in 1830, the New Testament in 1840, and the complete Bible in Setswana in 1857, the first complete Bible in an African language translated and printed in southern Africa. He set up a printing press at Kuruman, which printed the Bible, hymn books, school books and a newspaper. Moffat also wrote a book, Missionary Labours and Scenes in Southern Africa, in 1842, which made Kuruman famous in Britain.

Kuruman became a base for traders, hunters and explorers going north, including David Livingstone, who arrived in 1841 and married Moffat's daughter Mary in 1845.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Kuruman Eye, irrigation, the stone church of 1838, the school, the Setswana Bible and printing press, and Kuruman as a base for travellers.

## Subtopic: Relationships and Legacy

Moffat built relationships with many African leaders. Besides Mothibi of the Batlhaping and Griqua leaders, he became a friend of Mzilikazi, king of the Ndebele, whom he first visited in 1829 near today's Pretoria and visited several more times, even after the Ndebele moved north to today's Zimbabwe. Mzilikazi trusted Moffat, which helped Moffat's son John and other missionaries establish a mission among the Ndebele at Inyati in 1859.

Conversions at Kuruman were slow at first; for years few people joined the church. Over time a Christian community grew. Some Tswana valued literacy, irrigation and new skills, and some Tswana Christians became teachers and evangelists who spread Christianity further.

Moffat and Mary left Kuruman in 1870 after about fifty years, and returned to Britain, where Mary died in 1871 and Robert in 1883.

How should we judge Moffat's legacy? Many people honour his achievements: the Setswana Bible, which helped standardise written Setswana and spread literacy; the school and printing press; irrigation and agriculture; and his role as a peacemaker. The Moffat Mission at Kuruman is today a heritage site.

Historians also raise critical questions. Like most missionaries of his time, Moffat believed European culture was superior, and he often described African customs and beliefs negatively, for example opposing rainmakers and traditional healers. Missionaries encouraged changes in dress, family life and customs that disrupted Tswana society. Missions also helped open the interior to traders, hunters and later colonial expansion; some missionaries supported British annexation. Today historians try to see missionaries as complex figures, and also emphasise the role of African people, such as interpreters, teachers and converts, who shaped how Christianity was received and adapted.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking Moffat translated the Bible alone without Tswana help. Two: believing chiefs welcomed missionaries only for religious reasons. Three: seeing missionaries as either simply heroes or simply villains.

The questions for this section are with you now: Moffat's relationship with Mzilikazi, the slow growth of the church, his later life, and different views of his legacy, and the error museum.
