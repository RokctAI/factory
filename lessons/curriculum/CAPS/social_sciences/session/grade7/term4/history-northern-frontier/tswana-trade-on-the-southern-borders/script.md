# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the southern borders of the Tswana world and the trade between the Tswana and the Kora and Griqua. The anchor is Dithakong, the Batlhaping capital visited by travellers in 1801, who estimated that it had as many people as Cape Town at the time. The governing idea is that the Tswana were skilled farmers, hunters, craftspeople and traders with large, organised towns, and that their trade in ivory, skins and metalwork with the Kora and Griqua drew them into wider networks reaching the Cape, bringing new goods, guns, missionaries and pressures.

## Subtopic: The Tswana World

The Batswana are a Sotho-Tswana-speaking people whose ancestors had lived on the highveld and the edges of the Kalahari for many centuries. By the late eighteenth century, the Tswana world stretched across what is now the North West province, the Northern Cape around Kuruman, south-eastern Botswana and parts of the Free State and Limpopo.

Tswana society was organised into chiefdoms, each led by a kgosi, a chief, with a council of advisers and a public assembly, the kgotla, where men met to discuss matters and hear disputes. Important chiefdoms included the Bahurutshe, regarded as senior, the Bakwena, the Bangwaketse, the Barolong and, on the southern border, the Batlhaping, who split from the Barolong in the eighteenth century.

A remarkable feature of the Tswana world was its large towns. Unlike many southern African farmers, who lived in scattered homesteads, the Tswana lived in large, dense settlements, divided into wards, each with its own headman, around the kgosi's court and cattle enclosures. Towns such as Dithakong of the Batlhaping, Kaditshwene of the Bahurutshe, near today's Zeerust, and Molokwane of the Bakwena, near Rustenburg, had thousands of people. Travellers in 1801 estimated Dithakong's population at about ten to fifteen thousand. Some historians link these large towns to the dry environment, which made it sensible to settle near reliable water sources, and to the need for defence.

Around the towns lay fields, where women grew sorghum, beans, melons and pumpkins, and further out, cattle posts, where young men herded cattle on distant grazing land.

## Subtopic: Tswana Products and Crafts

The Tswana produced many goods that were valued in trade.

Hunting products: the Kalahari and highveld were rich in game. Tswana hunters, and the San who often hunted for or with them, hunted elephants for ivory, and many other animals, such as antelope, giraffe, jackal and lynx, for meat, hides, skins and furs. Ostrich feathers and eggshells were also collected.

Karosses: one of the most famous Tswana products was the kaross, a cloak or blanket made of carefully sewn animal furs, especially from jackal, wildcat and other small animals. Tswana craftspeople were renowned for the fine stitching and patterns of their karosses, which were warm in the cold highveld winters and were highly valued in trade, including in the Cape Colony.

Metalwork: the Tswana were skilled metalworkers. They mined and smelted iron ore to make hoes, axes, spearheads, knives and needles. They also mined and worked copper, for example from deposits in the western Transvaal and the region near today's Botswana, making copper beads, bracelets, earrings and wire. Metalworkers, often specialists, held special knowledge and status. Archaeologists have found remains of furnaces and mines in many places in the Tswana region.

Other crafts included pottery, woodcarving, basketry and the preparation of medicines and cosmetics, such as a shiny powder called sebilo, made from specularite, a sparkling iron mineral mined near today's Postmasburg, which people mixed with fat and used on their hair and bodies. It was traded widely.

These goods were exchanged among Tswana chiefdoms and with neighbouring peoples, long before Europeans arrived.

The questions for this section are with you now: hunting products such as ivory, hides, skins and furs; karosses; iron and copper metalwork; and other crafts including sebilo.

## Subtopic: Trade with the Kora and Griqua

On the southern borders of the Tswana world, the Batlhaping and Barolong met the Kora and Griqua along the Orange, Vaal and Harts rivers and around Kuruman. Trade between them grew rapidly in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries.

The Tswana supplied: ivory; hides and skins of many animals; furs and karosses; cattle; ostrich feathers; iron and copper goods, such as spears, hoes and copper beads; and sebilo.

In exchange, the Kora and Griqua supplied goods that came from, or through, the Cape Colony: glass beads, which became very popular for decoration and as a measure of wealth; tobacco; cloth and clothing; iron and brass wire, buttons and metal pots; later guns, gunpowder and horses; and sometimes cattle and sheep from the colony, as well as pack oxen and wagons.

The Batlhaping capital, Dithakong, became an important trading town, visited by Kora, Griqua and colonial traders, and travellers from the Cape. The Batlhaping kgosi, Mothibi, who ruled from about 1812, encouraged trade and welcomed missionaries partly because they brought access to traders and goods.

The Tswana were active and skilful traders, not passive partners. They bargained hard, sought particular kinds of beads that were fashionable, and controlled access to the trade routes further north, from where they obtained ivory and other goods from more distant chiefdoms. For a time, the Batlhaping and other southern Tswana acted as middlemen themselves, between the Kora and Griqua to the south and the Tswana and other peoples to the north.

## Subtopic: Changes on the Southern Tswana Borders

The growing trade and contact brought important changes to the southern Tswana.

New goods and wealth: beads, cloth and metal goods became more common, and chiefs who controlled trade gained wealth and influence.

Guns and horses: as guns and horses spread northwards, warfare and hunting changed. Hunting with guns greatly increased the killing of elephants and other animals for ivory and skins, and elephant populations in the region declined sharply by the mid-nineteenth century. Chiefs who obtained guns gained military advantages.

Missionaries: the London Missionary Society established a mission among the Batlhaping in 1816, which moved to the Kuruman River in 1824. Robert Moffat, whom we study in the next lesson, worked there for about fifty years. Missionaries brought literacy, Christianity, new farming methods and irrigation, but they also brought new ideas that challenged Tswana customs and the authority of chiefs.

Conflict and insecurity: in the 1820s, the region was affected by raids and displaced groups from the east, such as at Dithakong in 1823, and by Kora and Griqua raiding. Later, from the 1830s, Mzilikazi's Ndebele and then Boer trekkers moved into the Tswana world, bringing new conflicts over land.

Loss of land: in the later nineteenth century, the southern Tswana lost much of their land. The British annexed southern Tswana territory as British Bechuanaland in 1885, which became part of the Cape Colony in 1895, while the Bechuanaland Protectorate to the north became today's Botswana.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking the Tswana only received goods and had nothing to trade. Two: believing the Tswana lived in scattered small homesteads only. Three: forgetting that trade brought both new wealth and new dangers.

The questions for this section are with you now: new goods and wealth, the impact of guns and horses, missionaries, conflict, later loss of land, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Trading

The Tswana traded ivory, skins, karosses, cattle and metal goods with the Kora and Griqua. In return they got glass beads, tobacco, cloth, and later guns and horses. Trade brought wealth, but guns led to many elephants being killed, and missionaries, raids and later colonial rule changed the Tswana world.
