# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the expansion of trade on the northern frontier through the Kora and Griqua. The anchor is Griquatown, a settlement north of the Orange River, where Griqua traders loaded wagons with ivory and skins for the long journey to the colony and returned with guns, gunpowder, cloth, beads and tobacco. The governing idea is that the Kora and Griqua, people on the margins of colonial society, used horses, guns and their knowledge of both worlds to become powerful traders and intermediaries, linking the Cape economy with the Tswana and others in the interior.

## Subtopic: Who Were the Kora?

The Kora, also called the Korana, were Khoikhoi groups who had lived in the south-western Cape and moved north and north-east during the eighteenth century, as colonial settlement took their land. By the late 1700s, they lived along the middle Orange River, known as the Gariep, and the lower Vaal River, in a region of dry grassland and bush.

The Kora lived in several independent clans, each with its own leader, such as the Taaibosch and the Links. Like other Khoikhoi, they were herders of cattle and sheep, living in portable reed-mat huts and moving seasonally with their livestock along the rivers.

What made the Kora different from earlier Khoikhoi was that many of them had acquired horses and guns through contact with the colony, as servants, herders, hunters and traders. This made them powerful in the interior, where most people did not yet have firearms. Kora groups hunted elephants for ivory, traded, and sometimes raided the cattle of neighbouring peoples, such as the Tswana and the San. They also sometimes fought trekboers and colonial commandos.

The Kora spoke a Khoe language, also called !Ora, which is now spoken by very few people; efforts are being made to record and revive it.

The questions for this section are with you now: the origins of the Kora, where they lived, their way of life, and the importance of horses and guns.

## Subtopic: Who Were the Griqua?

The Griqua were a community of mixed descent, made up of people of Khoikhoi, European, enslaved and other origins, who formed in the eighteenth century on the margins of the Cape Colony. Many were the children of relationships between colonists and Khoikhoi or enslaved women. Colonial society did not give them the same rights as white colonists, and they were often denied land. They were first known as Basters, a term that is now considered offensive in many contexts, although some communities in Namibia, the Rehoboth Basters, use it with pride.

Many of them spoke Dutch, the forerunner of Afrikaans, wore European-style clothes, used wagons, horses and guns, and were Christians. Their way of life was similar in many ways to that of the trekboers, as stock farmers and hunters.

One important leader was Adam Kok I, a former slave who had gained his freedom, and who led a group of these people north to the Orange River area in the second half of the eighteenth century. Under his successors, including his son Cornelius Kok and grandson Adam Kok II, and under Barend Barends, the community grew as other people joined, including Khoikhoi, Kora, San and runaway slaves.

From 1801, missionaries of the London Missionary Society, William Anderson and Cornelius Kramer, worked with them, and in 1804 the community settled at Klaarwater, north of the Orange River. In 1813, the missionary John Campbell persuaded them to take the name Griqua, after a Khoikhoi group, the Chariguriqua, from whom some descended, and Klaarwater was renamed Griquatown. Andries Waterboer, a former mission teacher, became captain of Griquatown in 1820. In 1826 Adam Kok II's followers established another centre at Philippolis.

The questions for this section are with you now: the origins of the Griqua, their way of life, their leaders, and the founding of Griquatown.

## Subtopic: Trade with the Cape

The Kora and Griqua became important traders and middlemen, connecting the Cape Colony with the interior.

From the Cape, they obtained manufactured goods, things made in factories or workshops, especially in Europe: guns, gunpowder and lead for bullets; iron tools and pots; cloth and clothing; glass beads, buttons and copper and brass wire for decoration; and tobacco, which was in high demand in the interior and was used as a form of currency in trade. They also acquired horses, wagons, and pack oxen, oxen trained to carry loads on their backs, which were vital for transport in areas without roads, and saddles and harnesses.

They paid for these goods with products from the interior: ivory from elephants, which was in great demand in Europe for piano keys, billiard balls and ornaments; cattle and sheep; skins and hides; ostrich feathers; and karosses, cloaks made of animal furs.

Trade routes ran from the Orange River area south to the colony, to towns such as Graaff-Reinet and later Colesberg, and to Cape Town. Traders travelled with wagons and pack oxen across the Karoo. Colonial traders also travelled north to trade.

The Kora and Griqua also traded north and east with the Tswana and other peoples, exchanging Cape goods, especially beads, tobacco, iron and later guns, for ivory, cattle, skins, and Tswana metalwork. Because they controlled the flow of goods, especially guns and horses, between the colony and the interior, they gained wealth and power.

Colonial authorities tried to control the trade in guns and gunpowder, fearing they would be used against the colony, but smuggling and unofficial trade were common.

The questions for this section are with you now: manufactured goods, tobacco and pack oxen from the Cape; products from the interior; trade routes; and the role of the Kora and Griqua as middlemen.

## Subtopic: Effects of the Northern Frontier Trade

The trade had many effects on the northern frontier.

Wealth and power: the Kora and Griqua became powerful, with horses and guns giving them military advantages over neighbours. Griqua captains established states, called Griqua captaincies, with their own laws and councils, and Griquatown even issued its own coins in about 1815. Griquatown was briefly the most important settlement north of the Orange River.

Missionaries and traders: mission stations such as Griquatown became trading centres, where traders, hunters and travellers stopped. Missionaries helped spread literacy and Christianity, and acted as advisers and go-betweens.

Defence and alliances: in 1823, when large groups of people displaced by wars in the east, sometimes called the Phuthi, Hlakwana and Fokeng, approached the Tswana town of Dithakong, Griqua horsemen with guns, led by Andries Waterboer and others, and called in by the missionary Robert Moffat, defeated them. The battle showed the power of mounted gunmen, and many prisoners were taken back to the colony as labourers.

Raiding and conflict: some Kora and Griqua groups also raided their neighbours for cattle and captives, contributing to violence and instability on the frontier.

Changes in the interior: guns and horses spread through the interior, changing warfare and hunting. Elephant hunting for ivory greatly reduced elephant populations in the region.

Later, the Griqua lost much of their land. After diamonds were discovered near Kimberley in 1867, in land claimed by the Griqua under Nicolaas Waterboer, Britain annexed Griqualand West in 1871. Adam Kok III led his people on a long trek from Philippolis to Griqualand East, near Kokstad, in 1861 to 1863.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking the northern frontier had no trade, only war. Two: confusing the Kora with the Griqua. Three: believing ivory came from the Cape.

The questions for this section are with you now: the growth of Griqua power, missions as trading centres, the Battle of Dithakong, raiding, changes in the interior, the later loss of Griqua land, and the error museum.
