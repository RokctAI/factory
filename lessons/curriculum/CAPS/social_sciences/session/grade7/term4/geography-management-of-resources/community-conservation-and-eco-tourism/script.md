# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines community conservation projects and eco-tourism. The anchor is the Makuleke community, who won back their land in the northern Kruger and chose to keep it wild, earning an income from eco-tourism lodges and jobs. The governing idea is that conservation works best when local people have ownership, a voice in decisions and a share of the benefits, and that eco-tourism, done well, can create jobs while protecting nature and respecting local culture.

## Subtopic: What Is Community Conservation?

Community conservation, also called community-based natural resource management, means that local communities take part in managing and protecting the natural resources around them, and share in the benefits.

In the past, conservation in South Africa often excluded local communities. Parks were created by removing people, and those living around parks were sometimes treated as threats rather than partners. This led to resentment, and some people saw no reason to protect wildlife that brought them no benefits and sometimes damaged their crops.

Community conservation is based on several principles. Local people should have rights to, and ownership of, land and resources. They should take part in decisions about how resources are used. They should share in the benefits, such as jobs, income from tourism, and sustainable use of resources such as thatching grass or medicinal plants. And local knowledge, such as traditional knowledge about plants, animals and seasons, should be respected and used alongside scientific knowledge.

When communities benefit, they are more likely to protect resources, report poaching, and use resources sustainably. Community conservation can also reduce poverty, create jobs, and strengthen culture and pride.

There are challenges. Benefits may be captured by a few leaders and not reach ordinary members. Communities may lack skills, money or access to markets at first. Wildlife may still damage crops or threaten livestock. And there may be conflict between conservation goals and other needs, such as land for farming or housing. Good projects deal with these challenges through transparency, training and partnerships.

## Subtopic: Community Conservation Examples

The Makuleke community lived in the Pafuri area, between the Limpopo and Luvuvhu rivers, in the far north of what is now the Kruger National Park. In 1969 they were forcibly removed by the apartheid government to make way for the park. After 1994 they lodged a land claim, and in 1998 they won back ownership of about twenty-four thousand hectares. Instead of farming or settling it, they decided to keep it as part of the Kruger, under a contract with SANParks. They partnered with private companies to build eco-tourism lodges, earning rent and creating jobs, and some young people were trained as rangers and guides through the EcoTraining programme. The Makuleke region is known for its biodiversity, including fever-tree forests and baobabs.

Working for Water is a government programme started in 1995 that employs people, many of them from poor rural communities, to clear invasive alien plants, such as wattles, pines and eucalyptus, which use large amounts of water. It has created tens of thousands of jobs, many for women and young people, while increasing water flow in rivers and protecting biodiversity. Related programmes include Working on Fire, which trains firefighters, and Working for Wetlands.

Community-based projects can be small and local too. Examples include school food gardens using compost and water-wise methods; recycling projects in townships, where waste pickers collect and sell recyclable materials, reducing landfill waste; community nurseries that grow indigenous trees for greening; and community-managed fisheries along the coast, such as small-scale fishing cooperatives that receive fishing rights under the small-scale fisheries policy.

## Subtopic: What Is Eco-Tourism?

Tourism is travel for leisure, recreation or business. It is one of South Africa's most important industries, employing well over a million people directly and indirectly. Many tourists come for nature: wildlife, mountains, beaches, whales and flowers.

Mass tourism involves large numbers of tourists, often in big hotels and resorts, buses and crowded attractions. It can bring income, but it can also damage environments through building, waste, water use and crowds, and much of the money may go to large companies outside the local area.

Eco-tourism, or ecotourism, is responsible travel to natural areas that conserves the environment, sustains the wellbeing of local people, and involves learning and education. Its main principles are: minimise the impact on the environment, for example by using solar power, saving water, recycling and building with local materials; support conservation, by contributing money to protecting the area; benefit local communities, by employing local people, buying local products and sharing profits; respect local cultures; and educate visitors and hosts about nature and culture.

Eco-tourism activities include guided walks and game drives with trained guides, birdwatching, hiking trails, whale and dolphin watching from land or with responsible boat operators, visiting community projects, cultural tours, and staying in small lodges or community-run guesthouses.

Not everything called eco-tourism is truly sustainable. Some businesses use the label only for marketing, sometimes called greenwashing. Responsible tourists can check whether a business employs and benefits local people, cares for the environment, and supports conservation.

## Subtopic: Eco-Tourism Examples

South Africa has many eco-tourism examples.

Community-owned lodges: in the Makuleke region of the Kruger, lodges built in partnership with private operators pay rent and fees to the community and employ local people. In the Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal, other communities have similar partnerships with private game reserves.

The Wild Coast and the Amadiba Adventures trail: on the Pondoland Wild Coast in the Eastern Cape, community-run hiking and horse trails take visitors along spectacular coastline, staying in village homes and camps, with income going to local households. The community has also campaigned to protect their land from mining.

Whale watching in Hermanus: every year from about June to November, southern right whales come to the bays of the southern Cape coast to calve. Hermanus is known as one of the best land-based whale-watching sites in the world, and even has a whale crier who announces sightings. Whale watching brings visitors and income while encouraging protection.

The Kruger to Canyons area and many private reserves employ local guides, trackers and staff, and some fund schools and clinics in neighbouring communities.

The !Khwa ttu San Heritage Centre near Yzerfontein on the West Coast, partly owned by San communities, educates visitors about San history and culture and provides training for young San people.

Benefits of eco-tourism include jobs, income, skills, pride in culture, money for conservation, and education. Challenges include seasonal and uncertain income, dependence on international tourists, as shown when tourism collapsed during the COVID-19 lockdown, and making sure benefits are fairly shared.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking any tourism in nature is automatically eco-tourism. Two: believing local communities cannot be conservation leaders. Three: assuming eco-tourism always brings fair benefits without good management.
