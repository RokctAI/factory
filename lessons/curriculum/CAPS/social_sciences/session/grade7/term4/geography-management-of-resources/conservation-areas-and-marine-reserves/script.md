# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines conservation areas, including marine reserves: their purpose, location and a case study. The anchor is a map of South Africa dotted with protected areas, from the Kruger National Park in the north-east to the marine protected areas along the coast. The governing idea is that conservation areas protect biodiversity and ecosystem services, support tourism and research, and, in the case of marine reserves, act as nurseries that help fish stocks recover, benefiting fishers outside the reserve too.

## Subtopic: Types and Purposes of Conservation Areas

A conservation area, or protected area, is a clearly defined area of land or sea that is managed to conserve nature and its ecosystem services and cultural values over the long term.

In South Africa there are several types. National parks are large areas managed by South African National Parks, SANParks, for the whole nation. There are about nineteen national parks. Provincial nature reserves are managed by provincial agencies, such as CapeNature in the Western Cape and Ezemvelo KZN Wildlife in KwaZulu-Natal. Private and community reserves are owned by individuals, companies or communities. Marine protected areas, MPAs, protect parts of the ocean and coast. World Heritage Sites are recognised by UNESCO as having outstanding value for all humanity. Transfrontier conservation areas, often called peace parks, cross international borders.

Conservation areas have several purposes. They protect biodiversity, giving plants and animals a safe habitat and preventing extinction. They protect ecosystem services, such as water catchments, which supply rivers and dams. They provide places for scientific research and environmental education. They support tourism, creating jobs and income. They preserve scenery and cultural heritage, such as rock art and historical sites. And they give people places for recreation and spiritual renewal.

South Africa's goal, in line with international targets agreed in 2022, is to protect thirty percent of land and thirty percent of ocean by 2030. At present, about nine percent of land and about five percent of the ocean around mainland South Africa are formally protected.

## Subtopic: Where Are They?

South Africa's conservation areas are spread across all its biomes. Locating them on a map is an important geography skill.

In the north-east, the Kruger National Park, established in 1926 and covering nearly two million hectares in Limpopo and Mpumalanga, is one of Africa's largest parks, famous for the Big Five: lion, leopard, elephant, rhino and buffalo. It forms part of the Great Limpopo Transfrontier Park with Mozambique and Zimbabwe.

In KwaZulu-Natal, the Hluhluwe-iMfolozi Park, proclaimed in 1895, is one of Africa's oldest reserves and famous for saving the white rhino from extinction. The iSimangaliso Wetland Park, on the north coast, was South Africa's first World Heritage Site, in 1999, protecting lakes, swamps, coral reefs and turtle nesting beaches. The uKhahlamba-Drakensberg Park protects mountains and San rock art.

In the Northern Cape, the Kgalagadi Transfrontier Park, shared with Botswana, protects the red dunes of the Kalahari, and the Richtersveld and Namaqua National Parks protect succulent plants and spring flowers.

In the Western Cape, Table Mountain National Park protects fynbos on the Cape Peninsula, and the West Coast National Park protects the Langebaan Lagoon. The Karoo National Park near Beaufort West protects the Nama Karoo.

In the Eastern Cape, the Addo Elephant National Park, established in 1931 to save the last eleven elephants of the region, now has several hundred elephants and extends to the coast. The Mountain Zebra National Park saved the Cape mountain zebra.

Along the southern coast, the Garden Route National Park includes Tsitsikamma, Knysna and Wilderness.

## Subtopic: Marine Protected Areas

South Africa has a coastline of about three thousand kilometres, washed by two very different oceans: the cold, nutrient-rich Benguela Current along the west coast, which supports large fisheries of sardines, anchovies and hake, and the warm Agulhas Current along the east coast, which supports coral reefs and many tropical species.

A marine protected area, MPA, also called a marine reserve, is an area of ocean and coast where human activities, especially fishing, are restricted to protect marine life and habitats. Some MPAs are no-take zones, where no fishing or collecting of any kind is allowed. Others are zoned, with some areas where certain kinds of fishing are allowed under permits.

In 2019 South Africa declared twenty new MPAs, bringing the total to forty-one, and increasing ocean protection around the mainland from less than one percent to about five percent.

MPAs have several purposes. They protect breeding and nursery areas for fish and other species. They allow overfished species, such as many line fish, to recover. When fish populations grow inside a no-take zone, adult fish and larvae spill over into surrounding areas, so fishers outside the reserve catch more, known as the spillover effect. They protect habitats such as kelp forests, reefs, estuaries and the deep sea from damage. They protect endangered species such as African penguins, turtles and certain sharks. They support tourism such as diving, snorkelling and whale watching, and research and education.

Threats to marine life include overfishing, illegal poaching of abalone and rock lobster, pollution, especially plastic, coastal development, oil spills, and climate change, which warms and acidifies the oceans.

## Subtopic: Case Study: Tsitsikamma

The Tsitsikamma National Park was established in 1964 on the southern Cape coast, between Plettenberg Bay and Humansdorp, in the Eastern Cape. It included a marine protected area stretching along about sixty kilometres of rocky coastline and out to sea about five kilometres, making it the oldest marine protected area in Africa. In 2009 it was incorporated into the Garden Route National Park.

Location and features: the coast here has dramatic cliffs, rocky shores, the Storms River mouth with its suspension bridge, and indigenous forest with giant yellowwood trees. The sea is influenced by the Agulhas Current, and the rocky reefs are home to many fish species, including slow-growing, long-lived reef fish such as red stumpnose, roman and dageraad, which are easily overfished.

Purpose and management: the MPA was managed for many years as a no-take zone, where no fishing was allowed. Scientific studies showed that fish inside the reserve were more numerous and larger than outside, and that the reserve acted as a nursery from which fish spread to the fishing grounds nearby, supporting fishers on the rest of the coast.

Conflict and change: local fishing communities, some of whom had fished this coast for generations, protested that they had been excluded without consultation and had lost access to food and income. In 2016, the Minister allowed limited, controlled recreational fishing by local community members in some zones, but scientists and conservation groups objected, warning that this could undo decades of recovery. This debate shows the challenge of balancing conservation with the needs of local communities.

Tourism: Tsitsikamma attracts many tourists for the Otter Trail hiking route, snorkelling and diving trails, the Storms River suspension bridge, and forest walks, providing jobs in the area.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking MPAs harm all fishers, ignoring the spillover effect. Two: believing that conservation areas are only for tourists. Three: forgetting that local communities' needs must be considered.

## Subtopic: Reserves in the Sea

Marine protected areas protect parts of the sea. In no-take zones, no fishing is allowed, so fish grow bigger and more numerous, and some swim out to where fishers can catch them. Tsitsikamma, set up in 1964, is Africa's oldest marine reserve. Some local fishers felt left out, so finding a fair balance matters.
