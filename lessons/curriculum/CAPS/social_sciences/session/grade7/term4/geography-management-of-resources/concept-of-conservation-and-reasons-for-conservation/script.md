# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the concept of conservation and the reasons for it. The anchor is the rhino: South Africa is home to most of the world's rhinos, which were brought back from near-extinction in the twentieth century and are now threatened again by poaching. The governing idea is that conservation means the wise and sustainable management of natural resources, so that people can benefit from them now while protecting them for the future, and that there are environmental, economic, social and cultural reasons to conserve.

## Subtopic: What Is Conservation?

Conservation is the careful, wise management and protection of natural resources, such as plants, animals, water, soil and landscapes, so that they are used sustainably and not destroyed or wasted. Conservation aims to keep ecosystems healthy and to allow resources to be used without using them up.

Conservation is sometimes contrasted with preservation. Preservation means protecting an area or species from any human use, keeping it as untouched as possible, for example in a strict nature reserve or wilderness area. Conservation allows careful use, for example sustainable fishing quotas, controlled harvesting of plants, or tourism in national parks, as long as the resource is not damaged. Most conservation in South Africa combines both: some core areas are strictly protected, while other areas allow sustainable use.

Conservation can happen at different scales. Individuals can conserve by saving water and electricity, recycling and avoiding litter. Communities can manage local resources such as grazing land, forests and fisheries. Governments create laws and protected areas, such as national parks and marine protected areas. International agreements, such as the Convention on Biological Diversity and the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species, CITES, protect species and ecosystems across borders.

In South Africa, the Constitution, in section 24, gives everyone the right to an environment that is not harmful to their health or wellbeing, and the right to have the environment protected for present and future generations through reasonable measures that prevent pollution, promote conservation and secure sustainable development. Laws such as the National Environmental Management Act put this into practice.

## Subtopic: Biodiversity in South Africa

Biodiversity, short for biological diversity, is the variety of all living things: the number of different species of plants, animals and micro-organisms, the genetic variety within species, and the variety of ecosystems, such as forests, grasslands, wetlands and coral reefs.

South Africa is one of about seventeen countries in the world described as megadiverse, because together these countries contain most of the Earth's species. Although South Africa covers only about two percent of the world's land area, it is home to about ten percent of the world's plant species, seven percent of its reptile, bird and mammal species, and about fifteen percent of known coastal marine species.

South Africa has nine biomes, large areas with similar climate, vegetation and animals: forest, savanna, grassland, Nama Karoo, Succulent Karoo, desert, fynbos, Albany thicket and the Indian Ocean coastal belt. The Cape Floral Region, dominated by fynbos, is the smallest but richest of the world's six floral kingdoms, with more than nine thousand plant species, about seventy percent of which are found nowhere else. The Succulent Karoo is the richest arid region in the world for succulent plants.

Many species are endemic, found only in a particular area. Examples include the protea, South Africa's national flower, the bontebok, the Cape sugarbird, and the riverine rabbit of the Karoo, one of the most endangered mammals in the world.

Biodiversity is important because ecosystems provide ecosystem services, benefits people get from nature: clean water filtered by wetlands, pollination of crops by bees, fertile soil, food, medicines, building materials, flood control, and climate regulation by forests and oceans.

## Subtopic: Threats to Natural Resources

Many species and ecosystems in South Africa are threatened. The main threats are often remembered with the acronym HIPPO.

H, habitat loss: the destruction or breaking up of natural habitats for farming, mining, cities, roads and plantations is the biggest threat. Large parts of South Africa's grasslands, for example, have been ploughed or covered by plantations and mines.

I, invasive alien species: plants and animals brought from other countries that spread and out-compete local species. Invasive trees such as pines, wattles and eucalyptus use large amounts of water and fuel fierce fires in fynbos. Water hyacinth chokes rivers and dams.

P, pollution: of water, air and soil, as studied in the previous lesson, including plastic in the ocean, which kills seabirds, turtles and fish.

P, population growth and increasing consumption: more people using more resources increases pressure on land, water and wildlife.

O, overexploitation: using resources faster than they can recover, such as overfishing of species like abalone and some line fish, overharvesting of medicinal plants, and poaching. Rhino poaching for horn, which is illegally traded mainly to parts of Asia, killed more than a thousand rhinos a year in South Africa in several years between 2013 and 2017. Abalone poaching by organised crime has reduced wild populations dramatically.

Climate change adds further pressure, with rising temperatures, changing rainfall, more droughts and floods, and warming oceans.

When a species dies out completely it is extinct. South Africa has already lost species such as the quagga, a zebra with stripes only on the front half of its body, which became extinct in the 1880s, and the bluebuck, an antelope, around 1800.

## Subtopic: Why Conserve?

There are many reasons for conservation.

Environmental reasons: to protect biodiversity and prevent extinction; to keep ecosystems healthy so that they continue to provide clean water, fertile soil, pollination and climate regulation; and to protect water catchments, wetlands and coastlines.

Economic reasons: nature supports jobs and income. Tourism, much of it based on wildlife, scenery and beaches, is a major employer in South Africa. Fishing, forestry and farming depend on healthy ecosystems. Many medicines are developed from plants, such as rooibos and honeybush teas, and Pelargonium and Hoodia have been studied for medicines. Conserving resources is often cheaper than repairing damage, for example clearing alien plants to protect water supplies.

Social and health reasons: clean air and water protect people's health. Many rural communities depend directly on natural resources for food, firewood, medicinal plants, grazing and building materials. Green spaces support mental and physical wellbeing.

Cultural and ethical reasons: natural places and species are part of our heritage and identity, such as the springbok and protea. Many communities have spiritual connections to land, rivers and animals; for example, traditional healers use medicinal plants. Many people believe we have a responsibility to care for other living things and to pass on a healthy Earth to future generations.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking conservation means people may never use any resources. Two: believing that only rare animals matter, while plants and insects do not. Three: thinking conservation is only the government's job.

## Subtopic: What Conservation Means

Conservation means looking after nature and using it wisely, so that it lasts. It is different from preservation, which means not using an area at all. Everyone can help: individuals, communities and the government. Our Constitution says everyone has the right to a healthy environment, protected for the future.

South Africa has a huge variety of living things, called biodiversity. It has only two percent of the world's land but about ten percent of its plants, including fynbos. Many species are found nowhere else. But habitats are being destroyed, alien plants are spreading, rivers are polluted, and rhinos and abalone are being poached. The quagga is already extinct.

We conserve nature to protect plants and animals, to keep water and soil healthy, to create jobs in tourism and fishing, to protect people's health, and because nature is part of our heritage. Many rural families rely on nature for food, firewood and medicine.
