### subtopic_1 — Types and Purposes of Conservation Areas
**Assistant:** Were people moved off the land to create national parks?

**Tutor:** Yes, in many cases, and this is an important part of conservation history. When parks such as the Kruger National Park, Hluhluwe-iMfolozi and others were created or expanded in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries, many Black communities who lived on the land were forcibly removed, often with little or no compensation, and lost access to grazing, farming land, graves and sacred sites. A well-known example is the Makuleke community, who were removed from the Pafuri area in the northern Kruger in 1969. Under the apartheid government, such removals were part of a wider pattern of dispossession. After 1994, communities could lodge land claims. In 1998 the Makuleke won back ownership of about twenty-four thousand hectares, and chose to keep it as a conservation area within the Kruger, earning income from tourism partnerships and lodges. Other communities have similar agreements, called co-management. These stories show that conservation and social justice are connected. Many conservationists today recognise that parks must also benefit and respect the communities around them, which helps protect the parks in the long run.

### subtopic_3 — Marine Protected Areas
**Assistant:** How do scientists know fish spill over from a marine reserve?

**Tutor:** They use several research methods, often over many years. One is tagging: scientists catch fish, attach a small numbered tag, release them, and record where they are caught again, sometimes by fishers who report the tag. In Tsitsikamma, tagging studies showed that most adult reef fish stay in a small area, but some move outside the reserve. Another method is comparing catches: researchers do controlled fishing surveys inside and outside the reserve, measuring how many fish of which sizes are caught per hour, and they also study catch records from fishers along the coast. Underwater surveys by divers and baited video cameras count fish without catching them. Scientists also study fish larvae, the tiny young fish that drift in ocean currents; because larger, older female fish produce far more eggs, a reserve full of big fish can seed reefs many kilometres away. Genetic studies can even show whether young fish outside a reserve came from parents inside it. Together these methods show that well-protected reserves can benefit fisheries, although the size of the effect depends on the species, the currents and how well the reserve is enforced.

### subtopic_2 — Where Are They?
**Assistant:** What is a peace park?

**Tutor:** A peace park, or transfrontier conservation area, is a protected area that crosses the borders of two or more countries and is managed jointly by them. The idea is that wild animals do not recognise human borders; elephants, for example, migrate over large distances, and rivers and ecosystems stretch across countries. By removing fences and cooperating, countries can protect larger, connected ecosystems, allow animals to follow ancient migration routes, share tourism, and build friendship and cooperation between neighbours, hence the name. The Peace Parks Foundation was founded in 1997, with support from Nelson Mandela and others. South Africa is part of several, including the Kgalagadi Transfrontier Park with Botswana, opened in 2000 as Africa's first formally declared peace park; the Great Limpopo Transfrontier Park, linking the Kruger with parks in Mozambique and Zimbabwe; the |Ai-|Ais/Richtersveld Transfrontier Park with Namibia; and the Maloti-Drakensberg Transfrontier Park with Lesotho. Challenges include poaching across borders, differences in laws and resources between countries, and making sure local communities benefit. Peace parks show how conservation can be an example of regional cooperation in southern Africa.
