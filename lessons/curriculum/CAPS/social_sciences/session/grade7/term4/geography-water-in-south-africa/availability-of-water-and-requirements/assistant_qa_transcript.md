### subtopic_1 — A Water-Scarce Country
**Assistant:** Why does so much water evaporate in South Africa?

**Tutor:** Evaporation is the change of liquid water into water vapour, and it is driven mainly by heat from the sun, warm temperatures, dry air and wind. Much of South Africa lies in the subtropical high-pressure belt, where air sinks and skies are often clear, so the country receives a lot of sunshine. Summer temperatures are high in the interior, and the air is often dry, especially in the west, so water evaporates quickly from dams, rivers, soil and plants. In many areas, the potential evaporation, how much water could evaporate if there were an open water surface, is two thousand millimetres or more a year, far more than the rainfall. This is why large, shallow dams lose a lot of water; the Gariep and Vanderkloof dams together lose large volumes every year. It is also why rain from a quick thunderstorm can soak into dry soil and evaporate before ever reaching a river. Ways to reduce evaporation losses include storing water underground in aquifers, using deeper and narrower dams, covering small reservoirs, irrigating at night or early morning, and using drip irrigation and mulch on farms and gardens.

### subtopic_3 — Moving Water: Inter-Basin Transfers
**Assistant:** Does Lesotho benefit fairly from selling water to South Africa?

**Tutor:** Lesotho does benefit in several ways. It earns royalties, payments from South Africa for the water transferred, which form a significant part of the Lesotho government's income. The project produces hydro-electricity at the Muela power station, reducing Lesotho's need to import electricity. It also brought roads, infrastructure, and jobs during construction. However, many people have raised concerns. Thousands of people were affected when their villages, fields and grazing land were flooded or used for construction, and some felt that compensation was too low, came too slowly, or did not replace their way of life. Downstream environments in Lesotho were affected by reduced river flows. A major corruption case, in which international construction companies bribed a senior official of the project, was prosecuted in Lesotho's courts in the early 2000s, and several companies were convicted. Some people in Lesotho also question whether the price of the water reflects its true value to South Africa's economy. The debate shows that sharing resources between countries raises questions of fairness, sovereignty and the rights of local communities, not just engineering.

### subtopic_4 — Requirements and Closing the Gap
**Assistant:** How did Cape Town avoid Day Zero?

**Tutor:** Between 2015 and 2017, the Western Cape had three years of very low rainfall, and by early 2018 the dams supplying Cape Town fell to around twenty percent of capacity. The city announced that if levels dropped further, it would turn off most household taps, and residents would have to collect a daily ration of twenty-five litres from about two hundred collection points: Day Zero. To avoid this, the city introduced severe restrictions, first to eighty-seven litres and then to fifty litres per person per day; banned garden watering, filling pools and washing cars with municipal water; raised tariffs steeply for heavy users; installed water management devices that limited supply to high-using households; reduced water pressure to cut leaks; and ran intense awareness campaigns. Farmers' allocations from the shared dams were cut sharply. Residents responded remarkably, cutting the city's total use roughly in half, from about one point two billion litres a day to around five hundred million litres a day. Some extra water came from groundwater, small desalination plants and transfers from farmers. When good winter rains arrived in 2018, the dams began to fill again. The crisis showed how much water people can save when they understand the situation.
