### subtopic_2 — What Makes a River Healthy?
**Assistant:** Why are mayflies so sensitive to pollution?

**Tutor:** Mayfly nymphs, the young stage of mayflies that live in the water, and stonefly nymphs breathe through gills on the sides of their bodies, which take oxygen from the water. They need water with plenty of dissolved oxygen, which is found in cool, clean, flowing streams. When a river is polluted with sewage or fertilisers, bacteria break down the organic matter and use up oxygen, and algae blooms die and decay, using up even more. Chemicals and metals can also directly damage their gills and bodies. Sediment from eroded soil smothers the spaces between stones where they live and feed. So mayflies and stoneflies disappear quickly when a river becomes polluted. Other creatures, such as some worms, leeches and the larvae of certain flies, can survive in low-oxygen water; some fly larvae even have breathing tubes that reach the surface. This is why scientists use these creatures as bioindicators, living things whose presence or absence tells us about environmental conditions. The advantage of bioindicators is that they live in the river all the time, so they reflect the river's condition over weeks or months, while a chemical test only shows the situation at the moment the sample was taken.

### subtopic_3 — Threats to Rivers
**Assistant:** What happened at the Hartbeespoort Dam?

**Tutor:** The Hartbeespoort Dam, built in the 1920s on the Crocodile River west of Pretoria, became one of the most polluted dams in South Africa. Its catchment includes parts of Johannesburg, Midrand and Pretoria, and for decades it received large amounts of nutrients, especially phosphates and nitrates, from overloaded and failing sewage treatment works, as well as runoff from farms and industries. These nutrients caused severe eutrophication: thick blooms of blue-green algae, called cyanobacteria, some of which produce toxins harmful to people and animals, and huge mats of water hyacinth that at times covered a large part of the dam's surface. This damaged fishing, tourism, property values and the water quality for irrigation. In response, the government launched the Harties Metsi a Me remediation programme, which removed water hyacinth and algae, introduced biological control, such as insects that feed on water hyacinth, created floating wetlands to absorb nutrients, and pushed for better treatment of sewage upstream. Progress has been made but the problem returns whenever the upstream sewage works fail. The case shows clearly that the health of a water body depends on the whole catchment.

### subtopic_4 — Caring for Catchments
**Assistant:** Can learners really make a difference to a river?

**Tutor:** Yes, and there are many examples in South Africa. In KwaZulu-Natal, the Duzi-uMngeni Conservation Trust has trained hundreds of young people from local communities as enviro-champs, who monitor rivers with miniSASS, find and report sewage leaks and blocked drains, clear litter and alien plants, and educate their neighbours. Their reports have led municipalities to fix leaks that were polluting rivers for months. Schools across the country take part in programmes run by organisations such as the Wildlife and Environment Society of South Africa, WESSA, through the Eco-Schools programme, adopting streams, testing water quality, and running awareness campaigns. Learners have organised clean-ups that removed tonnes of rubbish from rivers and beaches, and some school projects have been featured on national news. By uploading miniSASS results to the online map, learners also contribute to a national picture of river health, which scientists and officials use. Learners can also influence their families: reducing plastic use, not pouring fat and oil down sinks, and saving water. Rivers are shared by everyone, and young people's actions, multiplied across many communities, can make a real difference.
