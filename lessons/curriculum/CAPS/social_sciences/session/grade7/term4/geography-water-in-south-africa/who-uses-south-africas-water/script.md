# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines who uses South Africa's water, using a pie chart. The anchor is a pie chart in which one slice, irrigation for agriculture, takes up more than half of the whole circle. The governing idea is that a pie chart shows how a whole is divided into parts, that agriculture is by far the largest user of water in South Africa, followed by municipal use, and that understanding water use helps us decide where savings and fairer sharing are possible.

## Subtopic: Reading and Drawing a Pie Chart

A pie chart is a circular graph divided into slices, called sectors, each showing a part of a whole. The whole circle represents one hundred percent of something, such as all the water used in a country. The bigger a slice, the bigger the share it represents.

A full circle has three hundred and sixty degrees. To draw a pie chart from percentages, multiply each percentage by three point six to find the angle of its sector, because three hundred and sixty divided by one hundred is three point six. For example, a category that makes up fifty percent of the total gets an angle of one hundred and eighty degrees, half the circle. A category of twenty-five percent gets ninety degrees, a quarter of the circle, a right angle. A category of ten percent gets thirty-six degrees.

To draw the chart, use a compass to draw a circle, draw a radius from the centre to the top, then use a protractor to measure each angle in turn, starting from the previous line, usually going clockwise from the biggest slice. Colour or shade each sector differently, and add a key or labels with the percentages. Give the chart a clear title, and state the source of the data.

When reading a pie chart, first read the title and key. Identify the largest and smallest sectors. Compare sectors: for example, one sector may be twice as big as another. Describe the pattern using the percentages as evidence.

Pie charts work well when there are only a few categories and they add up to a whole. They are not suitable for showing change over time; a line graph is better for that.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a pie chart shows, converting percentages to angles, how to draw and label a pie chart, and how to read one.

## Subtopic: South Africa's Water Users

The Department of Water and Sanitation estimates how South Africa's available water is used. The figures vary slightly between reports and years, but the overall pattern is clear. Approximately:

Irrigation for agriculture uses about sixty percent of the water. Farmers irrigate crops such as fruit, vegetables, sugar cane, wheat, maize, lucerne, citrus and wine grapes, especially in dry areas along rivers such as the Orange, the Vaal, the Breede and the Olifants, and in areas such as the Sundays River valley and the Lowveld.

Municipal use, water supplied by municipalities to homes, businesses, schools, hospitals and some industries in towns and cities, uses about twenty-seven percent. This includes drinking, cooking, washing, toilets and gardens.

Afforestation, commercial plantations of pine, eucalyptus and wattle for timber and paper, uses about three percent. Plantations are counted because they take up more water from the soil than natural grassland, reducing the flow into rivers.

Industry, factories that use water for cooling, cleaning and processing, uses about three and a half percent, not including industries supplied through municipalities.

Mining uses about two and a half percent, for processing ore and controlling dust.

Power generation, mainly cooling at coal-fired power stations, uses about two percent.

Livestock watering and nature conservation use about two percent.

On a pie chart, irrigation's sector would have an angle of about two hundred and sixteen degrees, sixty times three point six, more than half the circle, while municipal use would have about ninety-seven degrees.

## Subtopic: Comparing the Users

Why does agriculture use so much water? South Africa's rainfall is low and unreliable, and in many areas crops cannot grow without irrigation. Irrigated farming produces a large share of the country's fruit, vegetables and export crops, supports many jobs in rural areas, and contributes to food security. However, a lot of irrigation water can be lost through evaporation from open canals, leaking pipes and inefficient methods such as flood irrigation. Modern methods such as drip irrigation, which delivers water directly to plant roots, and micro-sprinklers can save large amounts. Farmers also use soil moisture sensors to irrigate only when needed.

Municipal water supports health and dignity. The Constitution recognises the right of access to sufficient water. Since 2001, the government's Free Basic Water policy has aimed to provide at least six thousand litres of free water per household per month to poor households, about twenty-five litres per person per day for a family of eight. However, South Africa's average domestic water use is high, about two hundred and thirty litres per person per day, more than the world average of about one hundred and seventy-three litres, because of gardens, swimming pools and leaks in wealthier suburbs, while many poor households still lack a reliable supply. Municipalities also lose a large share, around forty percent, of treated water as non-revenue water, through leaks, illegal connections and unbilled use.

Industry, mining and power generation use relatively little water by volume, but they are economically very important, and they can pollute water severely, for example through acid mine drainage and industrial waste.

## Subtopic: Sharing Water Fairly and Wisely

Water in South Africa is a national resource, held in trust by the government for all people. The National Water Act of 1998 states that water belongs to the nation, not to individual landowners, as it largely did before 1994, when access to water was tied to land ownership and was very unequal.

The Act sets aside a reserve, which has priority over all other uses. The reserve has two parts: the basic human needs reserve, enough water for every person's drinking, cooking and washing, and the ecological reserve, enough water to keep rivers and ecosystems healthy. All other users, including farmers, mines and industries, must apply for a water use licence.

Because water is scarce, users compete. During droughts, municipalities may restrict water use, and farmers may receive smaller allocations. In the Cape Town drought of 2015 to 2018, both city residents and farmers had to cut water use sharply.

Ways to use water more wisely include: fixing leaks, which is often the cheapest way to save water; water-efficient irrigation; reusing treated waste water for industry and gardens; harvesting rainwater in tanks; planting indigenous, water-wise gardens; clearing invasive alien plants; and saving water at home and school by taking short showers, turning off taps, and reporting leaks.

## Subtopic: Who Uses the Water?

Farmers watering crops use the most water in South Africa, about sixty percent. Towns and cities, for homes and businesses, use about twenty-seven percent. The rest is used by forestry plantations, factories, mines, power stations and livestock. So on a pie chart, the farming slice is more than half the circle.

## Subtopic: Sharing and Saving

In South Africa, water belongs to everyone. The law first sets aside enough water for people's basic needs and for healthy rivers; other users need a licence. Poor households should get free basic water. We can all save water: fix leaks, take short showers, catch rainwater and plant water-wise gardens.
