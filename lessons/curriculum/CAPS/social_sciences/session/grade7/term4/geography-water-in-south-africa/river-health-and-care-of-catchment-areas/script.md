# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines river health and the care of catchment areas. The anchor is a group of learners standing in a stream with a net, turning over stones and counting the small creatures they find, such as mayfly and stonefly nymphs, to score the health of their river. The governing idea is that a river reflects the health of its whole catchment, so what happens on the land, in towns, farms, mines and wetlands, affects the river, and that caring for rivers means caring for the entire catchment.

## Subtopic: Rivers and Catchments

A catchment, also called a drainage basin, is the area of land from which all the rainwater drains into a particular river and its tributaries. The boundary of a catchment is the watershed, a line of high ground, such as a ridge or mountain crest, that separates one catchment from the next. Rain falling on one side of the watershed flows into one river system, and rain on the other side flows into another.

The parts of a river system include the source, where the river begins, often in mountains or wetlands; tributaries, smaller rivers that join the main river; the confluence, where two rivers meet; the main channel; and the mouth, where the river enters the sea, a lake or another river. Many South African rivers end in an estuary, where fresh water mixes with seawater, an important nursery for fish.

A river can be divided into the upper course, steep and fast-flowing; the middle course, wider with meanders; and the lower course, broad and slow, near the mouth.

South Africa's largest catchment is that of the Orange River, which includes the Vaal, and drains much of the interior to the Atlantic Ocean. Other important rivers include the Limpopo, the Olifants, the Tugela, the Breede and the Great Fish.

Everything that happens in a catchment, on farms, in towns, in mines, in forests and wetlands, can affect the river. Water running off the land carries soil, chemicals, litter and sewage into streams. So the health of a river reflects the health of its whole catchment.

Wetlands, areas of land saturated with water for part or all of the year, such as marshes, vleis and swamps, are very important parts of catchments. They act like sponges, storing water and releasing it slowly, reducing floods and keeping rivers flowing in dry seasons, and they filter pollutants and provide habitats.

## Subtopic: What Makes a River Healthy?

A healthy river has clean water with enough oxygen, a natural flow pattern, natural banks with indigenous vegetation, a variety of habitats such as riffles, pools and reed beds, and a wide range of plants and animals, including fish, frogs, birds and many small invertebrates.

Signs of an unhealthy river include murky or discoloured water; a bad smell; foam or oily films; thick green algae, caused by too many nutrients from sewage and fertilisers, a process called eutrophication; dead fish; litter; eroded, bare banks; invasive alien plants; and few kinds of animals.

Scientists measure river health in several ways. Chemical tests measure oxygen levels, acidity, nutrients, salts and harmful bacteria, such as E. coli, which indicates sewage pollution. Physical observations include the water's clarity, temperature and flow. Biological monitoring looks at the living things in the river.

miniSASS, the mini Stream Assessment Scoring System, is a simple method developed in South Africa that anyone, including learners, can use. You collect small invertebrates, creatures without backbones, from the stream by kicking and turning over stones in front of a net. Then you identify which groups are present, such as flatworms, worms, leeches, crabs or shrimps, stoneflies, mayflies, damselflies, dragonflies, bugs and beetles, caddisflies, true flies, and snails. Each group has a sensitivity score. Some, like stonefly and mayfly nymphs, are very sensitive to pollution and only live in clean water, while others, like worms, leeches and certain fly larvae, can tolerate polluted water. The average score of the groups found shows whether the river is in natural, good, fair, poor or very poor condition. Results can be uploaded to an online map to help monitor rivers across the country.

## Subtopic: Threats to Rivers

South Africa's rivers face many threats. A national assessment found that a large share of river ecosystems are threatened, and many rivers are in a poor condition.

Pollution: many municipal sewage treatment works are failing or overloaded, discharging poorly treated sewage into rivers, as has happened repeatedly in rivers such as the Vaal and the Hennops. Fertilisers and pesticides wash off farms. Industries discharge chemicals. Acid mine drainage from old mines pollutes rivers such as the Olifants and the Klip. Litter, especially plastic, is washed into rivers from towns.

Invasive alien plants: trees such as wattle, eucalyptus and pine along river banks use much more water than indigenous vegetation, reducing river flow. Water hyacinth, a floating plant from South America, forms dense mats on rivers and dams, such as the Hartbeespoort Dam, blocking sunlight and reducing oxygen.

Changes to flow: dams and water abstraction reduce flow downstream and change natural flooding patterns. Some rivers no longer reach the sea in dry seasons.

Destruction of habitats: wetlands have been drained or filled for farming, mining and housing; it is estimated that half or more of South Africa's wetlands have been lost or damaged. River banks are cleared, and sand mining in river beds damages channels.

Soil erosion: overgrazing and poor farming practices wash soil into rivers, making them muddy and silting up dams.

Climate change: higher temperatures, more intense droughts and floods.

The questions for this section are with you now: pollution from sewage, farms, industry and mines; invasive alien plants; changes to river flow; the loss of wetlands; and erosion.

## Subtopic: Caring for Catchments

Because a river reflects its whole catchment, caring for rivers means managing the whole catchment.

Government and laws: the National Water Act requires an ecological reserve, water left in rivers to keep them healthy. Catchment management agencies plan and manage water in each water management area. Polluters can be fined, and municipalities are required to treat sewage to standards. The Green Drop programme assesses and reports on how well municipalities treat waste water, and the Blue Drop programme reports on drinking water quality.

Programmes: Working for Water clears invasive alien plants from catchments; Working for Wetlands rehabilitates damaged wetlands, for example by building small structures that slow water and stop erosion.

Farmers: keeping buffer strips of natural vegetation along river banks, which trap soil and chemicals; using fertilisers and pesticides carefully; preventing livestock from trampling banks; and using contour ploughing to reduce erosion.

Industries and mines: treating waste water before discharge, and rehabilitating old mines.

Communities and individuals: organising river clean-ups to remove litter; not dumping waste or pouring oil, paint or chemicals down drains; reporting sewage leaks and pollution to the municipality; planting indigenous trees along streams; monitoring rivers with miniSASS; and saving water. Organisations such as the Duzi-uMngeni Conservation Trust in KwaZulu-Natal train young people as enviro-champs to monitor rivers and fix problems.

Schools can adopt a stream, test its health regularly and share results.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking that only what happens in the river affects river health, and not what happens on the land. Two: believing that a clear-looking river is always safe to drink from. Three: thinking draining wetlands has no effect on rivers.

The questions for this section are with you now: the role of government and laws, Green Drop and Blue Drop, programmes, actions by farmers, industries and communities, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Healthy and Sick Rivers

A healthy river is clear, smells fresh and has lots of living things. A sick river may smell, have green slime, litter or dead fish. With miniSASS, you catch tiny creatures under stones. If you find mayflies and stoneflies, the river is probably clean, because they need clean water. If you only find worms and leeches, it is probably polluted.

## Subtopic: Caring for Rivers

Rivers are harmed by sewage, farm chemicals, mine water, litter, alien plants and drained wetlands. We can help by cleaning up litter, never pouring oil or paint down drains, reporting sewage leaks, planting indigenous trees along streams, and checking our local river with miniSASS. Farmers can keep strips of natural plants along rivers.
