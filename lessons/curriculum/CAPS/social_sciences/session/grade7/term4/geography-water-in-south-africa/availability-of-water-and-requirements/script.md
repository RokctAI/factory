# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the availability of water in South Africa and the country's water requirements. The anchor is the Lesotho Highlands Water Project, in which water from the mountains of Lesotho is sent through tunnels into the Vaal River system to supply Gauteng, the country's economic heartland, which has no large river of its own. The governing idea is that water availability in South Africa is limited and unevenly distributed, so the country stores water in dams, transfers it between catchments, and must increasingly reduce demand, reuse water and protect its sources.

## Subtopic: A Water-Scarce Country

South Africa's average annual rainfall is about four hundred and fifty to five hundred millimetres, roughly half the world average of about eight hundred and sixty millimetres. This makes it a semi-arid country, and it is often described as water-scarce or water-stressed.

Rainfall is unevenly distributed. It decreases from east to west: parts of the eastern escarpment and the KwaZulu-Natal coast receive more than one thousand millimetres a year, while the west coast and the Northern Cape receive less than two hundred millimetres. Only a small part of the country, mostly in the east and south, receives enough rain to produce most of the river flow.

Rainfall is also unreliable. It varies greatly from year to year, with droughts and floods common. Droughts linked to El Nino events, a warming of the Pacific Ocean that affects weather worldwide, have hit southern Africa severely, for example in 1992 and 2015 to 2016.

Evaporation is high. In much of the country, the potential evaporation, the amount of water that could evaporate from an open water surface, is much higher than the rainfall, because of high temperatures, sunshine and wind. As a result, only about nine percent of rainfall becomes river runoff, one of the lowest proportions in the world.

South Africa has no very large rivers by world standards. The Orange River is the largest, and together with the Vaal it drains much of the interior. Many rivers in the west flow only after rain, and are called non-perennial, or seasonal, rivers.

## Subtopic: Sources of Water

South Africa gets its fresh water from several sources.

Surface water, from rivers and dams, provides most of the country's water, about seventy-seven percent. Because river flow is unreliable, South Africa has built many dams to store water from wet seasons and years for use in dry ones. There are more than five thousand large dams. The largest by capacity is the Gariep Dam on the Orange River, followed by the Vanderkloof Dam, also on the Orange, and the Sterkfontein Dam in the Free State, which stores water for Gauteng. Dams reduce flooding and allow irrigation, but they also lose water to evaporation, flood land and change rivers downstream.

Groundwater, water stored underground in porous rocks called aquifers, supplies about nine percent of water used, but it is the main or only source for many small towns and rural communities, especially in the dry west and north, for example in Limpopo, the North West and the Northern Cape. Water is pumped from boreholes and wells. Groundwater is replenished slowly by rain, so it can be overused if too much is pumped.

Return flows and reuse: treated waste water from sewage works returns to rivers and is reused downstream. Some towns, such as Beaufort West, have treated waste water directly to drinking standard.

Other sources include desalination of seawater, used in a few coastal towns; and rainwater harvesting, collecting rain from roofs in tanks.

Water is also imported. Through the Lesotho Highlands Water Project, South Africa buys water from Lesotho.

## Subtopic: Moving Water: Inter-Basin Transfers

A catchment, or drainage basin, is the area of land drained by a river and its tributaries. In South Africa, the places with the most water are often far from the places with the greatest demand. Gauteng, which produces about a third of South Africa's economic output and has about a quarter of its people, lies on the watershed between the Vaal and Limpopo systems and has no large river nearby.

To solve this, South Africa uses inter-basin transfer schemes, which move water from one river basin to another through tunnels, pipelines and canals, sometimes pumping it over mountains.

The Lesotho Highlands Water Project, agreed by treaty in 1986, is one of the largest water transfer schemes in the world. Dams in the Lesotho mountains, such as the Katse Dam, completed in 1997, and the Mohale Dam, store water from the high-rainfall highlands, which then flows through tunnels into the Ash River and on to the Vaal Dam to supply Gauteng. Lesotho earns royalties from selling water and generates hydro-electricity at Muela. Phase two, including the Polihali Dam, is under construction. The project has also had costs: villages were relocated, farmland was flooded, and there was a corruption scandal in its construction.

The Tugela-Vaal scheme pumps water from the Tugela River in KwaZulu-Natal up the Drakensberg escarpment into the Sterkfontein Dam and then into the Vaal system.

The Orange-Fish River Tunnel, over eighty kilometres long and completed in 1975, carries water from the Gariep Dam to the Great Fish and Sundays rivers in the Eastern Cape for irrigation and for Gqeberha.

The Western Cape Water Supply System links dams such as Theewaterskloof and Berg River to supply Cape Town and farms.

## Subtopic: Requirements and Closing the Gap

Water requirements, or demand, are growing because of population growth, urbanisation, economic growth, and higher living standards. Climate change is expected to make rainfall more variable and increase evaporation, putting more pressure on supply. The Department of Water and Sanitation has warned that, unless action is taken, demand could exceed supply in many areas within the next decade or two.

Some areas are already stressed. Gauteng depends on transfers; the Western Cape faced Day Zero in the 2015 to 2018 drought, when Cape Town's taps were nearly turned off; the Eastern Cape's Nelson Mandela Bay faced severe shortages in 2022; and many small towns in the Karoo and Northern Cape rely on limited groundwater.

There are two ways to close the gap between supply and demand.

Increasing supply: building new dams and transfer schemes, such as the Polihali Dam; using more groundwater carefully; desalination; reusing treated waste water; and clearing invasive alien plants in catchments, which can increase river flow.

Reducing demand, also called water demand management: fixing leaks in municipal systems; using water-efficient irrigation; water-saving devices such as low-flow showerheads and dual-flush toilets; water restrictions and higher tariffs for heavy users during droughts; and education to change behaviour, as Cape Town did when it cut its water use by about half during the drought.

Protecting water quality is also essential, because polluted water reduces the usable supply.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking South Africa can solve water shortages only by building more dams. Two: believing that groundwater is unlimited. Three: assuming that because it rains a lot in one area, water is plentiful everywhere.

## Subtopic: A Dry Country

South Africa gets only about half the rain that the world gets on average. Most rain falls in the east; the west is very dry. Some years have droughts. Because it is hot and windy, much of the rain evaporates, so only a small part reaches the rivers.

## Subtopic: Where Water Comes From

Most of our water comes from rivers and dams. The Gariep Dam on the Orange River is the biggest. Many small towns and villages use groundwater, pumped from boreholes. Gauteng has lots of people but no big river, so water is brought from Lesotho through tunnels, in the Lesotho Highlands Water Project.

## Subtopic: Will There Be Enough?

More people and bigger cities need more water, and climate change may bring more droughts. Cape Town nearly ran out of water in 2018, a time called Day Zero. To have enough, we can find more water, like new dams and reusing cleaned waste water, and use less, by fixing leaks and saving water at home.

Pocket summary of the lesson. South Africa is dry, with uneven, unreliable rain. Most water comes from rivers and dams, some from groundwater, and some is transferred from Lesotho. Demand is growing, so we must both find more water and use less.
