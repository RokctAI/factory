### subtopic_2 — South Africa's Water Users
**Assistant:** Should farmers be given less water so cities get more?

**Tutor:** This is a real debate, and there is no simple answer. On one side, irrigated agriculture uses about sixty percent of the water, so even a small saving there could free up a lot for growing cities. Some economists argue that water should go where it creates the most jobs and income per litre. On the other side, irrigated farming produces much of South Africa's food, including fresh fruit and vegetables, earns valuable export income, and employs hundreds of thousands of people in rural areas where there are few other jobs. Taking water from farms could raise food prices and cause rural unemployment. There are also questions of fairness: under apartheid, water rights were mostly held by white commercial farmers, and land and water reform aims to give emerging Black farmers a fair share. Most experts suggest a combination of approaches: helping farmers become more efficient so they can produce the same with less water, trading water rights in some areas, reducing municipal leaks, reusing treated waste water, and planning carefully for droughts. Geography helps by showing where water is, who uses it and what the trade-offs are.

### subtopic_3 — Comparing the Users
**Assistant:** How much water does one person actually need?

**Tutor:** For survival, a person needs about two to four litres of drinking water a day, depending on the climate and activity. The World Health Organization suggests that about twenty to fifty litres per person per day is needed to meet basic needs for drinking, cooking and basic hygiene, and that around one hundred litres per person per day allows for good health and hygiene, including washing clothes and bathing. South Africa's Free Basic Water policy of six thousand litres per household per month works out to about twenty-five litres per person per day for a household of eight people, which covers basic needs but is low for larger families. In contrast, the average South African uses about two hundred and thirty litres per person per day, but this average hides huge inequality: some households in suburbs with gardens and pools use far more, while some rural households carry water from rivers or communal taps and use only a few litres per person. During the Cape Town drought, residents were asked to use only fifty litres per person per day, and many people learnt how to save water by reusing grey water, flushing toilets less, and taking very short showers.

### subtopic_4 — Sharing Water Fairly and Wisely
**Assistant:** Why did water use used to depend on owning land?

**Tutor:** Before 1998, South African water law, influenced by Roman-Dutch and English law, gave people who owned land along a river, called riparian owners, rights to use the river's water, and groundwater under a property was regarded as belonging to the landowner. Because colonial and apartheid laws, such as the Natives Land Act of 1913, had given most of the land to white South Africans, especially white farmers, this meant that most water rights were also in their hands. Many Black communities in the former homelands and townships had very little access to water, and millions of people had no safe water supply in 1994. The National Water Act of 1998 changed this by declaring water a national resource belonging to all, with the government as public trustee. It created the reserve to guarantee basic human needs and healthy rivers, and required users to obtain licences, so that water could be reallocated more fairly over time. Reallocation has been slower than many hoped, but the principle that water belongs to everyone is an important change. This is a good example of how history and geography together explain present-day inequalities.
