# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the arrival of the British at the Cape and the expanding frontiers of European settlement in the early nineteenth century. The anchor is the Battle of Blaauwberg in January 1806, when British troops defeated the Dutch garrison north of Cape Town and began more than a century of British rule. The governing idea is that the British took the Cape for strategic reasons, to protect the sea route to India, and that their rule brought new laws, settlers and conflicts as the colony's frontiers pushed into the lands of the Xhosa in the east and the Kora, Griqua and Tswana in the north.

## Subtopic: Why the British Came

By the late eighteenth century the Dutch East India Company was in serious trouble. It was deeply in debt because of corruption, the cost of wars and competition from British and French traders. It was formally dissolved in 1799.

Meanwhile, Europe was at war. The French Revolution of 1789 had led to wars between revolutionary France and much of Europe, including Britain. In 1795 French armies invaded the Netherlands and set up the Batavian Republic, allied to France.

Britain's most important overseas possession was India, and the sea route to India passed the Cape. Britain feared that France would use the Cape as a base to attack British ships and threaten India. The Cape was called the Tavern of the Seas, and whoever controlled it controlled the route to the East.

So in 1795 a British fleet arrived in False Bay. The Dutch Prince of Orange, who had fled to Britain, sent instructions to the Cape officials to let the British in. The Dutch commander refused at first, but after a brief fight at Muizenberg in August 1795, the Dutch surrendered in September. This is called the First British Occupation, from 1795 to 1803.

In 1802, the Treaty of Amiens made peace between Britain and France, and the Cape was returned to the Netherlands, now the Batavian Republic, which ruled from 1803 to 1806. The Batavian administrators introduced some reforms, such as religious tolerance.

But war broke out again, and in January 1806 a British force landed at Blaauwberg, north of Cape Town, defeated the Batavian troops at the Battle of Blaauwberg, and took the Cape again. This Second British Occupation became permanent; in 1814, the Netherlands formally ceded the Cape to Britain.

The questions for this section are with you now: the collapse of the VOC, the wars in Europe, why the Cape mattered to Britain, the first occupation, Batavian rule and the Battle of Blaauwberg.

## Subtopic: The Cape Under British Rule

The British took over a colony of about twenty-five thousand free burghers, about thirty thousand enslaved people, and about twenty thousand Khoikhoi, as well as free blacks, in an area stretching to the Great Fish River in the east and the Orange River region in the north.

At first, the British kept many Dutch laws and officials, and Dutch remained the language of the courts and government. Gradually, they made the Cape more British. English became the official language of government in 1822 and of the courts in 1828. British currency, pounds, shillings and pence, replaced the Dutch rixdollar. Laws were changed, and new courts, including circuit courts that travelled to the countryside from 1811, were introduced.

The British governed through a governor appointed by London, who held great power. Governors such as the Earl of Caledon, Sir John Cradock and Lord Charles Somerset, governor from 1814 to 1826, shaped the early period. A Legislative Council was created in 1834, but most people had no vote.

The economy changed. The British ended the VOC's trade monopolies and opened the Cape to British trade. Wine exports to Britain grew for a while because of lower taxes. Later, wool from merino sheep became the colony's most important export.

British humanitarian and evangelical ideas, especially those of the anti-slavery movement and missionary societies such as the London Missionary Society, influenced policies. This led to reforms affecting slaves and Khoikhoi, which caused tension with Dutch-speaking farmers.

The questions for this section are with you now: the population of the colony in 1806, how the Cape became more British, government by governors, economic changes, and humanitarian influences.

## Subtopic: Laws Affecting Khoikhoi and Slaves

British rule brought new laws that affected the Khoikhoi and enslaved people, some restricting them and some giving them more rights.

The Caledon Code of 1809, introduced by Governor Caledon, required every Khoikhoi person to have a fixed place of residence and to carry a pass, signed by a farmer or official, if they wanted to move from one district to another. Anyone without a pass could be arrested as a vagrant and forced to work. The code also required written contracts and some protection of wages, but in practice it tied Khoikhoi workers to farms and gave farmers great control over them.

In 1812 an apprenticeship law allowed farmers to indenture Khoikhoi children who had grown up on their farms for ten years from the age of eight, until eighteen, keeping families tied to the farm.

Missionaries, especially Dr John Philip of the London Missionary Society, campaigned against these laws. In 1828, Ordinance 50 was passed, which abolished the pass laws for Khoikhoi and free blacks, gave them the right to move freely, to own land and to choose their employers, and declared them equal before the law with white colonists. This was a major change, although many Khoikhoi remained poor and landless, and farmers complained of labour shortages. In 1829, the Kat River Settlement was established on the eastern frontier, where Khoikhoi families were given land to farm, partly to act as a buffer against the Xhosa.

The slave trade had ended in 1807 to 1808, and in the 1820s British reforms limited punishments of slaves, as we saw in Term 3, leading to abolition in 1834.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Caledon Code of 1809, the apprenticeship law of 1812, Ordinance 50 of 1828, and the Kat River Settlement.

## Subtopic: Expanding Frontiers

Under British rule, the frontiers of European settlement kept expanding in two main directions.

The eastern frontier: in the east, the colony bordered the lands of the Xhosa. Trekboers had been moving into the Zuurveld, between the Bushmans and Great Fish rivers, since the 1770s, and the area was contested. The British wanted a stable, fixed border and tried to separate colonists from the Xhosa. In 1812, British troops expelled about twenty thousand Xhosa from the Zuurveld across the Fish River, and the town of Grahamstown, now Makhanda, was founded as a military headquarters. In 1820 about four thousand British settlers were brought to the Zuurveld. The border was pushed further east over the following decades, through a series of frontier wars, and land between the Fish and Keiskamma rivers was taken. We study these wars and their leaders in the next lessons.

The northern frontier: in the north, colonists, traders and missionaries moved towards and across the Orange River, the Gariep. Here they met communities of Kora and Griqua, people of Khoikhoi and mixed descent who had moved north from the colony, and beyond them the Tswana chiefdoms. The northern frontier was a zone of trade, missionary activity and some conflict. Mission stations, such as Griquatown, founded in 1803, and Kuruman, founded in 1816 and moved in 1824, became centres of trade and influence.

A frontier was therefore a zone where different societies met, traded, worked together, intermarried, and sometimes fought. Both co-operation and conflict were part of frontier life.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking the British first arrived in 1806. Two: believing British rule brought only freedom, or only oppression; it brought both restrictions and reforms. Three: thinking the frontier was a fixed line.

The questions for this section are with you now: the expansion of the eastern frontier, the expulsion from the Zuurveld and Grahamstown, the northern frontier and mission towns, the nature of frontiers, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: The British Arrive

The VOC went bankrupt, and Britain was at war with France. Britain wanted to protect its sea route to India, which passed the Cape. So British ships took the Cape in 1795. It was returned to the Dutch in 1803, but in 1806 the British won the Battle of Blaauwberg and kept the Cape for good.

## Subtopic: New Rules

The British slowly made the Cape British: English became the official language, and British money was used. In 1809 the Caledon Code forced Khoikhoi to carry passes and stay on farms. But in 1828 Ordinance 50, after campaigns by missionaries like John Philip, gave Khoikhoi the right to move freely and equality before the law.

## Subtopic: Moving Frontiers

Settlers kept moving. In the east, the British pushed the Xhosa out of the Zuurveld in 1812 and built Grahamstown, and British settlers arrived in 1820. Frontier wars followed. In the north, traders and missionaries crossed the Orange River and met the Kora, Griqua and Tswana. Frontiers were places of both trade and conflict.
