# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the abolition of slavery at the Cape. The anchor is 1 December 1838, the day apprenticeship ended, when freed people gathered in celebration in Cape Town and on mission stations across the Western Cape, and many left the farms where they had been enslaved. The governing idea is that abolition resulted from a combination of campaigning in Britain, resistance by enslaved people and changing economic ideas, and that, although it was a historic step, freedom brought new struggles for land, wages and dignity.

## Subtopic: Why Slavery Was Abolished

Several forces combined to bring about the abolition of slavery in the British Empire.

The abolitionist movement in Britain: from the 1780s, campaigners organised to end the slave trade and slavery. They included Quakers and evangelical Christians such as William Wilberforce, a Member of Parliament, Thomas Clarkson, who gathered evidence, and Granville Sharp. Former enslaved Africans living in Britain, such as Olaudah Equiano, wrote powerful accounts of their experiences. Women played a big role, organising boycotts of slave-grown sugar and petitions. Abolitionists argued that slavery was cruel, un-Christian and against human rights.

Resistance by enslaved people: rebellions across the British Caribbean, in Barbados in 1816, Demerara in 1823 and especially the large Jamaican rebellion of 1831 to 1832, led by Samuel Sharpe, showed that slavery was unstable and costly to maintain. The successful Haitian Revolution of 1791 to 1804 had shown that enslaved people could overthrow slavery. At the Cape, the 1808 rebellion and the constant everyday resistance also mattered.

Economic and political change: some economists, influenced by Adam Smith, argued that free workers who were paid wages would work harder than enslaved people, and that slavery was inefficient. The Caribbean sugar economy was less important to Britain than before, while industry was growing. The reform of Parliament in 1832 brought in more members who supported abolition.

The British Parliament abolished the slave trade, the buying and selling of new slaves across the oceans, in 1807. Then, in 1833, it passed the Slavery Abolition Act, which abolished slavery itself throughout most of the British Empire, from 1 August 1834.

The questions for this section are with you now: the abolitionist movement and its leaders, resistance by enslaved people, economic and political changes, and the dates of the abolition of the slave trade and of slavery.

## Subtopic: Steps Towards Abolition at the Cape

At the Cape, abolition came in stages.

In 1807 the British abolished the slave trade, and from 1808 no new slaves could be imported to the Cape. The enslaved population could now only grow through births, and slave prices rose. Farmers began to rely more on Khoikhoi labour and on the children of enslaved people.

From 1816 all slaves had to be registered, so that officials could check that no new slaves were being illegally imported. The registers are now an important historical source.

In the 1820s the British introduced amelioration laws, laws to improve the conditions of enslaved people. In 1823 and 1826 ordinances limited working hours and punishments, required owners to provide adequate food and clothing, allowed enslaved people to marry and to have their families kept together, gave them the right to own property and to buy their freedom, and allowed Christian enslaved people to attend church. A Guardian or Protector of Slaves was appointed to hear complaints, and owners had to keep punishment record books.

Many slave owners were furious. They saw the laws as interference with their property. In 1831, when owners were ordered to keep punishment records, about two thousand slave owners gathered in Cape Town to protest, and some refused to obey.

On 1 December 1834, the Slavery Abolition Act came into force at the Cape. It started later than in other colonies because of the time needed to organise it. About thirty-nine thousand enslaved people were legally freed. However, the act declared that they must serve their former owners as apprentices for four more years, until 1 December 1838, working without wages for most of the week.

The questions for this section are with you now: the end of slave imports, slave registration, amelioration laws, the Protector of Slaves, owners' protests, and the start of abolition in 1834.

## Subtopic: Compensation and Reactions

The British government decided to compensate slave owners, but not the enslaved. Parliament set aside twenty million pounds, a huge sum at the time, to pay slave owners across the empire for the loss of their property. Cape slave owners were allocated about one point two million pounds, but this was much less than the value they claimed for their slaves. Moreover, claims had to be collected in London, and many owners sold their claims to agents at a discount. Many farmers, especially in the Western Cape, lost money and some became bankrupt. Formerly enslaved people received no compensation for their years of unpaid labour and suffering.

Reactions among slave owners varied. Many, both Dutch-speaking and English-speaking, felt angry and cheated. In the Eastern Cape, some Dutch-speaking farmers listed the loss of their slaves and the inadequate compensation among their grievances against British rule, and this was one of several reasons why some of them left the colony in the Great Trek from 1835. Their other grievances included Ordinance 50, insecurity on the frontier, and the return of Queen Adelaide Province.

For enslaved people, abolition was a moment of great significance, but apprenticeship was a disappointment. They had to continue working for their former owners, often under similar conditions. Special magistrates were appointed to oversee apprenticeship and hear complaints, and many apprentices did complain about mistreatment.

When apprenticeship ended on 1 December 1838, there were celebrations. In Cape Town and on mission stations, freed people gathered for church services and festivities. This day is still commemorated, and some link the Cape Town New Year carnival traditions to celebrations of freedom. Emancipation Day on 1 December is remembered by many communities.

The questions for this section are with you now: compensation for slave owners, why enslaved people received nothing, reactions of owners, the link to the Great Trek, apprenticeship, and the celebrations of 1838.

## Subtopic: What Freedom Meant

What happened to freed people after 1838?

Many left the farms where they had been enslaved, which showed how much they wanted independence. Some moved to Cape Town and other towns, where they worked as labourers, fishermen, craftsmen, washerwomen and servants, and joined the Muslim community or churches. Many moved to mission stations, such as Genadendal, Elim, Mamre and Pniel, where they could live with their families, keep gardens and livestock, and send their children to school. Mission populations grew rapidly after 1838. A few were able to buy or rent small plots of land.

But freedom was limited. Freed people had no land, no compensation and little money, so most had to work for wages, often on the same farms, for very low pay. In 1841, the Masters and Servants Ordinance was passed, which applied to all workers but in practice controlled farmworkers, making it a criminal offence to break a labour contract, desert an employer or disobey orders. Later versions of this law became stricter. Farmers often paid partly in wine, the tot system.

Freed slaves, Khoikhoi and others increasingly formed a community that was later categorised by the colonial and apartheid states as Coloured, though they had diverse origins.

The legacy of slavery includes inequality in land ownership and wealth, the tot system, and also the cultural legacies studied in Term 3: Islam, Afrikaans, Cape music and cuisine.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking enslaved people were completely free on 1 December 1834. Two: believing enslaved people were compensated. Three: thinking abolition was simply a gift from British campaigners, ignoring resistance by the enslaved.

The questions for this section are with you now: where freed people went, mission stations, the limits of freedom, the Masters and Servants Ordinance, legacies of slavery, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, in plain words, same facts: why slavery ended, how it ended at the Cape, and what freedom meant.

## Subtopic: Why Slavery Ended

In Britain, campaigners like William Wilberforce and formerly enslaved people like Olaudah Equiano fought to end slavery, saying it was cruel and wrong. Enslaved people rebelled in places like Jamaica. Some people also argued that paid workers were better than slaves. Britain ended the slave trade in 1807, and in 1833 passed a law to end slavery.

## Subtopic: Ending Slavery at the Cape

No new slaves could be brought to the Cape after 1808. In the 1820s new laws limited punishments and let slaves marry and complain to a Protector of Slaves. On 1 December 1834 slavery was abolished, but freed people had to work for their old owners as apprentices for four more years. Owners got money as compensation; the enslaved got nothing.

## Subtopic: Free at Last?

On 1 December 1838 apprenticeship ended, and freed people celebrated. Many left the farms for towns and mission stations like Genadendal. But they had no land and no money, so most had to work for low wages, and new laws punished workers who left their jobs. Freedom was real, but hard.
