### subtopic_3 — The Wars of the Early 1800s
**Assistant:** How could a few hundred soldiers defeat thousands of warriors at Grahamstown?

**Tutor:** Several factors explain the outcome. The British defenders had muskets, rifles and artillery, cannons firing grapeshot, small iron balls that spread out and caused terrible casualties at close range. The Xhosa fought mainly with assegais, spears that were very effective in close combat but had to be thrown or used at short distance. Makhanda's warriors had to cross open ground to reach the British positions, where they were exposed to gunfire. A group of Khoikhoi marksmen, led by Jan Boesak, arrived during the battle and added accurate fire. Some accounts suggest that the Xhosa attack was delayed, giving the defenders time to prepare. The Xhosa showed great bravery, charging repeatedly into the gunfire, and many were killed; estimates of Xhosa deaths range from several hundred to over a thousand, while British losses were very small. The battle showed the enormous advantage that firearms gave colonial forces, a pattern seen in many colonial wars in Africa. In later wars, the Xhosa adapted their tactics, avoiding open battles and using guerrilla warfare in the forests and mountains, which was much more successful, as Maqoma showed.

### subtopic_4 — Later Wars and Consequences
**Assistant:** Why did the Xhosa kill their own cattle in 1856?

**Tutor:** The cattle-killing is one of the most tragic and debated events in South African history. In 1856, a teenage girl named Nongqawuse, living near the Gxarha River, said that she had received messages from the ancestors: if the Xhosa killed all their cattle and destroyed their crops, the dead would rise, new healthy cattle and full grain pits would appear, and the colonists would be driven into the sea. Her uncle Mhlakaza spread the prophecy, and King Sarhili, Hintsa's son, supported it. To understand why many believed, historians point to the desperate situation: the Xhosa had lost much land and many people in the wars, and a lung sickness epidemic, brought by imported cattle, was killing their herds. A prophecy promising renewal offered hope. Not everyone believed; some chiefs and many people refused, which caused bitter divisions. When the promised day passed with no miracle, famine followed. An estimated forty thousand or more people died, and many thousands left their homes to seek food in the colony, where Governor Sir George Grey's administration required them to sign labour contracts. Grey used the crisis to take more land. The event shows how war, disease and colonialism can push a society to desperation.

### subtopic_2 — Causes of the Wars
**Assistant:** Were the wars really just about land?

**Tutor:** Land was the central issue, but the wars were also about other connected things: cattle, which were wealth and identity; political independence, the right of Xhosa chiefs to rule their own people without colonial interference; and dignity and justice, as Xhosa people responded to violence such as the killing of Hintsa, the reprisal system and the expulsion from the Zuurveld. Religion also played a role: Xhosa prophets such as Makhanda and Mlanjeni drew on both Xhosa beliefs and Christian ideas to inspire resistance and offer hope. On the colonial side, motives included land and cattle, security, the desire for labour, and later British imperial ideas about spreading what they called civilisation and Christianity. Some historians emphasise economic motives, others political or cultural ones. Most agree that the wars were a struggle over who would control land and people in the Eastern Cape. This is why some historians prefer the name Wars of Dispossession, which emphasises what the Xhosa lost, while others use Frontier Wars, which emphasises the meeting of two societies. Thinking about the names we give to events is an important part of studying history.
