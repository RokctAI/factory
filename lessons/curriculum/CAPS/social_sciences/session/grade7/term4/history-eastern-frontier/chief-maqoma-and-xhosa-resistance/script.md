# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a case study of Chief Maqoma, who lived from 1798 to 1873, and Xhosa resistance to British rule. The anchor is the Amathole mountains and the Waterkloof forest, where Maqoma's forces held out against British troops for months during the war of 1850 to 1853. The governing idea is that Maqoma's life shows both the attempts of Xhosa leaders to live alongside the colony and the reasons why many turned to armed resistance, as well as the heavy price they paid.

## Subtopic: Maqoma's Background

Maqoma was born in 1798, the eldest son of Ngqika, chief of the Rharhabe Xhosa, by his first wife. However, under Xhosa custom the heir to the chieftainship was the son of the great wife, who was chosen later, so Maqoma's younger half-brother, Sandile, born in 1820, became heir. Maqoma was the senior son of the right-hand house, a position with its own important status, and when Ngqika died in 1829, Maqoma acted as regent, ruling on behalf of the young Sandile for some years.

Maqoma grew up during a time of great change on the frontier. He saw the expulsion of the Xhosa from the Zuurveld in 1812, the war of 1818 to 1819 and the creation of the Ceded Territory, which took land between the Fish and Keiskamma rivers.

As a young man, Maqoma became known as a skilled leader, warrior and orator, a powerful public speaker. He also had close contact with colonists and missionaries; he traded with colonists, visited Grahamstown, and some missionaries, such as those at the Glasgow Missionary Society station at Lovedale, regarded him as intelligent and friendly. He is reported to have said that he wanted to live at peace with the colony.

In the 1820s, Maqoma and his followers were allowed to live in the fertile Kat River valley, in the Ceded Territory, which colonial officials tolerated for a time. This was land he regarded as his people's ancestral land, and he developed it with homesteads, gardens and cattle.

The questions for this section are with you now: Maqoma's family and position, why he was not the heir, his role as regent, his early experience of the frontier, and his settlement in the Kat River valley.

## Subtopic: Expulsion and the War of 1834 to 1835

In 1829 the colonial authorities expelled Maqoma and his followers from the Kat River valley, accusing him of raiding the Thembu, a neighbouring people. Soldiers burnt homesteads and drove out several thousand people and their cattle. The colony then gave the land to the Kat River Settlement for Khoikhoi families, partly to act as a buffer between the colony and the Xhosa. Maqoma was expelled again in 1833. He felt deeply betrayed; he had tried to live peacefully near the colony, and his people had lost their homes and gardens.

Maqoma's grievance was shared by many Xhosa. Land shortages east of the colonial border were severe, and colonial patrols continued to seize cattle under the reprisal system. In December 1834, after a patrol shot and wounded a Xhosa chief, Xhosa forces, led by Maqoma and his brother Tyhali, crossed into the colony. They attacked farms, took tens of thousands of cattle, and drove colonists back towards Grahamstown and Uitenhage. This began the sixth frontier war of 1834 to 1835.

Governor Sir Benjamin D'Urban and Colonel Harry Smith led a large British force into Xhosa territory. Maqoma's forces retreated to the Amathole mountains, using the forests and steep valleys to fight a guerrilla war, attacking and then disappearing. During the war, the paramount king Hintsa was killed while held by the British.

The British annexed land up to the Kei River as Queen Adelaide Province, but the British government, influenced by humanitarian reports, including evidence from missionaries such as John Philip and the testimony given in London, decided that the war had been caused partly by colonial injustice. Lord Glenelg, the colonial secretary, ordered the province returned to the Xhosa in 1836, and a system of treaties with Xhosa chiefs was tried for some years.

The questions for this section are with you now: the expulsion from the Kat River valley, its causes and effects, Maqoma's role in the war of 1834 to 1835, guerrilla tactics, and the return of the land in 1836.

## Subtopic: The War of 1850 to 1853

In 1847, after the War of the Axe, the British annexed the land between the Keiskamma and Kei rivers as a separate colony, British Kaffraria. Governor Sir Harry Smith treated Xhosa chiefs harshly; at a meeting in King William's Town, he is recorded as making Maqoma kneel while he placed his foot on Maqoma's neck, a deep humiliation. Chiefs' powers were replaced by British magistrates, and Xhosa customs were restricted.

In 1850 Smith deposed Sandile, now the Rharhabe chief. The prophet Mlanjeni had been preaching that the Xhosa could drive out the colonists and that guns would not harm them if they followed his teachings. In December 1850 war broke out: the eighth frontier war, also called Mlanjeni's War, lasting until 1853. It was the longest and most costly of the frontier wars.

Maqoma was the outstanding military leader of this war. From strongholds in the Amathole mountains and the dense forests of the Waterkloof, near Fort Beaufort, his forces fought a skilful guerrilla campaign, ambushing British columns, using the terrain, and avoiding open battles where British firepower was strongest. They held out for many months against thousands of British troops, and inflicted heavy casualties. Unusually, many Khoikhoi from the Kat River Settlement, angry at their own treatment by the colony, joined the rebellion, as did some Khoikhoi soldiers of the Cape Mounted Rifles.

The British eventually wore down the resistance by a scorched-earth policy: destroying crops, burning homesteads and capturing cattle, so that the Xhosa and their families faced starvation. In 1853, the Xhosa chiefs made peace. More land was taken, and the Kat River Settlement was broken up.

The questions for this section are with you now: British Kaffraria, Harry Smith's treatment of chiefs, the causes of the 1850 war, Maqoma's guerrilla leadership, and how the war ended.

## Subtopic: Robben Island and Legacy

After the cattle-killing of 1856 to 1857, which Maqoma had supported, the colonial authorities under Governor Sir George Grey arrested many chiefs, accusing them of plotting war. Maqoma was arrested in 1857, tried on charges connected with a murder, sentenced to death, with the sentence changed to twenty years' imprisonment, and sent to Robben Island, along with other Xhosa chiefs. His wife Katyi joined him there.

In 1869 Maqoma was released, old and in poor health, and returned to his former lands. But he found them occupied by settlers, and when he and his followers tried to resettle there, he was arrested again in 1871, without a trial, and sent back to Robben Island. He died there in September 1873, alone except for one servant, at about seventy-five years of age.

Maqoma's legacy is important. He is remembered as one of the greatest Xhosa military leaders, whose guerrilla tactics in the Amathole and the Waterkloof were respected even by British officers, some of whom called him a formidable opponent. He is also remembered for his eloquence, defending his people's right to their land.

His story connects to later history. Robben Island became a prison for leaders who resisted colonial and apartheid rule, from Makhanda and Maqoma in the nineteenth century to Nelson Mandela and other political prisoners in the twentieth century. In 1978, Maqoma's remains were returned from Robben Island and reburied at Ntaba kaNdoda in the Eastern Cape.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking Maqoma always opposed the colony; he first tried to live peacefully alongside it. Two: confusing Maqoma with Makhanda, the prophet who attacked Grahamstown in 1819. Three: forgetting that Maqoma died as a prisoner on Robben Island.

The questions for this section are with you now: Maqoma's arrest and imprisonment, his release and second arrest, his death on Robben Island, his legacy, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Who Was Maqoma?

Maqoma, born in 1798, was the eldest son of the Xhosa chief Ngqika. His younger brother Sandile was the heir, so Maqoma ruled for him for a while. Maqoma was a brave warrior and a great speaker. At first he tried to live peacefully near the colony, in the Kat River valley.

## Subtopic: Wars

In 1829 the colony drove Maqoma and his people out of the Kat River valley and burnt their homes. In 1834 he helped lead the Xhosa into the colony. In the war of 1850 to 1853, he fought brilliantly from the forests and mountains, ambushing British soldiers for months. The British only won by burning crops and homes so people starved.

## Subtopic: Prisoner and Hero

In 1857 Maqoma was arrested and sent to Robben Island. Released in 1869, he went home but found his land taken, and in 1871 he was sent back to Robben Island, where he died in 1873. Today he is honoured as a hero of resistance. His remains were brought home and reburied in the Eastern Cape in 1978.
