# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines soldiers and officials on the eastern frontier and the British immigration of 1820. The anchor is the landing of the 1820 Settlers at Algoa Bay, where Port Elizabeth, now Gqeberha, grew, as families climbed out of boats onto the beach with their belongings, bound for farms on land from which the Xhosa had been expelled. The governing idea is that the British used military power, administration and settlement together to secure the frontier, and that the settlers' arrival intensified pressure on Xhosa land while also shaping the economy and culture of the Eastern Cape.

## Subtopic: Soldiers on the Frontier

After 1806, the British stationed regiments of the British Army at the Cape, and many of them served on the eastern frontier. Their main tasks were to defend the colony's border, to carry out patrols to recover cattle, and to fight in the frontier wars.

To control the frontier, the British built a line of forts and military posts, especially after the expulsion from the Zuurveld in 1812. Grahamstown, now Makhanda, was founded in 1812 as the military headquarters. Forts were built along the Fish River and later further east, such as Fort Willshire on the Keiskamma River, Fort Beaufort on the Kat River, and Fort Hare, which later gave its name to the famous university, and King William's Town became a military centre after 1835.

The Cape Regiment, later called the Cape Mounted Riflemen, was a regiment made up mainly of Khoikhoi soldiers under British officers. Its origins lay in the Pandour Corps of the VOC period. Khoikhoi soldiers were skilled horsemen and marksmen who knew the land well, and they fought in all the frontier wars. For some Khoikhoi, the army offered an escape from farm labour and a wage. But in 1850 some Khoikhoi soldiers joined the rebellion against the colony, angered by their poor treatment.

Colonial burghers, Dutch-speaking and later British farmers, also formed commandos that were called up during wars.

Soldiers' lives were hard: long patrols, harsh discipline, disease and danger. Many British soldiers were young men from poor backgrounds in Britain and Ireland.

## Subtopic: Officials and Frontier Policy

British officials governed the frontier. The governor in Cape Town, appointed by London, made overall policy. On the frontier, landdrosts and later civil commissioners and magistrates ran districts, collected taxes, heard court cases and managed relations with Xhosa chiefs.

Frontier policy changed often, depending on governors and on debates in Britain.

The policy of separation: early governors, such as Lord Charles Somerset, tried to keep colonists and Xhosa apart with a fixed border, forts and the empty Ceded Territory. Trade was strictly controlled; in 1817 Somerset made an agreement with Ngqika under which stolen cattle could be recovered from any Xhosa homestead, which worsened tensions, and from 1824 trade fairs were held at Fort Willshire, where colonists and Xhosa traded ivory, hides and cattle for beads, buttons and other goods.

The treaty system: after the war of 1834 to 1835, the British government, influenced by humanitarians, ordered Governor D'Urban to return Queen Adelaide Province. Lieutenant-Governor Andries Stockenstrom introduced treaties with Xhosa chiefs from 1836, treating them as independent rulers responsible for their people. Many colonists disliked this policy.

Annexation and direct rule: after the War of the Axe in 1846 to 1847, Governor Harry Smith annexed British Kaffraria and placed British magistrates over the chiefs. Later, under Governor Sir George Grey, from 1854, policy aimed at what Grey called civilising the Xhosa: building schools, hospitals and roads, paying chiefs salaries to reduce their power, encouraging Xhosa to work for colonists, and settling Europeans, including German immigrants, in British Kaffraria.

Officials often disagreed with each other and with settlers, and policies were inconsistent, which added to instability.

The questions for this section are with you now: the role of governors and magistrates, the policy of separation and trade fairs, the treaty system, annexation, and Grey's policies.

## Subtopic: The 1820 Settlers

In 1820, about four thousand British men, women and children arrived in the Eastern Cape in a government-assisted emigration scheme. They are known as the 1820 Settlers.

Why did they come? In Britain, the end of the Napoleonic Wars in 1815 brought unemployment, as soldiers returned home and wartime industries closed. Poverty was widespread, and the British government was worried about unrest. In 1819 Parliament voted fifty thousand pounds to help people emigrate to the Cape. About ninety thousand people applied, and about four thousand were chosen, travelling in parties led by organisers. They were promised free passage and land: one hundred acres per family.

The government also had its own reasons. It wanted to strengthen the frontier by settling the Zuurveld, from which the Xhosa had been expelled, with a dense population of farmers who could act as a buffer against the Xhosa. It also hoped to increase the English-speaking population of the colony.

The settlers sailed in about twenty-one ships and landed at Algoa Bay from April 1820. Acting Governor Sir Rufane Donkin named the new town there Port Elizabeth, after his late wife; it was renamed Gqeberha in 2021. From the beach, the settlers were taken by ox wagon to their allocated farms in the Albany district, around Grahamstown, Bathurst and Salem.

Their first years were very hard. Many settlers were tradesmen and city people who knew little about farming. The farms were too small for the conditions. Wheat crops were destroyed by a disease called rust for several years in a row, and floods in 1823 caused further damage. Raids and the threat of war added to their fears. Many settlers abandoned their farms and moved to Grahamstown, Port Elizabeth and Cape Town to work as traders and craftsmen.

The questions for this section are with you now: who the 1820 Settlers were, why they came, the government's aims, their arrival at Algoa Bay, and their early difficulties.

## Subtopic: Impact of the Settlers

Although their farming started badly, the 1820 Settlers had a lasting impact on the Eastern Cape and South Africa.

Economic impact: many settlers became traders, merchants and craftsmen. Grahamstown became an important trading centre, and Port Elizabeth grew into a major port. Settlers traded with the Xhosa across the frontier, especially at the trade fairs, and later traders travelled into Xhosa territory. In the 1830s and 1840s, merino sheep farming for wool spread across the Eastern Cape, and wool became the colony's leading export.

Political and social impact: the settlers brought British ideas of press freedom and civil rights for themselves. Thomas Pringle, a settler and poet, and John Fairbairn started an independent newspaper, the South African Commercial Advertiser, in 1824, and fought Governor Somerset's attempts to censor it, winning press freedom in 1829. Settlers campaigned for representative government. They built churches, schools and institutions in Grahamstown, which became known for its schools.

Impact on the Xhosa and Khoikhoi: the settlers occupied land taken from the Xhosa, and their demand for more land, labour and security added to pressure on the frontier. Many settlers strongly supported harsh frontier policies and annexation, and some settler newspapers, such as the Graham's Town Journal edited by Robert Godlonton, presented the Xhosa very negatively and demanded more land. Some settlers, however, criticised injustice, such as Thomas Pringle, who wrote against slavery and the treatment of the Khoikhoi.

The settlers have been remembered differently. For a long time, they were celebrated as founders of English-speaking South Africa, with monuments such as the 1820 Settlers National Monument in Grahamstown, opened in 1974. Today historians also emphasise the cost of settlement for the Xhosa.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking the 1820 Settlers arrived on empty land. Two: believing all settlers became successful farmers. Three: forgetting the important role of Khoikhoi soldiers in the colonial army.

The questions for this section are with you now: the economic, political and social impact of the settlers, their impact on the Xhosa, how they have been remembered, and the error museum.
