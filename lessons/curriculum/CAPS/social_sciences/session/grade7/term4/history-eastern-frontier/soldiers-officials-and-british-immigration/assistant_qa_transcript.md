### subtopic_2 — Officials and Frontier Policy
**Assistant:** Why did officials keep changing frontier policy?

**Tutor:** Several forces pulled policy in different directions. The British government in London wanted the colony to be cheap to run and was reluctant to pay for costly wars or more territory. At the same time, humanitarian and missionary groups in Britain, influential after the abolition of the slave trade, pressed for fairer treatment of indigenous people, and their evidence led London to reverse D'Urban's annexation in 1836. Settlers on the frontier, both British and Dutch-speaking, wanted security, land and labour, and they lobbied loudly for tougher policies, through newspapers and petitions. Governors and military commanders had their own ideas and ambitions, and some, such as Harry Smith, favoured forceful action. Communication with London took months by ship, so governors often acted first and received instructions later. Each war also changed the situation on the ground, creating new problems and new demands. Xhosa chiefs, for their part, pursued their own strategies, sometimes negotiating, sometimes resisting, and their divisions affected outcomes. The result was a series of shifting, often contradictory policies, which many historians argue made the frontier more unstable and wars more likely.

### subtopic_3 — The 1820 Settlers
**Assistant:** Did any 1820 Settlers speak out against injustice?

**Tutor:** Yes, a few did, and Thomas Pringle is the best-known example. Pringle was a Scottish poet who led a party of settlers to the Baviaans River valley. He moved to Cape Town, where he ran a school and, with John Fairbairn, edited the South African Commercial Advertiser and other publications. When Governor Somerset tried to censor them for criticising his government, they resisted, and their struggle helped win the freedom of the press at the Cape in 1829. After returning to Britain in 1826, Pringle became secretary of the Anti-Slavery Society, and he helped publish The History of Mary Prince, the life story of an enslaved woman from the Caribbean, an important document in the anti-slavery campaign. He also wrote poems and accounts sympathetic to the Khoikhoi, San and Xhosa, criticising commandos and land seizures. Fairbairn, who married the daughter of John Philip, also supported Khoikhoi rights and Ordinance 50. Their views were unpopular with many settlers. Their example reminds us that settler communities were not all of one mind, and that some people within them challenged injustice, even when it made them unpopular.

### subtopic_4 — Impact of the Settlers
**Assistant:** Why was Port Elizabeth renamed Gqeberha?

**Tutor:** In 2021, the government officially renamed Port Elizabeth as Gqeberha, as part of a wider process of renaming places in South Africa to reflect the history and languages of all its people, not only colonial history. Gqeberha is the isiXhosa name for the Baakens River, which flows through the city, and it was already used by many isiXhosa-speaking residents to refer to the area, including the township of Walmer, also called Gqeberha. The old name honoured Elizabeth Donkin, the late wife of Acting Governor Sir Rufane Donkin, who named the town in 1820. Similarly, Grahamstown, named after Colonel John Graham, who led the expulsion of the Xhosa from the Zuurveld in 1812, was renamed Makhanda in 2018, after the prophet who led the attack on the town in 1819. The city's municipality had already been renamed Nelson Mandela Bay in 2001. Renaming is often debated: supporters say it corrects a one-sided history and honours African heritage; others feel attached to the old names or worry about cost. Historians see renaming as part of how societies continually reinterpret and remember their past.
