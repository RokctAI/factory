# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the frontier wars on the eastern frontier of the Cape Colony. The anchor is the attack on Grahamstown on 22 April 1819, when about six to ten thousand Xhosa warriors, inspired by the prophet Makhanda, tried to drive the British out of the Zuurveld. The governing idea is that the wars were fought mainly over land, cattle and independence, that the Xhosa resisted for a century with courage and skill, and that the wars ended with the loss of Xhosa land and independence to colonial rule.

## Subtopic: The Xhosa and the Frontier

The Xhosa are Nguni-speaking African farmers whose ancestors had lived in the Eastern Cape for centuries. By the eighteenth century, the Xhosa were organised into a number of chiefdoms, all descended from a common ancestor, Tshawe, and recognising the senior royal house. Around 1750 the kingdom divided into two main branches: the Gcaleka, east of the Kei River, and the Rharhabe, west of the Kei, closer to the colony. Important leaders in the early nineteenth century included Hintsa, king of the Gcaleka and paramount of all the Xhosa; Ngqika, chief of the Rharhabe; and Ndlambe, his uncle and rival.

The Xhosa were cattle farmers and crop growers. Cattle were central to wealth, status, marriage through lobola, and religion. Land was allocated by chiefs and held communally. Grazing land and water were essential.

From the 1770s, trekboers moved into the Zuurveld, between the Bushmans and the Great Fish rivers, where some Xhosa chiefdoms, such as the Gqunukhwebe, already lived together with Khoikhoi groups. Both trekboers and Xhosa were cattle farmers who needed the same land and water. At first there was trade and some co-operation; colonists traded for cattle and ivory, and some Xhosa worked on farms.

But competition grew. Cattle raiding happened on both sides. The colonial authorities claimed the Fish River as the boundary in 1778, but the Xhosa did not accept that a line drawn by the colony meant they had to leave land they lived on. Rivalries between Xhosa chiefs, such as between Ngqika and Ndlambe, also complicated relations, as each side sometimes sought colonial support.

## Subtopic: Causes of the Wars

Historians identify several causes of the frontier wars, which often worked together.

Land: the main cause was competition for land. Colonists wanted more land for farms and grazing; the Xhosa wanted to keep land they had lived on for generations. Each war ended with the colony taking more land.

Cattle: cattle were wealth for both sides. Cattle raiding and counter-raiding caused constant disputes. The colonial authorities used a commando or reprisal system, in which colonists who claimed to have lost cattle could follow the tracks into Xhosa territory and seize cattle from the nearest homestead, often from people who had nothing to do with the theft. This deeply angered the Xhosa.

Different ideas of land and borders: the colony drew boundary lines on maps and expected them to be obeyed; the Xhosa saw land as allocated by chiefs and shared, and borders as flexible.

Colonial policy: the British wanted a secure, fixed border, cheap to defend, and pursued aggressive policies, such as the expulsion from the Zuurveld in 1812 and the creation of buffer zones. Later governors tried to annex Xhosa territory.

Xhosa politics: rivalries between chiefs, such as Ngqika and Ndlambe, led some to ally with the colony against rivals, which the colony exploited.

Drought and population pressure: in dry years, competition for grazing and water intensified.

Missionaries and new ideas: missionaries arrived among the Xhosa from 1799; some Xhosa welcomed them, others saw them as agents of colonial control. Xhosa prophets, such as Nxele, later known as Makhanda, and Mlanjeni, offered spiritual leadership in resistance.

The questions for this section are with you now: land, cattle and the reprisal system, different ideas of borders, colonial policy, Xhosa politics, drought, and prophets.

## Subtopic: The Wars of the Early 1800s

There were nine frontier wars between 1779 and 1879. The first three, in 1779 to 1781, 1789 to 1793 and 1799 to 1803, were fought mainly between the Xhosa and the trekboers, with Khoikhoi also involved, and ended without a clear victory for the colony. In the third war, Khoikhoi servants rebelled against farmers and joined the Xhosa.

The fourth war, in 1811 to 1812, was when British troops under Colonel John Graham drove about twenty thousand Xhosa out of the Zuurveld.

The fifth war, 1818 to 1819: Ngqika had allied with the British, and when the British supported him against Ndlambe, Ndlambe's forces defeated Ngqika at the Battle of Amalinde in 1818. The British then attacked Ndlambe. In response, the prophet Makhanda, an adviser to Ndlambe, led an attack on Grahamstown on 22 April 1819, with about six to ten thousand warriors. The British garrison of a few hundred soldiers, helped by Khoikhoi marksmen, defeated the attack with musket and cannon fire, and many Xhosa were killed. Makhanda later surrendered to save his people and was imprisoned on Robben Island; he drowned in 1820 while trying to escape. After the war, the British forced Ngqika to accept that the land between the Fish and Keiskamma rivers, the Ceded Territory, should be kept empty of Xhosa, although colonists soon moved in.

The sixth war, 1834 to 1835, also called the War of Hintsa: Xhosa forces, led by chiefs including Maqoma and Tyhali, invaded the colony after the shooting of a Xhosa chief and continued land losses. The British, under Governor Sir Benjamin D'Urban and Colonel Harry Smith, invaded Xhosa territory. King Hintsa came to negotiate, was held captive, and was shot dead in 1835 while apparently trying to escape; his body was mutilated, which caused outrage. The British annexed the land up to the Kei River as Queen Adelaide Province, but the British government ordered it returned in 1836.

## Subtopic: Later Wars and Consequences

The later wars continued the pattern. The seventh war, 1846 to 1847, the War of the Axe, began when a Xhosa man accused of stealing an axe was freed by his companions, and grew into a major conflict; afterwards the British annexed the land between the Keiskamma and the Kei as British Kaffraria, a colonial name, with King William's Town as its capital. The eighth war, 1850 to 1853, Mlanjeni's War, inspired by the prophet Mlanjeni, was the longest and most costly, with Maqoma leading Xhosa forces from the Amathole mountains, and Khoikhoi at the Kat River Settlement joining the rebellion. In 1856 to 1857, the Xhosa cattle-killing movement, following prophecies by a young woman named Nongqawuse that killing cattle and destroying crops would bring the ancestors back and drive out the colonists, led to famine in which tens of thousands died and many survivors were forced into labour in the colony. The ninth war, 1877 to 1879, ended Xhosa independence.

The consequences of the wars were severe. The Xhosa lost most of their land, which became colonial farms; they lost hundreds of thousands of cattle; many thousands were killed, and many more died from hunger and displacement; chiefs lost their power to colonial magistrates; many Xhosa were forced to work for colonists for wages and became part of a labour force for farms and later mines; and Xhosa society was deeply disrupted. The colony gained land, but also bore huge costs, and colonists lived with insecurity for decades.

The wars are remembered differently. Colonial histories often portrayed the Xhosa as aggressors. Today historians see the wars as a long struggle to resist colonial conquest and dispossession, and leaders such as Makhanda, Hintsa and Maqoma are honoured as heroes of resistance.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking the frontier wars were a single war. Two: believing that all Xhosa chiefs always acted together. Three: describing the wars only from the colonists' point of view.

The questions for this section are with you now: the later wars, the cattle-killing, consequences for the Xhosa and the colony, how the wars are remembered, and the error museum.
