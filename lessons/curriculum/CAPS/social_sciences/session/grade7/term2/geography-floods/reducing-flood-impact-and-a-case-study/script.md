# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines how to reduce the impact of floods and applies the term's ideas to a case study. The anchor is the warning issued by the South African Weather Service before the April 2022 floods, and the question of why, despite the warning, so many lives were lost. The governing idea is that reducing flood impact needs both hard engineering, such as dams and drains, and soft approaches, such as warnings, planning, wetlands and education, and that a case study is a way of testing general ideas against one real event.

## Subtopic: Preparing: Forecasts, Warnings and Planning

The first line of defence against floods is information. The South African Weather Service, known as SAWS, monitors the weather with satellites, radar and weather stations and issues forecasts and warnings. Since 2019, SAWS has used an impact-based warning system, which warns not only how much rain is expected but what impact it is likely to have, colour-coded yellow, orange and red, with red the most severe. Warnings are broadcast on radio, television, social media and apps, and passed to municipal disaster management centres. River level gauges along major rivers give warning of rising water downstream.

A warning saves lives only if it reaches people, if they understand it and trust it, and if they know what to do and have somewhere to go. This is called the last mile of a warning system, and it is often the weakest link, especially for people without electricity, phones or data, or who speak languages other than those used in warnings.

Land-use planning keeps people out of harm's way. Municipalities map the flood lines along rivers, showing the areas likely to flood in a fifty-year or hundred-year flood, a flood so large that it is expected on average once in fifty or a hundred years, and should not allow homes to be built below them. Informal settlements on floodplains need safer, affordable alternatives.

Communities and households can prepare: knowing their risk, keeping drains clear of litter, preparing an emergency bag with documents, water, a torch, medicine and a phone charger, agreeing on safe meeting places, and knowing evacuation routes to high ground. Schools can practise evacuation drills.

Disaster management in South Africa is guided by the Disaster Management Act of 2002, which requires national, provincial and municipal disaster management centres to plan for, and co-ordinate the response to, disasters.

## Subtopic: Hard and Soft Engineering

Physical measures to reduce flooding are often divided into hard engineering and soft engineering.

Hard engineering uses built structures to control water. Dams store floodwater and release it gradually; South Africa has more than five thousand registered dams, mainly for water supply, but large dams such as the Gariep and Vaal can also reduce flood peaks downstream if they have space. Levees, or embankments, raise river banks so that water stays in the channel. Channelisation straightens, widens or lines river channels with concrete so that water flows away faster. Storm-water drains and culverts carry rain from streets into rivers. Bridges must be designed high enough and strong enough for big floods. Hard engineering can be very effective but is expensive, needs maintenance, and can fail: a levee that breaks or a drain that is blocked can make flooding sudden and worse, and speeding water away from one place can increase flooding further downstream.

Soft engineering works with natural processes. Afforestation and restoring vegetation in catchments increases interception and infiltration. Protecting and restoring wetlands lets them soak up floodwater. Leaving floodplains as parks, sports fields or farmland gives rivers space to flood safely. In cities, sustainable urban drainage uses permeable paving, green roofs, grass swales and retention ponds to slow water down and let it soak in. Removing invasive alien plants from river banks, as the Working for Water programme does, also helps rivers function naturally.

Most experts now recommend combining both approaches, and maintaining existing infrastructure. Many urban floods in South Africa are made worse by drains blocked with litter and silt and by poorly maintained storm-water systems.

The questions for this section are with you now: dams, levees, channelisation and drains; soft engineering with vegetation, wetlands, floodplains and sustainable drainage; and the importance of maintenance.

## Subtopic: Responding to a Flood

When a flood happens, safety comes first, and most deaths can be prevented by simple rules.

During a flood: move to higher ground immediately when warned; never walk, swim or drive through floodwater, because the depth, current and hidden dangers such as open manholes, debris and washed-away roads cannot be seen; stay away from rivers, storm-water channels and bridges over flooded rivers; avoid fallen power lines; listen to official warnings on the radio; and help elderly people, small children and people with disabilities.

The emergency response includes rescue of people trapped by water, often by boat or helicopter. In South Africa, the South African Police Service, the South African National Defence Force, municipal fire and rescue services, and volunteer organisations such as the National Sea Rescue Institute and Gift of the Givers take part. Evacuation centres, often in community halls and schools, provide shelter, blankets, food and water. Medical teams treat injuries and work to prevent outbreaks of disease by providing clean water, purification tablets and sanitation. Engineers inspect bridges and buildings for safety.

After a flood: return home only when authorities say it is safe; throw away food and medicine that floodwater has touched; boil or purify drinking water until the supply is declared safe; clean and disinfect homes; and watch out for snakes and other animals sheltering in debris.

Recovery involves rebuilding homes, roads, bridges and services, which can take years, restoring livelihoods, and providing counselling. As with earthquakes, the aim should be to build back better, rebuilding in safer places and to higher standards. A government may declare a national state of disaster, which allows emergency funds and resources to be released quickly, as happened after the April 2022 floods.

## Subtopic: Case Study: KwaZulu-Natal, April 2022

Location. The floods struck mainly the eThekwini metropolitan area around Durban and parts of the KwaZulu-Natal south and north coasts, and parts of the Eastern Cape.

Causes. Between about the eleventh and twelfth of April 2022, a cut-off low pressure system stalled off the east coast, drawing in warm, moist air from the Indian Ocean. Very heavy rain fell for many hours, with more than three hundred millimetres in about twenty-four hours in some places, more than many parts of South Africa receive in a year. The ground was already wet after earlier rain. Human factors made the disaster worse: many homes, including informal settlements, had been built on steep slopes and along river banks; much of the city's surface is paved; storm-water drains were blocked or poorly maintained; and some vegetation on slopes had been cleared.

Effects. More than four hundred and thirty people died, making it one of the deadliest natural disasters in South Africa's recorded history, and dozens remained missing. Tens of thousands of people were displaced, and thousands of homes were destroyed or damaged. Landslides buried houses. Roads and bridges were washed away. Water supply and electricity were cut to many areas for weeks. Hundreds of schools were damaged. The port of Durban, the busiest container port in sub-Saharan Africa, was closed for days, and shipping containers were washed into rivers and the sea. Businesses and factories were damaged and thousands of jobs affected.

Responses. SAWS issued warnings before the event, but many people did not receive them or could not act on them, and there were questions about how well the warnings were passed on. The government declared a national state of disaster. The army, police, rescue services and volunteers, including Gift of the Givers, rescued people, recovered bodies and distributed food and water. Evacuation centres were opened. Later, there were investigations into how relief funds were spent.

Lessons. The case shows how a natural hazard became a disaster through vulnerability: settlement on dangerous land, poor maintenance and weak warning communication. It shows the need for better land-use planning, maintained drains, stronger last-mile warnings and building back better. With climate change, such events may become more frequent.

Layout for a case study answer: name the place and date, state the causes, natural and human, describe the effects, social, economic and environmental, describe the responses, and give an evaluation or lesson learned.

## Subtopic: Getting Ready

The Weather Service watches the skies and warns people when heavy rain is coming, using yellow, orange and red warnings. Red is the most serious.

But a warning only helps if it reaches people and they know what to do. So listen to the radio, keep phones charged, and know the way to high ground.

Towns should not let people build homes in places that flood. Families can pack an emergency bag with documents, water, a torch and medicine. And everyone can help by keeping drains clear of litter.

## Subtopic: Walls and Wetlands

There are two ways to fight floods. Hard ways use building: dams to hold water, high walls along rivers, concrete channels and storm drains. They work, but they cost a lot, need fixing, and can push the problem further downstream.

Soft ways use nature: plant trees and grass so rain soaks in, protect wetlands that soak up water like sponges, and leave floodplains as parks and fields so rivers have room to spread out. In towns, paving that lets water through and ponds that hold rainwater help too.

The best plan uses both.

## Subtopic: Stay Safe and Learn

In a flood, go to high ground. Never walk or drive through floodwater: you cannot see how deep or fast it is. After the flood, throw away food the water touched and boil drinking water.

In April 2022 a cut-off low dropped more than three hundred millimetres of rain on Durban and the KwaZulu-Natal coast in about a day. More than four hundred and thirty people died. Homes on river banks and steep slopes were washed away, roads and bridges broke, and the port of Durban closed. Many people did not get the warnings in time. The army, police and volunteers helped rescue people.

The lessons: don't build in danger zones, keep drains clean, and make sure warnings reach everyone.
