# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the causes and effects of floods. The anchor is the flood of April 2022 in KwaZulu-Natal, when more than three hundred millimetres of rain fell in parts of eThekwini in about twenty-four hours, killing more than four hundred people. The governing idea is that a flood happens when water arrives faster than the land and rivers can carry it away, and that while heavy rain is the trigger, the way people use the land often decides how severe the flood becomes and who suffers.

## Subtopic: What a Flood Is and How Rain Becomes a Flood

A flood happens when water covers land that is normally dry. Most floods occur when a river overflows its banks and spreads over the flat land beside it, called the floodplain. Floods also occur when heavy rain cannot drain away from streets and low ground, called flash flooding, and when the sea floods the coast.

To understand floods, follow what happens to rain when it reaches the ground. Some rain is caught by the leaves of plants, a process called interception, and some evaporates back into the air. Some soaks into the soil, called infiltration, and moves slowly underground to rivers or into groundwater. The rest flows over the surface downhill into streams and rivers, called surface run-off. The area of land that drains into a particular river is called its drainage basin or catchment.

The key to floods is speed. Water that infiltrates reaches the river slowly, over days or weeks. Water that runs off over the surface reaches the river quickly, within hours. When a lot of rain becomes surface run-off at the same time, the river rises rapidly, fills its channel and overflows.

Floods can be slow or sudden. A river flood on a large, flat river may rise over days, giving time to prepare. A flash flood, in a small, steep catchment or a city, can rise within minutes of a storm, giving almost no warning, and is especially dangerous. Fast-flowing water is extremely powerful: water only thirty centimetres deep and moving fast can knock an adult over, and sixty centimetres can float a car.

## Subtopic: Natural Causes: Heavy Rain and Tsunamis

The main natural cause of floods is unusually heavy rain: more rain than the ground and rivers can handle. This may be very intense rain falling in a short time, or steady rain falling for many days until the soil is saturated, completely full of water, so that any further rain runs off.

In South Africa, several weather systems bring heavy rain. Cut-off lows are low-pressure systems that become cut off from the main westerly winds and stay over one area for days, bringing very heavy rain; the KwaZulu-Natal floods of April 2022 and floods in the Eastern Cape and Western Cape have been caused by cut-off lows. Tropical cyclones from the Indian Ocean bring torrential rain to Mozambique, Limpopo and Mpumalanga; Cyclone Eline in 2000 caused severe floods in Mozambique and the north-east of South Africa. Summer thunderstorms on the Highveld can cause local flash floods. Cold fronts in winter can bring heavy rain to the Western Cape.

Other natural factors increase flooding. Steep slopes make water run off fast. Clay soils and rock let little water soak in. Already saturated ground after earlier rain cannot absorb more. Melting snow adds water in some regions, though rarely in South Africa.

The sea can also flood the land. A tsunami, caused by an earthquake under the sea floor, can push a wall of water far inland, as in the Indian Ocean in 2004 and Japan in 2011. Storm surges, when strong winds and low air pressure in a cyclone pile sea water against the coast, cause coastal flooding, often combined with high tides and big waves.

## Subtopic: Human Causes: How Land Use Makes Floods Worse

Heavy rain triggers floods, but human activities often make them much worse, by reducing interception and infiltration so that more water runs off, faster.

Removing vegetation. Plants intercept rain, their roots help water soak into the soil, and leaf litter slows run-off. When forests, grassland and wetlands are cleared for farming, firewood or building, more rain runs straight off the land, carrying soil with it. Wetlands are especially important, because they act like sponges, soaking up floodwater and releasing it slowly.

Farming. Ploughing up and down slopes creates channels for water; overgrazing by too many cattle, sheep and goats strips the grass and compacts the soil, so less water infiltrates. Bare soil between seasons is easily washed away.

Fires. Veld and forest fires burn the vegetation and can make the soil surface hard and water-repellent, so heavy rain after a fire produces fast run-off and mudslides. The fires on the slopes above Cape Town have been followed by floods and mudslides.

Settlement and urbanisation. Towns cover the ground with roofs, roads, paving and parking areas, which are impermeable, meaning water cannot soak through. Storm drains carry the water quickly into rivers. So a city produces much more run-off, much faster, than the countryside it replaced. Building on floodplains and in river channels puts people directly in the path of floods. Blocked drains, filled with litter and rubbish, cause streets to flood.

Informal settlements are particularly vulnerable, because people with few choices often build on the cheapest, most dangerous land, along river banks, on floodplains or on steep slopes, with homes that cannot withstand fast water.

Climate change is expected to make extreme rainfall events more frequent and intense in many regions, including parts of South Africa.

The questions for this section are with you now: how removing vegetation, farming, fires and urbanisation increase run-off, why wetlands matter, and why informal settlements are vulnerable.

## Subtopic: Effects of Floods

Floods affect people, property, the economy and the environment.

Injury and loss of life. Most flood deaths are caused by drowning, often when people try to cross flooded rivers, bridges or roads on foot or in vehicles, and by being swept away or struck by debris. Collapsing buildings, mudslides and electrocution from fallen power lines also kill. The April 2022 KwaZulu-Natal floods killed more than four hundred people.

Disease. Floodwater mixes with sewage, rubbish and dead animals, contaminating drinking water. Diseases such as cholera, typhoid and diarrhoea spread. Standing water left after a flood becomes a breeding ground for mosquitoes, which can carry malaria in some regions.

Displacement. Thousands of people may lose their homes or be evacuated, living in community halls, schools or tents, sometimes for months. After the 2022 floods, tens of thousands of people in KwaZulu-Natal were displaced.

Damage to buildings and infrastructure. Floods destroy houses, schools and clinics, wash away roads and bridges, damage railways, ports, water treatment works, sewage systems and power lines. In 2022, the port of Durban, the busiest in Africa south of the Sahara, was closed for days, and many roads and bridges were destroyed.

Damage to fields and farming. Crops are drowned or buried in mud, livestock drown, farm buildings and irrigation systems are destroyed, which can cause food shortages and loss of income.

Soil erosion. Fast-flowing water strips away fertile topsoil and cuts gullies into the land. The soil is carried into rivers and dams, where it settles as silt, reducing the amount of water dams can store.

Floods also have some benefits in natural systems: they spread fertile silt over floodplains, refill wetlands and support fish breeding. The ancient farmers of the Nile valley depended on yearly floods.

## Subtopic: Too Much Water, Too Fast

A flood is when water covers land that is usually dry, often when a river spills over its banks onto the flat land next to it.

When rain falls, some soaks into the ground slowly. The rest runs over the top, quickly, into rivers. If too much runs off at once, the river overflows.

A flash flood comes in minutes, with no warning. Fast water is very strong: knee-deep water can knock you over, and water up to your waist can float a car.

## Subtopic: Why Floods Happen

Nature: very heavy rain, or rain for many days until the ground is full. In South Africa, weather systems called cut-off lows, and cyclones from the Indian Ocean, bring the heaviest rain. Tsunamis and storms can flood the coast too.

People make it worse. Cutting down trees and grass, overgrazing, and fires leave bare ground, so rain runs off fast. Cities cover the ground with roofs and roads, so water cannot soak in. Litter blocks drains. And people building next to rivers are right in the way.

## Subtopic: What Floods Do

Floods drown people, especially people trying to cross flooded rivers and roads. Dirty floodwater spreads diseases like cholera. People lose their homes. Roads, bridges, schools, power lines and even the port of Durban were damaged in the 2022 floods. Crops and animals are lost, and good soil is washed away into dams.

So remember the rule: if it's flooded, forget it. Never walk or drive through floodwater.

Heavy rain from cut-off lows and cyclones is the main trigger, and tsunamis and storm surges flood coasts. Clearing vegetation, overgrazing, fires, cities and building on floodplains make floods worse. Floods cause deaths, disease, displacement, damage to buildings, infrastructure and fields, and soil erosion.
