# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the transatlantic slave trade and examines slavery in West Africa before European involvement. The anchor is the Door of No Return, a symbolic doorway at slave forts on the West African coast through which captives passed to the ships. The governing idea is that slavery has existed in many societies, but that the transatlantic slave trade was different in scale, brutality and in its link to race, and that understanding what existed before helps explain how the Atlantic trade grew and why it was so destructive. This is a painful history, studied with respect for those who suffered.

## Subtopic: What the Transatlantic Slave Trade Was

Slavery is a system in which people are treated as property, owned by others, forced to work without pay, and denied their freedom and basic rights. An enslaved person could be bought and sold, and in many systems their children were born into slavery too. Historians today often use the words enslaved person rather than slave, to remind us that slavery was something done to people, not who they were.

The transatlantic slave trade was the forced transport of enslaved Africans across the Atlantic Ocean to the Americas, from the early sixteenth century until the second half of the nineteenth century. Portuguese traders began it, and they were soon followed by Spanish, British, French, Dutch, Danish and later American traders. Historians estimate, using the records of tens of thousands of voyages gathered in a project called the Slave Voyages database, that about twelve and a half million Africans were embarked on ships, and that about ten and a half million survived the crossing. Most were taken from West and West-Central Africa, from the region of present-day Senegal to Angola, and some from Mozambique and Madagascar.

Most enslaved Africans were taken to Brazil and the Caribbean islands, to work on sugar plantations. A smaller proportion, roughly four hundred thousand, were taken directly to North America, where the enslaved population grew through births to about four million by 1860.

Historians learn about the trade from many sources: ships' records and logbooks, merchants' account books, plantation records, laws, newspapers and advertisements, archaeology, and, most importantly, the testimony of enslaved people themselves, including autobiographies such as that of Olaudah Equiano, published in 1789, interviews with formerly enslaved people recorded in the 1930s, and songs and stories passed down by their descendants.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of slavery, the dates, places and numbers of the trade, and the sources historians use.

## Subtopic: West Africa Before the Atlantic Trade

Before Europeans arrived on its coasts in the fifteenth century, West Africa was a region of kingdoms, cities, farms and trade networks. Kingdoms such as Mali and Songhay, studied last term, Benin, Oyo and the Akan states had governments, armies, laws and skilled craftspeople working in gold, bronze, iron, textiles and pottery. Trade linked the forests, the savanna and, across the Sahara, North Africa and the Mediterranean.

Wealth and power in much of West Africa were measured less in land, which was relatively plentiful, than in people. A ruler or a household was powerful if it had many dependants: wives, children, followers, clients and workers. Labour to clear land, grow crops, build, mine and fight was precious.

This helps explain why various forms of unfree labour existed. Slavery was one; others included pawnship, in which a person, often a child, was given to a creditor as security for a debt and worked for them until the debt was repaid, and various kinds of service owed to rulers.

Slavery and the trade in people also existed in many other parts of the world at the time: in Europe, in the Mediterranean, in the Middle East and in Asia. The trade across the Sahara, studied last term, had carried enslaved Africans northwards for centuries before Europeans arrived on the Atlantic coast. Slavery was not unique to Africa, nor invented by Europeans, but its forms and consequences differed greatly from place to place.

The questions for this section are with you now: West African states and economies before European contact, why control of people mattered, pawnship, and slavery as a widespread institution.

## Subtopic: The Nature of Slavery in West Africa

Slavery in West Africa before the Atlantic trade took many forms, and historians warn against simple generalisations. However, several features were common.

How people became enslaved. Most enslaved people were captives taken in war or raids. Others were enslaved as punishment for crimes, because of debt, or were sold during famines. Some were born to enslaved parents.

Work and treatment. Enslaved people worked in agriculture, in households, as porters and in crafts, and some served as soldiers or even officials of rulers. Their treatment varied greatly, from harsh to relatively mild, depending on the society, the owner and the work. Those taken in war and sold far from home were usually the most vulnerable.

Integration. In many West African societies, enslaved people and especially their children could gradually become part of the owner's family or community, through marriage, adoption or long service. They might be able to own property, marry, and in some cases rise to positions of trust; in the Songhay empire and in Asante, some officials and soldiers were of slave origin. Over generations, descendants could lose the status of slaves, though they often remained socially inferior.

Not based on race. Enslaved people in West Africa were usually outsiders, people from another group, village or kingdom, but not defined by skin colour.

This does not mean that West African slavery was gentle. Enslaved people were denied freedom, were separated from their families and homes, could be sold, and suffered violence. Many lived hard lives. The point is that it differed in important ways from the slavery that developed in the Americas.

The questions for this section are with you now: how people became enslaved in West Africa, their work and treatment, integration over generations, and the absence of a racial basis.

## Subtopic: How the Atlantic Trade Changed Things

Portuguese ships reached the West African coast in the 1440s, at first trading for gold and pepper, and soon for captives. After Europeans colonised the Americas from 1492 and began growing sugar on plantations, the demand for labour exploded, and the trade grew rapidly from the sixteenth century, peaking in the eighteenth century.

The Atlantic trade transformed slavery in several ways.

Scale. The number of people taken was far greater than before, reaching around eighty thousand people a year at the height of the trade in the 1780s.

Chattel slavery. In the Americas, enslaved people were treated as chattels, legally the same as livestock or furniture, owned completely and permanently. Their children were automatically enslaved. There was little or no path to integration or freedom.

Race. Slavery in the Americas became linked to being African, and to dark skin. To justify it, Europeans developed racist ideas claiming that Africans were inferior, ideas that have caused harm long after slavery ended.

Effects in Africa. European traders mostly stayed on the coast, in forts such as Elmina in present-day Ghana and on islands such as Gorée off Senegal. They bought captives from African rulers and merchants, exchanging them for European goods, especially guns, gunpowder, cloth, metal goods and alcohol. Some African states, such as Dahomey and Asante, grew powerful from the trade; guns fuelled more wars and raids to capture people. Some African leaders opposed the trade, notably King Afonso the First of Kongo, who wrote to the king of Portugal in 1526 complaining that his people were being kidnapped. Millions of young people were lost from West and Central Africa, harming populations, economies and societies for generations.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: saying Africans were to blame for the Atlantic trade; some African elites took part, but the demand, the ships, the plantations and the racist laws came from Europe and the Americas. Two: saying that because slavery existed in Africa, the Atlantic trade was nothing new. Three: forgetting the enslaved as individual people with names, families and cultures.

The questions for this section are with you now: the growth of the Atlantic trade, chattel slavery, the link to race, effects in Africa, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: A Terrible Trade

Slavery is when people are treated as property: owned, forced to work without pay, and not free.

From the 1500s to the 1800s, European traders carried more than twelve million Africans across the Atlantic Ocean to the Americas. About ten and a half million survived the journey. Most went to Brazil and the Caribbean to grow sugar. Some went to North America.

We know about it from ships' records, plantation records, and above all the words of enslaved people themselves, like Olaudah Equiano, who wrote his life story in 1789.

## Subtopic: Slavery in West Africa Before

West Africa had kingdoms, cities and trade long before Europeans came. Power came from having many people: families, followers and workers.

Slavery already existed there, as it did in Europe, the Middle East and Asia. Most enslaved people were captives from wars. They worked on farms, in homes or even as soldiers. In many places, their children could slowly become part of the family or community. And it was not about skin colour: enslaved people were outsiders from other groups.

It was still unfair and often cruel. But it was different from what came later.

## Subtopic: What Changed

Portuguese ships reached West Africa in the 1440s. After Europeans took over the Americas and started huge sugar farms, they wanted millions of workers.

Everything got bigger and worse. Enslaved people in the Americas were treated like animals or furniture, owned forever, and their children were born enslaved. Slavery became linked to being African, and Europeans invented racist ideas to excuse it.

European traders bought captives at forts on the coast from African rulers and merchants, paying with guns, cloth and metal goods. Guns led to more wars and more captives. Some African leaders, like King Afonso of Kongo, protested.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The transatlantic slave trade carried over twelve million Africans to the Americas from the 1500s to the 1800s. Slavery existed in West Africa before, mostly of war captives who could often be integrated over time and was not based on race. The Atlantic trade made slavery far bigger, permanent and racial, and caused great harm to Africa.
