### subtopic_1 — The Layers of the Earth
**Assistant:** If the inside of the Earth is so hot, why don't we feel it?

**Tutor:** Because the crust is a very good insulator and the heat comes out very slowly over a huge area. Rock conducts heat poorly, so the thick layer of crust under our feet acts like a blanket. Most of the warmth we feel at the surface comes from the sun, not from inside the Earth. But the interior heat is there, and you can measure it. As you go deeper into the crust, the temperature rises, on average by about twenty-five to thirty degrees Celsius for every kilometre. That is why the deep gold mines of the Witwatersrand, about four kilometres down, have rock temperatures of around sixty degrees Celsius, and huge refrigeration and ventilation systems are needed to keep miners safe. In some places, the heat comes much closer to the surface: hot springs such as those at Aliwal North or Warmbaths, now Bela-Bela, bring warm water from deep underground. In volcanic countries such as Iceland and Kenya, the heat is so close to the surface that it is used to generate electricity, which is called geothermal energy.

### subtopic_3 — Tectonic Plates
**Assistant:** Will Africa really split into two?

**Tutor:** Very probably, but not in any time that matters to people. Along the East African Rift Valley, which runs from the Afar region of Ethiopia south through Kenya and Tanzania towards Mozambique, the African Plate is being stretched and is slowly separating into two parts, usually called the Nubian Plate in the west and the Somali Plate in the east. The rate is only a few millimetres a year. You can see the effects in the landscape: long valleys with steep walls, deep lakes such as Lake Tanganyika and Lake Malawi, and volcanoes such as Kilimanjaro and Nyiragongo. In 2018 a long crack opened in the ground in Kenya after heavy rain, and many news reports described it as Africa splitting, though geologists explained that it was mainly caused by erosion of soft ground along the rift. If the stretching continues for perhaps several million years, the sea could flood into the rift, creating a new ocean and separating East Africa from the rest of the continent, much as the Red Sea opened between Africa and Arabia.

### subtopic_4 — Plate Boundaries
**Assistant:** Why do volcanoes form where one plate sinks under another?

**Tutor:** Because the sinking plate carries water down into the hot mantle, and water makes rock melt more easily. When an oceanic plate is subducted, it plunges down at an angle into the mantle. The ocean floor rocks contain water, trapped in minerals and in sediment from the sea bed. As the plate goes deeper, at about a hundred kilometres down, the heat and pressure squeeze this water out into the hot mantle rock above. Adding water lowers the temperature at which rock melts, in a similar way that adding salt lowers the temperature at which ice melts on a road. So part of the mantle above the sinking plate melts, forming magma. Magma is lighter than the surrounding solid rock, so it rises slowly through the crust, collects in magma chambers and eventually erupts at the surface. That is why chains of volcanoes form in a line parallel to subduction zones, such as the Andes of South America and the volcanoes of Japan and Indonesia, a pattern studied in the next lesson.
