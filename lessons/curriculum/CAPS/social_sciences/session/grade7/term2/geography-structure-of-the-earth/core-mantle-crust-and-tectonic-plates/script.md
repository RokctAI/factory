# Part 1 — Expert

This session opens the term's study of volcanoes, earthquakes and floods with the structure of the Earth. The anchor is the fit of the coastlines of Africa and South America, which look like two pieces of a jigsaw puzzle. The governing idea is that the Earth's interior is hot and layered, and that heat escaping from the interior keeps the outer shell in slow motion, broken into plates. Almost every volcano and earthquake studied this term happens because of what the plates are doing, so this model is the foundation for the whole term.

## Subtopic: The Layers of the Earth

The Earth is a sphere with a radius of about six thousand three hundred and seventy kilometres. It is made of layers, like a peach with a thin skin, thick flesh and a hard stone in the middle.

The crust is the outer layer, the thin rocky skin on which we live. It is very thin compared with the whole Earth. There are two kinds. Continental crust, which forms the continents, is about thirty to fifty kilometres thick, and up to seventy kilometres under high mountain ranges. It is made of lighter rocks such as granite. Oceanic crust, which forms the ocean floors, is only about five to ten kilometres thick, but is made of heavier, denser rock called basalt.

The mantle lies beneath the crust and is about two thousand nine hundred kilometres thick, making up most of the Earth's volume. It is made of hot, dense rock. Although the mantle is mostly solid, it is so hot, from about a thousand degrees Celsius near the top to about three and a half thousand or more near the bottom, that over very long periods it flows extremely slowly, like very thick toffee. The uppermost part of the mantle is joined to the crust, and together they form a rigid outer shell called the lithosphere, about a hundred kilometres thick, which rests on a softer, partly molten layer beneath.

The core is at the centre and is made mainly of iron and nickel. The outer core, about two thousand two hundred kilometres thick, is liquid metal; its movement produces the Earth's magnetic field. The inner core, with a radius of about one thousand two hundred kilometres, is solid, because the enormous pressure keeps it solid even though its temperature is estimated at about five thousand to six thousand degrees Celsius, roughly as hot as the surface of the sun.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three layers, continental and oceanic crust, the mantle and lithosphere, and the outer and inner core.

## Subtopic: How We Know What Is Inside

The deepest hole ever drilled, the Kola Superdeep Borehole in Russia, reached about twelve kilometres, only a fraction of the way through the continental crust. The deepest mines in the world, the gold mines of the Witwatersrand in South Africa, such as Mponeng, reach about four kilometres, where the rock is hot enough that the mines must be cooled. Nobody has ever seen the mantle or the core directly. So how do we know about them?

The main evidence comes from earthquakes. When an earthquake happens, it sends out vibrations called seismic waves, which travel through the whole Earth and are recorded by instruments called seismographs around the world. Seismic waves change speed and direction when they pass from one kind of material into another, much as light bends when it passes from air into water. Some kinds of seismic waves cannot travel through liquid at all, and they disappear on the far side of the Earth, which revealed that the outer core is liquid. By studying how waves from thousands of earthquakes arrive at stations around the globe, scientists have mapped the layers inside the Earth.

Other evidence supports this picture. Volcanoes bring up molten rock and fragments of the upper mantle. The average density of the whole Earth is much higher than that of the rocks of the crust, so the inside must be made of something heavier, such as iron. Meteorites, pieces of other bodies from the early solar system, contain iron and nickel and stony material in similar proportions. And the Earth's magnetic field requires a moving liquid metal core.

The questions for this section are with you now: why we cannot see the deep interior, seismic waves as evidence, and other supporting evidence.

## Subtopic: Tectonic Plates

The lithosphere, the rigid shell made of the crust and the top of the mantle, is not one solid piece. It is cracked into about seven large plates and many smaller ones, called tectonic plates, which fit together like the pieces of a cracked eggshell. The large plates include the African Plate, the Eurasian Plate, the North American Plate, the South American Plate, the Pacific Plate, the Indo-Australian Plate and the Antarctic Plate. Most plates carry both continental and oceanic crust: the African Plate carries the whole continent of Africa and large parts of the Atlantic and Indian Ocean floors.

The plates move slowly, between about two and ten centimetres a year, about as fast as fingernails grow. The main cause is heat from the Earth's interior. Hot material in the mantle rises slowly, spreads out beneath the lithosphere, cools and sinks again, in huge, slow circulations called convection currents. Plates are also pulled down where their cold, heavy edges sink into the mantle, and pushed apart where new crust forms at mid-ocean ridges.

The idea that continents move was proposed by the German scientist Alfred Wegener in 1912, in his theory of continental drift. He noticed that the coastlines of Africa and South America fit together, that the same fossils, such as the reptile Mesosaurus, are found on both continents, and that rocks and mountain ranges match across the Atlantic. He argued that the continents were once joined in a supercontinent he called Pangaea, which began to break up about two hundred million years ago. The South African geologist Alexander du Toit gathered much of the strongest evidence for Wegener's idea from rocks in southern Africa and South America. But they could not explain how continents moved, and many scientists rejected the idea until the 1960s, when the discovery of sea-floor spreading led to the theory of plate tectonics.

The questions for this section are with you now: what tectonic plates are, the main plates, how fast and why they move, and the evidence of Wegener and du Toit.

## Subtopic: Plate Boundaries

Where two plates meet is called a plate boundary. Most volcanoes and earthquakes occur at or near plate boundaries, because that is where the crust is being stretched, squeezed or ground against itself. There are three main kinds of boundary.

At a divergent boundary, plates move apart. Molten rock rises from the mantle to fill the gap and cools to form new crust. Most divergent boundaries lie under the oceans, forming long underwater mountain chains called mid-ocean ridges, such as the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, which runs down the middle of the Atlantic Ocean. Iceland sits on top of it. On land, the East African Rift Valley is a place where the African Plate is slowly splitting into two pieces, the Nubian and the Somali plates, with volcanoes such as Kilimanjaro and Nyiragongo along it.

At a convergent boundary, plates move towards each other. When an oceanic plate meets a continental plate, the denser oceanic plate is forced down beneath the other into the mantle, a process called subduction. It melts, producing magma that rises to form volcanoes, and the grinding causes powerful earthquakes. The Andes mountains of South America formed this way. When two continental plates collide, neither sinks easily, so the crust crumples upwards to form fold mountains; the Himalayas, with Mount Everest, are still rising as India pushes into Asia.

At a transform boundary, plates slide past each other sideways. The plates lock together, stress builds up, and then they slip suddenly, causing earthquakes. The San Andreas Fault in California is the best-known example.

South Africa lies in the middle of the African Plate, far from any boundary, which is why it has no active volcanoes and few strong earthquakes.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking the mantle is a sea of liquid lava; it is mostly solid rock that flows very slowly. Two: thinking plates float on the liquid core. Three: thinking continents move fast enough to notice.

The questions for this section are with you now: divergent, convergent and transform boundaries, subduction, examples of each, why South Africa is relatively safe, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: inside the Earth, how we know, the giant puzzle pieces, and where the pieces meet.

## Subtopic: A Peach With a Metal Stone

Think of the Earth like a peach. The thin skin is the crust, where we live: only about five to seventy kilometres thick. Under the oceans it is thin; under the continents it is thicker.

The flesh is the mantle, about two thousand nine hundred kilometres of very hot rock that flows very, very slowly, like thick toffee.

The stone in the middle is the core, made of iron and nickel. The outer core is liquid metal. The inner core is solid, because it is squeezed so hard, even though it is about as hot as the surface of the sun.

## Subtopic: Giant Puzzle Pieces

Nobody has ever been to the mantle. Our deepest gold mines reach only about four kilometres. Scientists learn about the inside of the Earth from earthquake waves, which change as they pass through different layers.

The crust is cracked into giant pieces called tectonic plates. They move about as fast as your fingernails grow, a few centimetres a year, pushed by heat rising and sinking slowly inside the mantle.

Long ago, Alfred Wegener noticed that Africa and South America fit together like a jigsaw, and that the same fossils are found on both. Once, all the continents were joined in one giant land called Pangaea.

Try this part's questions on plates.

## Subtopic: Where Plates Meet

Where plates meet, the ground shakes and volcanoes grow.

Plates pulling apart let melted rock come up and make new crust, like under the middle of the Atlantic, or in the East African Rift.

Plates pushing together: one heavy ocean plate can sink under the other and melt, making volcanoes and big earthquakes. Two continents crashing together crumple up into huge mountains, like the Himalayas.

Plates sliding past each other get stuck, then jerk, making earthquakes.

South Africa is in the middle of the African Plate, so we have no active volcanoes and few strong earthquakes.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Earth has a thin crust, a thick slowly flowing mantle and an iron core, liquid outside and solid inside. The crust is broken into tectonic plates that move a few centimetres a year. Plates move apart, push together or slide past each other, and most volcanoes and earthquakes happen at these boundaries.
