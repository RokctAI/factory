# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the location and causes of volcanoes. The anchor is a world map on which volcanoes are marked as dots; the dots are not scattered randomly but form lines and rings. The governing idea is that volcanoes occur where molten rock can reach the surface, mostly at plate boundaries, where plates separate or where one plate sinks beneath another, and at a few hot spots. Knowing why volcanoes occur explains where they are, and therefore who lives with their dangers and benefits.

## Subtopic: What a Volcano Is

A volcano is an opening in the Earth's crust through which molten rock, gases and fragments of rock escape onto the surface. Over time, the material that erupts builds up around the opening to form a hill or mountain, which is also called a volcano.

Molten rock underground is called magma. When it reaches the surface it is called lava. Magma forms in the upper mantle or lower crust, where the rock partly melts, and it rises because it is less dense than the solid rock around it. It collects in a magma chamber, a large reservoir a few kilometres below the surface.

The parts of a volcano are as follows. The main vent is the central pipe through which magma rises from the chamber to the surface. The crater is the bowl-shaped hollow at the top, around the vent. Secondary vents may branch off from the main pipe and erupt on the side of the volcano, forming smaller cones. A very large crater formed when a volcano collapses into its emptied magma chamber is called a caldera.

During an eruption, a volcano may release lava flows; pyroclastic material, meaning fragments of rock blasted out, ranging from fine volcanic ash to large volcanic bombs; and gases, mainly water vapour, carbon dioxide and sulphur dioxide. The most dangerous product of explosive eruptions is a pyroclastic flow, an avalanche of hot gas, ash and rock that races down the slopes at speeds that can exceed a hundred kilometres an hour, at temperatures of several hundred degrees.

## Subtopic: The World Map of Volcanoes

When active volcanoes are plotted on a world map, a clear pattern appears. Most lie in narrow belts, and these belts match the boundaries of the tectonic plates.

The largest belt is the Pacific Ring of Fire, a horseshoe of volcanoes about forty thousand kilometres long around the edges of the Pacific Ocean. It runs from New Zealand north through Indonesia, the Philippines and Japan, around through the Kamchatka Peninsula and the Aleutian Islands to Alaska, and south along the west coasts of North and South America through the Cascades, Mexico, Central America and the Andes to Chile. About three-quarters of the world's active volcanoes lie on the Ring of Fire. Famous examples include Mount Fuji in Japan, Mount St Helens in the United States and Krakatoa in Indonesia.

A second belt runs through the Mediterranean region, with volcanoes such as Vesuvius and Etna in Italy, formed where the African Plate pushes north against the Eurasian Plate.

Volcanoes also lie along the mid-ocean ridges, mostly hidden under the sea. Iceland, on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, is one of the few places where such a ridge rises above sea level, and it has many active volcanoes.

In Africa, volcanoes lie mainly along the East African Rift Valley: Mount Kilimanjaro in Tanzania, Africa's highest mountain at about five thousand eight hundred and ninety-five metres, which is dormant; Mount Kenya, which is extinct; Ol Doinyo Lengai in Tanzania; and Nyiragongo in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, one of the most active volcanoes in the world. Mount Cameroon in West Africa and volcanoes on islands such as the Canaries also erupt.

## Subtopic: Why Volcanoes Occur

Volcanoes occur where magma can form and reach the surface. This happens in three main situations.

At divergent boundaries, where plates move apart, the pressure on the hot mantle beneath is reduced, and some of the mantle rock melts, in the same way that lowering the pressure lets a fizzy drink release its gas. The magma rises into the gap and erupts, mostly as runny basalt lava, building new crust. This is what happens along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, in Iceland and in the East African Rift.

At convergent boundaries where subduction occurs, an oceanic plate sinks into the mantle. As it goes down, water is squeezed out of its rocks into the hot mantle above, which lowers the melting temperature of the rock, so magma forms. This magma rises through the overlying plate to form chains of volcanoes, such as the Andes and the volcanoes of Japan and Indonesia. Because this magma is thick and sticky and full of gas, these volcanoes are often explosive.

At hot spots, volcanoes form far from plate boundaries, above plumes of unusually hot material rising from deep in the mantle. The hot spot stays in roughly the same place while the plate moves over it, so a chain of volcanoes forms, each one older than the next. The Hawaiian Islands in the middle of the Pacific Plate formed this way. Some scientists think that Réunion Island in the Indian Ocean and some volcanoes in Africa are linked to hot spots.

Where plates collide continent to continent, as in the Himalayas, there are few volcanoes, because neither plate sinks deep enough to melt. And in the stable middle of plates, such as South Africa, there are no active volcanoes, although South Africa does have the remains of very ancient ones, such as the Pilanesberg in North West, which erupted about one thousand three hundred million years ago.

## Subtopic: Kinds of Eruption and Volcano

Not all eruptions are the same. The main difference comes from the magma itself.

Runny magma, low in silica, such as basalt, lets gas escape easily. Eruptions are effusive: lava pours out in streams and fountains and flows long distances. Such volcanoes build gentle, broad slopes and are called shield volcanoes, because they look like a warrior's shield lying on the ground. Mauna Loa in Hawaii is the largest. These eruptions destroy property but rarely kill many people, because the lava flows slowly enough to walk away from, although Nyiragongo's unusually runny lava has flowed fast enough to cause deaths in the city of Goma in 1977 and 2002.

Sticky magma, high in silica, traps gas. Pressure builds until the volcano explodes, blasting out ash, rock fragments and pyroclastic flows. These volcanoes build steep cones from layers of ash and lava and are called composite volcanoes or stratovolcanoes. Mount Fuji, Vesuvius and Mount St Helens are examples. In the year 79, Vesuvius buried the Roman town of Pompeii under ash. In 1980, Mount St Helens blew out its whole side. In 1991, Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines threw so much ash and gas into the atmosphere that world temperatures dropped slightly for about a year.

Volcanoes are also classified by activity. An active volcano has erupted in recent history or shows signs that it may erupt. A dormant volcano has not erupted for a long time but could erupt again. An extinct volcano is not expected to erupt again. The line between dormant and extinct is not always certain: Vesuvius was thought to be harmless before 79.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: calling molten rock lava while it is still underground. Two: thinking volcanoes are randomly scattered. Three: thinking all eruptions are explosive.

The questions for this section are with you now: effusive and explosive eruptions, shield and composite volcanoes, famous eruptions, active, dormant and extinct, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: A Hole in the Crust

A volcano is a hole in the Earth's crust where melted rock comes out.

Under the ground, melted rock is called magma. It collects in a magma chamber, then rises up a pipe called the vent. When it comes out of the top, the crater, it is called lava.

Volcanoes can also blast out ash, rocks and gas. The most dangerous thing is a pyroclastic flow: a red-hot avalanche of gas and ash that rushes down the mountain faster than a car.

## Subtopic: Where and Why

On a world map, volcanoes make lines, not a random scatter. The biggest line is the Ring of Fire around the Pacific Ocean, with about three-quarters of all active volcanoes: Japan, Indonesia, the Andes and more.

They happen where plates meet. Where plates pull apart, rock melts and comes up. Where an ocean plate sinks under another plate, water helps rock melt, and volcanoes grow in a line. A few volcanoes, like Hawaii, sit over hot spots in the middle of plates.

In Africa, volcanoes are along the East African Rift, like Kilimanjaro. South Africa is in the middle of a plate, so no active volcanoes, only ancient ones like the Pilanesberg.

## Subtopic: Gentle or Explosive?

Runny magma lets gas out easily, so lava pours out gently and spreads far, building wide, flat shield volcanoes, like in Hawaii.

Sticky magma traps the gas like a shaken cooldrink bottle. Pressure builds until the volcano explodes, throwing out ash and rocks. These build steep cone volcanoes like Mount Fuji and Vesuvius, which buried Pompeii in the year 79.

Active volcanoes erupt now or recently. Dormant ones are sleeping and could wake up. Extinct ones are finished.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A volcano is an opening where magma reaches the surface as lava, ash and gas. Most volcanoes lie along plate boundaries, especially the Ring of Fire, and some over hot spots. Runny magma gives gentle eruptions and shield volcanoes; sticky magma gives explosive eruptions and steep cones. Volcanoes are active, dormant or extinct.
