### subtopic_1 — What Causes an Earthquake
**Assistant:** Does South Africa ever have earthquakes?

**Tutor:** Yes, though usually small ones, and they come from two main sources. Natural earthquakes occur occasionally because, even in the middle of a plate, stresses from distant plate movements and old faults can cause rocks to slip. The most damaging natural earthquake in South Africa's recorded history struck the Ceres and Tulbagh area of the Western Cape on 29 September 1969, with a magnitude of about six point three; it killed several people and damaged many buildings, including historic homes in Tulbagh. The second source is mining. The deep gold mines of Gauteng, the Free State and North West remove huge volumes of rock, which changes the stresses in the surrounding rock and causes tremors, sometimes strong enough to damage houses and to trap or kill miners underground. A magnitude five point five tremor near Orkney in North West in 2014 was felt as far away as Johannesburg and Pretoria. The Council for Geoscience records hundreds of small tremors every year, most too small to feel.

### subtopic_3 — Effects on People
**Assistant:** Why do buildings kill people and not the shaking itself?

**Tutor:** Because the ground shaking itself rarely injures anyone standing in an open field: at worst it knocks them over. The danger comes from what is around and above people when the shaking starts. Buildings are designed to hold up their own weight, pushing straight down. An earthquake adds powerful sideways forces, swinging the ground back and forth. Walls made of unreinforced brick, stone or concrete blocks crack and collapse; heavy roofs and floors fall onto the floors below, crushing those inside, a collapse sometimes called pancaking. Falling glass, chimneys, signs and furniture also injure many people. That is why geographers and engineers say that earthquakes do not kill people; buildings do. It also means that much of the risk can be reduced. Buildings can be designed with steel reinforcement, flexible frames, cross-bracing and foundations that absorb movement, and building codes can require these features. In Japan and Chile, strict codes have greatly reduced deaths, as the next lesson shows.

### subtopic_4 — Tsunamis and Why Effects Differ
**Assistant:** How did the 2004 tsunami reach Africa?

**Tutor:** By travelling right across the Indian Ocean. The earthquake on 26 December 2004 ruptured about thirteen hundred kilometres of the sea floor off Sumatra, lifting it by several metres and pushing up the whole depth of ocean water above. Waves spread outwards in all directions at the speed of a jet aircraft. They hit Indonesia within minutes, Thailand, Sri Lanka and India within about two hours, and reached the coast of East Africa about seven to eight hours after the earthquake. Somalia was hardest hit in Africa, with about three hundred people killed, and there were deaths in Kenya and Tanzania too. The waves reached South Africa's coast more than eleven hours later, where they were much smaller, but strong surges and currents still caused deaths. Because there was no tsunami warning system in the Indian Ocean in 2004, almost nobody was warned, even though hours passed before the waves reached some countries. Afterwards, an Indian Ocean Tsunami Warning System was created, using sea-floor sensors and buoys.
