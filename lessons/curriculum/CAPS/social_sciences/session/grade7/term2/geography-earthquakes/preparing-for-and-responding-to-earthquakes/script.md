# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines how people reduce the impact of earthquakes. The anchor is a comparison: the magnitude seven earthquake in Haiti in 2010, which killed well over a hundred thousand people, and the magnitude eight point eight earthquake in Chile the same year, about five hundred times more powerful, which killed about five hundred. The governing idea is that a natural hazard becomes a disaster when it strikes vulnerable people, and that preparation before, and effective response after, can greatly reduce loss of life and suffering.

## Subtopic: Hazard, Risk and Vulnerability

Geographers use three key terms when studying natural events such as earthquakes, volcanoes and floods.

A hazard is a natural event that has the potential to cause harm to people and property, such as an earthquake. An earthquake in an empty desert is still a hazard, but it harms no one.

A disaster happens when a hazard actually causes serious harm: large loss of life, injuries, destruction of homes and infrastructure, and disruption of a community's ability to cope on its own.

Vulnerability means how easily people can be harmed by a hazard. People are more vulnerable when they live in poorly built houses, in crowded areas, on unstable ground or steep slopes, when they are poor, sick, very young or elderly, when they lack information or warnings, and when emergency services are weak.

Risk combines the hazard and the vulnerability. A simple way to express it is that risk depends on how likely and how strong the hazard is, multiplied by how vulnerable the people exposed to it are. Reducing either part reduces the risk.

We cannot reduce the earthquake hazard itself. Plates will keep moving. So all efforts to reduce the impact of earthquakes focus on reducing vulnerability and on improving the capacity of people to cope.

Preparation before the event is called mitigation and preparedness; actions after the event are called response and recovery. Together these form the disaster management cycle: prepare, respond, recover, and prepare again, using the lessons learned.

The comparison between Haiti and Chile in 2010 shows these ideas clearly. Chile had strict building codes, experience of large earthquakes, emergency plans and greater wealth. Haiti, the poorest country in the Americas, had many poorly built concrete buildings, a crowded capital near the epicentre, and very weak government services.

## Subtopic: Building and Planning for Earthquakes

Because collapsing buildings cause most earthquake deaths, the single most important way to reduce the impact of earthquakes is to build structures that can withstand shaking.

Earthquake-resistant buildings use several techniques. Steel reinforcement inside concrete lets it bend without breaking. Cross-bracing, diagonal steel beams, stiffens frames against sideways movement. Base isolators, large rubber and steel pads placed between a building and its foundations, let the ground move beneath while the building sways gently. Some tall buildings have huge weights near the top, called dampers, that swing against the building's motion. Shear walls, strong reinforced walls, resist sideways forces. Lightweight roofs and materials reduce the weight that can fall on people. Even simple, cheap methods help in poor communities: timber frames, reinforced corners and bands of reinforced concrete around the walls make ordinary houses far safer.

Building codes are laws that set minimum standards for construction. Countries such as Japan, Chile and New Zealand have strict codes and enforce them with inspections. Codes save lives only if they are followed: in several recent earthquakes, many deaths occurred in new buildings that had not been built to the code.

Retrofitting means strengthening existing buildings, especially schools, hospitals and bridges, which must stay standing to shelter people and treat the injured.

Land-use planning keeps people away from the most dangerous places. Planners map active faults, steep slopes that could slide, and soft, waterlogged soils that shake more strongly, and restrict building there, using them instead for parks or farmland.

Infrastructure can be designed to fail safely: gas pipes with automatic shut-off valves prevent fires, and flexible pipe joints are less likely to break.

The questions for this section are with you now: earthquake-resistant building techniques, building codes, retrofitting, land-use planning and safer infrastructure.

## Subtopic: Warnings, Education and Drills

Scientists cannot yet predict exactly when and where an earthquake will strike. They can identify areas at high risk and estimate the probability of a large earthquake over decades, but not the day or hour.

Earthquake early warning systems are different from prediction. When an earthquake begins, the first, faster seismic waves reach seismograph stations seconds before the slower, more damaging waves reach cities further away. Computers detect the first waves and instantly send alerts to phones, schools, trains and factories. The warning may be only a few seconds to a minute, but that is enough for trains to brake, surgeons to stop operating, gas to be shut off and people to take cover. Japan, Mexico and the west coast of the United States have such systems.

Tsunami warning systems use seismographs, sea-level gauges and deep-ocean buoys to detect tsunamis and warn coastal areas, sometimes hours before the waves arrive. Sirens, signs marking evacuation routes to high ground, and public education help people respond.

Education teaches people what to do. The most widely recommended advice during shaking indoors is drop, cover and hold on: drop to your hands and knees, take cover under a sturdy table or desk, protecting your head and neck, and hold on until the shaking stops. Stay away from windows, shelves and heavy objects that could fall. Do not run outside during the shaking, because falling glass and masonry near the walls of buildings are a major danger. If outside, move to an open area away from buildings, trees and power lines. On the coast, if the shaking is strong or the sea draws back, move immediately to high ground.

Drills, regular practice in schools and workplaces, make these actions automatic. In Japan, schoolchildren practise earthquake drills several times a year.

Households can prepare an emergency kit with water, food, a torch, a radio, a first aid kit, medicines and copies of important documents, and agree on a family meeting place.

## Subtopic: Responding and Recovering

After an earthquake, the response must be fast, because the chances of rescuing trapped people fall sharply after the first two or three days.

Immediate response includes search and rescue, with trained teams using sniffer dogs, listening devices and cameras on long poles to find survivors under rubble, and heavy equipment to lift debris; much of the earliest rescue is done by neighbours and local people with their bare hands. Medical care sets up field hospitals and treats the injured. Emergency shelter provides tents and blankets for the homeless. Clean water, food and sanitation prevent disease. Fires must be put out and dangerous buildings cordoned off. Communication is restored so that families can find one another.

International aid often arrives within days. South Africa has its own search and rescue teams, such as Rescue South Africa, a non-profit organisation that has helped after earthquakes in Haiti, Nepal, Turkey and elsewhere, and the Gift of the Givers foundation, which has sent teams and supplies to many disasters, including the Turkey and Syria earthquakes of 2023.

Recovery is the long process of rebuilding homes, schools, hospitals, roads and the economy, which can take many years. A key principle is to build back better: to rebuild to higher standards so that the community is less vulnerable next time. Recovery also includes caring for people's mental health after trauma.

Governments, aid agencies, communities and individuals all have roles. In South Africa, the National Disaster Management Centre co-ordinates the response to disasters, including floods, fires and earthquakes.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: believing scientists can predict the exact date of an earthquake. Two: running outside during shaking. Three: rebuilding exactly as before, making the same mistakes.

The questions for this section are with you now: search and rescue, emergency needs, international aid including South African teams, building back better, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: why some places suffer more, strong buildings, what to do, and helping afterwards.

## Subtopic: Why Some Places Suffer More

An earthquake is a hazard: something that could hurt people. It becomes a disaster when it actually does serious harm.

How much harm depends on how vulnerable people are. Weak houses, crowded cities, poverty and no warnings make people more vulnerable.

In 2010, an earthquake in Haiti killed well over a hundred thousand people. A much bigger one in Chile killed about five hundred. Chile had strong buildings and good plans; Haiti, a very poor country, did not.

## Subtopic: Strong Buildings and Drills

Since falling buildings hurt most people, build them strong. Put steel inside concrete. Add diagonal steel braces. Put big rubber pads under the building so the ground can wiggle underneath. Make laws, called building codes, and check that builders follow them. Don't build on faults or soft, wet ground.

And practise. If the ground shakes and you are indoors: drop to the ground, take cover under a strong table, and hold on until it stops. Stay away from windows. Don't run outside while it is shaking. At the coast, if the sea pulls back, go to high ground.

Try this part's questions on staying safe.

## Subtopic: Helping Afterwards

After an earthquake, speed matters. Rescue teams, with dogs and listening machines, search the rubble for survivors, especially in the first three days. People need doctors, tents, clean water and food.

Help comes from around the world. South African teams like Rescue South Africa and Gift of the Givers have helped in Haiti, Nepal and Turkey.

Then comes rebuilding, which can take years. The smart way is to build back better: stronger than before.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Earthquakes cannot be stopped or predicted exactly, but harm can be reduced. Vulnerable people suffer most. Strong buildings, building codes, careful planning, warning systems, drills and emergency kits save lives. After an earthquake, fast rescue, medical care, shelter and clean water are vital, and rebuilding should build back better.
