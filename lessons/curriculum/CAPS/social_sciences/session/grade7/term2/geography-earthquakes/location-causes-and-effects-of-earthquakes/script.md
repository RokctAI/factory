# Part 1 — Expert

This session studies the causes, location and effects of earthquakes. The anchor is the earthquake of 26 December 2004 off Sumatra in Indonesia, whose tsunami killed about two hundred and thirty thousand people in fourteen countries, including people as far away as Somalia and South Africa. The governing idea is that earthquakes are caused by the sudden release of stress built up as plates move, so they are concentrated at plate boundaries, and that the harm they cause depends not only on their strength but on where people live and how they build.

## Subtopic: What Causes an Earthquake

An earthquake is a sudden shaking of the ground caused by the release of energy in the Earth's crust.

Tectonic plates move slowly and steadily, but their edges do not slide smoothly. Rough rock surfaces lock together by friction. As the plates keep moving, the rocks bend and stress builds up, like a ruler being bent further and further. Eventually the stress becomes greater than the strength of the rock or the friction holding it, and the rocks suddenly snap or slip, jumping to a new position. The stored energy is released in seconds as vibrations called seismic waves, which spread out through the Earth in all directions, like ripples from a stone thrown into a pond.

A crack in the crust along which rocks move is called a fault. Most earthquakes happen along faults, many of them at plate boundaries.

The point underground where the rocks first break is the focus, or hypocentre. It may be a few kilometres deep or, at subduction zones, hundreds of kilometres deep. The point on the Earth's surface directly above the focus is the epicentre. Shaking is usually strongest at and near the epicentre and becomes weaker with distance. Shallow earthquakes generally cause more damage at the surface than deep ones of the same size.

A large earthquake is often preceded by smaller foreshocks, though these are only recognised afterwards, and is usually followed by many aftershocks, smaller earthquakes as the rocks settle, which can continue for months and can bring down buildings already weakened by the main shock.

Not all earthquakes are natural. Human activities can trigger small earthquakes, for example deep mining, as in the gold mines around Johannesburg and Klerksdorp, or the filling of large dams.

## Subtopic: Where Earthquakes Happen and How They Are Measured

When the epicentres of earthquakes are plotted on a world map, they form narrow belts that match the plate boundaries, almost exactly the same belts as volcanoes. The Pacific Ring of Fire experiences about eighty percent of the world's largest earthquakes, in countries such as Japan, Indonesia, the Philippines, Chile, Mexico and the west coast of the United States. A second major belt, the Alpine-Himalayan belt, runs from the Mediterranean through Turkey, Iran and Pakistan to the Himalayas and China, where the African, Arabian and Indian plates push north into the Eurasian Plate. Smaller earthquakes occur along the mid-ocean ridges and the East African Rift.

Earthquakes are recorded by seismographs, instruments that detect and record ground movement. A record of shaking is called a seismogram. By comparing the arrival times of waves at several stations, scientists can locate the focus and calculate the size of an earthquake within minutes. The Council for Geoscience runs a network of seismograph stations across South Africa.

The size of an earthquake is measured as its magnitude, the amount of energy released. The original magnitude scale was devised by Charles Richter in 1935; scientists now use the moment magnitude scale, but the numbers are similar. The scale is logarithmic: each whole number up means about ten times more ground movement and about thirty-two times more energy. A magnitude seven earthquake releases about a thousand times more energy than a magnitude five. Earthquakes below about magnitude three are rarely felt; above six can cause serious damage; the largest ever recorded, in Chile in 1960, was magnitude nine point five.

Intensity is different from magnitude. It describes the effects of the shaking at a particular place, from not felt to total destruction, on the Modified Mercalli scale, numbered with Roman numerals from one to twelve. One earthquake has one magnitude but many intensities, decreasing with distance from the epicentre.

## Subtopic: Effects on People

The effects of an earthquake can be divided into primary effects, caused directly by the shaking, and secondary effects, which follow as a result.

Injury and loss of life. Most deaths in earthquakes are caused not by the ground shaking itself but by collapsing buildings, falling objects and debris. People are trapped under rubble, and survival depends on how quickly they are rescued. The 2010 earthquake in Haiti, of magnitude seven, killed somewhere between about a hundred thousand and more than two hundred thousand people, partly because many buildings were poorly constructed. The Turkey and Syria earthquakes of February 2023 killed more than fifty thousand people.

Damage to infrastructure. Infrastructure means the basic systems a society needs: roads, bridges, railways, water pipes, sewers, electricity lines, telephone networks, hospitals and schools. Earthquakes break pipes and cables, crack roads and bring down bridges, which also makes rescue harder, because rescuers and supplies cannot get through.

Fires. Broken gas pipes and fallen electrical wires start fires, and broken water mains mean there is no water to fight them. After the 1906 San Francisco earthquake, fires burned for days and destroyed far more of the city than the shaking; after the 1923 earthquake near Tokyo, fires killed tens of thousands.

Disease. Damaged water supplies and sewers lead to contaminated drinking water, and crowded emergency shelters help diseases spread. After the Haiti earthquake, an outbreak of cholera, a disease spread by contaminated water, killed thousands more people.

Displacement. People whose homes are destroyed or unsafe become homeless, often living in tents or shelters for months or years. Displacement disrupts schooling, work and family life.

Economic losses. Businesses close, jobs are lost and the cost of rebuilding can set a country's development back by years.

Landslides, triggered by shaking on steep slopes, can bury villages.

## Subtopic: Tsunamis and Why Effects Differ

A tsunami is a series of very long, powerful sea waves usually caused by an earthquake under the sea floor. When a large undersea earthquake, usually at a subduction zone, suddenly lifts or drops a section of the sea floor, the whole column of water above is pushed up or pulled down. Waves spread out across the ocean at speeds of up to about eight hundred kilometres an hour, as fast as a jet aircraft. In deep water they are low and hardly noticed by ships. As they reach shallow coastal water they slow down and pile up into walls of water that can be ten or even thirty metres high, flooding far inland. Often the first sign is the sea suddenly drawing back from the beach, exposing the sea floor, minutes before the wave arrives.

The 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, caused by a magnitude nine point one earthquake off Sumatra, struck Indonesia, Sri Lanka, India, Thailand and the coast of East Africa. Waves reached South Africa's east coast hours later, and lives were lost even there. The 2011 Tohoku earthquake in Japan, magnitude nine point zero, caused a tsunami that killed about eighteen thousand people and damaged the Fukushima nuclear power station.

The effects of earthquakes of the same size vary greatly from place to place. Important factors are: the magnitude and depth of the earthquake; the distance from the epicentre; the population density; the quality of buildings, since well-designed buildings survive shaking much better; the type of ground, since soft, wet soils shake more and can turn almost liquid; the time of day, since people at home asleep at night may be trapped; and the wealth and preparedness of the country, which decide the quality of buildings, warning systems and emergency services. This is why poorer countries often suffer many more deaths than richer countries from earthquakes of similar size.

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: why the ground shakes, where it shakes, what it does, and giant waves.

## Subtopic: Snap!

Bend a ruler slowly and it bends and bends, and then it snaps. Rocks do the same.

Plates move slowly, but their edges get stuck. Stress builds up. Then the rocks suddenly snap and jump. The shaking spreads out like ripples in a pond. That is an earthquake.

The place underground where the rocks break is the focus. The place on the surface right above it is the epicentre, where shaking is usually strongest. Smaller aftershocks can follow for months.

## Subtopic: Where and How Big

Earthquakes happen mostly where plates meet: around the Pacific Ocean in the Ring of Fire, and in a belt from the Mediterranean to the Himalayas.

Machines called seismographs record the shaking. The size of an earthquake is its magnitude. Each step up is much bigger: a seven is about thirty-two times more powerful than a six. Above six can cause serious damage.

## Subtopic: What Earthquakes Do

Most people hurt in earthquakes are hit or trapped by falling buildings. Roads, bridges, water pipes and power lines break, so help struggles to get through. Gas pipes break and start fires. Dirty water spreads diseases like cholera. People lose their homes and must live in tents.

Under the sea, an earthquake can push up the water and make a tsunami: giant waves racing across the ocean as fast as a jet. If the sea suddenly pulls back from the beach, run to high ground.

The same earthquake hurts poor places more, because buildings are weaker and help is slower.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Earthquakes happen when stuck rocks snap along faults, mostly at plate boundaries. The focus is underground and the epicentre above it. Magnitude measures energy, and each step is about thirty-two times more. Effects include deaths from falling buildings, broken infrastructure, fires, disease, homelessness and tsunamis. Weak buildings and poverty make effects worse.
