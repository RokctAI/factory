# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the experience of enslaved people on plantations in the American South and their culture and individual resistance. The anchor is the spiritual Go Down, Moses, with its refrain let my people go, sung by enslaved people about the Israelites' escape from slavery in Egypt, and understood as a song about their own longing for freedom. The governing idea is that slavery was a system of control and cruelty, but that enslaved people resisted it constantly, through culture, community and countless individual acts, preserving their dignity and humanity.

## Subtopic: Daily Life and Work

Most enslaved people in the American South worked as field hands. On large cotton plantations, work often ran from before sunrise to after sunset, six days a week, and longer at harvest. Under the gang system, workers laboured in groups driven by an overseer or driver with a whip, forced to keep a fast pace. Under the task system, used on rice plantations, each person was given a set task for the day and could sometimes use any time left over for themselves. At cotton harvest, each picker had a daily quota, often measured in pounds, and could be whipped for picking too little. Children began light work at about six or seven and full field work by their early teens. Elderly people cared for young children or worked in gardens.

A minority worked as house servants, cooking, cleaning, sewing and caring for the owner's children; this was less physically exhausting but kept them under constant watch. Others were skilled craftspeople: blacksmiths, carpenters, coopers and seamstresses, sometimes hired out to other employers, with wages paid to the owner.

Living conditions were poor. Families lived in small, crowded wooden cabins with dirt floors, few furnishings and little protection from cold. Food rations were usually cornmeal and a little salted pork, which enslaved people supplemented by growing vegetables in small garden plots, fishing and hunting when allowed. Clothing was rough and issued once or twice a year.

Control was enforced through violence and fear: whipping, branding, beatings and other punishments, and the threat of being sold away from family. Enslaved people needed written passes to leave the plantation, and patrols of armed white men stopped and punished those found without them. Laws prevented them from learning to read and write in most Southern states, from owning weapons, or from testifying against white people.

## Subtopic: Families and Community

Enslaved people's families had no legal protection. Marriages between enslaved people were not recognised by law, so owners could separate husbands and wives, and sell children away from their parents, whenever they chose, for profit, to pay debts, or when an owner died and the estate was divided. Historians estimate that a large share of enslaved people in the upper South experienced the sale of a close family member.

Despite this, family was at the centre of enslaved people's lives. Couples married in their own ceremonies, sometimes by jumping over a broom, a custom that is still remembered. Parents taught children how to survive under slavery, when to stay silent, and who they were. Extended families and communities took in children whose parents had been sold, and people who were not related became aunts, uncles and grandparents to one another. After slavery ended in 1865, thousands of formerly enslaved people placed newspaper advertisements searching for family members sold away years before.

The quarters were a community where enslaved people had some space away from the owner's eye, especially at night and on Sundays. There they cooked, told stories, sang, prayed, cared for the sick, held celebrations and mourned their dead. They kept memories of Africa alive, in words, music, foods such as okra and black-eyed peas, crafts such as basket weaving, and beliefs.

Over generations, these elements blended African traditions with new experiences in America, creating a distinct African American culture. Along the coast of South Carolina and Georgia, the Gullah Geechee people developed a creole language and culture with strong West African roots that survives today.

The questions for this section are with you now: the lack of legal protection for families, family and community life in the quarters, and the creation of African American culture.

## Subtopic: Songs, Stories and Faith

Culture was one of the strongest forms of survival and resistance.

Songs. Work songs set the rhythm of labour in the fields, often in a call-and-response pattern, in which a leader sang a line and the group answered, a musical form brought from West Africa. Spirituals were religious songs, often based on Bible stories of deliverance from slavery, such as Moses leading the Israelites out of Egypt, Daniel saved from the lions' den, and the crossing of the River Jordan to the promised land. Songs such as Go Down, Moses, Swing Low, Sweet Chariot and Steal Away to Jesus expressed sorrow, faith and longing for freedom. Some historians believe certain spirituals carried hidden messages about escape, although the evidence for specific codes is debated. The music of enslaved people later fed into gospel, blues, jazz and much of modern popular music.

Stories. Folk tales, many with roots in West African traditions, were told in the quarters. The most famous feature Brer Rabbit, a small, clever trickster who outwits stronger animals such as Brer Fox and Brer Bear, related to West African trickster tales such as those of Anansi the spider and the hare. The weak defeating the strong through wit carried an obvious meaning for listeners living under slavery.

Faith. Many enslaved people became Christians, but they often rejected the version preached by owners, which stressed obedience, and instead emphasised God's love for the oppressed and promise of freedom. They met secretly in the woods, in what historians call the invisible church, to worship in their own way, with singing, dancing and shouting. Some enslaved Africans were Muslims, and a few kept Islamic practices and wrote in Arabic; others kept African religious beliefs.

The questions for this section are with you now: work songs and call and response, spirituals and their meanings, trickster tales such as Brer Rabbit, and the invisible church.

## Subtopic: Everyday Resistance

Open rebellion was very dangerous and rare, but enslaved people resisted slavery every day in many ways. Historians call these acts individual or everyday resistance.

Resisting work. Working slowly, pretending to be ill, misunderstanding instructions on purpose, breaking tools and damaging crops reduced the owner's profit and asserted some control.

Taking back. Taking food from the owner's stores or livestock was seen by enslaved people not as stealing but as taking back what their labour had produced.

Learning. Learning to read and write was forbidden in most Southern states, so enslaved people learned in secret, from each other or from sympathetic white children, at great risk. Frederick Douglass, who escaped slavery in 1838 and became a famous writer and speaker against slavery, described how learning to read showed him the path to freedom.

Keeping culture and family. Holding on to names, languages, stories, songs, faith and family ties was itself a refusal to accept the owners' view that enslaved people were mere property.

Running away. Some ran away for short periods, hiding in woods and swamps to escape punishment or to visit family on other plantations. Others attempted permanent escape to the northern states or Canada, where slavery was illegal, a dangerous journey studied in a later lesson. In some places, groups of escaped people, called maroons, formed hidden communities in swamps and mountains, such as in the Great Dismal Swamp of Virginia and North Carolina, and in Jamaica and Brazil.

Self-purchase and legal action. A few saved money earned from extra work to buy their freedom, and a few went to court.

Desperate resistance. Some resisted by violence against owners or overseers, and some took their own lives rather than remain enslaved.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking enslaved people accepted slavery because large revolts were rare. Two: thinking all enslaved people had the same experience. Three: thinking African culture was completely destroyed in America.

The questions for this section are with you now: forms of everyday resistance, Frederick Douglass and literacy, running away and maroon communities, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: a day on the plantation, family and community, songs and stories, and fighting back every day.

## Subtopic: A Day on the Plantation

Most enslaved people worked in the fields from before sunrise to after sunset, six days a week. Overseers with whips forced them to work fast. At cotton harvest, each person had to pick a set amount or be whipped. Children started work young.

Some worked in the owner's house, cooking and cleaning. Some were skilled blacksmiths or carpenters.

They lived in small, crowded cabins and ate mostly cornmeal and a little pork. They needed a written pass to leave the plantation. Most were not allowed to learn to read.

The worst fear was being sold away from their families.

## Subtopic: Songs and Stories

The law said enslaved families did not count, so owners could sell parents and children apart. But families and communities held together. People married in their own ceremonies, like jumping the broom, and looked after children whose parents had been sold.

At night and on Sundays, in the quarters, they sang, told stories and prayed. Work songs used call and response: one person sings, everyone answers. Spirituals like Go Down, Moses, with its words let my people go, were about freedom. Stories about Brer Rabbit, a small, clever trickster who beats bigger animals, gave hope. Some met secretly in the woods to worship in their own way.

## Subtopic: Fighting Back Every Day

Big rebellions were rare and very dangerous. But enslaved people fought back every day. They worked slowly, pretended to be sick, broke tools, and took food. They learned to read in secret, like Frederick Douglass, who escaped and became a famous speaker against slavery. They ran away, sometimes for days, sometimes forever. Some lived in hidden communities in swamps.

And just keeping their names, songs, stories, faith and families alive was a way of saying: we are people, not property.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Enslaved people worked long hours under violence and the threat of sale, in poor conditions. They built strong families and communities and a rich culture of songs, spirituals, stories and faith. They resisted every day by slowing work, learning to read, running away and keeping their culture alive.
