# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines John Brown and the final struggle to abolish slavery in the United States. The anchor is the note John Brown gave a guard on the morning of his execution, predicting that the crimes of this guilty land would never be purged away but with blood. The governing idea is that the abolition of slavery came through many forms of struggle, peaceful and violent, by Black and white Americans, and that John Brown remains one of the most controversial figures in American history: a hero to some, a terrorist to others, a man historians must study from several perspectives.

## Subtopic: The Abolition Movement

Abolition means ending something by law. Abolitionists were people who campaigned to end the slave trade and slavery.

In Britain, a campaign led by people such as Thomas Clarkson, William Wilberforce, Olaudah Equiano and many ordinary men and women, together with the resistance of enslaved people in the Caribbean, led Parliament to abolish the British slave trade in 1807 and slavery in most of the British Empire in 1834, including in the Cape Colony, a subject of Term 4. The United States banned the import of enslaved people from Africa in 1808, but slavery itself continued and grew in the South.

In the United States, the abolition movement grew from the 1830s. Leading figures included William Lloyd Garrison, a white journalist who published The Liberator from 1831 and demanded immediate emancipation; Frederick Douglass, who had escaped slavery and became a brilliant speaker, writer and newspaper editor; Sojourner Truth, a formerly enslaved woman who spoke for the rights of Black people and of women; and the sisters Sarah and Angelina Grimké, white women from a slaveholding family in South Carolina who turned against slavery. Black abolitionists, free and formerly enslaved, were at the heart of the movement.

Abolitionists used many methods: newspapers, pamphlets, books and lectures; petitions to Congress; the testimony of formerly enslaved people; boycotts of slave-grown goods; and help for escapers through the Underground Railroad. The novel Uncle Tom's Cabin by Harriet Beecher Stowe, published in 1852, sold hundreds of thousands of copies and turned many Northerners against slavery.

Abolitionists were a minority, and many were attacked by angry mobs, even in the North. Most rejected violence. But as the conflict over slavery grew more bitter in the 1850s, some came to believe that slavery would never end peacefully.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of abolition, British abolition, leading American abolitionists, their methods, and the growing bitterness of the 1850s.

## Subtopic: Who Was John Brown?

John Brown was born in Connecticut in 1800 into a deeply religious white family who opposed slavery. He grew up in Ohio and tried many businesses, including farming, tanning leather and trading wool, most of which failed. He had twenty children, of whom eleven survived to adulthood.

Brown believed that slavery was a terrible sin against God and that he had a duty to fight it. He helped escapers on the Underground Railroad, and he lived for a time in a community of free Black farmers in North Elba, New York, which was unusual for a white man of his time. He treated Black people as equals and friends, which many white abolitionists did not.

In the 1850s, the conflict over slavery became violent in the territory of Kansas, where settlers were to vote on whether it would allow slavery. Pro-slavery and anti-slavery settlers fought, and the period became known as Bleeding Kansas. After pro-slavery forces attacked the anti-slavery town of Lawrence in 1856, Brown and a small group, including some of his sons, killed five pro-slavery settlers at Pottawatomie Creek, dragging them from their homes at night. This killing of unarmed men is one of the most criticised acts of his life. Brown and his supporters also fought pro-slavery forces in battles in Kansas, and one of his sons was killed.

Brown came to believe that slavery could only be ended by force. He planned to start an armed uprising in the South, attacking a federal armoury to seize weapons, arming enslaved people who would join him, and creating a free base in the Appalachian Mountains from which more raids could free people. He met Frederick Douglass and Harriet Tubman to seek support. Tubman helped him recruit and may have planned to join, but became ill. Douglass admired Brown but refused to join, believing the plan would fail.

The questions for this section are with you now: John Brown's background and beliefs, his relationships with Black Americans, Bleeding Kansas and Pottawatomie, and his plan.

## Subtopic: The Raid on Harpers Ferry, 1859

On the night of 16 October 1859, John Brown led a force of twenty-one men, sixteen white and five Black, including three of his sons, in an attack on the town of Harpers Ferry, Virginia, now in West Virginia, where the United States government had an armoury and arsenal storing tens of thousands of guns. The Black raiders included Dangerfield Newby, a free man who hoped to free his enslaved wife and children, whose letters begging him to rescue her were found on his body, and Shields Green, who had escaped slavery.

The raiders captured the armoury and took hostages, including Lewis Washington, a relative of George Washington. Brown expected enslaved people from the area to rise up and join him, but few did; news had not been spread in advance, there were relatively few enslaved people nearby, and the risks of joining were enormous.

Local militia surrounded the town, and fighting broke out. Brown and his remaining men barricaded themselves in a small brick fire-engine house. On 18 October, United States Marines under the command of Colonel Robert E. Lee, later the leading general of the South in the Civil War, stormed the building. Ten of the raiders were killed, including two of Brown's sons, and Brown was wounded and captured. Several townspeople and Marines were also killed; ironically, the first person killed by the raiders was Heyward Shepherd, a free Black railway baggage handler.

Brown was tried in Virginia for treason against the state, murder and conspiring with enslaved people to rebel. He was found guilty and sentenced to death. At his trial and in his letters from prison, he spoke calmly and powerfully, saying that he had acted to help the poor and despised, as the Bible taught, and that if he must die to further the ends of justice, he accepted it. He was hanged on 2 December 1859 in Charles Town, Virginia. Several other raiders, including Shields Green, were also executed.

The questions for this section are with you now: the raiders and their aims, the events of the raid, its failure, Brown's trial and his execution.

## Subtopic: Perspectives and the Road to Freedom

The raid failed, but its effects were enormous, because people in the North and the South saw John Brown in completely different ways.

To most white Southerners, Brown was a murderer, a terrorist and a madman who had tried to start a bloody slave revolt. The raid confirmed their fears that Northerners wanted to destroy slavery by force. Southern states strengthened their militias, and talk of leaving the United States grew.

In the North, opinions were divided. Many Northerners condemned the violence. But many abolitionists praised Brown's courage and his willingness to die for enslaved people. On the day of his execution, church bells rang in some Northern towns. The writer Henry David Thoreau called him a hero. Black communities held meetings honouring him as a martyr for freedom. Frederick Douglass later said that Brown had begun the war that ended slavery.

Historians today also differ. Some see him as a brave man who saw clearly that slavery, itself a system of violence, would only end by force, and who treated Black people as equals at a time when few white people did. Others focus on the killing of unarmed men in Kansas and see a fanatic whose methods cannot be justified. Most try to understand both his moral courage and his violence in the context of his time.

The road to freedom. In 1860 Abraham Lincoln, whose Republican Party opposed the spread of slavery into new territories, was elected president. Eleven Southern states left the Union and formed the Confederate States of America. The American Civil War, from 1861 to 1865, followed; Union soldiers marched to the song John Brown's Body. In 1863 Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation declared enslaved people in the Confederate states free, and about one hundred and eighty thousand Black soldiers fought for the Union. In 1865 the North won, and the Thirteenth Amendment to the Constitution abolished slavery throughout the United States. About four million people were freed. Their long struggle for equal rights, however, was only beginning.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking white abolitionists alone ended slavery; enslaved and free Black people led the struggle. Two: seeing John Brown as simply a hero or simply a villain. Three: thinking the end of slavery brought equality.

The questions for this section are with you now: Southern and Northern reactions, historians' perspectives, the Civil War, the Emancipation Proclamation, the Thirteenth Amendment and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Ending Slavery

Abolitionists were people who wanted slavery ended by law. Britain ended its slave trade in 1807 and slavery in most of its empire in 1834.

In the United States, abolitionists like Frederick Douglass, who had escaped slavery, Sojourner Truth and William Lloyd Garrison gave speeches, wrote newspapers and books, and helped escapers. A novel, Uncle Tom's Cabin, made many Northerners angry about slavery.

Most abolitionists did not believe in violence. But by the 1850s, some thought slavery would never end peacefully.

## Subtopic: John Brown's Raid

John Brown was a white man who believed slavery was a terrible sin. He lived among free Black farmers and treated Black people as equals. In Kansas, during violent fighting over slavery, his group killed five pro-slavery men, which many people condemn.

He decided slavery could only be ended by force. In October 1859, he and twenty-one men, white and Black, attacked the government armoury at Harpers Ferry, Virginia, hoping enslaved people would join them and rise up. Hardly anyone could join. Soldiers stormed the building, killed ten raiders, and captured Brown.

He was tried and hanged on 2 December 1859.

## Subtopic: Hero or Villain?

People saw John Brown very differently. Most white Southerners called him a murderer and terrorist. Many in the North, especially Black communities and abolitionists, called him a hero who died for freedom. Historians still argue: he was brave and fought a cruel system, but he also killed unarmed people.

His raid made the North and South even angrier with each other. In 1861 the Civil War began. In 1863 President Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation declared enslaved people in the South free, and about a hundred and eighty thousand Black soldiers fought for the North. In 1865 the North won, and the Thirteenth Amendment ended slavery in the whole United States. About four million people were freed. But the fight for equal rights went on.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Abolitionists, Black and white, campaigned to end slavery. John Brown, believing only force could end it, raided Harpers Ferry in 1859, failed, and was hanged. He was seen as a terrorist in the South and a martyr by many in the North. His raid helped lead to the Civil War, after which the Thirteenth Amendment of 1865 abolished slavery in the United States.
