### subtopic_3 — Songs, Stories and Faith
**Assistant:** Did spirituals really contain secret escape codes?

**Tutor:** Possibly some did, but historians are careful because much of the evidence comes from later memories and storytelling rather than from documents of the time. We know for certain that spirituals had double meanings. Frederick Douglass wrote that when enslaved people sang of going to Canaan, the promised land of the Bible, they also meant the North, where slavery was illegal. Songs about crossing the River Jordan or a chariot coming to carry them home spoke of heaven, but also of freedom on earth. It makes sense that people who could not speak openly used songs to share feelings and perhaps information that owners would not understand. The popular claim that particular songs, such as Follow the Drinking Gourd, gave detailed escape directions is more doubtful; that song was first published in the twentieth century, and historians have found little evidence that it was used by escapers. The careful conclusion is that spirituals were a powerful language of hope and resistance, even if specific codes cannot be proved.

### subtopic_4 — Everyday Resistance
**Assistant:** Why was learning to read forbidden?

**Tutor:** Because slaveholders feared what enslaved people could do with literacy. A person who could read could read the Bible for themselves, including its stories of freedom from slavery and its teaching that all people are equal before God, rather than relying on the owners' preachers. They could read newspapers and learn about abolitionists in the North, revolts elsewhere and the wider world. A person who could write could forge the passes needed to travel, which helped escapers. Literacy could also spread ideas of rebellion. After revolts such as Nat Turner's in 1831, studied in the next lesson, several Southern states made their laws against teaching enslaved people to read even stricter. Frederick Douglass recalled that when his owner discovered his mistress teaching him the alphabet, the owner said that learning would make him unfit to be a slave. Douglass concluded that this was exactly why he must learn. For this reason, secret learning was one of the bravest forms of resistance, and after emancipation formerly enslaved people founded schools with tremendous energy.

### subtopic_2 — Families and Community
**Assistant:** Why was being sold the thing enslaved people feared most?

**Tutor:** Because sale could destroy in a moment everything that made life bearable. An enslaved person had no legal rights over their own children, spouse or parents. A sale, perhaps because an owner needed money, died in debt, wanted to punish someone, or simply saw a chance of profit, could send a husband, wife or child hundreds of kilometres away, often to the Deep South cotton lands, and they might never see or hear from each other again. In the decades before 1860, around a million people were moved this way in the internal slave trade. Former slaves interviewed in the 1930s often spoke of the auction block and of family members taken away as their worst memories. Owners knew this and used the threat of sale to control people, sometimes more effectively than the whip. That is why families and communities worked so hard to stay connected, and why, after emancipation, so many people spent years searching for loved ones, placing advertisements in newspapers that historians still read today.
