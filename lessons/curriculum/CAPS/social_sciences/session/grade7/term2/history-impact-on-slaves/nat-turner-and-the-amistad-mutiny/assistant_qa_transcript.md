### subtopic_2 — Nat Turner's Revolt, 1831
**Assistant:** Is it fair to call Nat Turner a hero if innocent people died?

**Tutor:** This is one of the hardest questions in the history of slavery, and honest historians do not give a one-word answer. The revolt killed about fifty-five to sixty white people, many of them women and children who had harmed no one, and their deaths were real and terrible. Afterwards, white mobs killed many innocent Black people, so the violence caused enormous suffering on both sides. At the same time, the system Turner rose against kept millions of people in bondage through constant violence: whipping, the sale of children, sexual abuse and killing, all protected by law. Enslaved people had no peaceful, legal way to win their freedom. Some people therefore see Turner as a freedom fighter who used the only means available; others condemn the killing of children as wrong whatever the cause. Many historians try to hold both ideas together: to understand why the revolt happened and what it meant to enslaved people, while not ignoring the suffering of its victims. Learning to think through such questions carefully is part of studying history.

### subtopic_4 — Sengbe Pieh and the Amistad, 1839
**Assistant:** How did the Amistad Africans tell their story if nobody spoke their language?

**Tutor:** At first they could not, which was a serious problem, because in court the Spanish owners claimed they were Cuban-born slaves who spoke Spanish. The abolitionist supporters needed to find someone who spoke Mende. The scholar Josiah Gibbs learned to count to ten in Mende from the captives, then walked the docks of New York repeating the numbers until a sailor recognised them. That sailor was James Covey, a young man from the Mende region who had himself been rescued from a slave ship and was serving on a British vessel. Covey became the interpreter. Through him, Sengbe Pieh and the others described their kidnapping in Africa, the voyage to Cuba and the illegal sale, which proved they had been born in Africa and brought over unlawfully. The captives also learned English during their imprisonment, with help from teachers, and some, such as a boy named Kale, wrote letters, including one to John Quincy Adams. Their own testimony, made possible by this search, was crucial to winning the case.

### subtopic_1 — Rebellion in Context
**Assistant:** Why did the Haitian Revolution frighten American slaveholders so much?

**Tutor:** Because it showed that enslaved people could defeat their owners, the most powerful armies of Europe, and build their own nation. In 1791, enslaved people in the French colony of Saint-Domingue, the richest sugar colony in the world, rose up. Over thirteen years, under leaders such as Toussaint Louverture and Jean-Jacques Dessalines, they fought French, Spanish and British forces, and in 1804 declared the independent country of Haiti, where slavery was abolished. For enslaved people across the Americas, news of Haiti spread by word of mouth among sailors, travellers and refugees, and was a source of inspiration. For slaveholders in the United States, it was a nightmare. Some white refugees from Saint-Domingue fled to American cities with frightening stories. Southern leaders feared that the example of Haiti would inspire revolts at home, and they blamed later plots, such as that of Denmark Vesey, partly on Haitian influence. The United States refused to recognise Haiti as a country until 1862, during the Civil War.
