### subtopic_3 — The Raid on Harpers Ferry, 1859
**Assistant:** Why didn't the enslaved people near Harpers Ferry join the raid?

**Tutor:** There were several practical reasons, and they tell us more about the realities of slavery than about any lack of desire for freedom. First, Brown had not spread word in advance, partly to keep the plan secret, so enslaved people in the area did not know a raid was coming or what its goals were. Second, the area around Harpers Ferry had relatively few enslaved people compared with plantation regions further south. Third, joining an armed revolt was enormously dangerous. Enslaved people knew exactly what had happened after Nat Turner's revolt in 1831: the hangings, the revenge killings of innocent people and the harsher laws. Joining a small band of strangers who might be defeated within hours could mean death not only for themselves but for their families. Fourth, the raid moved quickly from attack to siege, giving little time for anyone to join even if they wished. A few enslaved men taken from nearby farms did end up in the armoury during the fighting, and their role is still debated by historians.

### subtopic_4 — Perspectives and the Road to Freedom
**Assistant:** Did the Civil War end racism in the United States?

**Tutor:** No. It ended slavery, which was an enormous achievement for which millions had struggled, but it did not end racism or bring equality. After the war, during a period called Reconstruction from 1865 to 1877, formerly enslaved people gained citizenship and Black men gained the vote through further amendments to the Constitution, and Black Americans were elected to Congress and state governments, founded schools, churches and businesses, and searched for lost family members. But many white Southerners fought back. Violent groups such as the Ku Klux Klan terrorised Black communities. When federal troops left the South in 1877, Southern states passed segregation laws, known as Jim Crow laws, that separated Black and white people in schools, transport and public places, and used tricks and violence to stop Black people voting. It took the civil rights movement of the 1950s and 1960s, led by people such as Martin Luther King Jr and Rosa Parks, to end legal segregation. South Africans will recognise many parallels with the struggle against apartheid.

### subtopic_1 — The Abolition Movement
**Assistant:** Did slavery in the British Empire affect South Africa?

**Tutor:** Yes, directly. The Cape was a Dutch colony with tens of thousands of enslaved people, brought mainly from the Indian Ocean world and East Africa, which you will study in Term 3. When Britain took control of the Cape at the beginning of the nineteenth century, British laws applied there. Britain's abolition of the slave trade in 1807 stopped new imports of enslaved people to the Cape from 1808. When Britain abolished slavery throughout most of its empire in 1834, about thirty-six thousand enslaved people at the Cape were declared free, although they had to serve a further four years as so-called apprentices to their former owners, so full freedom came only on 1 December 1838. Slave owners at the Cape received compensation from the British government, though many complained it was too little, and this grievance was one of the reasons some Dutch-speaking farmers gave for leaving the Cape on the Great Trek. Emancipation Day, 1 December, is still remembered by many descendants of enslaved people in the Western Cape.
