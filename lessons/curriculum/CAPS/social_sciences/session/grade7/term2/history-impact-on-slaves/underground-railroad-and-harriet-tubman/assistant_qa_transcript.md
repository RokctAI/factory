### subtopic_2 — How the Underground Railroad Worked
**Assistant:** Why did people risk prison to help escapers?

**Tutor:** For different reasons, but mostly because they believed slavery was deeply wrong and that a moral duty was more important than an unjust law. Many free Black people in the North had family members or friends still enslaved, or had escaped themselves, and helping others was a matter of solidarity and survival. Black churches and vigilance committees organised help as part of their community life. Many white helpers were Quakers, members of a Christian group that believed every person had an inner light from God and that slavery violated that equality; Quakers had opposed slavery since the eighteenth century. Others were inspired by the abolitionist movement, by religious revivals, or by meeting escapers and hearing their stories. The risks were real. Under the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, helping an escaper could bring a large fine and six months in prison, and in the South the penalties were much harsher. Some helpers were imprisoned, ruined financially or attacked. Their willingness to break a law they believed unjust is an example of civil disobedience.

### subtopic_4 — Moses of Her People
**Assistant:** Did Harriet Tubman really carry a gun?

**Tutor:** Yes, according to accounts from people who knew her and from Tubman herself in later interviews. She carried a revolver on her rescue journeys, mainly for protection against slave catchers. Several accounts also describe her using it to keep frightened escapers moving. On long, cold, dangerous journeys, some people became exhausted or terrified and wanted to turn back. A person who returned could be forced to reveal the route, the safe houses and the people who had helped, endangering everyone. Tubman is reported to have pointed her gun at such a person and told them they would go on or die, after which they kept going. There is no record that she ever had to fire it at anyone. Historians include this detail because it shows the life-and-death stakes of escape and the hard choices Tubman faced as a leader. It also corrects a gentle, simplified picture of her. She was deeply religious and caring, but she was also tough, determined and willing to take hard decisions to protect the people she led.

### subtopic_1 — Escaping to Freedom
**Assistant:** What happened to escapers who reached Canada?

**Tutor:** They were legally free, because the British Empire had abolished slavery in 1834 and Canada would not return escapers to the United States, but their lives were still often hard. Many settled in communities in what is now Ontario, such as St Catharines, Chatham, Windsor and Buxton, where Black settlements grew, especially after the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, when thousands fled from the northern states too. Some found work as labourers and farmers, built churches and schools, and published newspapers, such as The Provincial Freeman, edited by Mary Ann Shadd, the first Black woman to publish a newspaper in North America. But they also faced poverty, harsh winters, and racial discrimination, as some white Canadians did not welcome them, and some schools and businesses excluded Black people. After the Civil War ended slavery in the United States in 1865, many returned to the United States to find family members and take part in rebuilding the South, while others stayed and became part of Canada's Black community.
