# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the Underground Railroad and the life of Harriet Tubman. The anchor is Tubman's reported statement that on her journeys she never ran her train off the track and never lost a passenger. The governing idea is that escape from slavery required extraordinary courage from the escapers themselves, and that it was helped by a loose, secret network of free Black people, formerly enslaved people and white allies, who risked imprisonment and violence to oppose an unjust law.

## Subtopic: Escaping to Freedom

By the early nineteenth century, slavery had been abolished or was being gradually ended in the northern states of the United States, such as Pennsylvania, New York and Massachusetts. Slavery remained legal in the southern states. This division created a geography of freedom: an enslaved person who could reach the North, or better still Canada, then part of the British Empire, which abolished slavery in 1834, might live in freedom.

Escape was extremely difficult and dangerous. Escapers usually travelled on foot, mostly at night, through forests, swamps and across rivers, often for hundreds of kilometres, with little food and in all weather. They were hunted by slave catchers with dogs, by patrols and by anyone hoping to claim a reward, and newspapers carried advertisements describing runaways and offering money for their capture. If caught, they faced whipping, branding, being sold further south, or death.

Most successful escapers came from the upper South, states such as Maryland, Virginia and Kentucky, which bordered free states, and most were young men, because women were more often tied to young children. Many escaped alone or in small groups and relied on their own courage, intelligence and the help of other enslaved people, who might give them food or directions.

Escapers found the way by following the North Star, which stays in the same place in the night sky, and by following rivers and roads. Some disguised themselves; others used forged passes. Famous escapes included that of Frederick Douglass, who in 1838 travelled north by train and boat disguised as a sailor, using borrowed papers, and Henry Box Brown, who in 1849 had himself mailed in a wooden box from Richmond, Virginia, to Philadelphia, a journey of about twenty-seven hours.

The questions for this section are with you now: why the North and Canada meant freedom, the dangers of escape, who escaped, finding the way, and famous escapes.

## Subtopic: How the Underground Railroad Worked

The Underground Railroad was a secret network of routes, safe houses and people who helped enslaved people escape. It was not underground and not a railroad; the name was a metaphor, which came into use in the 1830s, when real railways were new. It was underground in the sense of secret and illegal.

People in the network used railway words as a code. Conductors were guides who led escapers from one place to the next. Stations or depots were safe houses, barns, churches and other hiding places where escapers could rest, eat and hide. Stationmasters were the people who sheltered them. Passengers or cargo were the escapers. Stockholders gave money, food and clothing. Terminals were the final destinations, often in the northern states or in Canada, in towns such as St Catharines in Ontario.

The network was not a single organisation with a leader and a map. It was a loose web of local groups and individuals, often working without knowing one another, which made it harder to betray. Free Black communities in the North, and Black churches and vigilance committees, did much of the work. William Still, a free Black man in Philadelphia, helped hundreds of escapers and kept careful written records of their stories, which he published in 1872. White abolitionists, many of them Quakers, such as Levi Coffin in Indiana and Ohio and Thomas Garrett in Delaware, also helped.

Estimates suggest that perhaps several tens of thousands of people escaped to the North and Canada between about 1830 and 1860, though exact numbers are unknown, because secrecy meant few records were kept. That was a small proportion of the four million enslaved, but each escape was a blow against slavery and a powerful story for abolitionists.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of the name, the code words, the loose network, the role of free Black communities and white allies, and the numbers.

## Subtopic: Harriet Tubman's Escape

Harriet Tubman was born into slavery around 1822 on a plantation in Dorchester County, Maryland, and named Araminta Ross, called Minty. Her parents, Ben and Harriet Ross, had nine children, and she saw several of her sisters sold away to the Deep South, a loss that stayed with her all her life.

She was hired out to work for other people from about the age of five or six, cleaning, caring for babies and later working in the fields and forests, where she became very strong. As a teenager, she suffered a terrible head injury when an overseer threw a heavy metal weight at another enslaved man and hit her instead. For the rest of her life she had severe headaches, seizures and sudden sleeping spells, and she also experienced vivid visions and dreams, which she believed were messages from God. Her deep Christian faith guided her actions.

In about 1844 she married John Tubman, a free Black man, and took the name Harriet, after her mother. In 1849, fearing that she would be sold after her owner died, she decided to escape. She set out on foot and travelled about one hundred and fifty kilometres to Pennsylvania, a free state, moving by night, guided by the North Star and helped by members of the Underground Railroad, including Quakers and free Black people.

She later described her feelings on crossing into Pennsylvania: she looked at her hands, to see if she was the same person now that she was free, and said there was such a glory over everything that she felt she was in heaven. But she also felt lonely, because her family remained enslaved, and she resolved to bring them to freedom.

The questions for this section are with you now: Harriet Tubman's early life, her injury and faith, her escape in 1849, and her decision to return.

## Subtopic: Moses of Her People

Between about 1850 and 1860, Harriet Tubman returned to Maryland about thirteen times and guided around seventy people, including her parents and brothers, to freedom. She also gave instructions that helped dozens more escape on their own. People called her Moses, after the Bible leader who led his people out of slavery in Egypt.

Her methods were careful and daring. She usually travelled in winter, when nights were long, and set off on Saturday nights, because runaway advertisements could not appear in newspapers until Monday. She used disguises, sometimes pretending to be an old woman. She used songs to signal danger or safety. She carried a gun for protection against slave catchers, and, according to accounts, to discourage frightened escapers from turning back and endangering the whole group. She relied on the Underground Railroad, especially Thomas Garrett in Delaware and William Still in Philadelphia. Slaveholders offered rewards for her capture, but she was never caught. She is reported to have said that she never ran her train off the track and never lost a passenger.

The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 made her work even harder. It required people in the free states to help return escapers and punished anyone who helped them, so escapers were no longer safe in the North. Tubman began leading people all the way to Canada, settling for a time in St Catharines, Ontario.

During the American Civil War, from 1861 to 1865, Tubman worked for the Union army as a cook, nurse and spy, and in 1863 she helped lead the Combahee River Raid in South Carolina, which freed more than seven hundred enslaved people, making her one of the first women to help lead an armed operation in the war. After the war she lived in Auburn, New York, cared for elderly and poor people, supported votes for women, and died in 1913.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking the Underground Railroad was a real railway. Two: thinking it was a single, organised company with maps. Three: forgetting that escapers themselves, not only helpers, made escape possible.

The questions for this section are with you now: Tubman's rescue journeys and methods, the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, her Civil War service, her later life, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Running North

In the northern states and in Canada, slavery was not allowed. So some enslaved people in the South ran north.

It was very dangerous. They walked at night through forests and rivers, sometimes hundreds of kilometres, hungry and cold. Slave catchers with dogs hunted them for reward money. If caught, they were punished terribly.

They followed the North Star. Some used clever tricks: Frederick Douglass dressed as a sailor, and Henry Box Brown mailed himself in a box!

## Subtopic: A Secret Network

The Underground Railroad was not under the ground and not a railway. It was a secret network of people who helped escapers. They used train words as a code: conductors were guides, stations were safe houses, and passengers were the people escaping.

Free Black people in the North did a lot of the work, along with white helpers, many of them Quakers. William Still in Philadelphia helped hundreds of people and wrote down their stories.

Helping was against the law, so they kept it secret.

## Subtopic: Harriet Tubman

Harriet Tubman was born enslaved in Maryland around 1822. As a teenager she was badly hurt in the head by a metal weight thrown by an overseer. In 1849 she escaped, walking about a hundred and fifty kilometres to Pennsylvania. She said she felt like she was in heaven, but she missed her family.

So she went back, about thirteen times, and led about seventy people to freedom, including her parents. People called her Moses. She travelled on long winter nights, used disguises and songs, and was never caught. She said she never lost a passenger.

Later she was a nurse and spy in the Civil War and helped free more than seven hundred people in a raid in 1863.
