# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines two rebellions against slavery, Nat Turner's revolt and the Amistad mutiny. The anchor is the question historians ask about both: were the rebels criminals, as the slave system called them, or freedom fighters, as many later came to see them? The governing idea is that open rebellion was rare because the slave system was designed to crush it, that it brought terrible consequences, and that it must be understood in the context of a system that was itself built on violence. Historians use multiple perspectives to understand such events rather than simply praising or condemning.

## Subtopic: Rebellion in Context

Large revolts by enslaved people were rare in the United States compared with the Caribbean and Brazil, for several reasons. Enslaved people in the American South were usually outnumbered by white people in most areas, plantations were smaller and more scattered, the land offered few hiding places far from settlements, and white society was heavily armed, with militias and patrols ready to crush any uprising. The penalty for rebellion was death, and whole communities could be punished.

Nevertheless, enslaved people planned and carried out revolts. In 1811, the German Coast Uprising near New Orleans involved perhaps two hundred to five hundred people, the largest revolt in United States history. In 1822 in Charleston, South Carolina, a free Black carpenter named Denmark Vesey was accused of planning a major revolt; it was betrayed before it began, and Vesey and dozens of others were executed.

The most successful revolt in the Americas took place in the French colony of Saint-Domingue, where enslaved people rose up in 1791 under leaders such as Toussaint Louverture, defeated French, Spanish and British armies, abolished slavery and in 1804 created the independent country of Haiti, the first free Black republic in the world. The Haitian Revolution inspired enslaved people across the Americas and terrified slaveholders in the United States.

When historians study rebellions, they ask: what were the causes; who took part and why; what happened; how did different groups react; and what were the consequences? They also consider perspectives: slaveholders saw rebels as murderers and criminals; enslaved people and abolitionists often saw them as heroes fighting for freedom; and historians today try to understand the violence on all sides, including the everyday violence of slavery itself.

The questions for this section are with you now: why large revolts were rare in the United States, earlier revolts and plots, the Haitian Revolution, and how historians study rebellions.

## Subtopic: Nat Turner's Revolt, 1831

Nat Turner was born into slavery in 1800 in Southampton County, Virginia. He learned to read as a child, which was unusual, and became deeply religious, a preacher respected by other enslaved people, who called him the Prophet. He believed that God had chosen him to lead his people out of bondage, and he interpreted natural events, such as a solar eclipse in February 1831, as signs from God that the time had come.

On the night of 21 August 1831, Turner and a small group of followers began the revolt by killing the family of his owner, Joseph Travis. Over about two days, the group grew to perhaps sixty or seventy enslaved and free Black men, moving from farm to farm. They killed about fifty-five to sixty white people, including women and children, and seized weapons and horses. They planned to march to the county seat, Jerusalem, to capture its armoury.

The revolt was crushed within about two days by armed white residents, militia and soldiers. Turner escaped and hid in the woods for about two months before he was captured on 30 October 1831. While in jail, he spoke to a white lawyer, Thomas Gray, who published The Confessions of Nat Turner. Turner was tried, sentenced to death and hanged on 11 November 1831.

The Confessions is an important but problematic source: it records Turner's words as written down and arranged by Gray, a white slaveholder who wanted to sell the pamphlet and who had his own views and purposes, so historians cannot be sure how accurately it reflects what Turner said.

The questions for this section are with you now: Nat Turner's background and beliefs, the events of the revolt, his capture and execution, and the Confessions as a source.

## Subtopic: Reactions and Consequences of the Revolt

The revolt caused panic across the South. In the weeks that followed, white mobs and militias killed many Black people in Southampton County and beyond, most of whom had nothing to do with the revolt; historians estimate that over a hundred, possibly as many as two hundred, were killed. Around fifty-five people were tried by the courts, and many were executed or sold out of the state.

Southern states responded with harsher laws. Several states, including Virginia, tightened laws forbidding enslaved and free Black people from learning to read and write, restricted Black preachers and religious gatherings without white supervision, limited the movement and rights of free Black people, and strengthened patrols. Slaveholders became more fearful and more determined to defend slavery, and less willing to discuss gradual emancipation; in 1832 the Virginia legislature debated ending slavery gradually, but rejected the idea.

In the North, the revolt drew attention to slavery. Abolitionists such as William Lloyd Garrison, who had begun publishing his anti-slavery newspaper The Liberator earlier in 1831, condemned slavery as the root cause of the violence. Slaveholders blamed abolitionist writings for stirring up rebellion.

How should Nat Turner be judged? Perspectives differ, and historians present them honestly. To white Southerners at the time, he was a murderer and fanatic, and the killing of children and unarmed people horrifies people today too. To many enslaved people and later African Americans, he became a symbol of resistance and courage against a system that was itself violent, which kept millions in bondage by force and terror. Many historians argue that the revolt must be understood in the context of slavery's own violence, while still recognising the suffering of all the victims.

The questions for this section are with you now: the violent reaction in Virginia, harsher laws, the effect on the debate over slavery, and different perspectives on Nat Turner.

## Subtopic: Sengbe Pieh and the Amistad, 1839

In 1839, Portuguese slave traders kidnapped a group of Mende people and others from the region of present-day Sierra Leone and shipped them illegally across the Atlantic to Havana in Cuba, then a Spanish colony. Although Britain, the United States and Spain had agreed to end the transatlantic slave trade, illegal trading continued, and traders forged documents claiming the captives had been born in Cuba.

In Havana, two Spaniards, José Ruiz and Pedro Montes, bought fifty-three of the captives, including four children, and put them aboard a schooner called La Amistad, meaning friendship, to carry them along the Cuban coast. On about 2 July 1839, the captives, led by a young rice farmer named Sengbe Pieh, known by the Spanish name Joseph Cinque, freed themselves from their chains using a nail or file, took weapons, and seized the ship. The captain and the cook were killed, and two of the Africans also died. They ordered Ruiz and Montes to sail east, back to Africa, but at night the Spaniards secretly steered north and west.

After about two months, the ship was taken by a United States Navy vessel off Long Island, New York, and the Africans were jailed in Connecticut. A complex legal battle followed. The Spanish government demanded the ship and the captives as property. Abolitionists formed a committee to support the Africans, found an interpreter who spoke Mende, James Covey, so that the captives could tell their story, and hired lawyers.

The case reached the United States Supreme Court in 1841. The former President John Quincy Adams argued for the Africans. The court ruled that they had been illegally kidnapped and were free people who had the right to resist unlawful enslavement. In 1842, with support from abolitionists and missionaries, the thirty-five survivors sailed home to Sierra Leone.

The case was a victory against the slave trade and gave abolitionists in the United States great publicity. But it did not free anyone enslaved in the United States; the ruling rested on the illegality of the international trade.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking the Amistad case ended slavery in the United States. Two: judging Nat Turner from only one perspective. Three: thinking enslaved people never rebelled.

The questions for this section are with you now: the illegal capture of the Amistad Africans, the mutiny, the legal case and the Supreme Court ruling of 1841, the return to Sierra Leone, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: Rising Up

Big revolts were rare in the United States. Enslaved people were often outnumbered, and white people had guns, patrols and soldiers. Rebels who were caught were killed.

But some did rise up. In Haiti, enslaved people won a long war and created a free country in 1804. That gave hope to enslaved people everywhere, and frightened slave owners in America.

When we study a rebellion, we ask: why did it happen, what happened, how did different people see it, and what changed afterwards?

## Subtopic: Nat Turner

Nat Turner was an enslaved preacher in Virginia who could read and believed God had chosen him to free his people. In August 1831 he and about sixty to seventy followers went from farm to farm, killing about fifty-five to sixty white people, including women and children.

The revolt was crushed in two days. Turner hid for two months, was caught, and was hanged in November 1831.

Afterwards, angry white mobs killed many Black people who had nothing to do with it. New laws made it even harder for Black people to read, meet or move.

Was he a murderer or a freedom fighter? Different people see him differently. We remember all the victims, and we remember that slavery itself was violent every day.

## Subtopic: The Amistad

In 1839, Africans from Sierra Leone were kidnapped and illegally taken to Cuba. Put on a ship called the Amistad, they broke their chains and took over the ship, led by Sengbe Pieh, also called Joseph Cinque. They tried to sail home, but the Spanish sailors tricked them and steered to America.

They were put in jail in the United States. Spain wanted them back as property. But abolitionists helped them, and in 1841 the Supreme Court said they had been kidnapped illegally and were free. In 1842 the survivors sailed home to Sierra Leone.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Large revolts were rare in the United States because the system was built to crush them, but Haiti won freedom in 1804. Nat Turner's 1831 revolt in Virginia was crushed, followed by revenge killings and harsher laws, and he is seen in different ways. The Amistad captives seized their ship in 1839, won their freedom in the Supreme Court in 1841 and went home.
