### subtopic_1 — Capture and the March to the Coast
**Assistant:** How do we know Equiano's story is true?

**Tutor:** Most of his account is supported by other evidence, but one part is debated, which is a good example of how historians test sources. Olaudah Equiano published his autobiography, The Interesting Narrative of the Life of Olaudah Equiano, in London in 1789. It became a bestseller and an important weapon in the campaign against the slave trade. Much of his later life, as an enslaved sailor, his purchase of his own freedom in 1766, his travels and his work as an abolitionist, is confirmed by ships' records, letters and other documents. In 1999 the historian Vincent Carretta found baptism and naval records stating that Equiano was born in South Carolina, not Africa, which led him to suggest that Equiano's account of his African childhood and the Middle Passage may have been based on other people's stories. Other historians disagree, arguing that such records were often inaccurate and that his descriptions of Igbo life are too detailed to have been invented. Either way, his description of the Middle Passage matches the accounts of many other survivors and of ships' crews.

### subtopic_3 — The Middle Passage
**Assistant:** Why did ships pack people so tightly if so many died?

**Tutor:** Because the traders calculated profit, not human life. Ship owners and captains made money on the number of captives who arrived alive and could be sold. Some followed a policy historians call tight packing: cramming as many people aboard as possible, expecting that even if more died, more would survive to be sold, so the profit would be greater. Others preferred loose packing, carrying fewer people in the hope of lower death rates. Both were business decisions. Over time, as traders learned that deaths cut into profits, and as laws were introduced, such as the British Dolben Act of 1788, which limited the number of captives per ton of ship, conditions improved somewhat and death rates fell. The fact that the Brookes, even under the new rules, was allowed to carry over four hundred people, shown in the famous plan, shocked the British public. That diagram became one of the most powerful images of the abolition campaign, because it showed so clearly how human beings were treated as cargo.

### subtopic_4 — Slave Markets and the Numbers
**Assistant:** Why were so few Africans taken to North America, yet four million were enslaved there by 1860?

**Tutor:** Because of differences in conditions and in births. In the Caribbean and Brazil, sugar plantations had extremely high death rates and relatively few women were imported, so the enslaved population did not grow naturally; planters kept buying new captives from Africa. In mainland North America, the climate was generally less deadly, the work on tobacco, rice and cotton, though brutal, killed fewer people than sugar, and more women were present, so the enslaved population grew through births. After the United States banned the import of enslaved people from Africa in 1808, slaveholders depended entirely on children born into slavery, and some actively encouraged births to increase their wealth. So the roughly four hundred thousand Africans brought directly to North America became the ancestors of about four million enslaved people by 1860. This also meant that by the nineteenth century most enslaved people in the United States had been born there, into an African American culture with deep African roots, the subject of the next lesson.
