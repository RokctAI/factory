# Part 1 — Expert

This session follows the experience of captives from capture to sale. The anchor is Olaudah Equiano's account of being kidnapped as a child of about eleven and later carried onto a slave ship, published in 1789. The governing idea is that the transatlantic slave trade was a chain of violence at every stage, organised for profit, and that the testimony of survivors allows historians to see it through the eyes of those who suffered, not only through the records of those who profited. The material is distressing, and it is studied to understand and remember, not to shock.

## Subtopic: Capture and the March to the Coast

Most captives were taken in the interior of West and West-Central Africa, sometimes hundreds of kilometres from the coast. They were enslaved in several ways.

War. Many were prisoners captured in wars between African states. As the Atlantic trade grew, some wars were fought partly in order to capture people for sale, and the guns that European traders supplied made raiding more destructive.

Raids and kidnapping. Armed bands raided villages, often at night, seizing whoever they could. Children were kidnapped while their parents were working. Olaudah Equiano wrote that he and his sister were kidnapped from their home in what is now south-eastern Nigeria when the adults were away in the fields.

Judicial enslavement. Some people were sold as punishment for crimes, sometimes minor ones, or accusations of witchcraft, and the number of such sentences increased when there was profit in selling convicts.

Debt and famine. Some were sold because of debt or during famines.

After capture, captives were tied together, often with ropes or wooden yokes around their necks, and marched in lines called coffles to the coast. The march could take weeks or months. Many died of hunger, thirst, exhaustion, disease or violence on the way. Captives were often sold several times, from one trader to another, before reaching the coast.

African traders and rulers who supplied captives were paid in European goods. The trade was organised by networks of African merchants inland and European and Afro-European traders on the coast.

The questions for this section are with you now: the ways people were captured, Equiano's kidnapping, the coffle and the march, and the role of African and European traders.

## Subtopic: Forts, Factories and Sale on the Coast

On the coast, captives were held until ships arrived to buy them. Europeans built or rented forts and trading posts, called factories, along the West African coast. The Portuguese built Elmina Castle in present-day Ghana in 1482; it was later taken by the Dutch. Cape Coast Castle, also in Ghana, became the British headquarters. Gorée Island off Dakar in Senegal and Bunce Island in Sierra Leone were other important sites. In some places, such as the Bight of Biafra, ships traded directly with African merchants without forts.

In the forts, captives were held in crowded, dark, airless dungeons, sometimes for weeks or months, with little food, water or sanitation, and many died. The dungeons at Cape Coast Castle could hold about a thousand people. Today visitors can still see them, and these sites are places of remembrance, several of them UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

When a ship arrived, the captain or ship's surgeon inspected the captives, examining their bodies, teeth and health like livestock, and rejecting the sick or elderly. Prices were negotiated in European goods, often counted in bundles of goods called bars or ounces. Young men were most in demand for plantation work; roughly two out of three captives were male, and about a quarter were children.

Captives were often branded with hot irons to mark them as the property of a particular company or trader, then rowed out to the ships, which might wait for months, buying a few captives at a time until the ship was full.

The doorway at the forts through which captives passed to the ships has come to be called the Door of No Return.

The questions for this section are with you now: the coastal forts, conditions in the dungeons, inspection and sale, branding, and the Door of No Return.

## Subtopic: The Middle Passage

The Middle Passage was the voyage across the Atlantic from Africa to the Americas, the middle leg of the triangular trade. It usually took about six to ten weeks, depending on the route and winds, and sometimes much longer if a ship was becalmed.

Conditions were horrific. Captives were chained in pairs below deck in spaces sometimes less than a metre high, packed so tightly that they could barely move. A plan of the British slave ship Brookes, published by abolitionists in 1788, showed how hundreds of people were packed like goods into the hold; on one voyage it carried over six hundred people. The heat, the darkness and the smell were overwhelming. Sanitation was almost nonexistent, and diseases such as dysentery, smallpox and fevers spread rapidly.

When weather allowed, captives were brought on deck once a day to eat and be exercised, sometimes forced to dance, to keep them alive for sale. Food was poor and water scarce. Crew members were often brutal, and women and girls suffered sexual abuse.

Olaudah Equiano described being brought on board: the stench below deck, the shrieks of women and the groans of the dying. He wrote that he was so sick and miserable that he wished for death, and that he was beaten for refusing to eat.

Death rates fell over time but remained high. Historians estimate that around one in seven or eight captives died during the Middle Passage overall, about one and a half to two million people, with far higher losses on some voyages. Crew members also died at high rates.

Captives resisted. Some refused to eat, and crews used force to make them. Some jumped overboard. Revolts broke out on perhaps one in ten voyages, though most were brutally crushed.

The questions for this section are with you now: the length of the voyage, conditions below deck, the Brookes plan, Equiano's testimony, death rates and resistance at sea.

## Subtopic: Slave Markets and the Numbers

When a ship reached the Americas, the surviving captives were prepared for sale: washed, sometimes oiled to make their skin look healthy, and given a little more food. Then they were sold.

Sales took several forms. At public auctions, people were displayed on platforms and sold to the highest bidder, examined by buyers as if they were animals. In a scramble, a fixed price was set, and at a signal buyers rushed onto the ship or into a yard to grab the captives they wanted, a terrifying experience that Equiano described. Others were sold privately to planters or to traders who resold them inland. Families and people from the same village or ship, who had formed bonds of friendship called shipmates, were often separated for ever.

In the United States, after the import of enslaved people from Africa was banned in 1808, a large internal slave trade developed. Around a million enslaved people were forcibly moved from the older states such as Virginia and Maryland to the new cotton lands of the Deep South between 1790 and 1860, sold in markets in cities such as New Orleans, Richmond and Charleston, again separating families.

The numbers. Of about twelve and a half million people embarked in Africa, about ten and a half million arrived. Roughly forty-five percent went to the Caribbean, about forty percent to Brazil, about five percent to Spanish America on the mainland, and about four percent, around four hundred thousand, directly to mainland North America. In North America, the enslaved population grew by births to about four million by 1860. Brazil was the last country in the Americas to abolish slavery, in 1888.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking most enslaved Africans went to the United States. Two: treating captives as cargo rather than people, as the traders did. Three: thinking families were usually kept together.

The questions for this section are with you now: auctions and scrambles, the separation of families, the internal slave trade in the United States, and where enslaved Africans were taken.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: taken from home, held at the coast, the crossing, and sold.

## Subtopic: Taken From Home

Most captives were taken far inland, in wars, in raids on villages, or kidnapped. Olaudah Equiano was kidnapped with his sister when he was about eleven, while the grown-ups were working in the fields.

Captives were tied together in lines and forced to walk to the coast, sometimes for weeks. Many died on the way. They were often sold again and again.

At the coast they were locked in dark, crowded dungeons in forts like Elmina and Cape Coast Castle, sometimes for months. Ship captains inspected them like animals and branded them with hot irons. Then they went through the Door of No Return to the ships.

## Subtopic: The Crossing

The voyage across the Atlantic was called the Middle Passage. It took six to ten weeks.

People were chained in pairs below deck in the dark, packed so tightly they could hardly move. It was hot and dirty, and diseases spread. Many people died, about one in every seven or eight.

Equiano wrote that he was so sick and sad he wished he could die.

But people fought back. Some refused to eat. Some jumped into the sea. On about one in ten ships, the captives rebelled.

## Subtopic: Sold

In the Americas, survivors were sold. Some were auctioned on a platform to the highest bidder. In a scramble, buyers rushed in to grab the people they wanted. Families and friends from the same ship were often split up forever.

Most enslaved Africans went to the Caribbean and Brazil. About four hundred thousand went straight to North America, where the number of enslaved people grew to about four million by 1860.

The Middle Passage lasted six to ten weeks in terrible conditions, and about one in seven or eight died, but many resisted. Survivors were sold at auctions and scrambles, and families were separated. Most went to the Caribbean and Brazil.
