# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the economic system that drove the transatlantic slave trade. The anchor is a bag of sugar or a cotton shirt in an eighteenth-century British shop, a product of enslaved labour thousands of kilometres away. The governing idea is that the slave trade was driven by profit: plantation crops sold for high prices in Europe, they required huge amounts of hard labour, and enslaved people could be forced to work without wages. The wealth this produced flowed mainly to plantation owners, merchants and manufacturers in Europe and the Americas, while the enslaved received nothing.

## Subtopic: The Plantation

A plantation was a large farm, often of hundreds or thousands of hectares, that specialised in growing one main cash crop for sale, usually for export. It was owned by a wealthy planter and worked by a large labour force, which in the Americas from the seventeenth to the nineteenth centuries was mostly enslaved Africans and their descendants.

A plantation was like a small town. It had the planter's large house, often called the big house; rows of small, crowded cabins or huts for the enslaved, called the quarters; fields; barns and storehouses; and, on sugar plantations, a mill and boiling house where cane was processed. Overseers, usually white men employed by the planter, supervised the work, often with great cruelty. Some enslaved men were made drivers, forced to push other workers.

The plantation system first developed on Atlantic islands such as Madeira and São Tomé, then spread to Brazil and the Caribbean islands, including Jamaica, Barbados and Haiti, then known as Saint-Domingue, and to the southern colonies of North America, which became the southern states of the United States after independence in 1776. This region is called the American South: states such as Virginia, the Carolinas, Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi and Louisiana.

The climate of these regions, warm with long growing seasons and good rainfall, suited tropical and subtropical crops that could not be grown in Europe, which is why European demand for them was so high.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a plantation was, how it was organised, where the plantation system spread, and why the climate mattered.

## Subtopic: Four Crops: Tobacco, Rice, Sugar and Cotton

Four crops dominated plantations in the American South and the Caribbean, and each demanded hard, constant labour.

Tobacco was the first great cash crop of the English colony of Virginia, from the 1610s, and later Maryland. Europeans had learned to smoke it from the indigenous peoples of the Americas, and demand grew fast. Tobacco needs careful work: planting seedlings, weeding, removing flowers and pests by hand, cutting and drying the leaves. It also exhausts the soil, so planters kept clearing new land.

Rice was grown in the coastal swamps of South Carolina and Georgia from the late seventeenth century. Many enslaved people came from the rice-growing regions of West Africa, such as Senegambia and Sierra Leone, and historians have shown that their knowledge of rice farming was vital to its success. Work in the flooded fields, in heat and among mosquitoes and snakes, was dangerous and unhealthy.

Sugar cane was the most profitable and most deadly crop. It was grown mainly in Brazil and the Caribbean, and in Louisiana. Cane had to be cut and crushed quickly at harvest, and the juice boiled in huge pots, so enslaved people worked day and night in the harvest season. Accidents in mills and boiling houses were common, and death rates were so high that planters relied on constant new arrivals from Africa rather than on births.

Cotton became the dominant crop of the American South after 1793, when Eli Whitney's cotton gin made it much faster to separate cotton fibre from its seeds. Cotton could now be grown profitably across huge areas, from South Carolina to Texas, and demand from British textile factories was enormous. By 1860 the South produced most of the world's raw cotton, and about four million people were enslaved in the United States, most of them working on cotton.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four crops, where each was grown, and why each needed so much labour.

## Subtopic: Why Enslaved Labour?

Planters needed a very large, cheap, controllable workforce. Historians identify several reasons why they turned to enslaved Africans.

Profit. Enslaved workers were not paid wages. After the purchase price, a planter only had to provide the minimum of food, clothing and shelter, so profits were higher. Enslaved people could be forced to work long hours in harsh conditions that free workers would refuse.

Indigenous peoples. The original inhabitants of the Americas were at first forced to work by the Spanish and Portuguese, but millions died from European diseases such as smallpox and measles, to which they had no immunity, and from violence and overwork. Those who survived could escape into land they knew well.

European workers. Some poor Europeans came to the colonies as indentured servants, working for several years to pay for their passage, after which they were free. But there were not enough of them, they had to be released after their contracts, and many died in the tropical climate.

Supply. The Atlantic slave trade could supply huge numbers of people. Europeans also believed, wrongly in many cases, that Africans were better able to survive tropical diseases such as malaria and yellow fever; some had partial immunity from exposure in Africa.

Control and law. Colonial laws, known as slave codes, made enslaved Africans and their children permanent property and denied them rights, so they could not leave, own property, testify against white people in court, or, in many places, learn to read and write.

Racism. To justify enslaving Africans, Europeans and white Americans claimed that Africans were inferior and suited to slavery. These racist ideas were invented to defend a profitable system, and they lasted long after slavery was abolished.

The questions for this section are with you now: profit, the fate of indigenous peoples, indentured servants, supply, slave codes and racism as justifications.

## Subtopic: The Triangular Trade and Where the Wealth Went

The transatlantic slave trade was part of a wider pattern often called the triangular trade, because ships could follow three legs between three continents.

Leg one: ships left European ports, such as Liverpool, Bristol and London in Britain, Nantes in France and Lisbon in Portugal, carrying manufactured goods: cloth, guns, gunpowder, iron bars, brass and copper goods, beads and alcohol.

Leg two: at the West African coast, these goods were exchanged for captives, who were then carried across the Atlantic to the Americas. This voyage was called the Middle Passage, studied in the next lesson.

Leg three: in the Americas, the captives were sold, and the ships returned to Europe loaded with plantation products: sugar, molasses and rum, tobacco, rice, cotton, coffee and indigo dye.

The real pattern was more complicated: many goods moved directly between Europe and the Americas, North American ships also traded, and Brazilian traders sailed directly to Angola. But the triangle captures the basic link between European goods, African captives and American crops.

What happened to the raw materials? In Europe, raw sugar was refined into white sugar, which became part of everyday life, sweetening tea, which itself came from Asia. Tobacco was sold for smoking and snuff. Raw cotton was spun and woven into cloth in the factories of Lancashire in north-west England, and the growth of the cotton industry was central to the Industrial Revolution, which Grade 8 History studies. Finished cotton cloth was then sold around the world, including back to Africa.

Who gained? Plantation owners, slave traders, ship owners, merchants, bankers, insurers, sugar refiners and factory owners in Europe and the Americas made great fortunes, and port cities such as Liverpool and Bristol grew rich. Governments collected taxes on the trade. Some historians argue that profits from slavery helped to finance British industrialisation. The enslaved, whose labour created this wealth, received nothing.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking enslaved labour was chosen because Africans were naturally suited to it. Two: thinking the slave trade only affected Africa and America; it shaped Europe's economy too. Three: imagining the triangle as the only route.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three legs of the triangular trade, what happened to raw materials in Europe, who gained, and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: giant farms, four crops, why they used enslaved people, and where the money went.

## Subtopic: Giant Farms

A plantation was a giant farm growing one main crop to sell overseas. The owner lived in the big house. Enslaved people lived in small, crowded huts called the quarters, and overseers forced them to work, often cruelly.

Plantations were in Brazil, the Caribbean islands and the American South, where it is warm, with a long growing season, good for crops Europe could not grow.

The four big crops were tobacco, rice, sugar cane and cotton. Sugar was the most deadly to work on. Cotton became the biggest crop in the South after a machine called the cotton gin was invented in 1793.

## Subtopic: Why Enslaved People?

Plantation owners wanted lots of workers who did not have to be paid. That meant more profit.

Many of the first people of the Americas died from European diseases like smallpox. Poor Europeans who came as servants worked for a few years and then became free. So the owners turned to enslaved Africans, who could be forced to work forever, and so could their children.

Laws called slave codes said enslaved people were property, with no rights. And to excuse all this, white people spread racist lies that Africans were inferior.

## Subtopic: Follow the Sugar

Ships sailed a triangle. From Europe to Africa with cloth, guns and metal. From Africa to the Americas with captives, the terrible Middle Passage. From the Americas back to Europe with sugar, tobacco, rice and cotton.

In Europe, sugar went into people's tea, tobacco was smoked, and cotton was turned into cloth in English factories, helping start the Industrial Revolution.

Plantation owners, traders, ship owners and factory owners got rich. Cities like Liverpool and Bristol grew rich. The enslaved people who did the work got nothing.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Plantations were giant farms growing tobacco, rice, sugar cane or cotton in the Americas. Owners used enslaved Africans for profit, using slave codes and racist ideas to justify it. The triangular trade linked Europe, Africa and the Americas. Plantation crops were processed and sold in Europe, and the wealth went to owners, traders and factory owners, never to the enslaved.
