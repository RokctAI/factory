### subtopic_2 — Four Crops: Tobacco, Rice, Sugar and Cotton
**Assistant:** Why was sugar so deadly to grow?

**Tutor:** Because of the kind of work it required and the way planters chose to run their plantations. Sugar cane must be cut at exactly the right time and crushed within about a day, before the juice spoils. So during the harvest, which lasted several months, enslaved people worked in shifts around the clock: cutting cane in the heat with heavy knives, feeding it into crushing mills where a moment's tiredness could cost an arm, and boiling the juice in huge copper pots in scorching boiling houses. Outside the harvest, they dug holes and planted cane in hard ground. Food was poor, rest was short and punishments were brutal. Diseases spread in crowded quarters. On many Caribbean sugar islands, so many enslaved people died that the population did not grow by births, and planters calculated that it was cheaper to buy new captives from Africa than to improve conditions. Historians see this as one of the clearest examples of how profit was put above human life in the slave system.

### subtopic_3 — Why Enslaved Labour?
**Assistant:** Were there people at the time who said slavery was wrong?

**Tutor:** Yes, and the most powerful voices were those of enslaved people themselves, who resisted slavery from the start, as later lessons show. Among free people, opposition grew over time. Some religious groups, especially the Quakers in Britain and Pennsylvania, began speaking out against slavery in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, arguing that it was against Christian teaching that all people are equal before God. Formerly enslaved writers such as Olaudah Equiano in Britain described the cruelty of the trade from experience. By the late eighteenth century, a movement for abolition, the ending of the slave trade and slavery, had grown in Britain and the northern United States. Abolitionists organised petitions, boycotts of slave-grown sugar, public meetings and books. Britain abolished its slave trade in 1807 and slavery in most of its colonies in 1834, which affected the Cape Colony too. But for a long time, many people who benefited from slavery, or who believed racist ideas, defended it, and in the United States it lasted until 1865.

### subtopic_4 — The Triangular Trade and Where the Wealth Went
**Assistant:** Did slavery help make Britain rich?

**Tutor:** Historians agree that it contributed, but they debate how much. The evidence of benefit is clear. Merchants, ship owners and planters made great fortunes; ports such as Liverpool and Bristol grew rapidly; banks and insurance companies developed partly through financing and insuring slaving voyages and plantations; and sugar and tobacco duties raised taxes for the government. Cotton grown by enslaved people in the American South supplied the textile factories at the heart of the Industrial Revolution. When Britain abolished slavery in 1834, the government paid twenty million pounds in compensation, not to the enslaved, but to slave owners for the loss of their property; the enslaved received nothing. In 1944 the historian Eric Williams, from Trinidad, argued in his book Capitalism and Slavery that the profits of slavery helped finance British industrialisation. Other historians argue that these profits were only one of many sources of investment. Either way, the wealth of slavery was built into many institutions, buildings and family fortunes in Britain, a subject of much public discussion today.
