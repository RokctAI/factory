# Part 1 — Expert

This session opens the Grade 7 History year. The anchor is a caravan of perhaps a thousand camels setting out across the Sahara in the fourteenth century, loaded with salt and cloth and bound for the markets of West Africa. The governing idea is that transport shapes history: the kind of transport available decides which goods can be traded, how far, and between which peoples. The camel, introduced to North Africa centuries earlier, turned the Sahara from a barrier into a highway, and that trade made the kingdom of Mali and the city of Timbuktu rich and famous.

## Subtopic: Doing History in Grade 7

History is the study of the past, based on evidence. Historians do not simply memorise facts; they ask questions, find sources, judge how reliable those sources are, and build explanations of what happened and why. In Grade 7 the four topics are the kingdom of Mali and the city of Timbuktu in the fourteenth century, the transatlantic slave trade, the colonisation of the Cape in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and co-operation and conflict on the frontiers of the Cape Colony in the early nineteenth century.

Historians work with sources. A primary source comes from the time being studied: a letter, a manuscript, a building, a coin, a traveller's account written by an eyewitness. A secondary source is written later by someone studying the past, such as a textbook or a historian's book. Both are useful, and both must be questioned: who made this source, when, why, and for whom? A source can be biased, meaning it presents a one-sided view, and part of a historian's work is to compare several sources and look for different perspectives.

Assessment in History follows the pattern of Geography: informal classwork and homework to build skills, and formal tasks that count towards the final mark. In the first term the formal task is a test with source-based questions and paragraph writing. Source-based questions give a source, such as an extract or a picture, and ask questions about it. Paragraph writing asks for a short, organised piece of writing that answers a question using evidence.

The language of history assessment includes the command words already met in Geography, name, describe, explain, compare, together with others. Identify means pick out from a source. Extract means quote from a source. Evaluate or assess means judge how useful, reliable or important something is, giving reasons. Discuss means look at different sides of a question.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Grade 7 History topics, primary and secondary sources, bias, and command words.

## Subtopic: Transport on Land Through Time

For almost all of human history, goods on land moved only as fast as people or animals could carry them. The earliest form of transport was human porterage: people carrying loads on their heads, backs or shoulders. In many parts of Africa, long-distance trade was carried on foot by porters well into the nineteenth century.

Animals made it possible to carry heavier loads further. Donkeys were domesticated in north-east Africa thousands of years ago and became important pack animals. Oxen pulled ploughs and later wagons; in southern Africa the ox-wagon became the main form of long-distance transport in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Horses, faster but more delicate, were used for riding and warfare.

The wheel was invented in south-west Asia about five and a half thousand years ago. Wheeled carts and wagons need fairly flat, firm ground or roads, so in deserts, swamps and steep mountains, pack animals remained more useful than wheels.

Each form of transport had limits. Horses and oxen need plenty of water and grazing every day. In a desert, where water holes may be many days apart and there is almost no grass, they die. For centuries the Sahara was therefore a barrier that only small groups could cross with difficulty, using horses and oxen along routes with good wells.

The great changes in land transport came much later: railways in the nineteenth century, cars and trucks in the twentieth. In the period studied this term, the fourteenth century, there were no engines anywhere in the world. Every journey across the Sahara depended on animals and on the skill of the people who guided them.

The questions for this section are with you now: porters, pack animals, the wheel, and why horses and oxen could not easily cross the desert.

## Subtopic: The Ship of the Desert

The dromedary, the one-humped camel, was the animal that unlocked the Sahara. Camels were domesticated in Arabia and spread into North Africa, and by about the third and fourth centuries of the common era they were widely used by the peoples of the Sahara, especially the Berber peoples such as the Tuareg.

The camel is remarkably adapted to the desert. It can go for many days without drinking, often a week or more in cool conditions, and then drink a huge amount of water in a few minutes. Its hump stores fat, not water, which provides energy when food is scarce. It loses little water through sweat and urine, and its body temperature can rise during the day without harm. Broad, padded feet stop it sinking into sand. Long eyelashes, hairy ears and nostrils that can close protect it from blowing sand. It can eat thorny, dry desert plants that other animals refuse.

A camel can carry a load of about one hundred and fifty kilograms and walk thirty to forty kilometres a day for weeks. Because of this, it was called the ship of the desert, carrying cargo across the sea of sand as ships carry cargo across the ocean.

Camels made regular, large-scale trade possible across the Sahara. Without them, the trade that made West African kingdoms such as Ghana, Mali and Songhay rich could never have reached the size it did.

The questions for this section are with you now: where the camel came from, its adaptations to the desert, its load, and why it was called the ship of the desert.

## Subtopic: The Camel Caravan

A caravan is a group of travellers journeying together for safety and support. Trans-Saharan caravans could be small, a few dozen camels, or enormous, with several thousand. Travelling together protected traders against raiders and allowed them to share guides, water and knowledge.

The journey from the trading towns of North Africa, such as Sijilmasa in what is now Morocco, to the markets of West Africa, such as Walata and later Timbuktu, was about one thousand five hundred to two thousand kilometres and took around two months. Caravans usually travelled in the cooler months, roughly from October to March, and often moved at night or in the early morning to avoid the worst heat.

The most important person in the caravan was the guide, often a Tuareg or another Berber desert expert. Guides found the way by the stars, by the shape of dunes and rocks, by the colour of the sand and even by smell, and they knew where every well and oasis lay. An oasis is a place in the desert where underground water reaches the surface, allowing date palms and crops to grow. A wrong turning that missed a well could kill an entire caravan, and accounts tell of caravans lost in sandstorms.

Along the way, caravans stopped at oases and at mining settlements such as Taghaza, where salt was dug from the ground. Desert peoples who controlled the wells and routes charged traders for water, protection and guidance, and grew rich from the trade too.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking the camel's hump is full of water. Two: picturing the Sahara as only sand dunes; much of it is rocky plateau and gravel plain. Three: imagining the traders as European; the trade was organised by North African, Berber and West African merchants.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a caravan is, the length and timing of the journey, the role of guides and oases, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: History Detectives

History is finding out about the past using evidence, like a detective.

Primary sources come from the time itself: an old letter, a building, an eyewitness story. Secondary sources were written later, like your textbook.

Always ask: who made this, when, and why? A source can be one-sided, or biased, so compare more than one.

This term we study the kingdom of Mali and its famous city, Timbuktu, about seven hundred years ago.

## Subtopic: The Amazing Camel

Before engines, people carried things themselves or used animals: donkeys, oxen, horses. But horses and oxen need water and grass every day. In the Sahara Desert there is almost none, so they died.

Then came the camel. It can go a week or more without drinking, then drink a huge amount at once. Its hump is fat, not water. Wide feet stop it sinking in sand. Long eyelashes and closing nostrils keep sand out. It carries about a hundred and fifty kilograms and walks thirty to forty kilometres a day.

No wonder people called it the ship of the desert.

## Subtopic: Two Months on Foot

Traders crossed the Sahara together in a caravan, sometimes with thousands of camels, for safety.

The trip was about fifteen hundred to two thousand kilometres and took about two months. They travelled in the cooler months, often at night.

The guide was the most important person. Guides used the stars, the shape of the dunes and the rocks to find the way from one well or oasis to the next. Miss a well, and the whole caravan could die.

Pocket summary of the lesson. History uses evidence from primary and secondary sources. Before engines, goods were carried by people and animals. Horses and oxen could not cross the desert easily, but camels could. Caravans of camels, led by expert guides, crossed the Sahara in about two months, stopping at oases.
