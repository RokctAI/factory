### subtopic_3 — The Ship of the Desert
**Assistant:** If camels came from Arabia, how did they get to the Sahara?

**Tutor:** Gradually, over many centuries, through trade and the movement of peoples. The one-humped camel was domesticated in the Arabian Peninsula, probably around three thousand years ago. From there, camels spread across the Red Sea into Egypt and along the edges of the Sahara. By Roman times camels were known in North Africa, and during the third and fourth centuries of the common era they became widespread among the Berber peoples of the desert. These desert communities discovered how valuable camels were for travelling between oases and for herding in dry country. Over the next centuries, as camel herding spread, crossing the full width of the Sahara became practical on a regular basis rather than a rare and dangerous adventure. The growth of the great trade routes after about the eighth century, and the rise of the West African kingdoms of Ghana, Mali and Songhay, followed. So the camel is a good example of how a new technology, in this case a domesticated animal, can change the history of a whole region.

### subtopic_4 — The Camel Caravan
**Assistant:** How could a guide find the way when the sand dunes keep moving?

**Tutor:** By using many clues at once, and by relying on the features that do not move. Dunes do shift slowly with the wind, but much of the Sahara is not dune at all: it is rocky plateau, gravel plain and mountain, and these landmarks stay put for centuries. Experienced guides knew the outlines of hills and rock formations, the dry river beds, and the places where the colour or texture of the ground changed. At night they steered by the stars, especially those near the northern horizon, which keep their positions. They watched the direction of the prevailing wind and the shape it gave to dunes and ripples, which acted as a kind of compass. Some guides were said to identify their position by the smell or taste of the sand. Above all, guides learned the routes from older guides through years of travelling, and knowledge of the wells and oases was passed down within families and clans. Even so, the journey was dangerous, and accounts record caravans that lost their way.

### subtopic_1 — Doing History in Grade 7
**Assistant:** How do historians know about Mali if it was so long ago?

**Tutor:** From several kinds of evidence, each with strengths and weaknesses. There are written accounts by visitors and scholars, many in Arabic. Ibn Battuta, a traveller from Morocco, visited Mali in 1352 and 1353 and wrote a detailed description. Arab historians such as Ibn Khaldun wrote about Mali's kings. Later, scholars in Timbuktu itself wrote histories of the region, and thousands of manuscripts survive there. There are oral traditions: in West Africa, griots, professional storytellers and historians, have passed down the history of Mali and its founder Sundiata Keita for centuries through songs and epics. There is archaeology: excavations of towns, mosques, graves and trade goods. And there are buildings that still stand, such as the great mosques of Timbuktu. Historians compare these sources. Outside visitors may misunderstand what they see, oral traditions may praise rulers, and archaeology shows objects but not always their meaning. Together, though, they give a rich picture.
