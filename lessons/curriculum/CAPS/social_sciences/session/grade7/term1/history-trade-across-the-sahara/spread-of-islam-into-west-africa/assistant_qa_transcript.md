### subtopic_2 — Traders Carry Islam South
**Assistant:** Why didn't the Arab armies just conquer West Africa too?

**Tutor:** Mainly because of the Sahara. The armies that conquered North Africa in the seventh and eighth centuries moved along the Mediterranean coast, where there were cities, farmland and supplies. Crossing the desert with an army, its horses and its supplies was a far harder matter than sending a caravan of traders. A few raids were attempted across the desert, but there was no lasting conquest. Beyond the desert, the West African kingdoms were organised states with their own armies, including cavalry, and they could defend themselves. There was also less reason to try. North African rulers and merchants got what they wanted, gold, through trade, without the cost of conquest. Much later, in 1591, the ruler of Morocco did send an army across the Sahara with firearms and defeated the Songhay empire, the successor of Mali, but the occupation was costly and the Moroccans could not control the region for long. So for most of this history, the desert ensured that Islam came south as a faith of traders and scholars rather than of conquerors.

### subtopic_3 — What Islam Brought: Writing, Learning and Law
**Assistant:** What is a griot and do they still exist?

**Tutor:** A griot, also called a jeli in the Mande languages of Mali, is a professional keeper of history, praise and music in West Africa, and yes, griots still exist and are respected today. Griots belong to families who have passed down their role for generations. They memorise the genealogies of noble families, the deeds of kings and heroes, and the history of their people, and they perform them as speech, song and music, often playing the kora, a harp-like instrument with twenty-one strings, or the balafon, a kind of xylophone. The most famous story they tell is the Epic of Sundiata, about Sundiata Keita, who founded the empire of Mali in the thirteenth century. Before writing arrived, griots were the libraries of their societies, and even after Arabic literacy spread, they remained central to royal courts as advisers and historians. Historians use griot traditions as sources, carefully, because they are designed to praise and to teach, but they preserve knowledge found nowhere else.

### subtopic_4 — African Islam: Blending and Choice
**Assistant:** If Ibn Battuta was biased, why do historians still use his account?

**Tutor:** Because every source is written from a particular point of view, and a biased source can still be extremely valuable if it is used carefully. Ibn Battuta actually travelled to Mali, stayed for several months, met the ruler Mansa Sulayman, and wrote down what he saw. That makes his account a rare eyewitness description of the kingdom at its height. Much of what he describes is the kind of detail that bias would not change: the route he took, how long the journey lasted, the food he ate, the safety of travel, the way people gathered for Friday prayers, the ceremonies at court. Where bias matters is in his judgements. When he praises or condemns, he is applying the standards of a strict scholar from Morocco. A good historian separates the observations from the opinions, notices what the writer approves and disapproves of and why, and checks the account against other sources, such as Arabic histories, griot traditions and archaeology. Used this way, a biased source tells us both about the place described and about the person describing it.
