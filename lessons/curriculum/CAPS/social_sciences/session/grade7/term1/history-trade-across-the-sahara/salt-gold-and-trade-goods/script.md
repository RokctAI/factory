# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the goods of the trans-Saharan trade. The anchor is the exchange of salt for gold, the heart of the trade. The governing idea is that trade happens when different regions have what others need and lack: the desert had salt but no gold; the forests and river valleys of West Africa had gold but little salt. Traders who linked the two, and rulers who controlled and taxed the routes, grew wealthy. The trade also carried people in captivity, and an honest history must include them.

## Subtopic: Why Trade Across the Desert

Trade is the exchange of goods between people or regions. Long-distance trade grows when different areas produce different things. The regions linked across the Sahara were very different. North Africa, around the Mediterranean Sea, was connected to Europe, the Middle East and Asia and had manufactured goods such as cloth, metalwork and glass. The Sahara itself contained salt deposits but little else of value to farmers. South of the desert lay the Sahel, a belt of dry grassland, and further south the savanna and forest of West Africa, with fertile land, rivers such as the Niger and Senegal, and gold fields.

Each region needed something the others had. That simple fact created one of the great trade networks of the medieval world, linking West Africa to North Africa, and through North Africa to Europe and the Middle East.

The trade was organised by merchants. North African and Berber merchants brought goods south by caravan to trading towns on the southern edge of the desert, such as Walata, Gao and Timbuktu. There they met West African merchants, especially the Wangara or Dyula traders, who carried goods further south by donkey, by canoe on the Niger River and by porters, and brought gold and other products back.

Rulers profited too. The kings of Ghana, and later of Mali, controlled the trading towns and the routes and taxed the goods passing through. Their wealth paid for armies, officials and impressive courts, which in turn kept the routes safe for trade.

## Subtopic: Salt From the North

Salt was the most important good carried south. Human beings need salt to stay alive: it helps the body keep its balance of water, and people who sweat a lot in hot climates lose salt quickly and must replace it. Salt was also essential for preserving meat and fish before refrigeration, and for keeping livestock healthy. Much of West Africa south of the desert had very little salt of its own, so demand was enormous.

The Sahara had salt in large quantities, left behind by ancient lakes that dried up thousands of years ago. The most famous mine was at Taghaza in the far north of present-day Mali. The Moroccan traveller Ibn Battuta, who passed through in 1352, described a village where the houses and even the mosque were built of blocks of salt and roofed with camel skins, and where enslaved workers dug the salt in harsh conditions. Food and water had to be brought in from outside. Later, salt was mined at Taoudenni, which still produces salt today and is still served by camel caravans.

Salt was cut into large slabs, each weighing roughly thirty kilograms, and a camel carried several. As it moved south, its value rose, because of the cost and danger of the journey. Medieval writers reported that in the markets of the south, salt could sell for a very high price, at times exchanged for gold of almost equal value. Some accounts may exaggerate, but there is no doubt that salt was precious. Slabs of salt were even used as a form of money.

The questions for this section are with you now: why people needed salt, where it was mined, how it was carried, and why it was so valuable in West Africa.

## Subtopic: Gold and Other Goods From the South

Gold was West Africa's most famous export. It came from gold fields in the south of the Mali empire and beyond, especially Bambuk, between the Senegal and Faleme rivers, Bure, on the upper Niger, and later the Akan forests in present-day Ghana. Most gold was found as small grains and nuggets in river gravels and shallow pits, washed and collected by local farming communities, often in the dry season when farming work was lighter. The exact locations of the gold fields were closely guarded secrets.

West African gold was enormously important to the wider world. Historians estimate that for several centuries a large share of the gold used in the Mediterranean world and Europe came from West Africa. It was made into coins in North Africa and in Europe, and helped pay for trade across the Mediterranean.

Other goods also went north. Ivory came from elephant tusks. Ostrich feathers were prized for decoration. Kola nuts, a mild stimulant that is allowed in Islam, unlike alcohol, were popular across North Africa. Hides and leather goods and gum were also traded.

From the north, besides salt, came cloth, including fine cotton and silk, copper, which was highly valued in West Africa, horses for the cavalry of West African armies, glass beads, swords, paper and books, and goods from as far away as Europe and Asia. Cowrie shells from the Indian Ocean were used as small change in West African markets.

A story told by ancient and medieval writers describes a silent trade, in which traders left goods on a riverbank and withdrew, and gold miners left gold beside them, until both sides were satisfied, without ever meeting. Historians are cautious: the story may be partly legend, but it shows how closely the gold miners guarded their secrets.

The questions for this section are with you now: the gold fields, the importance of West African gold to the wider world, other goods traded both ways, and the silent trade.

## Subtopic: The Trade in Enslaved People

The trans-Saharan trade also carried people. Enslaved men, women and children were taken north across the desert and sold in North Africa and the Middle East. Most had been captured in wars or raids, sometimes by the armies of West African states, sometimes by raiders. Many women and girls were sold as domestic servants; men worked as labourers, soldiers or servants. Enslaved people also worked in the salt mines of the desert and in the households and fields of West Africa itself.

The journey across the Sahara was terribly harsh for captives, who usually walked, and many died of thirst, heat and exhaustion. Historians estimate that over the many centuries of the trade, several million people were taken across the Sahara, though exact figures are uncertain.

It is important to understand this honestly and without simplifying. Slavery existed in many societies around the world at the time, in Africa, Europe, the Middle East and Asia. The trans-Saharan trade in enslaved people was different from the later transatlantic slave trade, studied next term, in important ways: it took place over a much longer period, in smaller numbers each year, and enslaved people in North Africa and the Middle East could sometimes gain their freedom and rise in society. But it was still the buying and selling of human beings, who suffered greatly and lost their families and homes.

A balanced history of Mali therefore holds two truths together. The trade brought great wealth, learning and achievement to West African cities and kingdoms. It also brought suffering to many thousands of people who were captured and sold.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: believing that only gold was traded. Two: thinking Africa was poor and isolated in this period; it was linked to a world trade network. Three: treating the slave trade across the Sahara as identical to the transatlantic trade.

The questions for this section are with you now: the trade in enslaved people across the Sahara, how it differed from the transatlantic trade, a balanced view, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: You Have What I Need

Trade happens when you have what I need, and I have what you need.

North Africa had cloth, metal goods and books. The Sahara had salt. West Africa had gold. So merchants carried salt and goods south, and gold north.

North African and Berber traders brought goods to towns like Timbuktu. West African traders called the Wangara took them further south and brought back gold.

The kings of Mali taxed the trade and became very rich.

## Subtopic: Salt and Gold

Salt kept people alive. In hot West Africa people sweat a lot and lose salt, and salt kept meat and fish from going bad. But there was very little salt there.

In the desert, at Taghaza, salt was dug out in big slabs. It was so plentiful that the houses were built of salt. Camels carried the slabs south, and by the time they reached the markets, salt was worth a lot, sometimes nearly as much as gold.

West African gold came from rivers and pits in places like Bambuk and Bure. It went north, and for centuries much of the gold in Europe and North Africa came from West Africa. Ivory, ostrich feathers and kola nuts went north too. Horses, cloth, copper and books came south.

## Subtopic: People Were Sold Too

There is a sad part of this story. People were traded too. Men, women and children captured in wars and raids were taken north across the desert and sold. Many died on the hot, dry walk.

This trade in people went on for many centuries. It was different from the later trade across the Atlantic Ocean that we study next term, but it still caused great suffering.

So the trade made Mali rich and its cities famous, and it also hurt many people. Good history tells both sides.
