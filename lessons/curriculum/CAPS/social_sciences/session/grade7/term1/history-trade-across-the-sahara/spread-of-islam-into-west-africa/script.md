# Part 1 — Expert

This session traces the spread of Islam into West Africa. The anchor is a Muslim merchant in a West African market town, praying, keeping written accounts in Arabic, and trading with partners who share his faith. The governing idea is that Islam spread into West Africa mainly peacefully, through trade and contact rather than conquest, and that it brought literacy, learning and links to a wider world that helped make Mali and Timbuktu great. The spread was gradual, uneven and shaped by African choices.

## Subtopic: Islam Spreads Across North Africa

Islam is a monotheistic religion, a religion that believes in one God, called Allah in Arabic. It began in the Arabian Peninsula in the early seventh century, when, according to Muslim belief, God's revelations were received by the Prophet Muhammad in the city of Mecca. Muhammad died in 632. Followers of Islam are called Muslims, and the holy book of Islam is the Quran, written in Arabic.

Muslims follow five central practices, often called the Five Pillars: the declaration of faith that there is no god but God and Muhammad is his messenger; prayer five times a day; giving to the poor, called zakat; fasting during the month of Ramadan; and, for those who are able, the pilgrimage to Mecca, called the hajj, at least once in a lifetime. The pilgrimage will matter later in this topic, when we study the hajj of Mansa Musa.

After Muhammad's death, Arab armies created a large empire. Between about 640 and 710 they conquered Egypt and the whole of North Africa as far as Morocco, and in 711 crossed into Spain. Over the following centuries, many of the peoples of North Africa, including most of the Berber peoples, became Muslim, and Arabic became an important language of religion, learning and government.

North Africa thus became part of a huge Muslim world stretching from Spain to India, linked by shared beliefs, the Arabic language, Islamic law and trade. The merchants who organised the caravans across the Sahara were increasingly part of this world.

## Subtopic: Traders Carry Islam South

Islam did not reach West Africa mainly through armies. It travelled with traders. From about the ninth century, Muslim merchants from North Africa, many of them Berbers, crossed the Sahara regularly and settled for long periods in the trading towns of the Sahel. In the capital of the kingdom of Ghana, the Arab geographer al-Bakri described in 1068 a separate Muslim town with twelve mosques, where the Muslim merchants lived, a few kilometres from the king's town, where traditional religion was practised.

Muslim merchants had good reasons to spread their faith, and West Africans had good reasons to accept it. Shared religion created trust. Muslim traders gave credit to, married into and formed partnerships with people who shared their beliefs, laws and customs. Islamic law provided agreed rules for contracts, debts and inheritance, which made long-distance business more reliable. A West African merchant who became Muslim gained access to a trading network stretching across the Muslim world.

The first West Africans to convert were usually merchants, especially the Wangara or Dyula traders who linked the desert trade to the gold fields. Kings and their courts followed. The king of Takrur, on the Senegal River, is recorded as Muslim in the early eleventh century, and the rulers of Gao around the same time. In the kingdom of Mali, rulers became Muslim during the thirteenth century, and by the time of Mansa Musa in the early fourteenth century, Mali's kings were known throughout the Muslim world as devout Muslims.

The spread was gradual. For centuries, Islam was mainly the religion of the towns, the traders and the rulers, while most farmers in the countryside continued to follow traditional African religions.

## Subtopic: What Islam Brought: Writing, Learning and Law

Islam brought major changes to West African society, especially in the towns.

Literacy. Muslims are expected to read the Quran in Arabic, so Islam brought the Arabic script, and with it the ability to write. Before this, West African societies kept their history and knowledge mainly through oral tradition, carried by specialists such as the griots. Writing allowed records, letters, contracts and books. Over time, West African scholars also used the Arabic script to write their own languages, such as Fulfulde and Hausa, a form of writing called Ajami.

Education. Quranic schools taught children to read and memorise the Quran. More advanced scholars studied law, theology, grammar, mathematics, astronomy and medicine. This tradition grew into the great centres of learning of Timbuktu, studied later this term.

Government. Muslim scholars who could read and write became advisers, secretaries and judges for West African kings. Written records helped rulers manage taxes and trade, and letters in Arabic allowed diplomatic contact with rulers in North Africa and Egypt.

Law. Islamic law, the sharia, provided rules for trade, marriage, inheritance and justice, interpreted by trained judges called qadis.

Architecture. Mosques were built in the towns, often in the West African style of sun-dried mud brick with wooden beams sticking out of the walls, a style still seen today in the great mosques of Timbuktu and Djenné.

Connections. Becoming Muslim connected West Africa to the wider Muslim world. Scholars travelled to study in Fez, Cairo and Mecca, and visitors came from North Africa and the Middle East.

The questions for this section are with you now: literacy and Ajami, Quranic schools, scholars in government, Islamic law, mosque architecture and wider connections.

## Subtopic: African Islam: Blending and Choice

The Islam that developed in West Africa was shaped by West Africans. For centuries, many people combined Islam with older beliefs and customs. Kings in particular often balanced the two. A king who ruled over Muslim merchants and over farmers who followed traditional religion had to respect both groups to keep their loyalty. Ibn Battuta, visiting Mali in 1352 and 1353, praised the people's devotion to prayer and the safety of travel in the kingdom, but disapproved of some customs at the royal court, such as the performances of masked dancers and the way women dressed, which he saw as un-Islamic. His criticism tells us as much about his own views, as a strict scholar from Morocco, as about Mali.

Historians describe this as a process of accommodation: Islam was adopted, but in ways that fitted local society. Over later centuries, particularly from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, reform movements tried to remove older practices, and Islam spread much more widely in the countryside.

Today Islam is the religion of the great majority of people in Mali, Senegal, Niger and other countries of the region, and West African Muslim scholarship is respected around the world.

It is important to avoid two mistaken views. One says that West Africa had no religion, learning or civilisation before Islam arrived; in fact it had complex societies, kingdoms, trade and rich religious traditions. The other sees Islam as simply imposed from outside; in fact, West Africans chose it, adapted it and made it their own.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: believing Islam was spread into West Africa by conquest. Two: thinking everyone in Mali was Muslim in the fourteenth century. Three: thinking a visitor's description is automatically neutral.

The questions for this section are with you now: blending Islam with older traditions, Ibn Battuta's perspective, accommodation and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: a new religion, how it travelled, what it brought, and how Africans made it their own.

## Subtopic: A Faith From Arabia

Islam is a religion that believes in one God, Allah. It began in Arabia about fourteen hundred years ago, with the Prophet Muhammad. Its holy book is the Quran, written in Arabic.

Muslims pray five times a day, give to the poor, fast in Ramadan, and, if they can, make a pilgrimage to Mecca called the hajj.

Soon after Muhammad died, Arab armies conquered North Africa, and over time most North Africans became Muslim.

## Subtopic: Carried by Camel

Islam reached West Africa with the traders, not with armies. From about the ninth century, Muslim merchants crossed the Sahara with their caravans and lived in West African market towns.

West African traders saw that sharing the same faith helped business: people trusted each other, gave each other credit, and followed the same rules for deals. So many traders became Muslim, then kings did too. Mali's kings were Muslim by the thirteenth century.

Most farmers in the villages kept their old religions for a long time.

## Subtopic: Books, Schools and Mud Mosques

Islam brought writing in Arabic. Children went to Quranic schools to learn to read. Scholars studied law, maths, astronomy and medicine. Kings used scholars as advisers and judges. Beautiful mosques were built of mud brick with wooden beams sticking out.

But West Africans made Islam their own. Kings mixed Islam with older customs, and some visitors, like Ibn Battuta from Morocco, did not approve of everything they saw. West Africa already had kingdoms, trade and religions before Islam came; people chose the new faith and shaped it.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Islam began in Arabia and spread across North Africa. From the ninth century, traders carried it across the Sahara. Merchants and kings converted first because a shared faith built trust. Islam brought writing, schools, scholars, law and mosques. West Africans adapted Islam to their own societies.
