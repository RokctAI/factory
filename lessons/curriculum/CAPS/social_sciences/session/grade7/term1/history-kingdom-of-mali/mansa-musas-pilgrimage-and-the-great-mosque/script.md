# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines Mansa Musa's hajj, his pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324 and 1325, and the building of the Great Mosque in Timbuktu that followed. The anchor is the report of the Egyptian scholar al-Umari that Musa's spending in Cairo lowered the value of gold there for years. The governing idea is that the pilgrimage was both an act of faith and an act of state: it fulfilled a religious duty, but it also displayed Mali's wealth to the world, built diplomatic and scholarly links, and brought back the people and ideas that made Timbuktu a centre of learning.

## Subtopic: Why Mansa Musa Went to Mecca

The hajj is the pilgrimage to Mecca, in Arabia, which every Muslim who is physically and financially able should make at least once. It takes place in a particular month of the Islamic calendar and includes prayers and rituals at the Kaaba, the holy shrine at the centre of the Great Mosque of Mecca, and at sites nearby.

Mansa Musa was not the first ruler of Mali to make the hajj; earlier mansas had done so. But his pilgrimage, in 1324 and 1325, was by far the most famous. Historians identify several reasons for it.

Religious duty. Musa was a devout Muslim, and the hajj was a pillar of his faith. Some accounts suggest he also sought forgiveness, perhaps for accidentally killing his mother, according to one tradition recorded later, though this cannot be confirmed.

Prestige. A splendid pilgrimage displayed Mali's wealth and power to the Muslim world, especially to Egypt, the most powerful Muslim state of the time, ruled by the Mamluk sultans.

Diplomacy and trade. Meeting other rulers and merchants strengthened Mali's position in the trade networks that stretched across North Africa and the Middle East.

Learning. Musa wanted to bring scholars, books and skilled people back to Mali, to strengthen Islam and learning in his kingdom.

The main sources for the pilgrimage are Arabic writers in Egypt, especially al-Umari, who wrote about twelve years later using the memories of officials and merchants who had met Musa in Cairo, and later chronicles written in Timbuktu. Because these accounts were written by people impressed by Musa's wealth, some numbers may be exaggerated.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of the hajj, the reasons for Musa's pilgrimage, and the sources that describe it.

## Subtopic: The Journey and Cairo

Mansa Musa set out from Mali in 1324 with an enormous following. The accounts describe thousands of people: officials, soldiers, scholars, servants and enslaved people, with numbers given as high as tens of thousands, which historians treat with caution. The caravan carried food, supplies and, above all, gold. Reports describe around eighty to a hundred camels each carrying a heavy load of gold dust, and servants carrying gold staffs.

The route probably went north-east across the Sahara by way of Walata and the oases of the desert to Egypt, a journey of several months covering several thousand kilometres.

In July 1324 Musa reached Cairo, one of the largest and richest cities in the world. There he camped near the pyramids and met the Mamluk sultan, al-Nasir Muhammad. Al-Umari records a famous moment of protocol: Musa at first refused to kiss the ground before the sultan, as custom demanded, saying that he bowed only to God, and the meeting was settled when he agreed to bow in that spirit.

Musa and his followers spent and gave away gold freely in Cairo, buying goods, giving gifts to officials and making donations to the poor and to religious institutions. Al-Umari reported that so much gold entered the market that its value fell, and that it had still not recovered twelve years later. By the end of his journey Musa had spent so much that he had to borrow money from merchants in Cairo for the return trip, which he later repaid.

From Cairo the caravan travelled with the Egyptian pilgrim caravan to Mecca and Medina, where Musa completed the rituals of the hajj and gave generously to the holy cities.

## Subtopic: Results of the Pilgrimage

The pilgrimage had important and lasting results for Mali.

Fame. Mali became known throughout the Muslim world and in Europe as a land of fabulous wealth. Within about fifty years, European map makers drew Mansa Musa on the Catalan Atlas holding a nugget of gold. This fame attracted traders and scholars to Mali.

Scholars and learning. Musa returned with scholars, including descendants of the Prophet Muhammad's family, legal experts and teachers, and with books. He encouraged learning in Timbuktu and other towns and sent students to study in Fez in Morocco. These links helped turn Timbuktu into a centre of Islamic scholarship.

Architecture. Musa brought back skilled people, most famously the poet and architect Abu Ishaq al-Sahili, from Granada in Muslim Spain, who had met him in Mecca. Al-Sahili is credited with designing buildings for Musa, including a royal audience chamber and work on the Great Mosque of Timbuktu.

Diplomacy and trade. Mali developed diplomatic links with Egypt and Morocco, and trade between them grew.

Expansion. While Musa was away, or soon after his return according to different sources, Mali's generals brought the trading city of Gao under Mali's control, and Timbuktu became part of the empire.

There were also costs. The pilgrimage used vast resources taken from Mali's people through taxes and tribute, and the enslaved people who travelled with the caravan had no choice in the journey. Some historians also note that advertising Mali's wealth may have made it a target for those who wanted to control the gold trade, although Mali's decline came mainly from later disputes over succession.

The questions for this section are with you now: the fame, learning, architecture, diplomacy and expansion that followed the pilgrimage, and its costs.

## Subtopic: The Great Mosque of Timbuktu

After his return, Mansa Musa ordered the building of a great Friday mosque in Timbuktu, completed in about 1327. It is known as the Djinguereber, from words meaning great mosque in the local languages. A Friday mosque is the main congregational mosque of a town, where the community gathers for the important midday prayer on Fridays.

The design is credited to al-Sahili, who, according to tradition, received a large payment in gold for his work. The mosque is built in the Sudano-Sahelian style of West Africa. Its walls are made of banco, sun-dried mud bricks covered in mud plaster, mixed with straw, and strengthened with limestone. Bundles of palm wood, called toron, stick out of the walls; they strengthen the structure and serve as permanent scaffolding for the yearly repairs. It has a tall, tapering minaret, a tower from which the call to prayer was made, and inside, rows of pillars support the roof, with space for about two thousand worshippers. It has been rebuilt and extended over the centuries, notably in the sixteenth century.

Mud buildings must be maintained. Each year, after the rainy season, the community replasters the walls with fresh mud. This maintenance is a community event, involving many people, and it keeps the building alive.

Two other famous mosques of the region should not be confused with it. The Sankore mosque, also in Timbuktu, became the heart of a great centre of learning. The Great Mosque of Djenné, the largest mud-brick building in the world, stands in the town of Djenné; the present building dates from 1907, on the site of a much older mosque.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking mud is a weak, primitive building material; well maintained, mud buildings last for centuries and suit the hot, dry climate. Two: confusing the Djinguereber with the Great Mosque of Djenné. Three: believing every number in the pilgrimage stories exactly.

The questions for this section are with you now: the building of the Djinguereber, its materials and style, annual maintenance, and other mosques of the region.

## Subtopic: A King Goes on Pilgrimage

Every Muslim who can should make the hajj, a pilgrimage to Mecca in Arabia, once in their life.

In 1324 Mansa Musa set off. He went because of his faith, but also to show the world how rich Mali was, to make friends with other rulers, and to bring back scholars and books.

Most of what we know comes from writers in Egypt, who heard about him from people who saw him. They were very impressed, so some numbers may be exaggerated.

## Subtopic: Gold in Cairo

Musa travelled with thousands of people and many camels carrying gold dust. After months crossing the desert, he reached Cairo in Egypt, a huge city, in July 1324.

He met the sultan of Egypt. He gave gold away to officials, to the poor and to mosques, and his people bought many things. So much gold poured in that gold became less valuable in Cairo for years.

He spent so much that he had to borrow money to get home, and he paid it back later. Then he went on to Mecca and completed the hajj.

## Subtopic: The Mud Mosque

Musa came home with scholars, books and an architect from Spain named al-Sahili. Now Mali was famous everywhere, even on European maps.

In Timbuktu, Musa built a great mosque, the Djinguereber, finished in about 1327. It is made of sun-dried mud bricks, with wooden beams sticking out of the walls. Every year, after the rains, the whole community helps to plaster it with fresh mud. It is still standing nearly seven hundred years later.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Mansa Musa made the hajj in 1324 and 1325, for faith, fame, friendship and learning. In Cairo he spent so much gold that its value fell. He brought back scholars, books and the architect al-Sahili. He built the Great Mosque of Timbuktu, the Djinguereber, of mud brick, and it is still cared for by the community.
