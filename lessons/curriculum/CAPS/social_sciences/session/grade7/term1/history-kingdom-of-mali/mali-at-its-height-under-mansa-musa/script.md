# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the kingdom of Mali at the height of its power. The anchor is the title mansa, meaning king or emperor in the Mande language, held by Mali's rulers. The governing idea is that Mali's power rested on three foundations: control of the trans-Saharan trade and the gold fields, an effective system of government that held together many different peoples, and the authority of its rulers, strengthened by Islam and by tradition. Under Mansa Musa, these foundations produced an empire whose wealth became famous across Africa, the Middle East and Europe.

## Subtopic: Sundiata and the Founding of Mali

Mali grew out of a small Mande-speaking kingdom on the upper Niger River, in the region of the present-day border between Mali and Guinea. In the twelfth and early thirteenth centuries, the older empire of Ghana, which had controlled the gold trade, declined, and the region was contested by rival states.

The founder of the empire of Mali was Sundiata Keita. According to the Epic of Sundiata, passed down by griots for centuries, Sundiata was the son of a Mande king, born unable to walk, mocked as a child and forced into exile with his mother. He eventually returned as a great warrior and, in about 1235, defeated Sumanguru Kante, the king of the Sosso, at the Battle of Kirina. After this victory Sundiata united the Mande clans and became the first mansa of Mali. Historians treat the epic with care: it is a heroic story designed to inspire and to honour Sundiata's descendants, and some details are legendary. But Arab sources confirm that a powerful kingdom of Mali arose in this period.

Sundiata and his successors expanded Mali by conquest and alliance. Tradition also credits Sundiata with organising the clans of the empire and setting out their roles and rights, sometimes called the Kurukan Fuga, a charter of rules agreed at a great assembly. The version known today was passed down orally and written down only recently, so historians debate its exact content, but it reflects how Mali brought many groups together under shared rules.

The capital of the empire was at Niani, according to tradition, though historians are still uncertain about its exact location.

## Subtopic: The Size and Wealth of the Empire

At its height in the early fourteenth century, Mali stretched about two thousand kilometres from the Atlantic coast of present-day Senegal and Gambia in the west to beyond the Niger bend in the east, including parts of what are now Mali, Senegal, Gambia, Guinea, Mauritania, Niger and Burkina Faso. It controlled the great trading towns of the Sahel, including Walata, Djenné, Timbuktu and Gao. It was among the largest states in the world at the time, larger than any kingdom in Europe.

Mali's wealth came from several sources. Most famous was gold. Mali controlled or had access to the gold fields of Bambuk and Bure and the trade routes that carried gold north. The mansa claimed all gold nuggets found in the empire, while gold dust could be traded by the people, which kept the price of gold high and gave the ruler a great reserve.

Trade was taxed. Goods entering and leaving the empire, especially salt, copper and gold, paid duties to the mansa's officials.

Agriculture fed the empire. The fertile floodplains of the Niger River produced millet, sorghum, rice and other crops, and herders kept cattle, sheep and goats. Fishing communities worked the river. Craftspeople included blacksmiths, weavers, potters and leatherworkers.

Tribute from conquered and allied peoples also flowed to the capital, in the form of gold, food, cloth and soldiers.

Mali's population was diverse, including Mande, Soninke, Fulani, Songhay, Tuareg and many other peoples, speaking different languages and following different religions.

## Subtopic: Governing a Great Empire

Holding together such a large and diverse empire required strong and flexible government.

At the centre was the mansa, who had great authority and was treated with elaborate ceremony. Ibn Battuta described how the mansa held audiences seated under a silk canopy, surrounded by officials, soldiers and musicians, and how people approaching him bowed low and sprinkled dust on their heads as a sign of respect. The mansa was the highest judge and commander of the army.

The empire was divided into provinces. Some were governed by officials, called farba, appointed by the mansa. Others were ruled by local kings who kept their thrones but acknowledged the mansa's authority and sent tribute, a flexible arrangement that allowed Mali to absorb conquered peoples without replacing all their leaders.

A large army, with cavalry using horses imported across the Sahara and infantry armed with bows and spears, protected the empire, kept order and guarded the trade routes. Ibn Battuta was struck by the safety of travel in Mali: he wrote that neither the traveller nor the resident had anything to fear from robbers.

Islam strengthened government. Muslim scholars served as secretaries, advisers and judges, keeping records in Arabic and corresponding with rulers abroad. At the same time, the mansa remained the heir of Sundiata in the eyes of followers of traditional religion, and court ceremonies included both Muslim prayers and older customs.

The questions for this section are with you now: the role of the mansa, provinces and local kings, the army and safety, and the role of Muslim scholars.

## Subtopic: Mansa Musa's Golden Age

Mansa Musa ruled Mali from about 1312 to about 1337. He came to power after his predecessor, Abu Bakr the Second, according to an account Musa himself gave in Cairo, set out with a great fleet to explore the Atlantic Ocean and never returned. Historians cannot confirm this story, but it is a remarkable claim.

Under Mansa Musa, Mali reached its greatest size and fame. His armies extended Mali's control over the trading cities of the Niger bend, including Gao and Timbuktu. He was a devout Muslim who encouraged learning: he invited scholars to Mali, supported the building of mosques and schools, and sent students to study in Morocco. His pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324 and 1325, studied in the next lesson, made him famous across the Muslim world and Europe.

European map makers took notice. The Catalan Atlas, made in Majorca in Spain in about 1375, shows a figure of Mansa Musa seated on a throne in West Africa, wearing a gold crown and holding a gold nugget, with a caption describing him as the richest and noblest king in the land. It is a striking primary source: it shows that Europeans knew of Mali's wealth, though they had never been there.

Modern writers sometimes call Mansa Musa the richest person who ever lived. Historians point out that his wealth cannot be measured in modern money, because it was the wealth of a state rather than a personal bank account, but there is no doubt that he controlled enormous wealth.

After Musa's death, Mali remained powerful for some decades, but disputes over succession and attacks weakened it. By the fifteenth century, the Songhay empire, based at Gao, had taken over the Niger bend and Timbuktu.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: calling the epic of Sundiata simply false or simply true. Two: imagining Mali was a small village kingdom. Three: thinking Europeans built or ran Mali; it was an African state that Europeans only heard about.

The questions for this section are with you now: Mansa Musa's reign, his support for learning, the Catalan Atlas, and Mali's later decline.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: the lion king of Mali, a huge rich empire, how it was run, and its most famous king.

## Subtopic: Sundiata, the Lion King

Mali was started by Sundiata Keita. The griots tell his story: as a boy he could not walk and was laughed at. He went into exile, came back strong, and in about 1235 beat the Sosso king Sumanguru at the Battle of Kirina.

Then Sundiata became the first mansa, which means king or emperor, of Mali.

His story is told as an exciting epic, so some parts may be legend. But other sources agree that a great kingdom of Mali began then.

## Subtopic: Big, Rich and Well Run

At its biggest, Mali was about two thousand kilometres across, from the Atlantic Ocean to beyond the Niger River bend, bigger than any country in Europe at the time. It included towns like Timbuktu, Djenné and Gao.

It got rich from gold, from taxing traders, from farming along the Niger River, and from gifts called tribute. The mansa kept every gold nugget for himself, so gold stayed valuable.

The mansa was ruler, judge and army chief. Some conquered kings stayed in charge if they stayed loyal and paid tribute. The army kept the roads so safe that the traveller Ibn Battuta said nobody feared robbers.

## Subtopic: Mansa Musa

The most famous king of Mali was Mansa Musa, who ruled from about 1312 to 1337. Mali was at its biggest and richest under him. He took over the trading cities of Gao and Timbuktu, built mosques, and brought scholars to teach.

He was so famous that a map made in Spain in about 1375, the Catalan Atlas, shows him on a throne with a golden crown, holding a gold nugget.

After he died, Mali slowly weakened, and later the Songhay empire took over Timbuktu.

The mansa ruled with officials, loyal local kings, a strong army and Muslim scholars. Mansa Musa's reign from about 1312 to 1337 was Mali's golden age, famous even in Europe.
