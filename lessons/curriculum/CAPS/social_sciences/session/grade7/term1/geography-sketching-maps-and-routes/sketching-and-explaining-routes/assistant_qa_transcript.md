### subtopic_1 — What a Route Map Needs
**Assistant:** Why shouldn't a route map show everything in the area?

**Tutor:** Because every extra detail competes for the traveller's attention. A route map has one job, to get someone from the start to the end, and the reader looks at it quickly, often while walking or driving. If it shows every house, every tree and every side street, the important information, the route and the turnings, is lost in the clutter, and the traveller may mistake a side street for the turning they need. Good map makers choose. They keep the roads along the route and the turnings near it so the traveller can count junctions, the landmarks that mark turning points, and a few features that confirm the traveller is on the right road. They leave out everything else. This process of choosing and simplifying is called generalisation, and every map uses it, even an atlas map, which cannot possibly show every village. Choosing what to leave out is one of the hardest and most important skills in map making.

### subtopic_3 — Explaining a Route in Words
**Assistant:** Why do emergency services ask for landmarks rather than just the address?

**Tutor:** Because in many places an address alone does not lead a driver to the door. In large parts of South Africa, especially rural villages and informal settlements, houses do not have street numbers, roads have no names or signs, and the official address may be a post box number or a stand number that does not appear on any map. Even in suburbs, street signs are sometimes missing and numbers hard to see at night. An ambulance crew under pressure needs directions they can follow quickly: from the main road, turn at the clinic, pass the church with the red roof, the third house after the water tank, with a white gate. That is a route description built on landmarks. More services now also use the location of the caller's phone, from its GPS, which helps enormously, but the landmark description remains a vital backup. Practising clear directions is therefore not just a classroom skill. One day it may help someone get help in time.

### subtopic_4 — Estimating Distances Along a Route
**Assistant:** Is my step really the same length every time?

**Tutor:** Not exactly, but it is close enough to be useful, and the error tends to even out over a longer distance. Step length depends on height, leg length, speed and the ground. Walking uphill, on sand or in a crowd shortens the step; striding quickly on a flat pavement lengthens it. Within one walk at a steady pace, however, most people's steps are surprisingly regular, which is why pacing has been used for centuries, by Roman soldiers, who counted a thousand double steps as a mile, and by surveyors and hikers since. To make pacing reliable, measure your own pace on a known distance such as a hundred-metre track: walk it normally two or three times, count the steps, and take the average. Then, when pacing a route, walk at the same natural speed and count carefully. The result will usually be within about ten percent of the true distance, which is ample for a sketch map or a set of directions.
