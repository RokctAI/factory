# Part 1 — Expert

This session develops two linked skills: showing a route on a sketch map and explaining it in words. The anchor is the everyday request for directions, from a visitor at the school gate to a caller phoning an ambulance. The governing idea is that a good route description, whether drawn or spoken, gives a clear start and end, follows the route in order, and uses fixed reference points, namely compass directions, landmarks and distances, rather than vague words such as there, just, or a little way.

## Subtopic: What a Route Map Needs

A route map is a sketch map drawn for one purpose: to show how to travel from one place to another. Because it has a single purpose, it does not need to show everything in the area. It should show what helps a traveller and leave out what distracts.

Every route map needs a few essential elements. A title says what the map shows, for example Route from Thembalethu Primary School to the Clinic. A clearly marked starting point and end point, often labelled S and E or shown with distinct symbols. The route itself, drawn as a bold or coloured line, sometimes with arrows to show the direction of travel. A north arrow, so that the map can be turned to match the real world. A key or legend explaining any symbols used. And, where possible, an approximate scale or distances written along the route.

Landmarks are the most important details on a route map. A landmark is a feature that is easy to see and recognise and unlikely to move: a church, a mosque, a water tower, a bridge, a garage, a police station, a cell phone mast, a spaza shop with a well-known name or a large tree. Landmarks are especially useful at turning points, where the traveller must change direction, because they tell the traveller when to turn.

Things that change or move are poor landmarks: parked cars, a vendor's stall that moves each day, a dog, or a building described only by its colour, which may be repainted.

A route map is simplified. Roads can be drawn as straight lines with corners at the junctions, and only the roads along the route and the turnings near it need names. Too much detail makes a sketch map harder to use.

## Subtopic: Sketching a Route Step by Step

Drawing a route map follows a sensible order, so that the map is accurate and easy to read.

Step one: plan. Think about, or better still walk, the route. Note each turning and the landmark at it, the names of the roads, and roughly how long each section is.

Step two: set up the page. Decide which way north is and draw the north arrow in a corner. Mark the starting point and the end point in roughly the right positions, so that the end point lies in the correct direction from the start. If the clinic is north-east of the school, the clinic must be towards the top right of the page.

Step three: draw the roads along the route, using two parallel lines or a single thick line, keeping the lengths roughly in proportion. A section of road twice as long as another should look about twice as long on the map.

Step four: add the landmarks at each turning and along the way, using simple symbols: a cross for a church, a small square for a building, a circle with a T for a tree, and so on.

Step five: draw the route along the roads with a bold or coloured line, with arrows showing the direction of travel.

Step six: complete the key, add the title and, if possible, distances or a rough scale.

Consider an example. From the school gate, the route goes north along School Road for about three hundred metres to the garage, turns east into Main Road, passes the police station on the left, and after about five hundred metres reaches the clinic on the right, opposite the post office. On the sketch, School Road runs up the page from the school, Main Road runs to the right from the garage, and the clinic sits towards the top right, north-east of the school.

The questions for this section are with you now: the order of steps, keeping directions and lengths roughly right, and simple symbols.

## Subtopic: Explaining a Route in Words

Often directions must be given in words, by phone, by text message or face to face. A good spoken or written route description follows the route in order, one step at a time, and gives the traveller something definite to check at each step.

A clear description uses four kinds of information. Starting point and orientation: where to begin and which way to face, for example leave the school by the main gate and turn north into School Road. Directions: compass directions, or left and right once the traveller is facing a known way. Landmarks: what the traveller will see, especially at turnings, for example at the Shell garage, turn right, east, into Main Road. Distances or times: how far to go, for example about three hundred metres, or about a five-minute walk.

Left and right can be useful in spoken directions, because a traveller on foot naturally thinks that way, but they only work if the traveller knows which way they are facing. Combining them with compass directions and landmarks makes directions more reliable: turn right, that is east, at the garage.

Vague words cause confusion. Phrases such as go down there, it's just past the shop, or keep going for a bit leave the traveller guessing. Replace them with definite information: continue east for about five hundred metres; the clinic is on the right, opposite the post office.

Finish with the end point and how to recognise it: a white building with a blue sign, behind a green fence, opposite the post office.

A good test is to ask someone who does not know the area to follow the directions. Where they hesitate, the directions need improving.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four kinds of information in a route description, combining left and right with compass directions, and avoiding vague words.

## Subtopic: Estimating Distances Along a Route

Distances make a route description far more useful, but nobody carries a measuring tape. Geographers and travellers estimate distance in several practical ways.

Pacing. An average Grade 7 learner's walking step is roughly sixty to seventy centimetres, so about one and a half steps make a metre and roughly fifteen hundred steps make a kilometre. A learner can measure their own pace by counting steps along a sports field of known length, such as a hundred-metre running track, and use that number afterwards.

Time. At a normal walking pace, most people cover about four to five kilometres in an hour, or roughly one kilometre in twelve to fifteen minutes. So a ten-minute walk is roughly seven to eight hundred metres. In a vehicle in town, about thirty to fifty kilometres an hour, distances are often given as minutes of driving.

Familiar lengths. A soccer field is about a hundred metres long. A typical suburban plot is about twenty to thirty metres wide, so ten houses along a street is roughly two to three hundred metres. Street blocks in many South African towns are about a hundred metres long. Electricity poles are often thirty to fifty metres apart.

Map measurement. If a map has a scale, distance can be measured, a skill developed later this term.

Estimates should be rounded sensibly: about three hundred metres, not three hundred and twelve metres, because an estimate is not that precise. They should also be checked when possible, for example against a map app's distance.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: confusing time and distance, for example saying a road is ten minutes long without saying walking or driving. Two: giving falsely exact estimates. Three: forgetting units; three hundred what, metres or steps?

The questions for this section are with you now: pacing, walking time, familiar lengths, sensible rounding and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: drawing the way, saying the way, and guessing how far.

## Subtopic: Drawing the Way

A route map shows how to get from one place to another. It only needs what helps the traveller.

Start with a north arrow. Mark the start and the end in the right directions from each other. Draw the roads. Put landmarks at every corner where you turn: the garage, the church, the big tree. Draw the route as a bold line with arrows. Add a key and a title.

Good landmarks stay put: buildings, bridges, towers. Bad landmarks move: cars, stalls, animals.

## Subtopic: Saying the Way

When you explain a route out loud, go step by step, in order.

Say where to start and which way to face. Say which way to go, using left or right and the compass direction too. Say what they will see at each turn. Say how far.

Like this: leave by the main gate and turn north into School Road. Walk about three hundred metres to the garage. Turn right, east, into Main Road. Walk about five hundred metres. The clinic is on your right, opposite the post office.

Throw away fuzzy words like over there and a little bit. Use real directions, landmarks and distances.

## Subtopic: How Far Is It?

You can guess distances without a tape measure.

Count your steps: about fifteen hundred steps is one kilometre. Watch the clock: walking, one kilometre takes about twelve to fifteen minutes. Use things you know: a soccer field is about a hundred metres long.

Round your guess: say about three hundred metres, not three hundred and twelve. And always say the unit: metres, kilometres, or minutes walking.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A route map has a title, start, end, route line, north arrow, key and landmarks at the turns. Spoken directions go step by step with directions, landmarks and distances. Estimate distance with steps, time or familiar lengths, round it, and give the unit.
