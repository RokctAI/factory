# Part 1 — Expert

This session links map skills to the real world through current events. The anchor is a news headline that names a place most learners could not point to on a map. The governing idea is that every event happens somewhere, and that knowing where it happened, its position on the globe, its continent, its neighbours and its distance from us, helps us to understand why it happened and whom it affects. The same skill works at two scales: on a world map with latitude and longitude, and on a local sketch map with compass directions.

## Subtopic: The World Map: Continents and Oceans

Before a news place can be located, the large framework of the world map must be secure. The Earth's land is divided into seven continents: Africa, Asia, Europe, North America, South America, Australia, which is often called Oceania together with the Pacific islands, and Antarctica. Asia is the largest continent by area and by population; Africa is the second largest. The five oceans are the Pacific, the largest and deepest, the Atlantic, the Indian, the Southern Ocean around Antarctica, and the Arctic Ocean around the North Pole.

South Africa lies at the southern tip of Africa, washed by the Atlantic Ocean on the west and the Indian Ocean on the east. The two oceans are usually said to meet at Cape Agulhas. Our neighbours are Namibia, Botswana, Zimbabwe, Mozambique and Eswatini, with Lesotho completely surrounded by South African territory.

A world map is a flat picture of a round Earth, and every flat world map distorts something: shape, size, distance or direction. On many common world maps, places far from the equator, such as Greenland and Russia, look much larger than they really are, while Africa looks smaller than it is. In fact Africa is about fourteen times larger than Greenland. When comparing sizes of countries in the news, a globe is more reliable than a flat map.

The world map also carries the grid. The equator crosses Africa through Gabon, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Uganda, Kenya and Somalia. The Prime Meridian crosses Africa through Ghana, Burkina Faso, Mali and Algeria, and meets the equator in the Gulf of Guinea, at zero degrees latitude and zero degrees longitude.

The questions for this section are with you now: the seven continents, the five oceans, South Africa's neighbours, and map distortion.

## Subtopic: Places in the News

News happens in places, and part of being an informed citizen is knowing where. A class news map is a world map on the wall, or in a workbook, on which learners mark each place that appears in the news, with a short label: the date, the event and the place. Over the year the map fills up and patterns appear.

Some patterns are physical. Reports of earthquakes and volcanic eruptions cluster around the edge of the Pacific Ocean, in countries such as Japan, Indonesia, Chile and the west coast of the United States, a pattern studied in the second term. Tropical cyclones appear in the warm oceans of the tropics: in our region they form over the south-west Indian Ocean and strike Madagascar and Mozambique, as Cyclone Idai did in March 2019, causing devastating floods around the city of Beira. Floods, droughts and heat waves can be marked too.

Other patterns are human: elections, conflicts, sporting events, trade agreements and scientific discoveries. A place in the news should be located on three levels. First, the continent and the ocean or sea it lies near. Second, the country and its neighbours. Third, its position in degrees.

When studying a news report, ask the geographer's questions. Where did it happen? Why there? Who is affected? Is it near us, and could it affect South Africa? Cyclone Idai, for example, brought heavy rain to parts of Zimbabwe as well as Mozambique, and South African rescue teams helped in the aftermath.

Good sources of news include newspapers, radio, television bulletins and reliable news websites. Because news sometimes contains mistakes or deliberately false stories, it is wise to check that more than one reliable source reports the same event before marking it on the map.

The questions for this section are with you now: the class news map, patterns in the news, and the three levels of location.

## Subtopic: Locating News Places in Degrees

The most exact way to describe the location of a news place is with its latitude and longitude. The method is the same as the one revised in the previous lesson: latitude first, north or south of the equator; longitude second, east or west of the Prime Meridian; each with its letter.

Consider some places that appear often in the news. Nairobi, the capital of Kenya and a centre for many international organisations, lies almost on the equator, at about one degree south and thirty-seven degrees east. Cairo in Egypt lies at about thirty degrees north, thirty-one degrees east, almost directly north of Durban, which is at about thirty degrees south, thirty-one degrees east. The two cities are mirror images across the equator. London lies at about fifty-one degrees north and almost zero degrees longitude. New York lies at about forty-one degrees north, seventy-four degrees west. Tokyo lies at about thirty-six degrees north, one hundred and forty degrees east. Rio de Janeiro lies at about twenty-three degrees south, forty-three degrees west, almost on the Tropic of Capricorn.

To read a position from an atlas, find the lines drawn on the map, which are usually every five, ten or twenty degrees, and estimate between them. If a place lies halfway between the lines for twenty and thirty degrees south, its latitude is about twenty-five degrees south. The atlas index helps too: most atlas indexes list each place with its latitude and longitude, so the position can be checked.

The degrees tell us a great deal before we even read the story. A place at sixty degrees north will be very cold in winter and have very short winter days. A place near zero degrees will be hot all year. A place with a longitude far from ours will be in a different time zone, so an event reported as happening in the evening in Tokyo happened around midday in South Africa.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading the latitude and longitude of news places, estimating between lines, and what the degrees tell us.

## Subtopic: Compass Directions on a Local Sketch Map

The same skill of describing direction is needed close to home. A sketch map is a simple map drawn by hand, not to an exact scale, that shows the main features of an area and how they are arranged. It is useful for showing a visitor the way, planning a walk or recording what is in a neighbourhood.

Every sketch map needs a north arrow so that directions can be read from it. To place the arrow correctly, find north in the real area. A compass gives the most reliable answer, and many phones have a compass app. Without one, use the sun: in the early morning it rises roughly in the east, so if you stand with your right arm pointing to the morning sun, you are facing roughly north. At midday in South Africa, your shadow points roughly south, because the sun is in the northern part of the sky.

Once north is known, draw the arrow in a corner of the map, usually the top right, and arrange the features so that what is to the north in reality is towards the top of the page. Then directions can be read using the eight points of the compass. A learner at a school might write: the clinic is north-east of the school; the sports field is south of the school; the taxi rank is west of the clinic.

On a local sketch map, use landmarks, features that are easy to recognise, such as a church, a large tree, a water tower, a petrol station or a bridge. Directions given from landmarks are easier to follow than directions given only from street names.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: drawing the north arrow last, by guesswork, after the map is finished. Two: forgetting that the direction from A to B is the opposite of the direction from B to A. Three: using left and right instead of compass directions; left and right change depending on which way you are facing, but north does not.

The questions for this section are with you now: sketch maps, finding north without a compass, and giving compass directions between local landmarks.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: the big world map, news places, their numbers, and directions at home.

## Subtopic: The Big Picture

The world has seven continents: Africa, Asia, Europe, North America, South America, Australia and Antarctica. It has five oceans: Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern and Arctic. The Pacific is the biggest.

South Africa is at the bottom of Africa, with the Atlantic Ocean on the west and the Indian Ocean on the east. Lesotho sits right inside South Africa.

Flat maps stretch some places. Greenland looks enormous on many maps, but Africa is really about fourteen times bigger. A globe shows true sizes.

Try this part's questions on continents, oceans and our neighbours.

## Subtopic: Pinning the News

When you hear about a place in the news, put a pin in it on a world map, with the date and what happened.

Ask: which continent? Which country? What are its numbers? Nairobi in Kenya is about one degree south, thirty-seven degrees east, almost on the equator. Cairo in Egypt is thirty north, thirty-one east; Durban is thirty south, thirty-one east, like a mirror.

After a few months, patterns appear. Earthquakes and volcanoes keep happening around the Pacific. Cyclones come from warm tropical seas; Cyclone Idai hit Mozambique in 2019.

And check your news: if only one strange post says it, wait until reliable news reports it too.

Try this part's questions on news places and their numbers.

## Subtopic: Which Way at Home?

A sketch map is a quick map you draw by hand of your own area.

First, find north. Use a compass, or use the sun: the sun rises in the east, and at lunchtime your shadow points south, so north is behind you. Draw the north arrow first.

Then put in landmarks: the school, the shop, a big tree, the taxi rank. Now you can say: the clinic is north-east of the school. Remember to flip it: the school is south-west of the clinic.

Use compass points, not left and right. Left and right change when you turn around. North stays north.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Know the seven continents and five oceans. Put news places on a world map and give their latitude and longitude. Look for patterns. At home, find north, draw the arrow first, and describe places with compass directions from landmarks.
