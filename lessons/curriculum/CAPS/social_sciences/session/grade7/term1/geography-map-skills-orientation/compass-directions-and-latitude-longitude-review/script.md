# Part 1 — Expert

This session opens the Grade 7 Geography year and revises the two map skills on which the whole of the term's work is built: compass direction and global position. The anchor is a simple question asked by anyone giving directions or reading a map of the world: where is it, and which way is it from here? The governing idea is that a map becomes useful only when we can describe direction and location precisely. Compass directions describe where one place lies in relation to another; latitude and longitude describe where a place lies on the Earth as a whole. Both are tested throughout the year, in map work, source questions and the term project on local maps.

## Subtopic: Social Sciences in Grade 7

Social Sciences in the Senior Phase is made up of two disciplines, Geography and History, which are taught side by side every term. Geography studies places, the natural environment and the way people live in and change it. History studies the past, how we know about it, and how it shaped the present. In Grade 7 Geography the four terms cover map skills with a focus on the world, volcanoes, earthquakes and floods, population growth and change, and natural resources and conservation in South Africa.

Geography has its own skills. A geographer observes, records, maps and explains. The questions a geographer asks are: where is it, why is it there, how did it get there, what is it like, and how does it affect people? Maps are the main tool for the first of these questions, which is why the year begins with map skills.

Assessment happens in two ways. Informal assessment is the everyday checking of understanding through classwork, homework, short quizzes and discussion. It does not count towards a promotion mark but tells you and your teacher what still needs work. Formal assessment is recorded and counts towards the final mark. In Grade 7 Geography the formal tasks include a project on local maps in the first term, controlled tests and an end-of-year examination. Each task usually carries fifty marks.

Questions in Geography use particular command words, and it pays to know them. Name or list means give the facts without explanation. Describe means say what something is like. Explain means give reasons, often using the word because. Compare means show similarities and differences. Calculate means work out a value and show the steps. A one-word answer to an explain question cannot earn full marks.

The questions for this section are with you now: the two disciplines of Social Sciences, formal and informal assessment, and command words.

## Subtopic: The Eight Points of the Compass

A compass is an instrument with a magnetised needle that swings to point towards magnetic north. Once north is known, every other direction follows. The four cardinal points are north, east, south and west. Going clockwise from north, they are always in the order north, east, south, west, which many learners remember with a phrase such as never eat soggy weetbix.

Between each pair of cardinal points lies an intermediate point, named by joining the two: north-east between north and east, south-east between south and east, south-west between south and west, and north-west between north and west. Together these eight points make up the eight-point compass rose. Notice that north and south always come first in the name of an intermediate direction: we say north-east, never east-north.

The angle between neighbouring points on an eight-point compass is forty-five degrees, because the full circle of three hundred and sixty degrees is divided into eight equal parts. North-east is therefore exactly halfway between north and east.

To give the direction of one place from another, imagine standing at the second place, the one the word from refers to, and facing north. Then ask which way you would have to turn to face the first place. For example, on a map of South Africa, Durban lies to the east of Kimberley, Polokwane lies north-east of Johannesburg, and Cape Town lies south-west of Bloemfontein. The direction from A to B is always the opposite of the direction from B to A: if Pretoria is north-east of Kimberley, then Kimberley is south-west of Pretoria.

Most maps are drawn with north at the top, and they show this with a north arrow. Always check the arrow first, because some maps, especially street maps, are turned to fit the page. Without a compass, the sun gives a rough guide: in South Africa it rises in the east, sets in the west, and at midday lies towards the north, because we live south of the tropics.

The questions for this section are with you now: cardinal and intermediate points, the forty-five degree angle, and giving the direction of one place from another.

## Subtopic: Latitude in Degrees

To give the position of a place on the whole Earth, geographers use a grid of imaginary lines. Lines of latitude run from east to west around the globe, parallel to one another, so they are also called parallels. They measure how far north or south a place is from the equator.

The equator is the line of zero degrees latitude, the great circle halfway between the North Pole and the South Pole. It divides the Earth into the Northern Hemisphere and the Southern Hemisphere. Latitude is measured in degrees north or south of the equator, up to ninety degrees north at the North Pole and ninety degrees south at the South Pole. A latitude must always carry its letter, N or S, because twenty degrees north and twenty degrees south are very different places, one in the Sahara Desert and the other near the border between Zimbabwe and Mozambique.

Some lines of latitude have special names. The Tropic of Cancer lies at about twenty-three and a half degrees north, and the Tropic of Capricorn at about twenty-three and a half degrees south. The Tropic of Capricorn passes through the north of South Africa, close to Polokwane in Limpopo. The Arctic Circle and the Antarctic Circle lie at about sixty-six and a half degrees north and south.

South Africa lies entirely in the Southern Hemisphere, between about twenty-two degrees south at the Limpopo River and almost thirty-five degrees south at Cape Agulhas, the southernmost point of Africa. The further south a place is, the larger its latitude in degrees south.

Each degree is divided into sixty minutes, which allows positions to be given more exactly. For Grade 7, positions to the nearest whole degree are usually enough.

The questions for this section are with you now: the equator and the hemispheres, the tropics, and the latitude of South Africa.

## Subtopic: Longitude and Giving a Position

Lines of longitude, also called meridians, run from the North Pole to the South Pole. They measure how far east or west a place is from the Prime Meridian, the line of zero degrees longitude that passes through Greenwich in London. Longitude is measured in degrees east or west, up to one hundred and eighty degrees, where east and west meet on the opposite side of the world in the Pacific Ocean. The Prime Meridian and the hundred and eighty degree line together divide the Earth into the Eastern and Western Hemispheres.

Unlike lines of latitude, meridians are not parallel: they are furthest apart at the equator and meet at the poles. Every meridian is half a great circle.

South Africa lies in the Eastern Hemisphere, between about sixteen degrees east on the west coast near Alexander Bay and about thirty-three degrees east on the KwaZulu-Natal coast.

To give the position of a place, read the latitude first and the longitude second, each with its letter. Cape Town lies at about thirty-four degrees south, eighteen degrees east, written 34 degrees S, 18 degrees E. Johannesburg lies at about twenty-six degrees south, twenty-eight degrees east. Durban lies at about thirty degrees south, thirty-one degrees east.

To find a place from its coordinates on an atlas map, move along the equator to the correct meridian, then up or down that meridian to the correct parallel. Where the two lines cross is the place. To read the coordinates of a place, do the reverse: find the parallel running through it and read its value at the side of the map, then find the meridian through it and read its value at the top or bottom.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: giving longitude before latitude. Two: leaving out N, S, E or W. Three: reading the number from the wrong edge of the map. Four: thinking lines of longitude are parallel.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Prime Meridian, the hemispheres, reading coordinates and the error museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

## Subtopic: A Year of Geography

Social Sciences has two parts, Geography and History. In Geography you learn about places, nature and people. This year: maps, volcanoes and earthquakes and floods, how populations grow, and how we look after South Africa's natural resources.

Some of your work is practice: classwork, homework and quick quizzes. That is informal, and it helps you find out what to work on. Some of your work counts for marks: projects, tests and exams. That is formal. This term's formal task is a project about maps of your own area.

Watch the question words. Name means just give the facts. Describe means say what it is like. Explain means give reasons. If the question says explain, use the word because.

## Subtopic: Which Way Is It?

The compass has four main points: north, east, south and west, in that order going clockwise. In between are four more: north-east, south-east, south-west and north-west. That makes eight. North or south always comes first in the name.

To say which way a place is, pretend you are standing at the place after the word from. Face north. Which way must you turn to see the other place? Durban is east of Kimberley. Cape Town is south-west of Bloemfontein.

Turn it round and the direction flips. If Pretoria is north-east of Kimberley, then Kimberley is south-west of Pretoria.

On most maps north is at the top, but always look for the north arrow first. And outside, the sun helps: it rises in the east, sets in the west, and in South Africa it is in the north at lunchtime.

## Subtopic: An Address for Every Place

The globe has an address grid of imaginary lines.

Lines of latitude go across, like the rungs of a ladder. The middle one is the equator, zero degrees. Places are so many degrees north or south of it. South Africa is south, between about twenty-two and thirty-five degrees south.

Lines of longitude go from pole to pole, like the segments of an orange. The starting line, zero degrees, goes through Greenwich in London. Places are so many degrees east or west of it. South Africa is east.

To write an address, latitude first, then longitude, with the letters. Cape Town: thirty-four degrees south, eighteen degrees east. Johannesburg: twenty-six degrees south, twenty-eight degrees east.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Social Sciences is Geography plus History. Formal tasks count for marks. The compass has eight points, forty-five degrees apart. Latitude tells you north or south of the equator; longitude tells you east or west of Greenwich. Write latitude first, and never forget the letters.

Take the final questions of the lesson: compass points, latitude and longitude.
