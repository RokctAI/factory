### subtopic_2 — The Eight Points of the Compass
**Assistant:** Why does a compass point north at all?

**Tutor:** Because the Earth itself behaves like a giant, weak magnet. Deep inside the planet, the liquid iron of the outer core moves slowly, and that moving metal produces a magnetic field around the whole Earth. A compass needle is a small, light magnet balanced so that it can swing freely, and it lines up with the Earth's field, one end pointing towards the magnetic pole in the far north. There is a small complication worth knowing. The magnetic north pole is not exactly at the geographic North Pole, the point the Earth spins around, and it slowly wanders over the years. So the direction a compass shows, magnetic north, differs a little from true north, the direction of the North Pole on the globe. In South Africa the difference is large enough to matter on detailed maps, which is why topographic maps print the difference, called magnetic declination, in the margin. For Grade 7 sketch maps and directions between towns, the difference does not matter, and north on the compass can be treated as north on the map.

### subtopic_3 — Latitude in Degrees
**Assistant:** If the equator is the middle of the Earth, why isn't Greenwich in the middle too?

**Tutor:** Because latitude and longitude start from different kinds of line. The equator is fixed by nature. The Earth spins on an axis through the two poles, and the equator is the circle exactly halfway between them, so everyone, everywhere, agrees on where it is. There is no natural starting line for longitude. Every meridian runs from pole to pole and every one is the same shape, so any one of them could be chosen as zero. For centuries different countries used their own capital or observatory as the starting line, which caused confusion for ships and map makers. In 1884 an international meeting in Washington agreed to use the meridian through the Royal Observatory at Greenwich, partly because most of the world's sea charts already used it. So Greenwich is zero by agreement, not by nature. That is also why the equator divides the Earth into two natural halves, north and south, while the east and west halves are simply a convention.

### subtopic_4 — Longitude and Giving a Position
**Assistant:** Why do we write latitude first?

**Tutor:** Mostly by agreement, so that everybody reads a position the same way. If some people wrote latitude first and others longitude first, a position such as twenty-eight, twenty-six could mean two places thousands of kilometres apart. Writing latitude first has become the international convention in atlases, navigation and examinations, and the letters N or S after the first number confirm it. There is also a historical reason. For centuries sailors could measure latitude easily by measuring the height of the sun at noon or of a star at night, but longitude was very hard to measure at sea until accurate clocks were invented in the eighteenth century. Latitude was known first and most reliably, so it came first. In an examination, writing the two the wrong way round, or leaving out the letters, usually loses the mark, so build the habit now: latitude, then longitude, each with its letter.

### subtopic_1 — Social Sciences in Grade 7
**Assistant:** What is the difference between describe and explain?

**Tutor:** Describe asks what something is like; explain asks why it is like that. If a question asks you to describe the location of Cape Town, you might say that it lies on the south-west coast of South Africa, at about thirty-four degrees south and eighteen degrees east, between Table Mountain and the Atlantic Ocean. That is a picture in words. If a question asks you to explain why Cape Town has a harbour, you need reasons: it lies on a sheltered bay on the sea route around Africa, so ships could stop for water and food. A helpful test is to look for the word because in your answer. Explanations almost always need it; descriptions often do not. Also look at the marks. A two-mark explain question usually expects one reason with a clear link, or two short reasons. Writing a long description when the question asks for an explanation is one of the commonest ways to lose marks.
