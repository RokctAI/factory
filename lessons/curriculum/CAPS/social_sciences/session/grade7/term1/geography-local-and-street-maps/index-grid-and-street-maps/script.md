# Part 1 — Expert

This session moves from the world map to the large-scale map of a town or city. The anchor is a familiar problem: a learner must find a friend's address in a suburb they have never visited. The governing idea is that a street map is organised so that information can be found quickly. The alphabetical index tells us where to look, and the grid of squares narrows the search to a small part of the page. The same organisation underlies the digital maps on phones, which add search, location and live information but still rely on the reader understanding direction, scale and symbols.

## Subtopic: What a Street Map Shows

A street map is a large-scale map of a town, city or suburb that shows its streets and roads in detail, usually with their names. Because it covers a small area on a sheet of paper, it can show features that a map of the whole country never could: individual streets, parks, schools, hospitals, police stations, places of worship, shopping centres, railway stations and taxi ranks.

Features are shown by symbols, explained in a key or legend. Main roads are usually drawn as thicker or coloured lines and minor streets as thinner white or grey lines. National roads carry the letter N and a number, such as the N1, which runs from Cape Town through Bloemfontein and Johannesburg to Beitbridge on the Zimbabwe border. Provincial roads carry the letter R. Railway lines are often drawn as a line with short cross-strokes. Parks and sports fields are usually green, and water is blue.

A street guide is a book of street maps covering a whole city, divided into numbered pages. Before maps on phones were common, street guides were the main way that drivers, delivery workers and visitors found their way around cities such as Johannesburg, Durban and Cape Town, and they are still used where mobile data is expensive or the signal is weak. A key map at the front shows which page covers which part of the city, and the edges of each page say which page continues the map to the north, south, east and west.

Like every map, a street map has a north arrow and a scale. Because a street map covers a small area in detail, it is a large-scale map, a distinction revised more fully later this term.

## Subtopic: The Index

A street map or street guide may show thousands of streets, so searching the map line by line would take a very long time. The index solves this problem. It is an alphabetical list of all the street names, usually printed at the back of a street guide or in a panel beside a single street map.

Each entry in the index gives the name of the street, sometimes the suburb in which it lies, and its position: in a street guide, the page number followed by a grid reference, and on a single map, just the grid reference. An index entry might read: Mandela Street, Central, 47 D3. This means turn to page forty-seven and look in grid square D3.

The suburb matters because many streets share names. A large city may have a Church Street, a Main Road or a Station Road in several suburbs, so the suburb tells the reader which one is meant. Some indexes also list important places separately, such as hospitals, schools, police stations, shopping centres and sports stadiums.

Using an index needs two simple skills. First, alphabetical order: names are sorted letter by letter, so Adderley comes before Albert, and Albert before Allen. Streets named after people are usually sorted by surname, and numbered streets such as Fourth Avenue are sorted as the word, not the number, though guides differ, so check the first page of the index. Second, reading the reference correctly, which the next section covers.

An index is an example of how geographers organise information so that it can be found again easily. The same idea is used in the index of an atlas, which gives the page and the latitude and longitude of each place, and in the search box of a map app.

The questions for this section are with you now: the purpose of an index, reading an index entry, and why the suburb matters.

## Subtopic: The Grid and Grid References

A street map is divided into squares by a grid of evenly spaced lines drawn across it. The columns, running from top to bottom, are labelled along the top and bottom edges, usually with letters: A, B, C, D and so on from left to right. The rows, running from side to side, are labelled down the left and right edges, usually with numbers: 1, 2, 3, 4 from top to bottom. Some maps reverse this, with numbers across and letters down, so always check the edges.

Each square is named by its column and row, giving an alphanumeric grid reference such as C4. To find square C4, go along to column C and down to row 4; the square where the column and row cross is C4. Every point inside that square has the same reference, so a grid reference narrows the search to one square, not to one point. It is then a quick job to scan that square for the street name.

A long street may run through several squares. The index usually gives the square in which the street starts or in which most of it lies.

Giving a grid reference for a feature on the map is the reverse process. Look up from the feature to the letter at the top or bottom edge, and across to the number at the side. Write the letter first and the number second, with no space, for example F2.

This kind of grid is different from latitude and longitude. A grid reference only works on the one map on which it is printed; on another map, square C4 is somewhere completely different. Latitude and longitude work on every map and globe in the world. The two systems share a principle, however: a position is found where a column and a row, or a meridian and a parallel, cross.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: reading the number before the letter. Two: reading the row from the bottom when the map numbers it from the top. Three: expecting a grid reference to give an exact point rather than a square.

The questions for this section are with you now: columns and rows, finding and giving grid references, and the difference between a grid reference and latitude and longitude.

## Subtopic: Street Maps and Map Apps

Today many people use map apps on smartphones, such as Google Maps, instead of printed street maps. A map app is a digital street map linked to the global positioning system, GPS, which uses signals from satellites to work out where the phone is. The app can show a blue dot for the user's position, search for any address or business by typing its name, calculate a route, estimate travel time, and give spoken directions turn by turn. Many apps show live traffic, public transport routes and satellite images, and allow the map to be zoomed in and out, which changes its scale.

Map apps have clear advantages: they are quick, they are updated often, they find places without needing an index, and they show where the user is. A search box does the job of the index, and zooming does the job of turning to a different page.

Printed maps still have advantages. They work without electricity, airtime or mobile data, which matters where data is expensive or the network is weak, for example in rural areas or mountains. A printed map shows a large area at once, which helps with understanding where places are in relation to one another and planning a whole route. Learners who rely only on spoken instructions often cannot say afterwards where they went, because they never formed a picture of the area in their minds.

Map apps also make mistakes. A new road or housing development may not yet appear, an address may be placed in the wrong spot, and the shortest route chosen by the app may go along a road that is unsafe, flooded or closed. Information about places, such as opening times, is often added by users and may be out of date.

The best approach is to use both: understand the printed map, its grid, key, north arrow and scale, and use the app as a fast tool, checking it against common sense and local knowledge.

The questions for this section are with you now: how map apps work, their advantages and limitations, and the advantages of printed maps.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: a map of the streets, finding a street in the index, the squares, and maps on phones.

## Subtopic: A Map of the Streets

A street map shows the streets of a town with their names, plus schools, hospitals, parks, shops and taxi ranks. The key tells you what each symbol means. Big roads are thick lines; small streets are thin lines.

A street guide is a whole book of street maps for a big city. The first page shows which page covers which area.

Roads with an N, like the N1, are national roads that go across the country. Roads with an R are provincial roads.

## Subtopic: Finding a Street

Looking for one street on a big map is like looking for one name in a crowd. So use the index: a list of every street name in alphabetical order, at the back of the street guide.

Find the name, and it tells you where to look: page forty-seven, square D3. If there are two streets with the same name, check the suburb.

Now the squares. The map has a grid of squares. Letters go across the top: A, B, C. Numbers go down the side: 1, 2, 3. To find D3, go across to D, then down to 3. Your street is somewhere in that square.

Letter first, number second.

## Subtopic: Maps on Your Phone

A map app like Google Maps is a street map on a phone. It uses satellites to show where you are as a blue dot. You type a name instead of using an index, and it can even tell you the way out loud.

Apps are fast. But they need data and battery, they can be out of date, and the quickest route is not always a safe route. A paper map works anywhere, and it helps you see the whole area at once.

So know how to read a paper map, and use the app as a helper, not a boss.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A street map shows streets, names and buildings, with a key, a north arrow and a scale. The index lists streets alphabetically with a page and a grid square. Grid squares are named letter first, number second, like C4. Map apps use GPS and a search box; printed maps work without data and show the big picture.
