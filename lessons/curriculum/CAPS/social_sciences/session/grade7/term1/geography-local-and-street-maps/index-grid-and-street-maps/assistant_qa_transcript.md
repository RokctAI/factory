### subtopic_2 — The Index
**Assistant:** How did people find streets before indexes and street guides existed?

**Tutor:** Mostly by asking, by memory and by landmarks. In small towns and villages people knew the area well, and directions were given from landmarks: past the church, left at the big tree, the third house after the river. Many places had no street names at all, and in many rural parts of South Africa this is still true, which is why directions there are often given by landmarks and why delivery and emergency services sometimes struggle to find homes. As cities grew in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, they became too large for anyone to know completely. Streets were named and houses numbered, partly so that post could be delivered. Printed city maps then needed a way to find the right street quickly, and the alphabetical index with a grid was the answer. It is a simple but powerful idea: it turns a slow search across a whole map into a quick look in one square, the same way that a book's index takes you straight to the right page.

### subtopic_3 — The Grid and Grid References
**Assistant:** Why do some maps put numbers across the top and letters down the side?

**Tutor:** Because there is no single world rule for street map grids, and different publishers have used different conventions. What matters is that the map itself makes the system clear by printing the labels along its edges. Most South African street guides and many school atlases use letters for columns across the top and numbers for rows down the side, but a learner may meet maps that do the opposite. So the habit to build is to look at the edges first and ask: which edge carries letters, which carries numbers, and where does each sequence start? Then write the reference in the form the map uses, usually letter then number. The same caution applies to row order. Most maps number rows from the top, but a few number them from the bottom, like a graph. In an examination, the map provided will always show the labels, and a learner who checks them before answering will not be caught out, while one who assumes the usual layout may find the wrong square.

### subtopic_4 — Street Maps and Map Apps
**Assistant:** Why might a map app send me down a dangerous road?

**Tutor:** Because the app is solving a mathematical problem, not making a human judgement. When it plans a route, it compares possible paths using information such as distance, speed limits and current traffic, and chooses the one with the shortest travel time or distance. Unless it has information that a road is unsafe, closed, flooded or poorly maintained, it has no reason to avoid it. A gravel shortcut through a farm, a road through an area with a high crime risk, or a low-water bridge after heavy rain can all look attractive to the calculation. Apps are improving, and users can report hazards, but the information is never complete, especially in rural areas and informal settlements. That is why local knowledge matters. A taxi driver or a resident knows which routes are safe at night and which flood in summer. The responsible way to use an app is to look at the whole route on the map before setting off, and to ask whether it makes sense.

### subtopic_1 — What a Street Map Shows
**Assistant:** What do the letters N, R and M on road signs mean?

**Tutor:** They show who is responsible for the road and how important it is in the road network. N roads are national roads, managed by the South African National Roads Agency, known as SANRAL. They are the long-distance routes that connect the major cities and border posts, such as the N1 from Cape Town to Beitbridge, the N2 along the south and east coast from Cape Town through Gqeberha, East London and Durban to Ermelo, and the N3 from Johannesburg to Durban. R roads are provincial or regional roads, managed by the provincial governments, linking towns within and between provinces. M roads are metropolitan roads, found within large cities and managed by the city, such as the M1 motorway in Johannesburg. On a street map these different classes are usually drawn in different colours or widths so that the reader can see at a glance which roads carry the most traffic and which lead out of the city.
