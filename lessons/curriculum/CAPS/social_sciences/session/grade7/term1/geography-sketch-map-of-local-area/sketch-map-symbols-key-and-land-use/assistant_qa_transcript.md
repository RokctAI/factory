### subtopic_3 — Observing Land Use and Vegetation
**Assistant:** Why does it matter whether trees are indigenous or alien?

**Tutor:** Because many alien trees cause real harm in South Africa, especially to our water. Trees such as eucalyptus, pine and wattle were brought from Australia, Europe and the Americas, mainly for timber and firewood. Many of them spread far beyond where they were planted, because the insects and diseases that controlled them at home are not here. They grow fast, crowd out indigenous plants, and drink far more water than the grassland and fynbos they replace, especially along rivers. Researchers estimate that invasive alien plants use a significant share of the country's runoff every year, in a dry country where water is precious. That is why the government's Working for Water programme, started in 1995, employs people to clear invasive aliens from catchments and river banks. Indigenous plants also support local birds, insects and animals. So when a learner notes on a sketch map that a river bank is lined with eucalyptus or wattle, they are recording something that matters for the water supply of the whole area.

### subtopic_1 — Symbols and the Key
**Assistant:** Why do maps use blue for water and green for plants?

**Tutor:** Partly because those colours resemble what we see, and partly because of long agreement among map makers. Water often looks blue, especially seen from above under a clear sky, and growing plants are green, so these colours are easy to understand without even reading the key. Over centuries, map makers in many countries settled on similar colour conventions, so that a person who can read one map can quickly read another. Government topographic maps in South Africa follow a fixed set of colours: blue for water features, green for cultivated land and orchards, brown for contour lines that show the shape of the land, and black or grey for buildings and railways. Conventions are not laws, and a sketch map may use other colours if the key explains them. But using familiar colours is a kindness to the reader. A sketch map that shows a river in red and roads in blue would be correct if the key said so, yet it would confuse almost everyone who looked at it quickly.

### subtopic_4 — Putting the Project Together
**Assistant:** What makes the difference between an average and an excellent project map?

**Tutor:** Usually three things: accuracy, completeness and clarity. Accuracy means that the map matches the real area: north points the right way, features are in the right order along each road, and distances are roughly in proportion. An excellent map is based on real observation, often paced and checked, rather than drawn from memory at home. Completeness means that everything the task asks for is there: title, frame, compass rose with eight points, scale, key with every symbol, land use and vegetation. Learners often lose marks not for poor drawing but for a missing element. Clarity means the map is easy to read: neat lines drawn with a ruler where appropriate, symbols that are distinct from one another, labels written horizontally, consistent colours, and no clutter. Finally, the written description should use the map, describing patterns with compass directions and land-use terms. Before handing in, check the map against the task's rubric line by line, as a marker would.
