# Part 1 — Expert

This session brings the term's map skills together in the project on a local sketch map. The anchor is the learner's own neighbourhood, a place they know well but have probably never mapped. The governing idea is that a map is a record of careful observation, organised through symbols, a key, direction and scale, so that someone who has never visited the area can understand what is there and where. Land use and vegetation are the two kinds of observation the project asks learners to record.

## Subtopic: Symbols and the Key

A map cannot show features as they really look, so it uses symbols, simple signs that stand for real features. Good symbols have three qualities: they are simple to draw, easy to tell apart, and, where possible, they look a little like what they represent.

There are three kinds of map symbol. Point symbols show features at one place, such as a school, clinic, church, mosque, shop, tree or water tap; they are often small pictures, letters or shapes. Line symbols show features that are long and narrow, such as roads, railways, rivers, paths, fences and power lines. Area symbols show features that cover a space, such as a sports field, a cemetery, a park, a field of crops or a patch of bush; they are shown with colour or a pattern of shading.

Colour helps. Many maps use agreed colours: blue for water, green for vegetation and parks, brown or orange for main roads or contours, black for buildings and railways, and red for important roads. Using these familiar colours makes a sketch map easier to read.

Every symbol used on the map must be explained in the key, also called the legend, normally in a box in a corner of the map. The key shows each symbol exactly as it appears on the map, next to its meaning. A symbol that is not in the key leaves the reader guessing, and a key symbol that differs from the one on the map causes confusion.

Labels add names. A sketch map of a neighbourhood might label the main roads, the school, the river and important buildings, but labelling everything creates clutter.

The questions for this section are with you now: point, line and area symbols, conventional colours, and the role of the key.

## Subtopic: Direction and Scale on a Sketch Map

The project requires the eight compass directions to be shown. The simplest way is a compass rose, a small diagram with N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W and NW marked at forty-five degree intervals, drawn in a corner of the map. North must point towards real north in the area, found with a compass, a phone compass or the sun, as practised earlier this term. The map is then drawn so that features to the north of the school are towards the top of the page.

Once the compass rose is in place, directions can be described from the map: the clinic lies north-east of the school, the river runs from north-west to south-east across the area.

A sketch map is not drawn to an exact scale, but a simple approximate scale makes it much more useful. To create one, the learner estimates or paces a known distance in the area, such as the length of the street outside the school, and decides how long to draw it on the page. If the street is about two hundred metres long and is drawn four centimetres long, then one centimetre on the map represents about fifty metres on the ground. This can be shown as a word scale, one centimetre represents fifty metres, or as a line scale, a line divided into one-centimetre sections labelled zero, fifty, one hundred, one hundred and fifty metres.

Keeping proportions roughly right matters more than precision. If one street is twice as long as another, it should look about twice as long. If the shop is halfway between the school and the clinic, it should be drawn about halfway.

The questions for this section are with you now: the compass rose, orienting the map to real north, and creating an approximate scale.

## Subtopic: Observing Land Use and Vegetation

Land use means the purpose for which people use a piece of land. Geographers group land uses into categories. Residential land is used for housing, from formal houses and flats to informal dwellings. Commercial land is used for buying and selling: shops, markets, spaza shops, offices and filling stations. Industrial land is used for making and repairing things: factories, workshops and warehouses. Recreational land is used for leisure: parks, sports fields and community halls. Institutional or public land is used for services: schools, clinics, police stations, libraries and places of worship. Transport land carries roads, railways and taxi ranks. Agricultural land is used for crops and livestock, from large farms to food gardens. Some land is open or vacant.

Vegetation means the plants that cover the land. A local survey can record the main kinds: grassland, shrubs and bushes, trees, which may be planted along streets or growing naturally, cultivated crops and gardens, and bare ground with little vegetation. It can also note whether the plants are indigenous, meaning they occur naturally in South Africa, such as acacias and aloes, or alien, introduced from elsewhere, such as eucalyptus or wattle, which use a great deal of water.

Fieldwork is observing and recording information in the real world. A simple method is to walk an agreed route with a base map, divide the area into blocks, and for each block record the main land use and the main vegetation, using a code or symbol. A table with columns for the location, land use, vegetation and comments keeps the record organised. Photographs or small field sketches help. Safety comes first: fieldwork should be done in a group, in daylight, with permission and adult supervision, and private property should not be entered.

The questions for this section are with you now: land-use categories, kinds of vegetation, and how to record observations safely.

## Subtopic: Putting the Project Together

The final sketch map combines everything. A good order of work is as follows. First, draw a frame and the title, for example A sketch map of the area around Lindelani Secondary School. Second, add the compass rose, oriented to real north. Third, draw the main roads and the river, if there is one, as line symbols, keeping proportions roughly right. Fourth, add the school and other key buildings as point symbols. Fifth, shade the land-use areas using colours or patterns, and add vegetation symbols. Sixth, complete the key with every symbol and colour. Seventh, add the scale. Eighth, check: does every symbol appear in the key? Is north correct? Are the main features labelled?

A project mark usually rewards accuracy, completeness and neatness. Use a pencil first and ink over it when the layout is correct. Use a ruler for frames and keys. Write labels horizontally where possible so the map does not have to be turned to read them. Keep the key and scale outside the main map area so they do not cover features.

The written part of the project asks the learner to describe the area using the map. A strong description gives the general pattern first, then details, using compass directions and land-use terms. For example: the area is mainly residential, with commercial land along Main Road to the north of the school. A sports field and a community hall lie to the east. The river runs from north-west to south-east and is lined with alien eucalyptus trees. The south of the area is open grassland.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: symbols on the map that are missing from the key. Two: a north arrow pointing up the page without checking real north. Three: features drawn at random sizes, so a spaza shop looks bigger than the school. Four: describing the map without directions, for example the shop is near the school, instead of the shop is two hundred metres north of the school.

The questions for this section are with you now: the order of work, presentation, and writing a description from the map.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: map symbols, direction and scale, looking at the land, and finishing the project.

## Subtopic: Little Pictures for Big Things

A map uses symbols, little signs that stand for real things.

Some are points, for one spot: a tap, a tree, a school. Some are lines, for long things: roads, rivers, fences. Some are areas, for spaces: a soccer field, a park, a mealie field.

Use the usual colours: blue for water, green for plants.

Every symbol must go in the key, the box that says what each symbol means. If it is on the map, it must be in the key.

Try this part's questions on symbols and the key.

## Subtopic: Which Way and How Big

Draw a compass rose with all eight points. Make sure north really points north: check with a compass, a phone or the sun.

Then make a rough scale. Pace a street. If it is about two hundred metres and you draw it four centimetres long, then one centimetre on your map is about fifty metres.

Keep things in proportion. A long street looks long. A small shop looks smaller than the school.

Try this part's questions on direction and scale.

## Subtopic: Be a Geographer Outside

Walk around your area with a friend and an adult, in daylight. Write down what each piece of land is used for. Houses: residential. Shops: commercial. Factories: industrial. Parks and fields: recreation. Schools and clinics: services. Farms and food gardens: agriculture.

Write down the plants too: grass, bushes, trees, crops, or bare ground.

Then put it all on your map with colours and symbols, finish the key and scale, and write a description: the big pattern first, then the details, with compass directions.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Use point, line and area symbols and explain them all in the key. Show eight compass points and a rough scale. Record land use and vegetation safely during fieldwork. Describe your map with the pattern first and directions throughout.

Take the final questions of the lesson: symbols, scale, land use and the project.
