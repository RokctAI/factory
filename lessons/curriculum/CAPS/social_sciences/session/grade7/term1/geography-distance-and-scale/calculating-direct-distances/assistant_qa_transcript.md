### subtopic_1 — Direct Distance: As the Crow Flies
**Assistant:** Do aeroplanes really fly in straight lines?

**Tutor:** Very nearly, but not exactly, and the reasons are interesting. Over long distances, the shortest path between two places on a round Earth is part of a great circle, a circle that divides the Earth into two equal halves. On a flat map, a great circle route often looks curved, which is why the route of a long flight drawn on an in-flight map appears to bend towards the pole. On a globe, a piece of string stretched tightly between two cities shows the true shortest path. Aircraft also leave the shortest path for practical reasons. They follow agreed air routes, called airways, so that air traffic controllers can keep them safely apart. They may detour around storms, mountains or closed airspace, and they choose routes that use favourable winds, since a strong tailwind high in the atmosphere can save a lot of fuel. For flights within South Africa, such as Johannesburg to Cape Town, the flight path is close to the direct distance of about twelve hundred and sixty kilometres.

### subtopic_3 — Measuring and Calculating Accurately
**Assistant:** Why multiply by the scale and not divide?

**Tutor:** Because the map is a shrunken version of the ground, and to get back to the real size you must enlarge, which means multiplying. The scale one centimetre represents twenty-five kilometres says that every single centimetre on the map stands for twenty-five kilometres of ground. If two towns are eight centimetres apart, then there are eight lots of twenty-five kilometres between them, and eight lots of twenty-five is eight times twenty-five. Division would be used for the reverse problem: if you know the ground distance and want to know how long to draw it on the map, you divide by the scale. Two hundred kilometres divided by twenty-five kilometres per centimetre gives eight centimetres. A quick sense check always helps. Ground distances are much bigger than map distances, so if your answer for the ground is smaller than the number of centimetres you measured, you have divided when you should have multiplied.

### subtopic_4 — Checking and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How accurate can a distance measured on a map really be?

**Tutor:** It depends mainly on the scale of the map, and it is worth knowing the limits so as not to claim too much. A good ruler can be read to about half a millimetre. On a large-scale street map where one centimetre represents a hundred metres, half a millimetre is only five metres on the ground, so distances can be measured very accurately. On a small-scale map of South Africa, where one centimetre might represent a hundred kilometres, the same half millimetre represents five kilometres, and the dot used for a city may itself be two or three millimetres wide, covering twenty or thirty kilometres. So a distance measured on an atlas map is accurate only to within ten kilometres or so. That is why answers from small-scale maps should be rounded, and why, when an examination memorandum gives an answer, it usually allows a range of answers, for example two hundred to two hundred and twenty kilometres. Showing the working allows the marker to see that the method was correct.
