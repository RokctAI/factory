### subtopic_1 — Indirect Distance
**Assistant:** Why do mountain roads zigzag instead of going straight up?

**Tutor:** Because a road that went straight up a steep mountainside would be far too steep for vehicles, and dangerous for everyone. The steepness of a road is called its gradient. Heavy trucks and buses need gentle gradients to climb without stalling and, more importantly, to descend without their brakes overheating. Most major roads are designed so that the gradient stays within safe limits. On a steep slope, the only way to keep the gradient gentle is to make the road longer, so it climbs gradually across the slope, turns sharply at a hairpin bend, and climbs back across the slope in the other direction. Each zigzag gains a little height over a long distance. The result is a road many times longer than the straight-line distance, which is why the indirect distance on mountain passes such as Sani Pass or the Swartberg Pass is so much greater than the direct distance. Engineers sometimes avoid the climb altogether with a tunnel, which is expensive to build but shortens the journey for every vehicle afterwards.

### subtopic_3 — The String Method With a Line Scale
**Assistant:** Couldn't I just use a map app to measure the route?

**Tutor:** Yes, and map apps are very good at it, but the string method is still worth knowing for three reasons. First, many maps are not on an app. Examinations, textbooks, historical maps, topographic maps and printed tourist maps all need to be measured by hand, and in an examination there is no phone. Second, an app only measures along the roads it knows. If you want the length of a river, a hiking trail, a fence, a proposed new road or a route on a map of a hundred years ago, the app cannot help, but a piece of string can. Third, doing it by hand builds understanding. Someone who has traced a winding road with string knows why the road is longer and has a feel for how scale works. When the app gives an answer that is wrong, perhaps because it has chosen a different route, that person is much more likely to notice. The best geographers use both: the app for speed, and their own skills for checking and for everything the app cannot do.

### subtopic_4 — Comparing Distances and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do footbridges change people's lives?

**Tutor:** Often dramatically, because for many rural South Africans the nearest school, clinic or shop lies across a river, and in summer, when rivers flood, the crossing becomes dangerous or impossible. Children have drowned trying to cross swollen rivers on their way to school, and learners miss days of school during the rainy season when the river cannot be crossed. Where there is no bridge, the alternative may be a detour of many kilometres to the nearest road bridge, which turns a short walk into a journey of hours. Building a footbridge shortens the indirect distance to something close to the direct distance and makes the crossing safe all year. Government departments, provincial governments and non-profit organisations have built many pedestrian bridges in rural areas of the Eastern Cape, KwaZulu-Natal and elsewhere for exactly this reason. Measuring direct and indirect distances on a map is one of the first steps planners take when deciding where such a bridge would help the most people.
