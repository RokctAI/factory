# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises scale, the idea that makes it possible to measure real distances from a map. The anchor is the comparison of two maps of the same size, one of a school and one of South Africa. The governing idea is that every map is a reduced picture of the Earth, and scale tells us exactly how much it has been reduced. Without scale, a map can show where things are but not how far apart they are. The calculation of distances, which follows in the next lessons, depends on reading scale correctly.

## Subtopic: What Scale Means

Every map is smaller than the area it represents. Scale is the relationship between a distance measured on the map and the real distance on the ground. If a map has a scale of one centimetre to one kilometre, then every centimetre measured on the map stands for one kilometre in reality, and two places four centimetres apart on the map are four kilometres apart on the ground.

The units matter. Map distances are usually measured in centimetres or millimetres with a ruler. Ground distances are usually given in metres or kilometres. Remember the conversions: one hundred centimetres make one metre, and one thousand metres make one kilometre, so one hundred thousand centimetres make one kilometre.

Scale must apply equally across the whole map for the map to be useful for measuring. On maps of small areas, such as a town, this is almost exactly true. On maps of very large areas, such as the whole world, the curvature of the Earth means the scale cannot be the same everywhere, which is one reason why distances measured on world maps are only approximate.

Scale also decides how much detail a map can show. If one centimetre represents ten metres, a house can be drawn to its real shape. If one centimetre represents a hundred kilometres, a whole city shrinks to a dot, and only the most important features can be shown.

## Subtopic: Word Scales

A word scale states the scale in words, for example one centimetre represents five kilometres, or one centimetre to five kilometres. It is the easiest kind of scale to understand, and it makes calculation simple: measure the map distance in centimetres and multiply by the ground distance that one centimetre represents.

Example one. On a map with the word scale one centimetre represents five kilometres, two towns are seven centimetres apart. The real distance is seven times five, which is thirty-five kilometres.

Example two. On a street map with the scale one centimetre represents two hundred metres, the school and the library are three and a half centimetres apart. The real distance is three and a half times two hundred, which is seven hundred metres.

Word scales can also be used in reverse, to work out how long to draw a distance. If a sketch map uses one centimetre represents fifty metres, a street two hundred and fifty metres long must be drawn two hundred and fifty divided by fifty, which is five centimetres long.

Word scales have one weakness. If a map is enlarged or reduced on a photocopier or displayed on a screen, the map changes size but the words stay the same, so the scale becomes wrong. A map printed with one centimetre represents five kilometres and then enlarged to double its size now really has one centimetre representing two and a half kilometres, but the words still say five.

## Subtopic: Line Scales

A line scale, also called a linear or bar scale, is a line drawn on the map and divided into equal sections, each labelled with the ground distance it represents. For example, a line divided into one-centimetre sections labelled zero, ten, twenty, thirty and forty kilometres shows that one centimetre represents ten kilometres.

Many line scales have a section to the left of zero divided into smaller parts. This extension, sometimes called the primary section, allows smaller distances to be read more accurately.

The great advantage of a line scale is that it stays correct when the map is enlarged or reduced, because the line changes size together with the map. That is why line scales are used on almost all printed maps, on photocopied worksheets and on map apps, where the scale bar adjusts automatically when the user zooms in or out.

A line scale can be used without any calculation. Mark the distance between two places on the edge of a strip of paper, then lay the paper along the line scale with one mark at zero, and read off the ground distance at the other mark. If the distance is longer than the line scale, mark off the full length of the scale, move the paper along, and add the sections together.

A line scale can also be turned into a word scale by measuring it with a ruler. If the section from zero to twenty kilometres measures four centimetres, then one centimetre represents twenty divided by four, which is five kilometres.

## Subtopic: Large-Scale and Small-Scale Maps

Maps are described as large-scale or small-scale according to how much they have reduced the real world.

A large-scale map shows a small area in a lot of detail. Things on it appear relatively large. Examples are a plan of a school, a street map of a suburb, or a map of a farm. On such maps one centimetre may represent ten, a hundred or a few hundred metres, so individual buildings, streets and sports fields can be shown.

A small-scale map shows a large area with little detail. Things on it appear small. Examples are a map of South Africa, of Africa, or of the world in an atlas. On such maps one centimetre may represent tens, hundreds or thousands of kilometres, so only major cities, main rivers, borders and mountain ranges can be shown.

Many learners find the names back to front, because the large-scale map shows the small area. A useful way to remember: on a large-scale map, things look large. A school building looks large on a school plan but is invisible on a map of Africa.

Choosing the right scale depends on the purpose. To walk to a friend's house, a large-scale street map is needed. To plan a journey from Johannesburg to Cape Town, a small-scale road map of South Africa shows the whole route, and the detail of every street would only get in the way.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: calling a world map large-scale because it covers a large area. Two: forgetting to convert centimetres to kilometres. Three: using a word scale on a photocopied, enlarged map. Four: reading the line scale from the end of the bar instead of from zero.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: shrinking the world, scales in words and lines, and big and small scale maps.

## Subtopic: Shrinking the World

A map is the world shrunk down to fit on paper. Scale tells you how much it was shrunk.

If the scale is one centimetre to one kilometre, then every centimetre on the map is one kilometre in real life. Measure six centimetres on the map and you know it is six kilometres on the ground.

Remember: a hundred centimetres make a metre, and a thousand metres make a kilometre.

The more you shrink, the less detail you can show. Shrink a lot, and a whole city becomes one dot.

## Subtopic: Scales in Words and Lines

A word scale says it in words: one centimetre represents five kilometres. To use it, measure in centimetres and multiply. Seven centimetres times five kilometres is thirty-five kilometres.

A line scale is a little ruler drawn on the map, with marks like zero, ten, twenty kilometres. Mark the distance on a strip of paper, put it on the line scale starting at zero, and read the answer.

If you photocopy the map bigger, the line scale grows too, so it stays right. The word scale does not grow, so it goes wrong.

## Subtopic: Big Scale, Small Scale

Here is the tricky part. A large-scale map shows a small area, like your school or your street, with lots of detail. Things look large on it.

A small-scale map shows a big area, like South Africa or the world, with little detail. Things look small on it.

The trick: on a large-scale map, things look large.

Use a large-scale map to find a friend's house. Use a small-scale map to plan a trip across the country.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Scale links map distance to real distance. A word scale says it in words: measure and multiply. A line scale is a little ruler on the map that stays right when copied bigger. Large-scale maps show small areas in detail; small-scale maps show big areas with little detail.
