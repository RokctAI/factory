# Part 1 — Expert

This session teaches the calculation of direct distances on maps. The anchor is the phrase as the crow flies, the straight-line distance a bird could fly between two places, ignoring roads, rivers and mountains. The governing idea is that a map drawn to scale lets us measure real distances, provided we measure carefully, convert units correctly and check our answer against a sensible estimate. Estimating first and measuring second is how geographers catch their own mistakes.

## Subtopic: Direct Distance: As the Crow Flies

A direct distance, also called a straight-line distance, is the shortest distance between two points, measured in a straight line across the map. It ignores the routes people actually travel. The phrase as the crow flies describes it well: a crow, flying straight from one place to another, does not follow roads.

Direct distances are useful for many purposes. They show how close places are to one another, for example how far a village is from the nearest hospital. They are used to plan the flight paths of aircraft, which travel almost in straight lines between airports. They are used to decide which areas lie within a certain distance of a school, clinic or water source. Emergency planners use them to see which towns lie within a certain radius of a flood, a fire or a chemical spill.

Direct distances are always shorter than, or at best equal to, the distance by road, because roads bend around hills, rivers, buildings and property boundaries. In flat country with straight roads, such as parts of the Free State, the road distance may be only a little longer than the direct distance. In mountainous areas, such as the Drakensberg or the passes of the Western Cape, the road distance may be much longer.

To calculate a direct distance, two things are needed: an accurate measurement of the map distance between the two points, and the scale. The points used should be the centres of the places marked, usually the dot or symbol that represents the town.

## Subtopic: Estimating With the Scale

Before measuring accurately, make a quick estimate. An estimate gives a rough idea of the answer, so that a mistake in the accurate calculation can be spotted at once.

To estimate with a line scale, look at the line scale, then look at the gap between the two places and judge how many lengths of the scale, or which part of it, would fit. If a line scale shows zero to one hundred kilometres and the gap between two towns looks about one and a half times as long as the bar, the distance is roughly one hundred and fifty kilometres.

To estimate with a word scale, judge the gap in centimetres by eye, perhaps using the width of a finger, which is roughly one and a half to two centimetres for most Grade 7 learners, or the width of a pencil, about seven millimetres. If the scale is one centimetre represents twenty kilometres and the gap looks about five centimetres, the estimate is roughly one hundred kilometres.

A good estimate is deliberately rounded. It should be said as about one hundred kilometres or between one hundred and one hundred and fifty kilometres, never as an exact number.

Estimation also uses general knowledge. Learners who know that Johannesburg to Pretoria is about fifty-five kilometres in a straight line, and Johannesburg to Durban about five hundred, have a sense of the size of the country that helps them judge whether a calculated answer is reasonable. If a calculation says that two neighbouring towns are three thousand kilometres apart, something has gone wrong, because South Africa is only about one thousand six hundred kilometres across from east to west at its widest.

## Subtopic: Measuring and Calculating Accurately

To calculate a direct distance accurately with a word scale, follow four steps and write each one down.

Step one: measure the map distance with a ruler, from the centre of one place to the centre of the other, in centimetres, to the nearest millimetre. Place the zero of the ruler exactly on the first point, not the end of the ruler, which may be worn or have a margin.

Step two: write down the scale, for example one centimetre represents twenty-five kilometres.

Step three: multiply the map distance by the ground distance represented by one centimetre.

Step four: write the answer with its unit, and compare it with the estimate.

Worked example one. On a map of the Free State with the scale one centimetre represents twenty-five kilometres, Bloemfontein and Kroonstad are measured eight point four centimetres apart. Eight point four times twenty-five is two hundred and ten, so the direct distance is about two hundred and ten kilometres. The estimate, about eight centimetres times twenty-five, two hundred, agrees.

Worked example two. On a street map with the scale one centimetre represents one hundred and fifty metres, the station and the stadium are six point two centimetres apart. Six point two times one hundred and fifty is nine hundred and thirty, so the distance is nine hundred and thirty metres, or zero point nine three kilometres.

Converting units may be needed. If a question asks for kilometres and the scale gives metres, divide by a thousand at the end. If the map distance was measured in millimetres, divide by ten to change it to centimetres before using a scale given in centimetres.

To use a line scale instead, mark the two points on the straight edge of a strip of paper, place the first mark on zero of the line scale, and read the distance at the second mark. If the distance is longer than the bar, mark the end of the bar on the paper, move the paper so that this mark is at zero, and continue, adding the parts.

## Subtopic: Checking and the Error Museum

A calculated distance should always be checked, because small mistakes in measuring or multiplying can produce answers that are badly wrong.

Check one: compare with the estimate. If the estimate was about two hundred kilometres and the calculation gives two thousand, a decimal point or a zero has gone astray.

Check two: check the units. A distance between towns in kilometres, a distance across a suburb in metres or kilometres, never a distance between cities in centimetres.

Check three: measure again, from the other place. A second measurement should give the same result within a millimetre or so.

Check four: if possible, check against another source, such as the distance between cities given in an atlas table or the straight-line measurement tool in a map app.

Some accuracy is always lost. Rulers can only be read to about half a millimetre, town symbols cover an area rather than a point, and on small-scale maps a millimetre may represent several kilometres. On a map with the scale one centimetre represents one hundred kilometres, an error of one millimetre in measuring means an error of ten kilometres on the ground. So answers from small-scale maps should be rounded sensibly, to the nearest five or ten kilometres.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: measuring from the edge of the ruler instead of the zero mark. Two: measuring to the edges of town names instead of the symbols. Three: dividing by the scale instead of multiplying. Four: forgetting to convert millimetres to centimetres. Five: giving an answer without units.

Layout for full marks in a calculation question: write the map distance with its unit, write the scale, show the multiplication, and give the answer with its unit. For example: map distance eight point four centimetres; scale one centimetre represents twenty-five kilometres; eight point four times twenty-five equals two hundred and ten; direct distance two hundred and ten kilometres.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: straight-line distance, a good guess, the exact answer, and checking it.

## Subtopic: As the Crow Flies

A direct distance is a straight line from one place to another, like a bird flying. It ignores roads.

Roads bend around hills, rivers and buildings, so the road is usually longer than the straight line. In flat places the difference is small. In mountains it can be big.

Aeroplanes fly almost in straight lines, so a flight distance is close to the direct distance.

## Subtopic: Guess, Then Measure

First guess. Look at the scale bar and ask how many bars would fit between the two places. One and a half bars of a hundred kilometres? About a hundred and fifty kilometres.

Then measure. Put the ruler's zero on the centre of the first dot. Read the centimetres to the second dot. Multiply by the scale.

If the scale is one centimetre to twenty-five kilometres and you measure eight centimetres, eight times twenty-five is two hundred kilometres.

## Subtopic: Does It Make Sense?

Always check your answer. Is it close to your guess? Does it have a unit, kilometres or metres? Would you get the same answer measuring again?

If your guess was two hundred kilometres and your answer is two thousand, you slipped a zero. South Africa is only about sixteen hundred kilometres across, so two towns next to each other cannot be two thousand kilometres apart.

Write it out for full marks: the map distance, the scale, the sum, and the answer with its unit.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A direct distance is the straight line between two places. Guess first with the scale. Measure from the centres with the ruler's zero. Multiply by the scale. Check the answer against the guess, give the unit, and show every step.
