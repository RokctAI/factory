# Part 1 — Expert

This session completes the term's work on distance by moving from straight lines to real routes. The anchor is the taxi journey or school walk that follows roads, turning at junctions and bending around obstacles. The governing idea is that the distance travelled along a route can be measured on a map by following every bend, and that the difference between indirect and direct distance tells us something real about the landscape and the road network. The string method, using a line scale, is the practical skill the curriculum asks for.

## Subtopic: Indirect Distance

An indirect distance is the distance measured along a route that is not straight, such as a road, a railway line, a footpath or a river. It is the distance a person actually travels, and it is the distance that matters for fuel costs, taxi fares, travel time and the length of a walk to school.

Indirect distances are always longer than the direct distance between the same two places, unless the route happens to be perfectly straight. How much longer depends on the landscape and the road network. In flat country with a grid of straight roads, the road distance may be only ten or twenty percent more than the direct distance. In mountainous country, where roads climb by zigzagging up passes, it can be double or more. A famous example is the road over Sani Pass between KwaZulu-Natal and Lesotho, which climbs more than a thousand metres through a long series of hairpin bends, although the top is only a few kilometres in a straight line from the bottom.

Rivers are also measured as indirect distances. Rivers that flow across flat land often form meanders, wide loops that make the river much longer than the valley it flows down. The Orange River, South Africa's longest, is about two thousand two hundred kilometres long, much longer than the direct distance from its source in the Lesotho highlands to its mouth at Alexander Bay.

Comparing the two distances is useful. Planners use the difference to decide where a new road or bridge would save the most travel. A ratio of road to direct distance close to one means an efficient route; a much larger ratio may show a mountain barrier, a river without a bridge, or a road network that does not connect communities well.

## Subtopic: Adding Straight Sections

The simplest method for measuring an indirect distance works well for routes made up of fairly straight sections, such as roads in a town with a grid of streets.

Step one: mark the start and end of the route on the map, and mark each place where the route changes direction, usually the junctions where it turns.

Step two: measure each straight section separately with a ruler, in centimetres, to the nearest millimetre. Write each measurement down in a list or table.

Step three: add the sections together to get the total map distance.

Step four: multiply the total map distance by the scale to get the ground distance.

Worked example. On a street map with the scale one centimetre represents one hundred metres, the route from the school to the library goes north along School Road for three point two centimetres, east along Main Road for four point five centimetres, and north again along Library Lane for one point three centimetres. The total map distance is three point two plus four point five plus one point three, which is nine centimetres. Nine times one hundred is nine hundred, so the route is nine hundred metres long. The direct distance between the school and the library on the same map measures six point five centimetres, six hundred and fifty metres, so the route is two hundred and fifty metres longer than the straight line.

It is better to add the map distances first and multiply once, rather than converting each section, because there are fewer chances to make an error. Recording the sections in a table helps when checking.

This method becomes inaccurate when a route curves, because a curve is longer than the straight line joining its ends. For curving roads and rivers, the paper strip or string method is needed.

## Subtopic: The String Method With a Line Scale

For routes that curve, such as winding roads, railways and rivers, string is the most practical tool. The method has five steps.

Step one: take a piece of thin, non-stretchy string, such as cotton thread, a little longer than the route looks. Thick or stretchy string, such as wool or elastic, gives inaccurate results.

Step two: hold one end of the string firmly on the starting point with a finger or a small piece of removable tape.

Step three: lay the string carefully along the route, following every bend and curve, pressing it down with the other hand as you go. Work slowly; the accuracy of the whole method depends on this step.

Step four: when the string reaches the end point, pinch it or mark it with a pen at that point.

Step five: straighten the string and measure it. With a line scale, lay the straightened string along the scale bar, starting at zero, and read the ground distance at the pinch mark. If the string is longer than the scale bar, mark off one full bar length on the string, move along and add. Alternatively, measure the straightened string with a ruler and multiply by a word scale.

Worked example. On a street map with a line scale showing zero to five hundred metres, a curving route along Riverside Drive is traced with string. Laid against the line scale, the string covers the bar once completely, five hundred metres, and then reaches the three hundred mark on the second placement. The route is five hundred plus three hundred, which is eight hundred metres.

A strip of paper can be used in a similar way. Lay the straight edge of the paper along the first section of the route, mark the start and the point where the route bends away; pivot the paper at that mark to follow the next section, mark again, and so on. The total length marked on the paper is then read against the line scale. This works for gentle curves, but string is better for tight bends.

## Subtopic: Comparing Distances and the Error Museum

After measuring an indirect distance, it is useful to compare it with the direct distance between the same two places. Subtracting gives the extra distance caused by the route. Dividing the indirect distance by the direct distance gives a simple ratio: a result of one point two means the route is twenty percent longer than the straight line.

Such comparisons explain real experiences. A learner whose home is only one kilometre from school as the crow flies may have to walk two kilometres because a river without a footbridge, a railway line or a fenced-off property lies in between. Communities often ask for footbridges and new crossings for exactly this reason, and building one can cut long walks dramatically. Similar reasoning lies behind major projects such as tunnels: the Huguenot Tunnel on the N1 near Paarl, opened in 1988, shortened the route over the Du Toitskloof Pass by about eleven kilometres and avoided its steep climb.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: using stretchy string, which gives a different length each time. Two: letting the string slip off the route at the bends, cutting corners and making the distance too short. Three: starting the reading at the end of the line scale rather than at zero. Four: forgetting to add the extra bar lengths when the string is longer than the scale. Five: confusing direct and indirect distance, giving the straight-line distance when the question asks for the distance by road.

Layout for full marks: state the method used, give the measured map distance or the number of bar lengths, show the calculation, and give the answer with its unit. If asked to compare, state both distances and the difference.

## Subtopic: The Real Way Round

The straight line is how a bird flies. The indirect distance is how you really travel: along roads that turn corners, up mountain passes that zigzag, along rivers that loop.

The real way round is always longer than the straight line. In flat towns with straight roads, only a little longer. In mountains, much longer.

This is the distance that decides how long a walk takes and how much a taxi costs.

## Subtopic: Corners and String

If the route has straight bits with corners, measure each straight bit with a ruler and add them up. Then multiply by the scale. Three centimetres plus four plus two is nine centimetres. If one centimetre is a hundred metres, that is nine hundred metres.

If the route curves, use string. Thin cotton, not stretchy wool. Hold one end on the start. Lay the string along every bend. Pinch it at the end. Straighten it, and lay it on the line scale from zero. Read the answer.

## Subtopic: Why the Way Round Matters

Compare the two distances. If school is one kilometre away in a straight line but two kilometres by the path, something is in the way: a river with no bridge, a railway, or a fence. A footbridge could cut the walk in half.

Big projects do the same. The Huguenot Tunnel near Paarl cut about eleven kilometres off the N1 by going through the mountain instead of over it.

Watch out: if the string slips off the bends, it cuts corners and your answer is too short.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Indirect distance is the distance along the real route. For straight sections, measure each one, add, then multiply by the scale. For curves, use thin string, follow every bend, and read it on the line scale from zero. Comparing indirect and direct distances shows barriers and where shortcuts could help.
