### subtopic_2 — Threats to the Manuscripts
**Assistant:** How do conservators repair a crumbling manuscript?

**Tutor:** Slowly and carefully, using methods that do as little harm as possible and can be undone later if better methods are found. First the manuscript is examined and photographed, so that its condition is recorded. Dust and sand are removed gently with soft brushes. Torn or weakened pages may be supported with very thin Japanese tissue paper and special glue that does not damage the original. Missing corners can be filled with matching paper. Insects are killed, often by freezing the manuscript or placing it in a sealed bag without oxygen, rather than with chemicals. The conservator does not rewrite faded words or try to make the manuscript look new; the aim is to stabilise it, not to change it. Finally, it is stored in a box made of acid-free card, sized to fit, in a room where temperature and humidity are kept as steady as possible. Many pages are also digitised, so that scholars can read them on a screen without handling the fragile original. This is exactly the work Malian conservators learned in Pretoria.

### subtopic_3 — South Africa and the Timbuktu Manuscripts Project
**Assistant:** Why would South Africa care about books in Mali?

**Tutor:** Because many South Africans see Timbuktu's history as part of a shared African heritage, and because the project served goals that mattered in South Africa after 1994. Under colonialism and apartheid, South African schools taught that Africa's history began with the arrival of Europeans, and the achievements of African societies were ignored or denied. After democracy, there was a strong wish to recover and celebrate African history. President Thabo Mbeki's idea of an African Renaissance called for African countries to rediscover their achievements and work together to solve their problems. Timbuktu's manuscripts were powerful evidence of Africa's intellectual traditions, and helping to save them was a way of showing that African countries could co-operate to protect their own heritage without depending on former colonial powers. The project also strengthened diplomatic relations between South Africa and Mali, and through the University of Cape Town it brought Timbuktu's history into South African research and teaching, which is partly why it appears in our curriculum today.

### subtopic_4 — Saving the Manuscripts and World Heritage
**Assistant:** Does being a World Heritage Site protect a place?

**Tutor:** It helps, but it does not guarantee protection, as Timbuktu shows. When a country nominates a site and UNESCO lists it, the country promises to protect and manage it according to international standards. In return, the site gains international recognition, which can attract tourism and funding, and it can receive expert advice and emergency assistance from the World Heritage Fund. When a site is threatened, UNESCO can place it on the List of World Heritage in Danger, which calls international attention to the problem and mobilises help, as happened with Timbuktu in 2012. But UNESCO has no army or police. If war breaks out, or a government lacks the money or will to protect a site, the status alone cannot stop destruction. What it can do is help afterwards, as with the rebuilding of Timbuktu's mausoleums, and support legal action: the 2016 conviction at the International Criminal Court showed that destroying heritage can be punished. In the end, heritage survives because people, like the families and librarians of Timbuktu, decide to protect it.
