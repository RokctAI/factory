# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines Timbuktu as a centre of scholarship, at its height in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. The anchor is Leo Africanus's remark that books brought more profit in Timbuktu than any other merchandise. The governing idea is that the wealth of trade, the support of rulers and the spread of Islamic literacy combined to create a community of scholars, teachers and students whose work covered both religious and practical knowledge, and whose manuscripts survive to this day.

## Subtopic: How Trade Built Learning

Learning flourished in Timbuktu for several connected reasons.

Wealth. Trade made Timbuktu rich, and wealthy merchants and families could afford to support scholars, buy books and pay for their children's education. Many scholarly families were also merchant families.

Islam and literacy. Islam placed great value on knowledge and on reading the Quran. Arabic literacy, brought by Islam, made it possible to read and copy books from across the Muslim world.

Royal support. Rulers of Mali, especially Mansa Musa, and later of Songhay, especially Askia Muhammad, who ruled from 1493 to 1528, honoured scholars, gave them gifts and positions as judges and advisers, and paid for mosques. Leo Africanus noted that the king paid scholars well.

Connections. Timbuktu's trade routes linked it to centres of learning in Fez, Marrakesh, Tunis, Cairo and Mecca. Scholars travelled along the caravan routes to study and teach, and books arrived with the caravans. Paper was imported from North Africa and, later, from Europe.

Peace and independence. For long periods, Timbuktu's scholars enjoyed a good deal of independence, and the city was governed partly by its leading scholarly families, whose judges, the qadis, settled disputes.

At its height, according to the Timbuktu chronicles, the city had about a hundred and fifty to a hundred and eighty Quranic schools, and scholars and students numbered in the thousands, out of a population of perhaps fifty thousand or more. Students came from across West Africa and beyond.

## Subtopic: Mosques, Teachers and Students

Timbuktu did not have a university with one campus, as modern universities do. Learning was organised around mosques and around individual teachers and their homes.

Three great mosques were centres of study: the Djinguereber, built by Mansa Musa; the Sidi Yahya mosque, built in the fifteenth century; and above all the Sankore mosque, in the north of the city, which was associated with many of the most learned families. Historians sometimes speak of the University of Sankore, but this means a community of scholars and students who taught and studied in and around the mosque, not a single institution with formal departments.

Education happened in stages. Young children attended Quranic schools, where they learned the Arabic alphabet, writing on wooden boards with ink, and memorised the Quran. Older students then studied with individual scholars, often in the scholar's home, reading particular books line by line, discussing them and copying them. When a teacher was satisfied that a student had mastered a book, he gave the student a certificate, called an ijaza, which authorised the student to teach that book in turn. A serious student collected certificates from several teachers and might study for ten years or more.

Teaching was based on texts, memory and discussion. Students learned grammar and rhetoric to understand Arabic deeply, then moved on to more advanced subjects. Many students later returned to their home towns across West Africa as teachers, judges and advisers, spreading learning far beyond Timbuktu.

Women's education was mostly informal and less recorded, but some women in scholarly families were educated, and women owned and inherited manuscripts.

## Subtopic: What Scholars Studied

The scholars of Timbuktu studied and wrote about a wide range of subjects. Religious subjects were at the centre, but practical and scientific subjects were important too, often because they served religious and everyday needs.

Islam: the Quran and its interpretation, the sayings and actions of the Prophet Muhammad, called hadith, theology, and Sufism, the mystical tradition of Islam.

Law and government: Islamic law was a major subject, because scholars served as judges and advisers. Manuscripts include legal opinions, called fatwas, on questions of trade, marriage, inheritance, slavery and conflict, and advice to rulers on how to govern justly. Some letters advise rulers to settle disputes peacefully and avoid bloodshed.

Mathematics: arithmetic was needed for trade, for dividing inheritances according to Islamic law, and for calculating the calendar. Manuscripts include works on arithmetic, algebra and geometry.

Astronomy: scholars observed the stars and planets to calculate the times of prayer, the direction of Mecca, the beginning of months in the lunar calendar and the seasons. Some manuscripts include star charts and discuss the movement of the planets and eclipses.

Medicine: manuscripts describe diseases and their treatment with plants and other remedies, as well as hygiene and diet.

History and geography: the Timbuktu chronicles, the Tarikh al-Sudan, written by Abd al-Rahman al-Sadi in about 1655, and the Tarikh al-Fattash, compiled by several authors, record the history of Mali, Songhay and Timbuktu. Other works describe regions, routes and peoples.

Language and literature: grammar, poetry and rhetoric in Arabic, and some writing of local languages in Arabic script.

The questions for this section are with you now: the subjects studied in Timbuktu and the practical reasons for many of them.

## Subtopic: Ahmad Baba and the Meaning of Timbuktu

The most famous scholar of Timbuktu was Ahmad Baba, born in 1556 into a leading scholarly family. He studied with many teachers, became a renowned jurist, an expert in Islamic law, and wrote more than forty works. He reportedly owned a library of about one thousand six hundred books, and said that his was one of the smaller libraries in his family.

In 1591, a Moroccan army armed with firearms crossed the Sahara and defeated the Songhay empire at the Battle of Tondibi. The Moroccans occupied Timbuktu. In 1593 Ahmad Baba and other scholars, accused of opposing the occupation, were arrested, their libraries were taken, and they were deported in chains to Marrakesh in Morocco. In Marrakesh, Ahmad Baba's learning was so respected that scholars came to study with him. He was eventually allowed to return to Timbuktu in 1607. One of his notable writings argued that Muslims of West Africa must not be enslaved, an important argument about the injustice of the slave trade in his time, though it did not reject slavery altogether, as was common among writers of the period.

The Moroccan invasion marked the beginning of Timbuktu's decline as a great centre of learning, although scholarship continued, and families kept and hid their manuscripts for centuries.

Today, a major library and research institute in Timbuktu is named after him: the Ahmed Baba Institute.

Timbuktu's history of scholarship matters because some European writers in the colonial period claimed that Africa had no history or written learning before Europeans arrived. Timbuktu's mosques, schools and hundreds of thousands of manuscripts prove that claim false.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: imagining Timbuktu's scholars studied only religion. Two: imagining a modern university campus at Sankore. Three: believing Africa had no written history before colonialism.

The questions for this section are with you now: Ahmad Baba's life and work, the Moroccan invasion of 1591, and why Timbuktu's scholarship matters today.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again, plain words, same facts: why Timbuktu became a city of books, how students learned, what they studied, and its most famous scholar.

## Subtopic: A City of Books

Trade made Timbuktu rich, and rich families paid for teachers and bought books. Islam brought reading and writing in Arabic. Kings like Mansa Musa and the Songhay ruler Askia Muhammad respected scholars and gave them good jobs.

Caravans brought books and paper from North Africa, and scholars travelled along the trade routes to Fez, Cairo and Mecca.

At its best, Timbuktu had more than a hundred and fifty schools and thousands of students.

## Subtopic: How Students Learned

Small children went to Quranic school and learned to read and write Arabic on wooden boards. Older students studied with a master, often at his home or at a mosque like Sankore, reading one book at a time.

When the master was happy, he gave the student a certificate called an ijaza, which meant: you may now teach this book. Students collected certificates from many teachers, then went home across West Africa to teach.

They studied Islam and law, but also maths for trade, astronomy for prayer times and calendars, medicine, history, geography and poetry.

## Subtopic: Ahmad Baba

Timbuktu's most famous scholar was Ahmad Baba, born in 1556. He was an expert in law, wrote more than forty books, and had a library of about sixteen hundred books.

In 1591 an army from Morocco, with guns, beat the Songhay empire and took Timbuktu. Ahmad Baba was taken in chains to Morocco. Even there, students came to learn from him. Years later he came home.

Today a library in Timbuktu is named after him. Timbuktu proves that Africa had great books and scholars long ago.
