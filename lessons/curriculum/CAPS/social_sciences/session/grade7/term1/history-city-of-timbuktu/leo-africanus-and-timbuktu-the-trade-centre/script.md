# Part 1 — Expert

This session studies Timbuktu as a trading city, using one of the most famous primary sources about it. The anchor is Leo Africanus's description of Timbuktu's rich king, crowded markets, many weavers and scholars, and its trade in salt, cloth, books and gold. The governing idea is that Timbuktu's wealth came from its location where the camel routes of the Sahara met the boat routes of the Niger River, and that eyewitness sources, though precious, must be read critically, asking who wrote them, when, why and for whom.

## Subtopic: Leo Africanus: The Man and His Book

Leo Africanus was the name by which Europeans knew al-Hasan ibn Muhammad al-Wazzan, a Muslim scholar and diplomat. He was born in Granada in Muslim Spain in about 1494, shortly after the Christian rulers of Spain conquered the city in 1492, and his family moved to Fez in Morocco, where he was educated.

As a young man he travelled widely for the sultan of Fez, on diplomatic and trading missions. In about 1510, travelling with his uncle on a mission, he crossed the Sahara and visited Timbuktu, then part of the Songhay empire, which had replaced Mali as the great power of the Niger bend. He may have visited a second time a few years later.

In about 1518, while returning from a journey in the eastern Mediterranean, he was captured by Christian pirates and given as a gift to Pope Leo the Tenth in Rome. He was baptised as a Christian and given the name Johannes Leo de Medici, from which comes the name Leo Africanus, Leo the African. In Rome he taught Arabic and wrote, in Italian, a long description of Africa based on his travels and his knowledge. It was finished in about 1526 and published in 1550 by the Venetian editor Giovanni Battista Ramusio, under the title Description of Africa. He later appears to have returned to North Africa.

For more than two hundred years, his book was the main source of information about Timbuktu and West Africa available to Europeans. Many later European ideas about the region came from it.

## Subtopic: What Leo Africanus Saw

Leo Africanus's description of Timbuktu is short but vivid. Paraphrasing his account: the houses of Timbuktu are huts of clay-covered wattle with thatched roofs, but there is a magnificent mosque of stone and lime and a royal palace, built by an architect from Granada. There are many shops of craftsmen, merchants and especially weavers of cotton cloth. Cloth from Europe is brought by merchants from North Africa. The women cover their faces, except for the enslaved women who sell food.

He describes the wealth of the inhabitants, especially the merchants, and the king, who has many plates and sceptres of gold, some weighing a great deal, and a court where those who approach him kneel. The king keeps a large army of cavalry and foot soldiers with bows.

He notes that there are many judges, scholars and priests, all paid well by the king, who honours learned men greatly, and that many handwritten books from North Africa are sold, bringing more profit than any other merchandise.

He describes the water supply, from wells, and the fact that the Niger floods the land near the city, which is reached by channels in the flood season. Corn, cattle, milk and butter are plentiful, but salt is very scarce, brought from Taghaza about eight hundred kilometres away; he says that a single load of salt could sell for a high price.

He also describes the caravan routes: the journey across the desert from Morocco, the dangers of thirst, the wells along the way, and the merchants who make the journey.

## Subtopic: Testing an Eyewitness Source

Description of Africa is a primary source, written by someone who was there. But a careful historian asks questions of every source before trusting it.

Who wrote it? A well-educated Muslim scholar and diplomat from North Africa, who knew Arabic, Islamic law and trade, and so understood much of what he saw. He was an outsider to Timbuktu, however, and he visited only briefly.

When was it written? About fifteen years after his visit, from memory and perhaps notes, while he was living in Rome. Memories fade and mix over time.

Why and for whom? He was writing in Italian for European readers in Rome, after his capture and conversion. Some historians suggest he may have shaped his account to suit or impress his European patrons, and to present North African and West African societies in particular ways. He sometimes expressed the attitudes of a North African city dweller towards peoples of the south, attitudes that are biased.

Is it supported by other evidence? Many details are confirmed by other sources: Timbuktu chronicles written by local scholars in the seventeenth century, Arabic documents, archaeology, and the surviving buildings and manuscripts. Some details are doubtful or exaggerated, such as the exact weight of the king's gold. The book was also changed by its editor Ramusio, who adjusted the text before publication, and a manuscript closer to Leo's original was found in Rome only in the twentieth century.

The result: Description of Africa is valuable evidence about Timbuktu around 1510, especially for its markets, scholars and trade, but it should be read critically and checked against other sources.

The questions for this section are with you now: the questions historians ask of a source, the strengths and limitations of Leo Africanus's account, and how other evidence helps.

## Subtopic: Timbuktu, Port of the Desert

Timbuktu was founded around the year 1100 as a seasonal camp of Tuareg herders, near a well, a little north of the Niger River. According to tradition, the name comes from the well of a woman named Buktu who looked after it. It grew rapidly because of its location.

Timbuktu lies on the southern edge of the Sahara, just north of the great bend of the Niger River, where the river flows furthest north into the desert. This made it a meeting place of two transport systems. From the north, camel caravans arrived across the desert from Morocco, Algeria and Libya. From the south and west, boats and canoes on the Niger connected Timbuktu to Djenné, the gold-producing regions and the farming lands of the river valley. Camels could not live long in the humid south, where disease-carrying flies killed them, and boats could not cross the desert. So goods had to change hands at Timbuktu, which became a port of the desert, a break-of-bulk point where loads were unpacked, traded and repacked.

From the north came salt from Taghaza and later Taoudenni, cloth from North Africa and Europe, copper, glass, horses, paper and books. From the south came gold, kola nuts, grain, ivory and enslaved people. Timbuktu's merchants grew rich as middlemen, buying and selling, storing goods, lending money and providing lodging. Djenné, upriver, played a similar role as Timbuktu's twin city in the south.

Under Mali from about the 1320s, and under Songhay from 1468, Timbuktu reached its height in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, with a population estimated at perhaps fifty thousand or more. Its decline began after a Moroccan army conquered Songhay in 1591, and later as Atlantic sea trade with Europeans drew trade away from the desert routes.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking Timbuktu is an imaginary place. Two: thinking it was a port on the sea; it was a port of the desert and river. Three: reading Leo Africanus as completely neutral.

The questions for this section are with you now: Timbuktu's founding, its location where camel and river routes met, the goods traded, its height and decline, and the error museum.

## Subtopic: A Traveller Writes a Book

Leo Africanus was born in Granada in Spain in about 1494 and grew up in Fez in Morocco. His real name was al-Hasan al-Wazzan. In about 1510 he crossed the Sahara and visited Timbuktu.

Later he was captured by pirates and taken to the Pope in Rome. There he wrote a book about Africa, called Description of Africa. It was published in 1550.

For two hundred years, it was the main way Europeans learned about Timbuktu.

## Subtopic: What He Saw, and Can We Trust It?

Leo Africanus saw a busy city: clay houses, a great mosque, many weavers and merchants, a rich king with gold, and lots of scholars. He said that handwritten books made more money than anything else sold there. Salt was scarce and expensive.

Can we trust him? He was really there, and he was well educated, which helps. But he wrote about fifteen years later, from memory, for European readers in Rome, and an editor changed his words. So historians check his story against other sources, like books written in Timbuktu and the buildings that survive.

## Subtopic: Where Camels Meet Boats

Timbuktu was rich because of where it is. It sits at the edge of the Sahara, just above the bend of the Niger River.

Camels came across the desert from the north, bringing salt, cloth, copper, horses and books. Boats came along the river from the south, bringing gold, kola nuts and grain. Camels could not go into the wet south, and boats could not cross the desert, so everything was swapped at Timbuktu. That is why people call it a port of the desert.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Leo Africanus visited Timbuktu in about 1510 and described it in Description of Africa, published in 1550. His book is valuable but must be checked. Timbuktu grew where desert camel routes met Niger River boat routes, trading salt and northern goods for gold. It was richest in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries.
