### subtopic_2 — Mosques, Teachers and Students
**Assistant:** Was Sankore a real university like the ones today?

**Tutor:** Not in the modern sense, and it is worth being precise. A modern university is a single institution with a campus, departments, enrolment, examinations and degrees awarded by the institution itself. Sankore had none of these. What existed in Timbuktu was a large and highly respected community of scholars, many of them linked to the Sankore mosque and its quarter, who taught students in mosques and in their own homes. Each scholar decided whom to teach and what, and the qualification was the ijaza, a personal certificate from teacher to student, not a degree from an institution. In some ways this system resembled the early universities of medieval Europe, such as Bologna and Paris, which also grew from groups of teachers and students, and the great centres of learning in Fez and Cairo. So when people speak of the University of Sankore, they are using a modern word to express something true: that Timbuktu was a place of advanced higher learning that attracted students from far away. The details simply worked differently.

### subtopic_3 — What Scholars Studied
**Assistant:** How could astronomy tell scholars the direction of Mecca?

**Tutor:** By combining observations of the sky with mathematics and geography. Muslims face the Kaaba in Mecca when they pray, so every mosque must be built facing the right direction, called the qibla. In Timbuktu, Mecca lies roughly to the east, but not exactly. To find the precise direction, scholars needed to know the latitude and longitude of both places, which could be worked out from astronomical observations, such as the height of the sun at noon or the position of particular stars, and from the travelling distances recorded by pilgrims and traders. They then used geometry developed over centuries by Muslim mathematicians to calculate the angle. In practice, simpler methods were also used, such as the direction of sunrise on particular days of the year. Astronomy was also needed to calculate the times of the five daily prayers, which depend on the position of the sun, and the start of each month of the Islamic lunar calendar, which depends on the first sighting of the new moon, including the month of fasting, Ramadan.

### subtopic_4 — Ahmad Baba and the Meaning of Timbuktu
**Assistant:** Why did some Europeans claim Africa had no history?

**Tutor:** Mainly because such claims helped to justify slavery and colonialism, and because many Europeans knew very little about Africa. During the slave trade and the colonial period of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, some European writers and philosophers argued that Africans had no history, no writing and no civilisation of their own. The German philosopher Hegel, for example, wrote in the 1830s that Africa was not a historical part of the world. Ideas like these were used to argue that Europeans had a right or a duty to rule Africans. They ignored the evidence: kingdoms such as Mali, Songhay, Great Zimbabwe and Mapungubwe, long-distance trade networks, oral historians such as the griots, and written traditions in places such as Timbuktu, Ethiopia and Egypt. Historians, including many African historians since the 1950s, have shown how wrong these claims were. The manuscripts of Timbuktu are some of the clearest evidence, which is one reason South Africa chose to help preserve them, as the next lesson shows.
