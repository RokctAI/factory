# Part 1 — Expert

This session brings the history of Timbuktu into the present. The anchor is the image of manuscripts packed into metal trunks and smuggled out of Timbuktu by donkey cart, boat and car in 2012 and 2013 to save them from destruction. The governing idea is that heritage, the inheritance of the past that a community values, must be actively protected, and that protecting it can involve families, governments, international organisations and other countries, including South Africa, which saw in Timbuktu a shared African heritage.

## Subtopic: The Timbuktu Manuscripts

The Timbuktu manuscripts are handwritten documents and books, mostly in Arabic, with some in African languages written in Arabic script, known as Ajami. Estimates of their number vary widely, but there are hundreds of thousands in Timbuktu and the surrounding region. Most date from the sixteenth to the early twentieth centuries, and some are older, from the thirteenth century.

They cover the subjects studied in Timbuktu's schools: the Quran and its interpretation, Islamic law, theology and Sufism, but also mathematics, astronomy, medicine, pharmacy, history, geography, grammar and poetry. Many are practical documents: letters between rulers, contracts, records of trade and prices, legal decisions, and the records of family property. Some are decorated with beautiful calligraphy and coloured inks, and bound in leather covers.

Most manuscripts are held not in public institutions but by families, who have kept them for generations as precious inheritances, sometimes hidden in chests, walls or underground to protect them during wars and colonial rule. There are dozens of private family libraries in Timbuktu, such as the Mamma Haidara library, as well as the public Ahmed Baba Institute, founded in 1970.

The manuscripts are extremely valuable as historical sources. They show West Africans writing their own history, debating law and ethics, and studying science and medicine. They record the trade, politics and daily life of the region from an African point of view, rather than through the eyes of foreign visitors. And they disprove the old colonial claim that Africa had no written history.

The questions for this section are with you now: what the manuscripts are, their subjects and age, who owns them, and why they matter.

## Subtopic: Threats to the Manuscripts

Manuscripts are fragile, and Timbuktu's have faced many dangers.

Environment. Paper and leather are damaged by heat, dust, damp during the rainy season, sand and insects such as termites, which eat paper. Many manuscripts were stored in hot, dusty rooms or buried, and have crumbled, faded or been eaten.

Poor storage and lack of resources. Families kept manuscripts in metal trunks, wooden boxes and sacks, often without the money or knowledge to protect them properly. Ink containing certain chemicals can eat into paper over time.

Loss and theft. Over the twentieth century, some manuscripts were sold to traders and collectors and taken out of the country, a loss for Mali's heritage. During colonial rule, some families hid their manuscripts from French officials, fearing they would be taken.

Conflict. In 2012, armed groups, including some linked to extremist movements, took control of northern Mali, including Timbuktu. Some of these groups disapproved of the Sufi traditions represented in the city's shrines and manuscripts. They destroyed several of the mausoleums, the tombs of Timbuktu's saints, and in January 2013, as they retreated, they set fire to manuscripts at the new Ahmed Baba Institute building. About four thousand manuscripts were destroyed or damaged.

Because the manuscripts are irreplaceable, every loss is permanent. That is why conservation, the careful protection and repair of objects, and digitisation, making digital photographs of every page, are so important.

The questions for this section are with you now: environmental threats, poor storage, loss and theft, the conflict of 2012 and 2013, and why conservation and digitisation matter.

## Subtopic: South Africa and the Timbuktu Manuscripts Project

In 2001, President Thabo Mbeki of South Africa visited Timbuktu and saw the manuscripts and the difficult conditions in which many were stored. Mbeki promoted the idea of an African Renaissance, a rebirth of African confidence and achievement, and Timbuktu's manuscripts were a powerful symbol of Africa's intellectual history. He offered South Africa's help.

The South Africa-Mali Project, known as the Timbuktu Manuscripts Project, became the first official cultural project of the New Partnership for Africa's Development, known as NEPAD. Its goals were to build a new, modern home for the Ahmed Baba Institute, with proper storage, conservation laboratories and reading rooms, and to train Malian staff in conservation.

South Africa raised money through a trust that included government and private donors. Malian conservators were trained at the National Archives of South Africa in Pretoria, learning to repair, clean and store manuscripts and to make protective boxes from acid-free materials. South African and Malian experts worked together, and researchers at the University of Cape Town founded the Tombouctou Manuscripts Project to study the manuscripts and the history of African scholarship.

The new Ahmed Baba Institute building in Timbuktu, designed in the local mud-coloured style, was handed over in 2009.

The project matters for several reasons. It was an example of African countries co-operating to protect African heritage, rather than relying on former colonial powers. It improved the care of thousands of manuscripts. And it helped many South Africans learn about a part of African history that had been ignored in their own schooling during apartheid.

The questions for this section are with you now: Thabo Mbeki's visit, the African Renaissance, NEPAD, the training of conservators, the new building and the significance of the project.

## Subtopic: Saving the Manuscripts and World Heritage

When armed groups occupied Timbuktu in 2012, librarians and families feared the manuscripts would be destroyed. Led by people such as Abdel Kader Haidara, a librarian from a manuscript-owning family, volunteers secretly packed manuscripts into metal trunks and moved them, over many months, by donkey cart, car, river boat and road, past checkpoints, to safety in the capital, Bamako, more than eight hundred kilometres away. Estimates suggest that the great majority of the manuscripts in Timbuktu's libraries, hundreds of thousands, were saved this way, though only a part of those in the new Ahmed Baba building could be rescued before the fire. Some have since been returning to Timbuktu as security allows.

In 1988 UNESCO, the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization, declared Timbuktu a World Heritage Site. A World Heritage Site is a place recognised as having outstanding universal value, meaning that it is important to all of humanity, not only to one country. Timbuktu was listed for its three great mud-brick mosques, the Djinguereber, Sankore and Sidi Yahya, and its sixteen cemeteries and mausoleums, which represent its golden age as a centre of Islamic learning and trade in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, and for the spread of Islam in Africa.

In 2012, because of the conflict, Timbuktu was placed on the List of World Heritage in Danger. Local masons and UNESCO later rebuilt the destroyed mausoleums using traditional techniques, completing the work in 2015. In 2016 the International Criminal Court in The Hague convicted one of the men responsible for the destruction, the first time that destroying cultural heritage was tried as a war crime at that court.

South Africa has its own World Heritage Sites, including Robben Island, the Cradle of Humankind and Mapungubwe.

The error museum, three exhibits. One: thinking that all the manuscripts were destroyed in 2013. Two: thinking World Heritage status protects a site automatically. Three: thinking heritage belongs only to the past; it is kept alive by people today.

The questions for this section are with you now: the rescue of the manuscripts, the meaning of World Heritage status, why Timbuktu was listed, the danger list and rebuilding, and the error museum.

South Africa raised money to build a new library for the Ahmed Baba Institute, opened in 2009, and trained Malian experts in Pretoria to repair and store manuscripts properly. ## Subtopic: The Great Rescue

In 2012 armed groups took over Timbuktu. They destroyed holy tombs and, in 2013, burned thousands of manuscripts.

But librarians and families had a plan. Secretly, they packed manuscripts into metal trunks and moved them on donkey carts, boats and cars to Bamako, the capital. Most of the manuscripts were saved.

Timbuktu has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1988, for its three great mud mosques and its tombs. The damaged tombs were rebuilt by local builders.
