### subtopic_1 — Leo Africanus: The Man and His Book
**Assistant:** Why did Leo Africanus become a Christian in Rome?

**Tutor:** We do not know exactly what he believed in his heart, and historians have debated this for a long time. What we know is that he was captured by Christian pirates in about 1518 and presented as a captive to Pope Leo the Tenth. A Muslim scholar who could speak Arabic and knew Africa was valuable to the Pope's court, and in 1520 he was baptised, with the Pope himself as his godfather, and given the Pope's family names. For a captive far from home, accepting baptism brought freedom, protection, a salary and a respected position as a teacher and writer. Some historians think his conversion was sincere; others think it was a practical choice made to survive, and point to evidence that he later returned to North Africa and to Islam, though even that is uncertain. A modern historian, Natalie Zemon Davis, wrote a book about him called Trickster Travels, exploring how he moved between two worlds. His life reminds us that the authors of historical sources were real people in difficult situations, and that their circumstances shaped what they wrote.

### subtopic_3 — Testing an Eyewitness Source
**Assistant:** If Leo Africanus might be biased, is his book still useful?

**Tutor:** Yes, very useful, and that is true of almost every historical source. No source is perfectly neutral, because every one was made by a person with a point of view, for a purpose and an audience. The skill is not to throw away biased sources but to use them carefully. Leo Africanus gives information we could not get elsewhere: an educated eyewitness account of Timbuktu in about 1510, during the Songhay period, describing its buildings, markets, crafts, king, scholars and the importance of the book trade. Where his account agrees with independent sources, such as the chronicles written by Timbuktu's own scholars in the seventeenth century, Arabic documents, the surviving mosques and the manuscripts, we can be more confident. Where he makes sweeping judgements about African peoples, we read them as evidence of his own attitudes and those of his readers. A historian treats a source like a witness in court: listening carefully, noticing what the witness could and could not know, and comparing the testimony with other witnesses.

### subtopic_4 — Timbuktu, Port of the Desert
**Assistant:** Why did people in Europe think Timbuktu wasn't real?

**Tutor:** Because for centuries almost no Europeans had been there, and the stories they heard made it sound like a legend. After Mansa Musa's pilgrimage and Leo Africanus's book, Europeans imagined Timbuktu as a city of gold far beyond the desert. From the late eighteenth century, European explorers tried to reach it, and several died on the way. The first European known to reach Timbuktu and return was the Frenchman René Caillié in 1828, travelling in disguise. By then the city had declined from its peak, and he was disappointed to find mud houses rather than golden palaces, which added to the mystery. In English, Timbuktu became a word for anywhere impossibly remote, as in the expression from here to Timbuktu, and some people came to think it was imaginary. This says much more about European ignorance of Africa than about Timbuktu, which for West Africans and North Africans had always been a well-known, real city on a busy trade route, and still is today.
