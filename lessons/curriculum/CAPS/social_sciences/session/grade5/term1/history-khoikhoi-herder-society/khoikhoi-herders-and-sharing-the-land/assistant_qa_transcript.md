### subtopic_1 — The Pastoral Way of Life
**Assistant:** Why are fat-tailed sheep so good for dry areas?

**Tutor:** Fat-tailed sheep are well suited to dry places like the Karoo, Namaqualand and the Richtersveld. When there is plenty of grass after the rains, they eat well and store extra fat in their broad, heavy tails, a bit like a camel stores fat in its hump. When the dry season comes and food is scarce, their bodies can use that stored fat for energy, so they can survive until the next rains. Their hair is also different from the woolly sheep that came later from Europe. Many have short hair, which suits hot weather. Herders valued the tail fat too, for cooking and for rubbing on skin and leather to keep them soft. Breeds of sheep with fat tails or fat rumps are still farmed in South Africa today, for example the Persian-type black-headed sheep and the Damara.

### subtopic_2 — Moving with the Herds
**Assistant:** What happened to the Khoikhoi after the Dutch arrived at the Cape?

**Tutor:** When the Dutch set up a station at the Cape in 1652, they at first traded with the Khoikhoi for cattle and sheep, giving them copper, beads, tobacco and alcohol. But soon the Dutch wanted more land for their own farms, and settlers began to take over the Khoikhoi grazing lands. This led to wars between the Khoikhoi and the Dutch. Then, in 1713, a smallpox epidemic, a disease brought by ships, killed a very large number of Khoikhoi people, because they had no protection against it. Many Khoikhoi lost their land and animals and were forced to work on settler farms, often under harsh conditions. Some moved north and kept their way of life for longer. Their descendants include the Nama, the Griqua and many coloured South Africans. Today, many people are reviving Khoikhoi languages, history and identity.

### subtopic_3 — Sharing the Landscape
**Assistant:** Is it fair to call both groups Khoisan?

**Tutor:** The word Khoisan was made up by scholars in the twentieth century, by joining Khoikhoi and San. It is useful because the two groups were closely linked: they lived in the same areas, traded, intermarried, had related ways of life and spoke languages with clicks. Sometimes the same family could move from hunting to herding and back again. But some people feel that the word hides important differences between the groups. Herders and hunter-gatherers had different ways of getting food, different kinds of leaders and different ideas about owning things. Some descendants also prefer to be called by their own group names, such as Nama, Griqua or Khomani. So it is fair to use the word Khoisan when talking about both groups together, as long as we remember that they were not exactly the same, and respect the names people choose for themselves.
