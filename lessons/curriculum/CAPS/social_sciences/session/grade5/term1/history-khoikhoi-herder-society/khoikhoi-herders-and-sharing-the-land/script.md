# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers Khoikhoi herder society in the Later Stone Age: their pastoral way of life, and how the San and the Khoikhoi shared the same landscape. The anchor is a Grade 5 class from Port Nolloth on a trip to the Richtersveld, a dry, mountainous area along the Gariep River, in the far north-west of the Northern Cape. The Richtersveld is a World Heritage Site, partly because Nama people, who are descendants of the Khoikhoi, still live here in a way that is many hundreds of years old. The class visits a stock post, where a Nama family is caring for a herd of goats and fat-tailed sheep. Next to the animal pen stands a round, dome-shaped house made of bent poles covered with reed mats. Inside, it is cool and shady. An elder explains that the family will take the house down, roll up the mats and move with the animals to fresh grazing when the season changes. The big idea is that the Khoikhoi lived by herding animals, moving with the seasons to find grass and water, and that for hundreds of years they shared the land with San hunter-gatherers, sometimes in friendship and sometimes in conflict. The Khoikhoi way of life and their relationship with the San are examinable.

## Subtopic: The Pastoral Way of Life

A pastoral way of life means living mainly by herding animals. People who live this way are called pastoralists, or herders.

About two thousand years ago, herders with sheep began to appear in southern Africa. Later, cattle arrived too. Historians are still discussing exactly how herding began here. Some think herders moved in from further north, bringing their animals with them. Others think some local people got animals through trade and became herders. It may have been a mix of both. These herders were the Khoikhoi.

The Khoikhoi kept fat-tailed sheep, which store fat in their thick tails to survive dry times, and long-horned cattle. Later, many also kept goats. Their animals gave them milk, which was their main food, drunk fresh or sour. Animals were not killed often, because they were too valuable. Meat was eaten mainly at special occasions, such as weddings, or when an animal was old or sick. Animals also gave skins for clothes and blankets, and fat for cooking and for rubbing on the skin. Some oxen were trained to carry loads and even to be ridden.

The Khoikhoi still hunted and gathered too. They collected wild plants, honey and, near the sea, shellfish, and they hunted wild animals. They also made clay pots, which were useful for storing milk and cooking.

The Khoikhoi lived mostly in the western and southern parts of southern Africa, where there was grass for their animals: in the Western Cape, the Northern Cape, southern Namibia and parts of the Eastern Cape.

The questions for this section are with you now: the pastoral way of life, and the animals the Khoikhoi kept.

## Subtopic: Moving with the Herds

Like the San, the Khoikhoi moved with the seasons, but for a different reason. The San followed wild plants and animals. The Khoikhoi followed grass and water for their herds. When the grass in one area had been eaten, or the water had dried up, they moved on to fresh grazing. Often, they moved between the same places year after year.

Their houses were made to move. A Khoikhoi house was a dome of bent wooden poles, tied together at the top, covered with mats woven from reeds or rushes. In summer, the mats let air through and kept the house cool. In winter, the mats swelled in the rain and kept the water out. When it was time to move, the women took the house apart, rolled up the mats, and loaded them, with the poles, pots and other belongings, onto the backs of pack oxen. The house in the Richtersveld is built in the same way.

At a camp, the houses were usually built in a large circle. At night, the animals were brought into the middle of the circle, or into a kraal, where they were safe from lions, leopards, jackals and thieves.

Khoikhoi groups were bigger than San bands. Several families who were related formed a clan, and several clans could form a larger group, with a chief or leader. A family's wealth was measured in animals. Some families had large herds and others had few, so, unlike the San, Khoikhoi society was not completely equal. When the Dutch arrived at the Cape in 1652, groups such as the Goringhaiqua and the Cochoqua were living and grazing their herds there.

The questions for this section are with you now: Khoikhoi homes, camps and how their society was organised.

## Subtopic: Sharing the Landscape

For hundreds of years, San hunter-gatherers and Khoikhoi herders lived in the same landscape. Their relationship was not simple. Sometimes they helped each other, and sometimes they clashed.

They traded. San hunters could offer meat, skins, ostrich eggshell beads and other goods. Khoikhoi herders could offer milk, and sometimes animals or pots. Some San people worked for Khoikhoi herders, looking after their animals or hunting for them, and received milk, food or animals in return. People from the two groups also married each other, and their languages had many things in common, including click sounds.

The line between them was not always sharp. Some San got animals and became herders. Some Khoikhoi who lost their animals in a drought, to disease or in a raid went back to hunting and gathering to survive, until they could build up a herd again. That is one reason why historians often use the word Khoisan for both groups.

But there was also conflict. Herds of sheep and cattle ate the grass and drank the water that wild animals needed, so there was less game for San hunters. Herders sometimes took over the best waterholes. San hunters sometimes killed sheep or cattle, which the herders saw as theft. These clashes could lead to fighting.

San rock art shows us part of this story. In some areas, there are paintings of fat-tailed sheep and cattle, showing that San artists saw and knew the herders and their animals.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the Khoikhoi ate meat every day; milk was their main food, and animals were rarely killed. Two: thinking the Khoikhoi stayed in one place; they moved with their herds to find grass and water. Three: thinking the San and Khoikhoi were always enemies; they traded, worked together and married, though they also clashed. Four: thinking the Khoikhoi have disappeared; the Nama and many other South Africans are their descendants.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the San and Khoikhoi shared the landscape.
