# Part 1 — Expert

This session opens Grade 5 Geography. It explains what Geography is, what topics are studied this year and how learners' work is assessed, revises the four main compass directions from Grade 4, and introduces this term's topic, map skills with a focus on Africa. The anchor is the first Geography lesson of the year in a Grade 5 classroom in Upington, in the Northern Cape, a town on the banks of the Gariep, or Orange, River. On the wall is a big new map of Africa that the teacher has just unrolled. Next to it, on the teacher's desk, is a globe. Through the window, the learners can see the green vineyards along the river, with the dry, red Kalahari sand beyond them. The big idea is that Geography helps us to understand places, people and the environment, and that maps are one of the most important tools a geographer uses. The basic compass directions and the difference between a map and a globe are examinable.

## Subtopic: Geography and the Grade 5 Year

Geography is the study of the Earth: its places, the people who live there, and the environment around them. A geographer asks three big questions about any place. Where is it? What is it like? And why is it like that? For example, why are there green vineyards next to the river in Upington, while the land a short drive away is dry and sandy? A geographer would answer that the farmers use water from the Gariep River to grow their grapes.

In Grade 5 Geography there are four topics, one for each term. In Term 1, we learn map skills, with a focus on Africa. In Term 2, we learn about the physical features of South Africa, such as mountains, plateaus and rivers. In Term 3, we learn about weather, climate and natural vegetation in South Africa. In Term 4, we learn about minerals and mining in South Africa. Geography is one half of Social Sciences. The other half is History, which has its own topics each term.

Your work is assessed in two ways. Informal assessment happens almost every day. It includes class work, homework, short activities and quick questions. It does not count for your report, but it helps you and your teacher see what you understand and what you still need to practise. Formal assessment counts for your report. In Geography this year it includes tests, a controlled test at the end of the term, and a project in which you observe and record the weather. Each formal task has marks, and it may include questions about maps, pictures and short pieces of writing, which are called sources, and a short paragraph that you write yourself.

The questions for this section are with you now: what Geography is, the four Grade 5 topics and the two kinds of assessment.

## Subtopic: Revising Compass Directions

In Grade 4, you learnt the four main compass directions: north, east, south and west. They are also called the cardinal points, and we write them with capital letters: N, E, S and W.

Going around clockwise, the way the hands of a clock move, the order is north, east, south, west. A sentence to help you remember is Never Eat Soggy Weet-Bix. North and south are opposites, and east and west are opposites.

Compass directions do not change when you turn around, but left and right do. That is why mapmakers, pilots and sailors use compass directions.

The sun gives a rough guide. It rises in the east in the morning and sets in the west in the evening. In Upington, the learners see the sun come up on one side of the town in the morning, and go down on the other side, in the west, in the evening. A compass gives an exact guide, because its magnetic needle always swings round to point north.

On most maps, north is at the top. A north arrow, an arrow with the letter N at its tip, shows which way north is. If the north arrow points up, then east is on the right of the map, south is at the bottom and west is on the left. On the wall map in Upington, Egypt is at the top of Africa, in the north, and South Africa is at the bottom, in the south.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four compass directions, their order, the sun and the north arrow.

## Subtopic: Maps, Globes and Africa

A globe is a model of the Earth. It is round, like a ball, because the Earth is round. A globe shows the true shapes and sizes of the land and the sea, but you can only see half of it at a time, and it is too small to show much detail.

A map is a flat drawing of a place, seen from above. A map can show the whole world, one continent, one country or even one street. Maps are easy to carry, can be folded or printed in a book, and can show much more detail than a globe. But when the round Earth is drawn on a flat page, some shapes and sizes are stretched. On many world maps, places near the top and bottom look bigger than they really are.

Map skills are the skills we need to read and use maps. They include finding directions, using the key to understand symbols, finding places and describing where places are. This term, we use these skills on maps of the world and of Africa.

We start with Africa because it is our own continent. South Africa is at the southern tip of Africa. Africa is a huge continent, the second biggest in the world, with more than fifty countries, many long rivers, high mountains and big deserts. Learning the map of Africa helps us to understand where we live, and how we are connected to our neighbours.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking Geography is only about maps; it is the study of places, people and the environment, and maps are one of its tools. Two: thinking informal assessment does not matter; it shows you what to practise before the formal tasks. Three: mixing up east and west; go clockwise, Never Eat Soggy Weet-Bix. Four: thinking a map is always better than a globe; a globe shows true shapes and sizes, while a map is flat and easy to carry.

The questions for this section are with you now: maps, globes, map skills and why we start with Africa.
