### subtopic_1 — What a Country Is
**Assistant:** Are there any places in Africa that people argue about being a country?

**Tutor:** Yes, there are a few. The best-known example is Western Sahara, a large, dry area on the north-west coast of Africa, south of Morocco. Some people who live there want it to be an independent country, and some African countries, including South Africa, support them. Morocco says the area belongs to Morocco, and controls most of it. The United Nations has been trying to help settle the disagreement for many years. This is why you may see different numbers of African countries in different books. The number fifty-four counts the countries that are members of the United Nations. The African Union, which is the organisation of African countries, counts fifty-five members, because it includes Western Sahara. It is a good example of how maps and borders can be about people and politics, not only about land.

### subtopic_2 — Capital Cities
**Assistant:** Why is Pretoria also called Tshwane?

**Tutor:** Pretoria is the name of the city itself, and it was named after Andries Pretorius, a Voortrekker leader, in the eighteen hundreds. In 2000, the city and the towns around it were joined into one big municipality, the area that one council governs. That municipality was named the City of Tshwane. The name Tshwane is linked to a local chief from long ago, and to the Apies River, which some people say was called Tshwane. Since then, there has been a lot of discussion about whether the city itself should be renamed. Many people use both names. On most maps, you will see Pretoria for the city and Tshwane for the municipality. Name changes like this are part of how South Africans think about their history and identity, and people have different views about them.

### subtopic_3 — Borders on a Map
**Assistant:** What happens at a border post like Beitbridge?

**Tutor:** A border post is the official place where people and goods may cross from one country into another. When travellers arrive, they must show their passports, and sometimes visas, which are permissions to visit. Officials stamp the passports with the date. This keeps a record of who enters and leaves the country. Customs officials check goods, especially on trucks, to make sure that nothing dangerous or illegal is brought in, and that the right taxes are paid on things that are bought and sold. Health officials may check for diseases in people, plants or animals. At a very busy post like Beitbridge, thousands of people and hundreds of trucks may cross in a day, so there are often long queues, especially over holidays. Countries work together to try to make crossing faster while still keeping it safe.
