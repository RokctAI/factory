# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers four big cities of Africa: Cairo, Lagos, Johannesburg and Nairobi. The anchor is a Grade 5 school tour of OR Tambo International Airport, near Johannesburg, one of the busiest airports in Africa. The class stands under the big departures board, watching the names of cities flip and flash. One flight is leaving for Cairo, another for Lagos, and another for Nairobi. Through the tall windows, the learners watch an aeroplane take off into the sky over the Highveld. The guide tells them that every day, thousands of people fly between Africa's big cities to work, trade, study and visit family. The big idea is that Africa's big cities grew where there were good reasons for people to gather, such as a river, a port, a government or gold, and that each city has its own story. The positions of these cities on a map of Africa, and why they grew, are examinable.

## Subtopic: What Makes a Big City

A city is a very large settlement where many people live and work close together. Cities have many buildings, roads, shops, factories, offices, schools, hospitals and services such as water, electricity and transport.

Africa has many big cities, and they are growing quickly. Every year, many people move from rural areas to cities, hoping to find jobs, schools and better services. This movement of people from the countryside to the cities is called urbanisation.

Cities usually grow in places where there is a good reason for people to come together. Some cities grow next to rivers, which give water for drinking and farming, and a way to transport goods. Some grow on the coast, around a port where ships can load and unload goods. Some grow where valuable minerals, such as gold, are found. And some grow because the government of a country is there.

Big cities bring many good things. There are more jobs, more schools and universities, more hospitals, and more places for sport, music and entertainment. But big cities also have problems. There can be heavy traffic, crowded housing, informal settlements without enough services, pollution, and not enough jobs for everyone who arrives.

On a map of Africa, big cities are usually shown with a dot or a square, and the name is printed next to it. Bigger dots or bigger print often mean a bigger city. Capital cities may have a star. Check the key.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a city is, why cities grow, and their good and bad sides.

## Subtopic: Cairo and Lagos

Cairo is the capital city of Egypt, in the north-east of Africa. It is one of the biggest cities in Africa, and many millions of people live in and around it. Cairo grew on the banks of the River Nile, just before the river spreads out into its wide delta and flows into the Mediterranean Sea. Without the water of the Nile, there could be no big city here, because the land on both sides is desert.

Cairo has been an important city for more than a thousand years. Just outside the city, at Giza, stand the great pyramids, built by the ancient Egyptians more than four thousand years ago. Today, Cairo is a busy, crowded city with ancient mosques, markets, museums, universities, factories and an underground railway.

Lagos is the biggest city in Nigeria, the country with the most people in Africa. It is in West Africa, on the coast of the Atlantic Ocean, where the coast curves inwards at the Gulf of Guinea. Lagos is built partly on islands and around a lagoon, which is how it got its name. It grew as a port, where ships from all over the world load and unload goods. Lagos was Nigeria's capital city until 1991, when the government moved to Abuja, in the middle of the country. But Lagos is still Nigeria's most important city for business and trade. It is also famous for its music and film industries. Nigerian films, made in and around Lagos, are watched all over Africa.

The questions for this section are with you now: Cairo and Lagos.

## Subtopic: Johannesburg and Nairobi

Johannesburg is in Gauteng, in the north-east of South Africa, on the high, grassy Highveld. It is South Africa's biggest city, but not one of its capitals. Johannesburg grew very quickly after gold was discovered on the Witwatersrand in 1886. Thousands of people came from all over southern Africa and the world to work in the gold mines. That is why it is often called eGoli, which means the place of gold. Today, most of the gold mines have closed, but Johannesburg is the centre of South Africa's business and banking, and people come from all over Africa to work, study and trade there. Soweto, a large township in Johannesburg, played an important role in South Africa's history.

Nairobi is the capital city of Kenya, in East Africa, just south of the equator. It is nearly one thousand eight hundred metres above sea level, so it is cooler than you might expect so close to the equator. Nairobi began in 1899 as a stop on a railway being built from the coast at Mombasa to Lake Victoria. Its name comes from a Maasai phrase that means a place of cool water. Today, Nairobi is a big centre for business, technology and international organisations. It has a famous national park right next to the city, where lions, giraffes and rhinos can be seen with tall city buildings in the background.

On a map of Africa, Cairo is in the far north-east, Lagos is in the west, Nairobi is in the east, and Johannesburg is in the south. From Johannesburg, Lagos is to the north-west, and Cairo and Nairobi are both to the north.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking Johannesburg is South Africa's capital; it is the biggest city, but the capitals are Pretoria, Cape Town and Bloemfontein. Two: thinking Lagos is Nigeria's capital; the capital is Abuja. Three: thinking Cairo is next to the sea; it is on the Nile, inland from the Mediterranean. Four: thinking cities grow anywhere by chance; they grow where there is water, a port, minerals or a government.

The questions for this section are with you now: Johannesburg and Nairobi, and where the four cities are.
