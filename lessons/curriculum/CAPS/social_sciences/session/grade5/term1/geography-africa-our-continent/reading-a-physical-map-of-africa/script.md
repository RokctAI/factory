# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers features on a physical map of Africa: high and low areas, rivers and lakes, and how height above sea level is shown. The anchor is a Grade 5 classroom in Bloemfontein, in the Free State. Bloemfontein lies high up on a wide, flat area of land, and on cold winter mornings there is frost on the school field. The teacher asks the class to open their atlases at two maps of Africa side by side. One is coloured with bright patches, a different colour for each country. The other is coloured in greens, yellows and browns, with blue lines wriggling across it. The big idea is that a physical map shows the natural shape of the land, its high and low places, its rivers and its lakes, and that colours and a key tell us how high the land is above sea level. Reading a physical map is examinable.

## Subtopic: Political and Physical Maps

There are different kinds of maps for different jobs. Two important kinds are political maps and physical maps.

A political map shows countries, borders, capital cities and other cities. Each country is usually a different colour, so that you can see where one ends and the next begins. This is the first map in the atlas in Bloemfontein. It is useful if you want to know which country a place is in, or what its capital city is.

A physical map shows the natural features of the land. It shows mountains, plateaus, valleys, plains, rivers, lakes and deserts. These are called physical features, because they are made by nature, not by people. This is the second map in the atlas. A physical map is useful if you want to know where the high and low places are, where rivers flow, or where it might be hard to build a road.

Many physical maps also show some borders and cities, so that you can find your way, but their main job is to show the land itself.

The title of a map tells you what kind of map it is. Always read the title first, then the key, before you try to read the map. On the physical map of Africa, the title might be Africa: Physical. On the political map, it might be Africa: Countries and Capitals.

The questions for this section are with you now: the difference between political and physical maps.

## Subtopic: Sea Level and Height

To describe how high a place is, we need a starting point. Geographers use the level of the sea as the starting point. This is called sea level.

The sea goes up and down with the tides and the waves, so sea level is the average height of the surface of the sea, halfway between high tide and low tide. Sea level is given as zero metres.

The height of a place is measured from sea level, in metres. We say that a place is a certain number of metres above sea level. A beach is at about zero metres. Durban is only a little above sea level. Bloemfontein is about one thousand four hundred metres above sea level. Johannesburg is even higher, at about one thousand seven hundred metres. The top of Mount Kilimanjaro, the highest point in Africa, is five thousand eight hundred and ninety-five metres above sea level.

A few places are even lower than sea level. In Djibouti, in the Horn of Africa, the shore of Lake Assal is more than one hundred and fifty metres below sea level. It is the lowest point in Africa.

On a map, a single height is often shown by a small dot or triangle with a number next to it, such as 5895. This is called a spot height. It tells you the exact height of that place in metres above sea level.

Being high above sea level affects a place. In general, the higher a place is, the colder it is. That is one reason why Bloemfontein and Johannesburg have frost in winter, while Durban, near sea level, almost never does.

The questions for this section are with you now: sea level, height above sea level and spot heights.

## Subtopic: Colours, Rivers and Lakes

On a physical map, colours show the height of the land. This is called colour layering. Each colour stands for a band of heights, and the key tells you which.

Usually, dark green shows the lowest land, near sea level. Light green and yellow show land that is a little higher. Orange and light brown show high land. Dark brown, red or purple shows the highest land, such as high mountains. White is sometimes used for snow-capped mountain tops. The key is often a column of colour blocks, with heights in metres written next to them, such as zero to two hundred metres, two hundred to five hundred metres, and so on.

On the physical map of Africa, the learners in Bloemfontein notice a pattern. Much of the north and the west of Africa is lower, and coloured green and yellow. The east and the south are higher, coloured orange and brown. They find the high mountains of East Africa, and the high land of South Africa and Lesotho, where they live.

Water is shown in blue. The sea is light or dark blue. Rivers are blue lines, thicker for bigger rivers. Rivers always flow from high land down to low land, and most end in the sea. Lakes are blue shapes surrounded by land. On the map of Africa, the long blue line of the Nile flows north into the Mediterranean Sea, and big blue lakes lie in East Africa.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a physical map shows only countries; it shows the natural shape of the land. Two: thinking height is measured from the ground where you stand; it is measured from sea level. Three: thinking green on a physical map always means grass or forest; it means low land. Four: thinking rivers can flow uphill; they always flow from high land to low land.

The questions for this section are with you now: colour layering, rivers and lakes on a physical map.

## Subtopic: Two Kinds of Map

Political maps show countries. Physical maps show the land.

Keep the two maps side by side in mind and try the questions for this part: kinds of maps.

## Subtopic: How High?

Sea level is zero metres.

Height is measured above sea level.

## Subtopic: Green Low, Brown High

Green is low. Brown is high. Blue is water.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A political map shows countries, borders and cities. A physical map shows natural features: high and low land, rivers and lakes. Height is measured in metres above sea level, which is zero metres. A spot height gives the exact height of one place. On a physical map, colours show height: green is low, yellow and orange are higher, and brown is highest. Rivers and lakes are blue.
