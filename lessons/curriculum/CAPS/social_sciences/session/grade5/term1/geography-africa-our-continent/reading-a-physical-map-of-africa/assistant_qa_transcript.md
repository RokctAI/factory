### subtopic_1 — Political and Physical Maps
**Assistant:** How do people measure how high a mountain is?

**Tutor:** Long ago, surveyors measured the heights of mountains using special instruments that measure angles. They would stand at a place whose height they knew, measure the angle up to the top of the mountain, and measure the distance to it. Then they used mathematics, especially the study of triangles, to work out the height. It was slow, careful work, and the answers were sometimes off by a few metres. Today, people also use satellites. Climbers can carry a GPS device to the top of a mountain, and it measures the height using signals from satellites in space. Satellites and aeroplanes can also measure the height of the land all over the Earth using radar and lasers. That is how modern maps can show the heights of places very accurately, even in places where nobody has ever climbed.

### subtopic_2 — Sea Level and Height
**Assistant:** Is sea level the same everywhere in the world?

**Tutor:** Not exactly, which is a clever question. Sea level changes a little from place to place, because of winds, currents, the shape of the coast and even the pull of the Earth's gravity, which is a bit stronger in some places than others. It also changes during the day with the tides, which is why sea level is an average. Each country chooses a starting point for its own maps, usually by measuring the sea at one place for many years. Sea level is also slowly rising around the world. As the Earth gets warmer, ice on land melts and the water in the oceans expands a little as it warms. Scientists measure this rise very carefully, because it matters for people who live in low places near the coast.

### subtopic_3 — Colours, Rivers and Lakes
**Assistant:** Why does a physical map use green for low land, even in deserts?

**Tutor:** Mapmakers chose the colours as a code for height, not for what the land looks like. Green, yellow and brown were chosen long ago because they look a little like a landscape from above: green for low valleys, brown for rocky mountains. But it is only a code. A low desert, like parts of the Sahara near the coast, can be coloured green on a physical map, even though there is hardly any plant there. And a high grassland, like the Highveld, can be coloured orange or brown, even though it is covered with grass. That is why it is so important to read the key on every map. The key tells you exactly what each colour means. Some maps use different colours for deserts, so always check before you decide.
