# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how we find out about hunter-gatherers by observing living societies, a method called ethnography, and the care we must take when using it. The anchor is a Grade 5 class from Atlantis, near Cape Town, on a visit to a San heritage centre on the West Coast. The centre is run with San people, and its guides are young San men and women from South Africa and Namibia. On a walk through the fynbos, a guide stops to pick a leaf, crushes it and explains what it is used for. Later, in a circle under a reed shelter, another guide greets the class in her own language, with soft click sounds, then explains it in English. The big idea is that one way of learning about how people lived in the past is to watch and talk to people who live in a similar way today, as long as we remember that the present is not the same as the past. Understanding ethnography and its strengths and limits is examinable.

## Subtopic: What Ethnography Is

Ethnography is the study of a living group of people and their way of life, by spending a long time with them. Ethno means people or group, and graphy means writing. So ethnography is writing about a people.

An ethnographer is a researcher who does this work. Ethnographers live with a community, often for months or years. They learn the language, watch how people find food, build shelters, raise children, solve problems and celebrate. They ask questions, listen, take notes, draw, take photographs and sometimes make recordings. Then they write about what they have seen and learnt.

Ethnography is useful for History because some ways of life change very slowly. If a group of people today still hunt, gather and share in ways that are similar to those of long ago, watching them can help us understand objects and paintings from the past. For example, archaeologists may find a stone with a hole through the middle. On its own, it is a puzzle. But if ethnographers have seen San women push a stone like this onto a digging stick, to make the stick heavier for digging up roots, the puzzle is solved.

Ethnography is different from archaeology. Archaeologists study objects that people left behind long ago, while ethnographers study people who are alive today, and can ask them questions. The two kinds of research work best together.

## Subtopic: Learning from the San of the Kalahari

In the middle of the nineteen hundreds, some San groups in the Kalahari, in Botswana and Namibia, still lived mostly by hunting and gathering. From the 1950s, several researchers and their families went to live with these groups, sometimes for years. They learnt the languages and recorded what they saw. Some made films and took thousands of photographs. San people also told them about their lives.

What they recorded helps us to understand San life. They saw that people lived in small groups of related families, and moved between camps during the year, following water and food. They saw that women gathered much of the food, such as roots, berries, nuts and melons, and that men hunted with small bows and poisoned arrows, and tracked animals with amazing skill. They saw that meat was shared out carefully, so that everyone got some. They saw healing dances at night, in which the women clapped and sang while healers danced until they entered a trance, a special state in which they believed they could heal and enter the spirit world. And they heard many stories, songs and jokes.

Some of these observations help to explain rock paintings. Paintings that show people bending forward with their arms stretched back, or with lines coming from their noses, look very like what ethnographers saw in trance dances. So many researchers now believe that much rock art is linked to healers and the spirit world.

At the heritage centre, the guides explain some of the same things in their own words: how to find water in the dry season, which plants heal, and why sharing matters.

The questions for this section are with you now: what researchers learnt from San communities in the Kalahari.

## Subtopic: Being Careful and Respectful

Ethnography is very helpful, but we must use it carefully.

First, the present is not the same as the past. The San of the Kalahari in the nineteen hundreds were not living exactly like the San of thousands of years ago. They had traded with herders and farmers for a long time, some kept a few goats, some used metal for arrowheads and knives, and their land had already been affected by governments and farms. So we cannot simply say that what was seen in 1955 is what happened ten thousand years ago.

Second, different San groups lived in different ways. Groups in the dry Kalahari lived differently from groups in the mountains of the Drakensberg or near the sea. What is true of one group may not be true of another.

Third, the researcher's own ideas can affect what they notice and how they describe it. That is why we ask who wrote the ethnography and why, just as we do with any source.

Fourth, ethnography must be respectful. Researchers should ask permission, explain what they are doing, protect people's privacy, and share what they learn with the community. Today, San people increasingly tell their own history, lead research and decide how their knowledge is used.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking that people living today are living fossils, exactly like people long ago; all societies change over time. Two: thinking all San groups lived in exactly the same way; there were many groups in different places. Three: thinking ethnography is just watching from far away; it means living with people and learning their language. Four: forgetting to ask permission; ethnography must be respectful.

The questions for this section are with you now: the limits of ethnography and why respect matters.
