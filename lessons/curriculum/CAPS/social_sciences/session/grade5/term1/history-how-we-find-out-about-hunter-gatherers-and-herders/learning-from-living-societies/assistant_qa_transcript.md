### subtopic_1 — What Ethnography Is
**Assistant:** Why did San hunters use poison on their arrows?

**Tutor:** A San bow is small and light, so its arrows do not fly very hard or very far. On their own, they could not kill a large animal such as an eland or a gemsbok. So hunters coated the arrowheads with poison. In the Kalahari, a common poison came from the larvae, or young, of certain small beetles, which hunters dug up from the sand under particular bushes. In other areas, poison was made from plants or snake venom. The poison was put just behind the tip, not on the very point, so that hunters would not scratch themselves. When an arrow hit an animal, the poison slowly spread through its body. The hunters then followed the animal's tracks, sometimes for a day or more, until it became weak and fell. The poison did not make the meat unsafe to eat, as long as the meat around the wound was cut away.

### subtopic_2 — Learning from the San of the Kalahari
**Assistant:** What is a trance?

**Tutor:** A trance is a special state of mind, a bit like being half-asleep and half-awake at the same time, in which a person is very focused and may not notice what is happening around them. Among the San, healers entered a trance during the healing dance. The dance often happened at night, around a fire. The women sat in a circle, clapping and singing special songs, while the men, especially the healers, danced around them for hours. The rhythm, the singing and the effort of dancing helped healers to enter the trance. San people believed that in this state, healers could draw sickness out of people, see into the spirit world, and even travel there. Healers sometimes trembled, bled from the nose or fell down. San people value this healing power very highly, and some still hold healing dances today.

### subtopic_3 — Being Careful and Respectful
**Assistant:** Can people visit San communities today?

**Tutor:** Yes, and there are respectful ways to do it. In South Africa, Namibia and Botswana, there are heritage centres, cultural villages and tours that are run by San communities, or together with them. At these places, San guides share stories, demonstrate tracking and making fire, teach about plants, and sell crafts such as ostrich eggshell jewellery. When the community is in charge, the visitors' money helps the community, and the San decide what they want to share. It is important to visit in a way that respects people. Visitors should not treat people as if they are on show, should ask before taking photographs, and should remember that San people today live modern lives too, with phones, schools and jobs, while also keeping their culture alive. A good visit is a chance to listen and learn, not just to look.
