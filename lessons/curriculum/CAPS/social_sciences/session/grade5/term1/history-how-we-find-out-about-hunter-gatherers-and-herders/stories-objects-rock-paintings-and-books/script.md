# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how we find out about hunter-gatherers and herders from four kinds of sources: stories, objects, rock paintings and books. The anchor is a Grade 5 class from Mossel Bay, on the southern Cape coast, at a museum display about the caves along this coastline. Behind the glass are tiny shell beads with holes drilled through them, pieces of red ochre with criss-cross lines scratched into them, stone points no longer than a finger, and a copy of an old notebook filled with handwriting. On the wall is a large photograph of a rock painting of running people. A museum educator says: none of the people who made these things left us a letter. So how can we know anything about them? The big idea is that historians use many different kinds of sources, and compare them, to build up a picture of how the San and Khoikhoi lived. Knowing the different kinds of sources and how to use them carefully is examinable.

## Subtopic: Stories and Objects

The San and the Khoikhoi did not write. Instead, they passed on their knowledge, history and beliefs by telling stories, singing songs and teaching their children by example. Stories passed down by word of mouth from one generation to the next are called oral history or oral tradition.

In the 1870s, in Cape Town, a language scholar called Wilhelm Bleek and his sister-in-law, Lucy Lloyd, worked with a small group of San men and women from the Northern Cape. Some of these men had been sent to prison in Cape Town. Over many years, they told Bleek and Lloyd hundreds of stories, which were carefully written down in their own language, the language of the people called the Xam, with English beside them. The stories tell about animals, the stars, the rain, hunting, healing and the spirit world. This collection is now protected as one of the world's most important records. It is a precious source, because it gives us San people's own words.

Objects are things that people made and used. Most of what we know about life thousands of years ago comes from objects found by archaeologists. Archaeologists dig carefully in caves and old campsites, layer by layer, and record exactly where each object is found. They find stone tools, such as scrapers and points, bone arrowheads, ostrich eggshell beads and water flasks, grindstones, pots, and the bones of animals that were eaten.

At Blombos Cave, not far from Mossel Bay, archaeologists found shell beads and pieces of ochre with patterns scratched on them that are more than seventy thousand years old. They are some of the oldest known decorated objects in the world, and they show that people here thought and created in clever ways long ago.

The questions for this section are with you now: oral stories, archaeology and objects.

## Subtopic: Rock Paintings and Books

Rock art is another important source. San artists painted pictures on the walls of caves and rock shelters, and in some places they carved pictures, called engravings, into rocks. There are many thousands of rock art sites in southern Africa. The paintings show people, animals, especially the eland, and many strange figures that are part human and part animal. Rock art tells us about the animals people knew, the tools they used and, most of all, their beliefs. In the photograph at the museum, the running people carry bows and bags.

Books are written sources. From the late fourteen hundreds, European sailors began to stop along the coast of southern Africa, and from 1652, the Dutch set up a station at the Cape. Sailors, settlers, travellers and missionaries wrote letters, diaries and books describing the Khoikhoi and the San they met. Some made drawings too. These writings tell us about clothing, houses, cattle, trade and the first meetings between Europeans and Khoikhoi.

But written sources by outsiders must be read carefully. Many of the writers did not speak the Khoikhoi or San languages, and did not understand their customs. Some looked down on them and described them unfairly, because they believed their own way of life was better. Some wrote to persuade others to take the land. So historians always ask: who wrote this, when, and why? Does the writer have a reason to tell only one side?

Today, there are also books written by historians and archaeologists who study all the sources, and books written by San and Khoikhoi descendants themselves.

The questions for this section are with you now: rock paintings and written sources.

## Subtopic: Using Sources Carefully

Each kind of source has strengths and weaknesses.

Stories give the people's own voices and beliefs, but they may change a little each time they are told, and they rarely give dates. Objects are real evidence of what people made and used, and scientists can work out how old some of them are, but objects cannot tell us what people thought or felt. Rock art shows us what people chose to paint, and gives clues to their beliefs, but we cannot ask the artists what they meant. Books by outsiders give descriptions and dates, but they may be biased, which means they show only one side or are unfair.

That is why historians use many sources together, and compare them. If a story, an object and a painting all point to the same thing, we can be more confident. For example, San stories tell of hunting the eland with bows and arrows, archaeologists have found bone arrowheads, and rock paintings show hunters with bows. Together, these sources give strong evidence about San hunting.

When you answer a source-based question, read or look at the source carefully, think about who made it and why, and use clues from the source in your answer.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking that because the San did not write, we cannot know anything about them; we learn from stories, objects and rock art. Two: thinking everything in an old book must be true; outsiders' writing can be biased. Three: thinking archaeologists just dig quickly for treasure; they dig slowly and record every layer. Four: using only one source; historians compare many sources.

The questions for this section are with you now: the strengths and weaknesses of sources, and comparing them.
