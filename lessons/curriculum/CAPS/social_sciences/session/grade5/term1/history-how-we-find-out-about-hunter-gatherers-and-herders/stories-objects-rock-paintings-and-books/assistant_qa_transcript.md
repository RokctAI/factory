### subtopic_1 — Stories and Objects
**Assistant:** Why were San men in prison in Cape Town in the 1870s?

**Tutor:** By the 1870s, settler farmers had moved into the dry lands of the Northern Cape where the Xam San lived and hunted. They took over the land and the water, and wild animals became scarce. Many San people were left hungry, and some took sheep or other animals from the farmers to survive. Colonial courts punished them harshly, and some were sent to work as prisoners at the Breakwater in Cape Town, building the harbour wall. There were also violent conflicts in which many San people were killed. Wilhelm Bleek asked for some of these prisoners to come and live at his home, so that he could learn their language, and some of their family members later joined them. Their stories survived because of this, but we should remember the hard and unfair situation that brought them there.

### subtopic_2 — Rock Paintings and Books
**Assistant:** How do archaeologists know how old a bead or a tool is?

**Tutor:** There are a few ways. The first is the layers. In a cave, things build up over time, like pages piling up on a desk. Usually, the deeper the layer, the older it is. So archaeologists record exactly which layer every object comes from. The second is science. Some materials change slowly and steadily over time. For example, things that were once alive, such as charcoal from a fire or a piece of bone, contain a special kind of carbon that slowly disappears at a known speed. By measuring how much is left, scientists can work out roughly how long ago the plant or animal died. For very old layers, such as those at Blombos Cave, scientists use other methods, such as measuring how long ago grains of sand were last in sunlight. Using several methods together gives more reliable dates.

### subtopic_3 — Using Sources Carefully
**Assistant:** Did the Khoikhoi themselves write anything down later?

**Tutor:** Long ago, the Khoikhoi did not use writing, but over time, some Khoikhoi people learnt to read and write, often at mission schools. Some of their letters, statements and requests to the government were written down and still survive in archives. For example, there are records of Khoikhoi leaders and communities asking for land and fair treatment, and of people telling their own stories in court. Missionaries also wrote down Khoikhoi languages, such as Nama, and translated parts of the Bible into them. Today, many descendants of the Khoikhoi, such as Nama people, write books, poems, songs and histories about their own people, in their own words. These are important sources, because they give us the view from inside the community, not only from outsiders.
