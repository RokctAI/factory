# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the Term 1 topics. In Geography, the topic was map skills with a focus on Africa: the globe, the equator and the continents, the eight points of the compass, Africa's oceans, countries, capital cities and borders, physical maps, and Africa's neighbours, mountains, lakes, rivers, waterfalls and deserts. In History, the topic was hunter-gatherers and herders in southern Africa: how we find out about them, San hunter-gatherer society, rock art, and the Khoikhoi herders. The anchor is a revision afternoon in a Grade 5 class in Mahikeng, in North West province. The school hall has three tables. Table 1 has a globe and a big compass rose drawn on chart paper. Table 2 has a physical map of Africa and a pile of cards with the names of countries, cities, rivers and mountains. Table 3 has pictures of a stone tool, a bow and arrow, a rock painting of an eland and a Khoikhoi mat house. Groups move from table to table, answering the questions on each card. The big idea is that maps help us find our way around Africa and the world, and that sources help us find out how the San and Khoikhoi lived. The whole of Term 1 is examinable.

## Subtopic: The World and the Compass

At Table 1, the globe reminds the class that the Earth is round. The North Pole is at the top and the South Pole at the bottom. The equator is an imaginary line around the middle, which divides the Earth into the Northern and Southern Hemispheres. South Africa is in the Southern Hemisphere.

There are seven continents: Africa, Asia, Antarctica, Australia, Europe, North America and South America. Asia is the biggest and Africa is the second biggest. There are five oceans: the Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern and Arctic Oceans.

The compass rose shows the eight points of the compass: north, north-east, east, south-east, south, south-west, west and north-west. North or south always comes first in an in-between direction. A direction is always given from a fixed point. If the dam is north-east of the school, then the school is south-west of the dam. On most maps, north is at the top, shown by a north arrow.

A globe is a round model of the Earth, which shows true shapes and sizes. A map is a flat drawing, which is easy to carry and can show more detail.

The questions for this section are with you now: the globe, the continents, the oceans and the eight compass directions.

## Subtopic: The Map of Africa

At Table 2, the physical map of Africa reminds the class that Africa lies across the equator, with land in both hemispheres. The Atlantic Ocean is to the west, the Indian Ocean to the east, the Mediterranean Sea to the north and the Red Sea to the north-east. Europe lies to the north and Asia to the north-east.

Africa has fifty-four countries. A country has its own government, laws and flag. A capital city is where the government works. South Africa has three capitals: Pretoria, Cape Town and Bloemfontein. Borders separate countries, and some follow rivers, such as the Limpopo, or cross lakes, such as Lake Victoria. Coastal countries touch the sea, and landlocked countries, such as Lesotho and Botswana, do not. Kenya and Uganda are on the equator. Madagascar is a large island country, and Zanzibar is part of Tanzania. Big cities include Cairo, Lagos, Johannesburg and Nairobi.

South Africa's six neighbours and their capitals are Namibia, Windhoek; Botswana, Gaborone; Zimbabwe, Harare; Mozambique, Maputo; Eswatini, Mbabane; and Lesotho, Maseru.

On a physical map, colours show height above sea level: green is low and brown is high. Kilimanjaro, in Tanzania, is Africa's highest mountain, and Mount Kenya is second. Lake Victoria is the largest lake, Lake Tanganyika the longest, and Lake Malawi the third largest. The great rivers are the Nile, which flows north, the Niger, the Congo, the Zambezi, the Limpopo and the Gariep, or Orange. Victoria Falls is on the Zambezi, and Maletsunyane Falls is in Lesotho. The Sahara is the largest hot desert in the world, and the Namib is on the coast of Namibia.

The questions for this section are with you now: Africa's oceans, countries, cities and physical features.

## Subtopic: Hunter-Gatherers and Herders

At Table 3, the pictures remind the class about History. The San were hunter-gatherers who lived in southern Africa for tens of thousands of years. The Khoikhoi were herders who arrived about two thousand years ago. Together they are often called the Khoisan.

We find out about them from stories, such as those recorded by Bleek and Lloyd in the 1870s, from objects found by archaeologists, from rock art, and from books by outsiders, which may be biased. We also learn from ethnography, by living with San communities today, but we must remember that the present is not exactly the same as the past.

The San lived off the environment. Women gathered plants and men hunted. They moved with the seasons, stored water in ostrich eggshells and carried few belongings. The bow and arrow let hunters shoot from a safe distance, using poison, and then track the animal. San bands shared food equally and had no chiefs. They believed in a spirit world, the eland was special, and healers danced into a trance. San rock art, painted and engraved, is linked to these beliefs. Figures from the Linton panel are on South Africa's coat of arms.

The Khoikhoi herded fat-tailed sheep and cattle, lived mainly on milk, and moved with mat houses carried on oxen. They had chiefs, and wealth was counted in animals. The San and Khoikhoi traded, worked together and married, but also clashed over water and grazing.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying east-north instead of north-east; north or south comes first. Two: thinking Johannesburg is South Africa's capital; the capitals are Pretoria, Cape Town and Bloemfontein. Three: thinking rock art is just decoration; it is linked to San beliefs. Four: thinking the Khoikhoi ate meat every day; milk was their main food.

The questions for this section are with you now: sources, the San and the Khoikhoi.
