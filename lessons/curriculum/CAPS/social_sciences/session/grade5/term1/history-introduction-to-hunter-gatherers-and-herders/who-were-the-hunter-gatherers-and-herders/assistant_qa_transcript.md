### subtopic_1 — History and the Grade 5 Year
**Assistant:** Why do some people say San and others say Bushmen?

**Tutor:** This is a good question about names and respect. Many different names have been used for the hunter-gatherers of southern Africa. The name Bushmen was given by European settlers, and for a long time it was often used in an insulting way, so many people stopped using it. The name San is believed to come from a Khoikhoi word, and some say it meant people who forage, or people who do not own cattle. Historians and many books now use San. But the people themselves usually prefer to be called by the names of their own groups, such as the Khomani, the Khwe or the Ju, and some have even said they do not mind the name Bushmen when it is used with respect. The best rule is to listen to what people call themselves and to use names with care.

### subtopic_2 — Hunter-Gatherers and Herders
**Assistant:** How do historians know the San were here so long ago?

**Tutor:** Mostly from archaeology, the careful study of things left behind by people long ago. In caves and rock shelters all over southern Africa, archaeologists dig down slowly, layer by layer. The deeper the layer, usually the older it is. In the layers, they find stone tools, bone points, beads made from ostrich eggshell, the bones of animals that were eaten, and the remains of old fires. Scientists can work out how old some of these things are by measuring tiny changes in certain materials, such as charcoal from the fires, that happen at a steady speed over time. The tools and beads found in the oldest layers are very like the ones that San people were still making in recent times. That is strong evidence that the San and their ancestors lived in southern Africa for many tens of thousands of years.

### subtopic_3 — A Very Long Time Ago
**Assistant:** Are there still San people living as hunter-gatherers today?

**Tutor:** Very few San people today live only by hunting and gathering. Over the last few hundred years, their lives changed a great deal. Farmers and governments took over much of the land, wild animals became fewer, and hunting was limited by laws. Many San people now live in villages and towns in Botswana, Namibia and South Africa, and work in many different jobs, as farmers, rangers, trackers, teachers, artists and guides. Some still gather wild plants and hunt in certain areas, and many keep their languages, stories, songs, dances and knowledge of plants and tracking alive, and teach them to their children. In South Africa, in 1999, the Khomani San won back some of their land in the Kalahari. So the San are a living people, not just people from History books.
