# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the invention of the bow and arrow, and how it made San hunting more effective. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in KwaDukuza, on the north coast of KwaZulu-Natal, looking at a museum display about Sibudu Cave, a large rock shelter above a river, not far away. Behind the glass are tiny, sharp stone points, each smaller than a fingernail, next to a drawing of how they might have been fixed to the end of an arrow. Beside them is a small San bow, a quiver made of bark, and a row of thin reed arrows. The learners are surprised at how small and light everything is. The big idea is that the bow and arrow was one of the most important inventions of the Stone Age, because it let hunters kill animals from a safe distance, and that San hunters combined it with poison and great tracking skill. The bow and arrow and how it changed hunting are examinable.

## Subtopic: Before the Bow

Before the bow and arrow was invented, people hunted with simpler weapons. They used spears, made from long, straight sticks with sharpened points or with stone points tied to the end. A hunter had to get very close to an animal to stab it with a spear, or throw it from a short distance. This was dangerous, especially with large animals such as buffalo, which could turn and charge.

People also hunted with clubs and throwing sticks, which could stun or kill small animals and birds. They dug pit traps, and they set snares, which are loops of string or plant fibre that tighten around an animal's leg or neck when it walks into them. Snares were set along animal paths and checked every day. Some hunters chased animals for hours in the heat, until the animals were too tired to run any further.

These methods worked, and people continued to use them for a long time, even after the bow and arrow was invented. But each had limits. Spears needed the hunter to get close, traps needed animals to come to them, and chasing took enormous energy.

The questions for this section are with you now: hunting before the bow and arrow.

## Subtopic: The Bow and Arrow

A bow is a curved piece of springy wood, with a string tied tightly from one end to the other. When the hunter pulls the string back and lets go, the bow springs straight and shoots the arrow forward, much faster and further than a person can throw.

Nobody knows exactly when or where the bow and arrow was first invented, but some of the oldest evidence in the world comes from southern Africa. At Sibudu Cave, scientists found tiny stone points with marks of damage on their tips, as if they had hit something hard at speed, and traces of blood, bone and glue on them. Some scientists think these points were used on arrows about sixty thousand years ago or more. This is still being studied, but it shows that people in this part of Africa were inventors.

San bows were small, usually about a metre long or less, made from a bent branch with a string of twisted animal sinew. Arrows were made from thin reeds. The arrowhead was made from bone, and later sometimes from metal, which the San got by trade. Between the reed shaft and the head, there was often a short joining piece, called a link shaft. When the arrow hit an animal, the reed shaft fell off, but the poisoned head stayed in.

Arrows were carried in a quiver, a long, narrow container made from bark or animal skin, and hung over the shoulder. Hunters also carried a bag with a fire-making kit, a knife, a spare string and a few other tools.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the bow and arrow worked and how San bows and arrows were made.

## Subtopic: Hunting with Poison and Tracking

A San bow is light, so the arrow does not hit very hard. On its own, it would not kill a large antelope. So San hunters put poison on their arrows. In the Kalahari, poison often came from the larvae of certain beetles, dug up from under particular bushes. In other areas, it came from plants or snake venom. The poison was put just behind the tip, so that the hunter would not cut himself on it.

The bow and arrow made hunting much more effective. Hunters could shoot from a distance, often twenty or thirty metres away, hidden behind a bush. They did not have to get dangerously close. They could hunt large antelope, such as eland, kudu and gemsbok, that were hard to catch with spears.

After the arrow hit, the poison worked slowly. The hunters followed the animal for hours, sometimes for a day or more, using their tracking skills to read footprints, broken grass and other signs. When the animal grew weak and stopped, the hunters finished the hunt. Then they cut up the meat and carried it back to camp, where it was shared.

Hunting was usually done by small groups of men. Boys began to practise with small bows and arrows when they were young, and they learnt to track by following their fathers and uncles. A young man's first large antelope was an important event, and was marked with a special ceremony.

The San respected the animals they hunted. They believed that animals, especially the eland, had special power, and they had rules about how to hunt, how to behave during a hunt, and how to share the meat.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the arrow alone killed large animals; it was the poison that did most of the work. Two: thinking San bows were big and heavy; they were small and light. Three: thinking people stopped using spears and snares when the bow was invented; they kept using all of these methods. Four: thinking the hunt ended when the arrow hit; hunters often had to track the animal for many hours.

The questions for this section are with you now: poison, tracking and why the bow and arrow made hunting more effective.
