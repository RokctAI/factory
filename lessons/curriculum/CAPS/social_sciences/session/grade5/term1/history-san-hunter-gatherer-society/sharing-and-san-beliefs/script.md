# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers San social organisation, especially equal sharing within a group, and San beliefs and religion. The anchor is a Grade 5 school camp near the Kgalagadi Transfrontier Park, in the far north of the Northern Cape, where red sand dunes stretch to the horizon. After supper, the class sits on logs around a fire. Above them, the Milky Way is a bright band across the dark sky. A San storyteller from a nearby community joins them. She tells them that her grandmother used to say the Milky Way was made by a girl who threw the ashes of a fire into the sky, to light the way home at night. Then she tells them how people in the old days shared everything they had. The big idea is that San society was built on sharing and equality, and that San beliefs connected people, animals, the land and the spirit world. San social organisation and beliefs are examinable.

## Subtopic: Small Groups and Equal Sharing

San hunter-gatherers lived in small groups, often called bands, usually made up of a few related families. A band might have perhaps twenty to fifty people, including grandparents, parents, children, aunts, uncles and cousins. Bands that lived near each other were linked by marriage and friendship, and people sometimes moved from one band to another to visit relatives or when food was scarce in one area.

Sharing was at the heart of San life. When hunters brought meat back to camp, it was not kept by the hunter who made the kill. It was shared out according to rules, so that every family got a portion. Often, the meat belonged first to the person who owned the arrow that killed the animal, and that person shared it out. Because people swapped arrows, the owner might not even be the hunter. Gathered food was mostly shared within each family, and also with others who needed it.

There were good reasons for sharing. Hunting did not succeed every day. A hunter who was lucky today might be unlucky for the next two weeks. By sharing, everyone ate when anyone was lucky, so no one went hungry. Sharing also kept people equal. No one could become rich while others were poor, and no one could boast. In fact, if a hunter boasted about a big kill, others would tease him and make jokes about how small and thin the animal was, to keep him humble.

Gift-giving was also important. People gave each other gifts, such as ostrich eggshell beads, arrows and tools, and later received gifts back. These gifts built friendships between people in different bands, so that they could visit each other and share resources in hard times.

The questions for this section are with you now: San bands and why sharing was so important.

## Subtopic: Decisions Without Chiefs

The San did not have chiefs or kings who gave orders. There were no rulers and no servants. Decisions that affected the whole band, such as when to move camp, where to go, or how to solve a quarrel, were made by talking together until people agreed.

Older people were respected because of their knowledge and experience. People who were especially skilled, such as good hunters, wise elders or powerful healers, were listened to carefully. But they did not have the power to force others to obey them.

Men and women had different jobs. Women gathered most of the plant food and looked after young children. Men did most of the hunting. Both men and women took part in decisions, told stories and made music. Women's knowledge of plants was as important as men's knowledge of animals.

If people quarrelled, they talked it out, often loudly and at length, until the problem was settled. If that did not work, one family might go to live with another band for a while, until tempers cooled. Joking was also a way to keep the peace.

At the camp in the Kgalagadi, the storyteller says that in the old days, if one person had more than they needed, the others would say so, and the person would share.

The questions for this section are with you now: how San bands made decisions, and the roles of men and women.

## Subtopic: San Beliefs

The San had rich religious beliefs, which were part of everyday life. Beliefs differed from group to group, but some ideas were shared widely.

Many San believed in a great god or creator, and in a spirit world that existed alongside the everyday world. Some San groups in the Cape told stories about a trickster spirit called Kaggen, who could take the form of a praying mantis, and who made the eland, the animal he loved most.

The eland, the largest antelope, was very special to many San groups. It was believed to have great spiritual power, and it was linked to rain, healing, marriage and becoming an adult. The eland is the animal painted most often in many areas of San rock art.

Healing was very important. Some people, both men and women, were healers. At a healing dance, women sat in a circle around the fire, clapping and singing, and healers danced around them for hours. The healers believed that a special power rose up inside them, and they entered a trance, a deep, dream-like state. In the trance, they believed they could see sickness and draw it out of people, travel to the spirit world, and even make rain.

The San also had many stories about the sun, moon and stars, animals and the first people. These stories taught children how to behave and how the world began.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the San had chiefs who gave orders; decisions were made by talking until people agreed. Two: thinking the hunter kept the meat; it was shared with everyone in the band. Three: thinking the San had no religion; they had rich beliefs about a spirit world, healing and the eland. Four: thinking only men could be healers; women could be healers too.

The questions for this section are with you now: San beliefs, the eland and healing.
