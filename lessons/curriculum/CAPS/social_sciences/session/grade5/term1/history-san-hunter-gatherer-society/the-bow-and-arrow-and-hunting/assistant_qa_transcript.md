### subtopic_1 — Before the Bow
**Assistant:** How do scientists know the Sibudu points were arrows and not spears?

**Tutor:** They cannot be completely sure, which is why they keep studying them. But they look at several clues. First, the size and shape: the points are tiny and light, much too small to make a strong spear, but just right for the tip of a light arrow. Second, the damage: under a microscope, the tips show tiny fractures, the kind of breakage that happens when a point hits something hard at high speed, as an arrow does. Third, the remains: some points have traces of blood and bone, showing they were used to hunt, and traces of glue made from plant gum, showing they were fixed onto a shaft. Scientists have also made copies of these points, fixed them to arrows, and shot them into animal carcasses to see whether they break in the same way. All of this together makes them think these were arrows.

### subtopic_2 — The Bow and Arrow
**Assistant:** Is the poison on San arrows dangerous to people?

**Tutor:** Yes, it can be very dangerous, which is why hunters were extremely careful with it. The poison from beetle larvae, for example, attacks the blood and can kill a person if it gets into a cut. That is why San hunters put the poison behind the arrow tip, not on the sharp point, and why they kept their arrows in a closed quiver, often with the points turned inwards. Children were taught never to touch the arrows. The poison was not dangerous to eat in the meat, as long as the meat around the wound was cut away, because this kind of poison needs to get into the blood through a wound to work. Today, scientists study these traditional poisons with interest, because San people discovered them by careful observation over a very long time.

### subtopic_3 — Hunting with Poison and Tracking
**Assistant:** Why was a boy's first antelope hunt so important?

**Tutor:** Among many San groups, hunting a large antelope was a sign that a boy was becoming a man. It showed that he had learnt the skills he needed to provide food for his family and his group: how to shoot, how to track and how to stay patient. It also showed that he understood the rules of the hunt and of sharing. In some groups, after a young man's first big kill, older men marked him with small cuts on his skin, into which they rubbed medicine, as a sign of his new place in the group. This was believed to give him strength and good luck for future hunts. Only after this was he seen as ready to marry. For the group, it was a moment of pride and celebration, because a new hunter meant more food for everyone in the future.
