# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how San hunter-gatherers of the Later Stone Age lived off the environment, moving with the seasons as wild resources became available. The anchor is a Grade 5 class from Clanwilliam, in the Western Cape, on a guided walk in the Cederberg mountains. The path winds between huge orange sandstone rocks, shaped by wind and water into arches and overhangs. The guide stops at a rock shelter, a shallow cave under an overhanging rock, where the floor is dry and dusty, and points out the black marks of old fires on the ceiling. She kneels by a bush and shows the class a bulb hidden under the soil. The big idea is that the San knew their environment so well that they could find everything they needed in it, food, water, shelter, tools and medicine, and that they moved from place to place through the year to follow the food. How the San lived off the environment is examinable.

## Subtopic: Gathering and Hunting

The San got all their food from the wild. They did not plant crops or keep animals. Their way of getting food is called hunting and gathering.

Gathering was mostly done by women, often with children helping and learning. Women went out in small groups with digging sticks and leather bags. They dug up roots, bulbs and tubers, and collected berries, fruits, nuts, seeds, leaves, honey, eggs, and insects such as termites and caterpillars. In the Kalahari, they gathered tsamma melons, which store water. Near the coast, people also collected shellfish from the rocks. In many areas, the food that women gathered made up a large part of what the group ate, and it could be found almost every day.

Hunting was mostly done by men. They hunted antelope such as eland, springbok, kudu and steenbok, as well as smaller animals such as hares, tortoises and birds. They used bows and arrows, spears and clubs, and they set snares and traps. Hunting was exciting, and meat was very valued, but hunts did not always succeed, so the group could not depend on meat alone.

The San knew hundreds of plants. They knew which plants were good to eat, which were poisonous, which could heal illnesses, and which could be used to make poison for arrows. They knew the habits of every animal, and could read the tracks in the sand like a book. This knowledge was passed on from parents and grandparents to children.

The San also used plants and animals for much more than food. Animal skins became clothes, bags and blankets. Sinew, the tough string-like part of an animal's muscles, became bowstrings and thread. Bones became arrowheads and needles. Plants gave medicine, poison, fibre for string and nets, and wood for bows, arrows and fire. Very little was wasted.

The questions for this section are with you now: gathering, hunting and the San's knowledge of plants and animals.

## Subtopic: Moving with the Seasons

The San were nomadic, which means they moved from place to place, rather than living in one place all the time. But they did not wander about at random. They moved in a planned way, following the seasons.

Each group had a home area, which it knew very well, with waterholes, rock shelters, and places where certain plants grew. During the year, the group moved between camps in its home area. When the rains came, plants grew, fruit ripened and animals moved to the fresh grass. When the dry season came, water became scarce, and the group would move to a place with a reliable waterhole or spring. In the Cederberg area, for example, a group might move between the mountains and the coast at different times of the year, to be where bulbs, fruits, animals or shellfish were easiest to find. Some archaeologists think that groups in this area did exactly that.

Moving often meant that the San could not have many belongings. Everything had to be carried. So they owned only what was useful and light: bows and arrows, digging sticks, bags, karosses, ostrich eggshell water flasks, and a few ornaments.

Moving also helped to protect the environment. By moving on before all the plants and animals in one area were used up, the San allowed them to grow back. They took what they needed, and no more.

The questions for this section are with you now: nomadic life and moving with the seasons.

## Subtopic: Water, Shelter and Tools

Water was the most important need, especially in dry areas. The San found water in rivers, springs and waterholes. In the dry Kalahari, they also sucked water from damp sand through a hollow reed, and squeezed it from roots and melons. They stored water in ostrich eggshells, sealed with grass or wax, and sometimes buried the full shells at places along their route, so that they would find water later.

For shelter, the San used rock shelters and caves in mountain areas, like the one on the Cederberg walk. In open country, they built light shelters from branches and grass, which could be put up quickly and left behind when the group moved.

The San made clever tools from the materials around them. A digging stick was a strong, pointed stick, sometimes made heavier with a round stone that had a hole bored through it. Karosses, cloaks made from animal skins, kept people warm at night and were used to carry things during the day. Bags were made from skins, and nets from plant fibres. Stone was chipped into sharp scrapers to clean skins, and into small blades and points. Fire was made by spinning a hard stick very fast against a softer piece of wood, until the dust began to glow.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the San ate mainly meat; gathered plant food was very important and could be found almost every day. Two: thinking the San wandered without a plan; they moved in planned ways within their home areas, following the seasons. Three: thinking the San were poor because they had few belongings; they carried only what they needed, because they moved often. Four: thinking the San harmed the environment; they moved on before using everything up.

The questions for this section are with you now: water, shelter and tools.
