### subtopic_1 — Where and When
**Assistant:** Was it right to cut the Linton panel out of the rock shelter?

**Tutor:** That is a question many people ask today. In 1917, people from the museum believed they were saving a very beautiful painting by moving it to Cape Town, where it would be safe from weather and damage. It was cut out of the rock and carried all the way to Cape Town. Thanks to that, many people have been able to see it, and it was well protected. But today, most people believe that rock art should stay where it was made, as part of the landscape and the place that gave it meaning. Cutting it out also damaged the shelter, and it was done without asking any San descendants. Laws now protect rock art sites, and removing rock art is not allowed. The Linton story helps us think about who owns heritage, and who should decide what happens to it.

### subtopic_2 — How It Was Made
**Assistant:** How do researchers date rock paintings?

**Tutor:** It is very difficult, which is why the dates of most paintings are not known exactly. Paint made from ochre and clay does not contain the kind of material that is easy to date. But scientists have found ways in some cases. Some paintings contain tiny bits of charcoal or animal products, which can be dated by measuring a special kind of carbon that slowly disappears over time. Sometimes a painting is covered by a thin skin of minerals that formed on top of it, and scientists can date that skin, which tells them the painting must be older. In some caves, painted stones fell from the walls and were buried in layers on the floor, which archaeologists can date. And paintings that show horses or wagons must have been made after those things arrived. Putting clues together, researchers can say roughly how old some paintings are.

### subtopic_3 — Why It Was Made
**Assistant:** Can we still see the Game Pass Shelter paintings today?

**Tutor:** Yes. Game Pass Shelter is in the Kamberg area of the uKhahlamba-Drakensberg Park, which is a World Heritage Site because of both its natural beauty and its rock art. Visitors can go there on a guided walk with a trained guide, who explains the paintings and makes sure they are protected. The walk is a few hours there and back, up a hillside, so you need good shoes and water. There is also a rock art centre nearby that explains San art and beliefs. The guides ask visitors not to touch the rock, not to throw water on the paintings to make them look brighter, and not to leave litter. Some people call Game Pass Shelter the Rosetta Stone of San rock art, because studying its paintings helped researchers understand the link between the eland and trance.
