# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers San rock art: where, when, how and why it was created, how it is interpreted, and its link to the South African coat of arms and the Linton rock art panel. The anchor is a Grade 5 class from Pietermaritzburg on a guided hike to Game Pass Shelter, at Kamberg, in the uKhahlamba-Drakensberg Park in KwaZulu-Natal. After a steep climb up a grassy hillside, they reach a long overhang of yellow sandstone. On the smooth rock wall, painted in red, white and dark brown, are eland, some standing, some running, and one stumbling with its head down. Next to the dying eland is a human figure holding its tail, with crossed legs and hair standing on end. The guide asks the class not to touch anything, and to look for a long time before speaking. The big idea is that San rock art is a precious record of San life and beliefs, painted and engraved over thousands of years, and that we must study it with care and protect it. San rock art and its meaning are examinable.

## Subtopic: Where and When

San rock art is found all over southern Africa. There are many thousands of sites in South Africa alone.

There are two main kinds of rock art. Paintings were made on the walls of caves and rock shelters, mostly in mountain areas such as the Drakensberg, the Cederberg, the Eastern Cape mountains and parts of Limpopo. Engravings were carved or scratched into the surface of rocks and boulders, mostly in the flat, open interior of the country, such as the Northern Cape, North West and the Free State.

Rock art was made over a very long time. Some of the oldest dated painted stones in Africa, found in a cave in southern Namibia, may be around thirty thousand years old. Most paintings that survive on rock shelter walls in South Africa are much younger, from the last few thousand years, because paint slowly fades and flakes off. Some paintings were made only a few hundred years ago. They show things that arrived recently, such as cattle, sheep, horses, wagons, and men with hats and guns. These paintings show us that San artists recorded the meeting of their world with herders, farmers and European settlers.

The questions for this section are with you now: where rock art is found and when it was made.

## Subtopic: How It Was Made

San artists made paint from natural materials. Red, orange and yellow came from ochre, a soft rock or earth containing iron, which was ground into powder. Black came from charcoal or certain minerals. White came from white clay or similar soft white minerals. The powder was mixed with a liquid to make it stick. Researchers think artists may have used things such as animal fat, blood, plant juices, egg or water, and recent studies have found traces of animal products in some paint.

Artists painted with brushes made from feathers, animal hair, thin sticks or reeds, and sometimes with their fingers. Some paintings have very fine, thin lines, which show great skill. Artists showed animals in many positions, from the front, the side and even from behind, and they used shading to make some eland look round and solid.

Engravings were made by pecking or scratching. The artist used a hard stone to tap many tiny dents into the surface of a rock, or scraped lines into it, until the picture appeared, lighter than the dark rock around it.

The questions for this section are with you now: how paint was made and how paintings and engravings were made.

## Subtopic: Why It Was Made

For a long time, many people thought rock art was simply decoration, or pictures of everyday life, such as hunting scenes, or a way of recording events. Some people even thought it was made by people from outside Africa. Those ideas were wrong.

Researchers now understand rock art much better, mainly by studying what San people said about their beliefs, in the stories recorded from the Xam San in the 1870s, and by learning from San communities in the Kalahari. Many researchers now believe that much rock art is connected to San religion, especially to healers and the trance experience. The eland was painted more than any other animal in many areas, not because it was eaten most, but because it had special spiritual power. The dying eland at Game Pass Shelter, with its head down and legs crossing, may be linked to the way a healer in trance was said to feel: weak, trembling and close to death. The human figure next to it, with crossed legs and hair on end, may show a healer entering the spirit world. But we cannot ask the artists, so these are interpretations, and researchers still discuss them.

The Linton panel is a beautiful painted rock slab that was cut from a rock shelter on a farm called Linton, in the Eastern Cape, in 1917, and taken to the South African Museum in Cape Town, where it can still be seen. It shows human figures in fine detail, with lines and dots that may be linked to trance and healing. In 2000, South Africa adopted a new coat of arms. At its centre are two human figures, taken from the Linton panel, facing each other in greeting, as a sign of unity. The motto underneath is in the language of the Xam San, and means diverse people unite.

Rock art is protected by law. It is very old and very fragile. Never touch, wet, scratch or chalk a painting, and never take any rock from a site.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking rock art is just decoration; it is closely linked to San beliefs. Two: thinking all rock art is paintings; there are also engravings carved into rocks. Three: thinking rock art was made by people from outside Africa; it was made by San artists. Four: thinking it is fine to touch or wet paintings to see them better; this damages them forever.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of rock art, the Linton panel and the coat of arms.
