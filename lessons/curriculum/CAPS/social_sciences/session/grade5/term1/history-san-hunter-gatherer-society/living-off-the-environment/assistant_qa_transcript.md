### subtopic_1 — Gathering and Hunting
**Assistant:** How could San trackers know so much from footprints?

**Tutor:** San trackers spent their whole lives learning to read the ground, starting as small children, who played tracking games and copied animal prints in the sand. A good tracker could tell which animal made a print, whether it was male or female, young or old, how fast it was moving, and how long ago it passed. They noticed small signs: a bent blade of grass, a broken twig, a turned-over stone, or the way the edge of a print had crumbled in the wind. Fresh prints have sharp edges, and old ones are softer. They could even guess where the animal was going by thinking like the animal. Tracking was a kind of science, using evidence to work out what happened. Today, San trackers are respected around the world, and some work with rangers and scientists to protect wildlife and catch poachers.

### subtopic_2 — Moving with the Seasons
**Assistant:** What did San children do all day?

**Tutor:** San children spent much of their time playing, and play was also how they learnt. Small children stayed close to their mothers and went with them when they gathered, learning which plants to pick and where to find them. Older children played in groups at camp. Boys practised with small bows and arrows, shooting at targets, beetles or small birds, and they played games of tracking and hunting. Girls learnt to gather, to carry, to make beads and to sing the songs of the healing dance. Children also listened to stories around the fire in the evenings, which taught them about animals, the stars and how to behave. Children were not expected to do hard work until they were older. As they grew, they slowly took on more of the work of adults, by joining in.

### subtopic_3 — Water, Shelter and Tools
**Assistant:** Did the San really bury eggshells full of water?

**Tutor:** Yes. Ostrich eggshells are strong, and one shell can hold about a litre of water. The San made a small hole at one end, emptied the egg and ate it, then filled the shell with water and plugged the hole with grass or a stopper. They carried several of these in a net bag. In dry areas, people sometimes buried full eggshells in the sand at places they knew they would pass again. Months later, when the land was dry, they could dig up the water and drink it. This needed excellent memory and knowledge of the land, because the hiding places had no signs. Archaeologists have found pieces of ostrich eggshell flasks, some decorated with scratched patterns, at sites many thousands of years old, so people in southern Africa have used them for a very long time.
