### subtopic_1 — Small Groups and Equal Sharing
**Assistant:** Why did San hunters swap arrows with each other?

**Tutor:** Swapping arrows was a clever custom that helped to keep San society equal and friendly. Among some San groups, the meat of an animal belonged first to the person who owned the arrow that killed it, not to the person who shot it. That person then had the job of sharing out the meat. Because people lent and swapped arrows, the owner might be a young person, an old man who no longer hunted, or even a woman. This meant that the honour of sharing out meat moved around the group, and no single great hunter could become too important or too proud. It also meant that everyone felt they had a part in the hunt. Researchers who lived with San groups described this custom, and it shows how carefully the San thought about fairness.

### subtopic_2 — Decisions Without Chiefs
**Assistant:** Did the San have names for the stars?

**Tutor:** Yes. The San watched the night sky very closely, and they had names and stories for many stars and groups of stars. The stories were different in different groups. In the stories recorded from the Xam San in the 1870s, for example, the Milky Way was made when a girl threw wood ashes into the sky, so that people could find their way home at night. Some stars were seen as people or animals. The bright stars that we call Orion's Belt were seen by some San as three zebras, or as tortoises. The San also used the stars as a calendar. The rising of certain stars in the early morning told them that a new season was coming, and that certain plants would soon be ready to gather.

### subtopic_3 — San Beliefs
**Assistant:** Do San people still hold healing dances today?

**Tutor:** Yes, in some places they do. In parts of Namibia and Botswana especially, some San communities still hold healing dances, sometimes as part of everyday life, when someone is ill or when the community needs healing, and sometimes at special gatherings. The dances are very important to the people who take part, and they are not performances for visitors, although at some cultural centres San people may show parts of a dance and explain its meaning. Many San people today are also Christians or follow other religions, and some combine these with older beliefs. Like all cultures, San culture has changed over time, but many people are proud of their traditions and work to keep their songs, dances and knowledge alive for their children.
