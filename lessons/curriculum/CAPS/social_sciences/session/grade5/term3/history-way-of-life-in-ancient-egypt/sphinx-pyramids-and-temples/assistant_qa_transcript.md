### subtopic_1 — The Pyramids
**Assistant:** How do we know the workers were not slaves?

**Tutor:** For a long time, many people believed that the pyramids were built by huge crowds of slaves, partly because of stories told by later writers. But archaeologists who dug near the Giza pyramids from the 1990s onwards found new evidence. They found the remains of a town where the workers lived, with bakeries that made large amounts of bread, places for brewing beer, and the bones of many cattle, sheep and goats, which shows the workers were well fed. They also found the workers' graves close to the pyramids, which was an honour, with bread and beer left for their afterlife. Some skeletons show healed broken bones, which means injured workers were cared for. Writing left by work gangs gives their team names. Together, this shows that the builders were organised, respected workers. They worked very hard, and the work was dangerous, but they were not slaves.

### subtopic_2 — Building the Pyramids
**Assistant:** Why does the Sphinx have no nose?

**Tutor:** The Great Sphinx's nose is missing, and nobody knows exactly when or how it was lost. A popular story says that soldiers in Napoleon's French army shot it off with a cannon in about 1800, but this story is not true, because drawings made before Napoleon arrived already show the Sphinx without a nose. Some historians think, based on an old written account, that the nose was deliberately damaged hundreds of years ago, in about the 1300s. Experts who have studied the face have found marks that suggest tools were used to break the nose off on purpose. Wind, sand and weather over more than four thousand years have also worn away parts of the soft limestone. The Sphinx was buried up to its shoulders in sand for long periods, which may have protected its body. Today, experts work carefully to protect it from further damage.

### subtopic_3 — The Sphinx and the Temples
**Assistant:** How were the Abu Simbel temples moved?

**Tutor:** When Egypt built the Aswan High Dam in the 1960s, the water behind it was going to form a huge lake, Lake Nasser, which would drown many ancient monuments, including the two temples at Abu Simbel, carved into a cliff by the pharaoh Ramesses the Second more than three thousand years ago. Countries from around the world joined a campaign led by UNESCO to save them. Engineers cut the temples, including their giant statues, into more than a thousand huge blocks, some weighing many tonnes. Each block was numbered, lifted and moved to a higher place, about sixty-five metres up, away from the water. There, they were put back together exactly, inside an artificial hill, built to look like the original cliff. The work took about four years. This rescue helped to inspire the idea of World Heritage Sites, places so important that the whole world helps to protect them.
