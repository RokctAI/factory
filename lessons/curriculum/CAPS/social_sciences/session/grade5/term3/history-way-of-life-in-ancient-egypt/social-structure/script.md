# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers social structure in ancient Egypt. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in East London, in the Eastern Cape, making a wall display. They have cut a huge triangle out of brown paper and divided it into layers, like the levels of a pyramid. At the very top, in the small point, they stick one picture: a pharaoh wearing a tall crown. In the next layer, there are a few pictures of officials and priests. Below that, scribes with reed pens and papyrus, and soldiers with spears and shields. Then a wider layer of craftspeople and traders. And at the bottom, the widest layer of all, they stick picture after picture of farmers with hoes, sickles and baskets of grain, and, at the very base, servants and enslaved people. When it is finished, the learners see at a glance that there were very few people at the top and very many at the bottom. The big idea is that ancient Egyptian society was organised like a pyramid, with the pharaoh at the top and most people working as farmers at the bottom, and that each group had its own role and rights. Social structure in ancient Egypt is examinable.

## Subtopic: The Pharaoh and the Powerful

Social structure means the way a society is arranged into groups, from the most powerful to the least powerful.

At the top was the pharaoh, the king of Egypt. The word pharaoh first meant great house, the name for the royal palace, and later it was used for the king himself. The Egyptians believed the pharaoh was a god on Earth, the link between the gods and the people. He owned all the land, made the laws, led the army, collected taxes and was the chief priest, responsible for keeping order and pleasing the gods so that the Nile would flood and the harvest would be good.

Most pharaohs were men, but some women ruled as pharaoh. The most famous is Hatshepsut, who ruled about three and a half thousand years ago and sent trading expeditions and built great temples.

Below the pharaoh was the vizier, the chief minister, who ran the government and was the highest judge. Then came the nobles, who governed the regions of Egypt, and the priests, who looked after the temples and the gods. Priests were powerful and wealthy, because temples owned much land.

The questions for this section are with you now: the pharaoh, the vizier, nobles and priests.

## Subtopic: Scribes, Soldiers and Craftspeople

Scribes were people who could read and write. Most people in ancient Egypt could not, so scribes were very important and respected. They kept records of taxes, harvests, the flood and the pharaoh's laws, wrote letters, and copied religious texts. Boys from families who could afford it went to a school for scribes, where they trained for many years, copying signs over and over. A clever scribe could rise to a high position in the government.

Soldiers protected Egypt from its enemies, guarded the borders and went with the pharaoh on military campaigns. In peaceful times, soldiers sometimes helped with building projects.

Craftspeople were skilled workers. They included carpenters, stonemasons, potters, weavers, jewellers, goldsmiths, painters, sculptors and builders. They made the furniture, tools, pots, cloth, jewellery and statues that Egypt is famous for, and decorated the tombs and temples. Traders and merchants bought and sold goods, in Egypt and in other lands, bringing back wood, incense, ivory, gold and precious stones.

The questions for this section are with you now: scribes, soldiers, craftspeople and traders.

## Subtopic: Farmers, Servants, Women and Children

Most Egyptians were farmers. They worked the land, which belonged to the pharaoh, the temples or nobles, and gave a large part of their harvest as taxes. During the flood season, when the fields were under water, many farmers were called up to work on the pharaoh's building projects, such as temples and pyramids.

At the bottom were servants and enslaved people. Many enslaved people were prisoners captured in wars. They worked in homes, temples, fields and mines.

Women in ancient Egypt had more rights than women in many other ancient societies. They could own and inherit property, buy and sell, run businesses, make contracts and go to court. Most women ran the home and raised children, but some worked as weavers, bakers, musicians, dancers, priestesses and even officials. Still, the most powerful positions were mostly held by men.

Children helped their parents from a young age, and most boys learnt their father's trade, while most girls learnt from their mothers. Children played games with balls, dolls, spinning tops and board games. One popular board game was called senet, played by moving pieces along a board of thirty squares.

Families were important to the Egyptians. Paintings in tombs often show husbands and wives together, with their children, and the Egyptians wrote about loving and respecting their parents.

It was possible, but not common, to move up from one level to another, for example, by becoming a scribe.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking most Egyptians were rich; most were farmers who paid taxes. Two: thinking only men could be pharaoh; Hatshepsut was a female pharaoh. Three: thinking everyone could read; only scribes and some others could. Four: thinking Egyptian women had no rights; they could own property and go to court.

The questions for this section are with you now: farmers, servants, women and children.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the paper pyramid in East London — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: At the Top

The pharaoh was at the top. People believed he was a god on Earth. He owned all the land, made laws and led the army.

The vizier ran the government. Nobles ran the regions, and priests looked after the temples.

Hatshepsut was a woman who ruled as pharaoh.

Keep the one picture of the pharaoh at the point of the paper pyramid in mind and try the questions for this part: the pharaoh and the powerful.

## Subtopic: The Middle

Scribes could read and write. They kept records and trained for many years.

Soldiers protected Egypt. Craftspeople made furniture, jewellery, pots and statues. Traders brought goods from other lands.

Hold the scribes with their reed pens in mind and try this part's questions: scribes, soldiers and craftspeople.

## Subtopic: The Bottom

Most people were farmers who paid taxes with part of their harvest. In the flood season, many worked on the pharaoh's buildings. Servants and enslaved people were at the bottom. Women could own property and go to court.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Ancient Egyptian society was shaped like a pyramid. The pharaoh, believed to be a god on Earth, was at the top, then the vizier, nobles and priests, then scribes and soldiers, then craftspeople and traders, then the large group of farmers, and at the bottom, servants and enslaved people. Women had more rights than in many ancient societies, and Hatshepsut ruled as pharaoh.

Hold the wide bottom layer, full of farmers with baskets of grain, in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: farmers, women and children.
