# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers hieroglyphics, mathematics and the study of the stars in ancient Egypt. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Gqeberha, in the Eastern Cape, during a code-breaking morning. On each desk is a sheet of hieroglyphic signs with the sounds they stand for: a vulture, a reed leaf, a quail chick, a foot, a mouth, a lion and many more. Each learner writes their own name in hieroglyphics, from the sounds, inside an oval loop like the ones used for pharaohs' names. On the board, the teacher has written a sum in Egyptian number signs: a lotus flower, three coils of rope, two heel bones and five short strokes, and the learners work out that it means 1,325. For homework, the teacher asks them to go outside before dawn one morning, look towards the east, and find Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky, which was very important to the ancient Egyptians. The big idea is that the ancient Egyptians developed one of the world's earliest writing systems, a practical system of mathematics, and a calendar based on the sun, the stars and the Nile, and that these achievements helped them to run a great society. Hieroglyphics, mathematics and the stars are examinable.

## Subtopic: Hieroglyphics

Hieroglyphics is the name for the picture-writing of ancient Egypt. The word comes from Greek, meaning sacred carvings, because the Greeks saw it carved on temples and tombs. The Egyptians called it the words of the gods.

There were more than seven hundred signs. Some signs stood for sounds, like letters of our alphabet. Others stood for whole words or ideas. For example, a picture of a house could mean house. Some signs were added at the end of a word to show what kind of thing it was. There were no vowels written, and no spaces or full stops.

Hieroglyphics could be written in rows or columns, and from left to right or from right to left. To know which way to read, you look at the people and animals: they face the beginning of the line.

The names of pharaohs and queens were written inside an oval loop called a cartouche, which showed they were royal.

Hieroglyphics took a long time to draw, so scribes also used a quicker, joined-up form of writing for everyday records and letters on papyrus, called hieratic. Scribes wrote with reed pens and black and red ink.

After ancient Egypt ended, people forgot how to read hieroglyphics for more than a thousand years. Then, in 1799, French soldiers found the Rosetta Stone, a stone with the same message written in hieroglyphics, in another Egyptian script, and in ancient Greek, which scholars could read. Using it, a French scholar, Jean-François Champollion, worked out how to read hieroglyphics in 1822.

The questions for this section are with you now: hieroglyphics, cartouches and the Rosetta Stone.

## Subtopic: Mathematics

The Egyptians used mathematics every day: to count harvests and taxes, to measure land, to plan buildings, and to trade.

They counted in tens, like we do, but they had a different sign for each power of ten. A short stroke meant one. A heel bone meant ten. A coil of rope meant a hundred. A lotus flower meant a thousand. A finger meant ten thousand. A tadpole meant a hundred thousand. And a kneeling god with his arms raised meant a million. To write a number, scribes repeated each sign as many times as needed. So two heel bones and three strokes meant twenty-three. There was no sign for zero.

The Egyptians could add, subtract, multiply and divide, and they used fractions. They measured length in cubits. A cubit was the length from the elbow to the tip of the middle finger, about half a metre. The royal cubit was a fixed, official length, marked on measuring rods.

Measuring land was especially important. Every year, the flood washed away the boundaries between fields, so officials called rope-stretchers measured the fields again with knotted ropes, so that each family got its land back and paid the right tax. This helped the Egyptians to develop geometry, the mathematics of shapes, areas and angles. They also used it to plan the pyramids, whose sides are very nearly square and line up closely with the compass directions.

Old papyrus scrolls with mathematics problems and answers have survived, showing how scribes learnt.

The questions for this section are with you now: Egyptian numbers, measuring and geometry.

## Subtopic: The Stars and the Calendar

The Egyptians watched the sky carefully. They noticed that the bright star Sirius, which they called Sopdet, disappeared from the night sky for about seventy days each year. When it first appeared again, just before sunrise in the east, the Nile flood was about to begin. So this became the start of their new year.

They made a calendar of 365 days. It had twelve months of thirty days each, which makes 360 days, plus five extra days at the end of the year, which were holidays and celebrations of the birthdays of gods. This is very close to the real length of the year, and our own calendar of 365 days comes partly from the Egyptian one.

The Egyptians also divided the night into twelve hours, using groups of stars that rose one after another, and the day into twelve hours, using shadow clocks and sundials. Our twenty-four hours in a day come from this. They also used water clocks, which measured time by water dripping out of a container.

The Egyptians believed the sky was the home of gods, and that the sun, moon and stars affected people's lives. They made calendars of lucky and unlucky days. This belief that the sky controls people's lives is called astrology. It is not science, but it was an important part of their beliefs. At the same time, their careful observations of the sky were real science, which today we call astronomy.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking hieroglyphics are only pictures of things; many signs stand for sounds. Two: thinking the Egyptians had a sign for zero; they did not. Three: thinking our 365-day calendar has nothing to do with Egypt; it comes partly from the Egyptian calendar. Four: thinking astrology and astronomy are the same; astronomy is the science of the sky, astrology is a belief.

The questions for this section are with you now: Sirius, the calendar, hours and astrology.
