### subtopic_1 — Physicians and Their Books
**Assistant:** Did Egyptian medicine actually work?

**Tutor:** Some of it did, and some of it did not. On the good side, Egyptian physicians examined patients carefully, which is still the first step in medicine today. They cleaned and closed wounds, used linen bandages, and set broken bones with splints, which really helped healing. Honey, which they put on wounds, does help to stop germs, and some modern hospitals still use special medical honey. Some of their plant medicines contain substances that can ease pain or fight infection. On the other side, many remedies were based on beliefs rather than evidence, and some used ingredients that would not help, or could even make wounds worse, such as animal dung. Spells and amulets could not cure disease, although they may have comforted patients. Overall, historians see Egyptian medicine as an important early step, because physicians wrote down what they did, so that others could learn and build on it.

### subtopic_2 — Diseases and Treatments
**Assistant:** How did mummification help them learn about the body?

**Tutor:** To make a mummy, embalmers made a cut in the side of the body and removed the lungs, liver, stomach and intestines, which were dried and stored, often in special containers called canopic jars. They usually left the heart in the body, because they believed it would be needed in the afterlife, and they removed the brain, often through the nose, because they did not think it was important. Doing this many times gave embalmers knowledge of what the organs looked like and where they were. However, embalmers and physicians were usually different people, and the Egyptians did not cut open bodies just to study them, as doctors do today. So their knowledge of anatomy was limited, and their ideas about how organs worked were often wrong. Still, they were among the first people in history to name and describe many organs in writing.

### subtopic_3 — The Body and the Examination
**Assistant:** What is bilharzia, and is it still a problem?

**Tutor:** Bilharzia, also called schistosomiasis, is a disease caused by tiny worms. Their young live in certain freshwater snails, and then swim out into the water. If a person swims, wades or washes in that water, the young worms can go through the skin and live inside the body, where they can damage organs over time. Scientists have found signs of bilharzia in Egyptian mummies thousands of years old, so it was a problem for farmers working in flooded fields and canals. It is still a problem in many parts of Africa today, including some rivers and dams in the north and east of South Africa, such as in Limpopo, Mpumalanga and KwaZulu-Natal. It can be treated with medicine. To stay safe, avoid swimming in slow-moving rivers or dams where bilharzia is known to occur, and use clean, safe water for drinking and washing.
