### subtopic_1 — Hieroglyphics
**Assistant:** How can I write my name in hieroglyphics?

**Tutor:** First, say your name slowly and break it into its sounds. Then find a hieroglyphic sign for each sound. For example, a reed leaf is often used for an i sound, a foot for b, a mouth for r, an owl for m, water for n, a lion for l, and a folded cloth or a door bolt for s. The Egyptians did not usually write vowels, but for names from other languages, they used certain signs, such as the vulture for an a sound and the quail chick for a u or w sound. Write the signs in order, from left to right, with people and animals facing left, towards the start. Then draw an oval around your name: a cartouche, like a pharaoh's. Remember, this is a simplified way to do it. Real hieroglyphic spelling had many rules, and experts still study the details.

### subtopic_2 — Mathematics
**Assistant:** Why didn't the Egyptians need a zero?

**Tutor:** In our number system, the position of a digit tells you its value. In 205, the 2 means two hundreds because it is in the hundreds place, and the zero holds the tens place empty, so that it is not confused with 25. So we need a zero. The Egyptian system worked differently. Each power of ten had its own sign: strokes for ones, heel bones for tens, coils of rope for hundreds. To write 205, a scribe simply drew two coils of rope and five strokes, with no heel bones. Because each sign already showed its own value, there was no need for a sign to hold an empty place. The downside was that big numbers needed many signs: 999 took twenty-seven signs. Our system, with ten digits and a zero, is much quicker, and it came from India, through Arab scholars, much later.

### subtopic_3 — The Stars and the Calendar
**Assistant:** Was the Egyptian calendar perfect?

**Tutor:** It was very good, but not perfect. The real time it takes the Earth to go around the sun is about 365 and a quarter days. The Egyptian calendar had exactly 365 days, so every year it fell about a quarter of a day behind the real seasons. That does not sound like much, but after four years, it was a whole day behind, and after many hundreds of years, the calendar months had drifted right around the seasons, so that a month named after the flood could fall in the harvest season. The Egyptians knew this and still used the stars and the river to keep track of the real seasons. Much later, the Romans, with help from an astronomer from Egypt, added a leap day every four years to fix the problem. Our calendar still uses leap years today, with an extra day, the 29th of February.
