### subtopic_1 — The Pharaoh and the Powerful
**Assistant:** Why was the society shaped like a pyramid?

**Tutor:** Because there were very few people with great power and wealth, and very many ordinary people who did most of the work. If you drew a block for every person, with the most powerful at the top, you would get a shape with a tiny point and a huge, wide base, like a pyramid. At the point was one person, the pharaoh. Just below were a small number of officials, nobles and priests, who helped the pharaoh to rule. Then came the scribes, soldiers, craftspeople and traders, who were more numerous. At the bottom were the farmers, who were by far the largest group, and the servants and enslaved people. The farmers' work fed everyone else, and their taxes paid for the government, the temples and the great buildings. Historians use this picture of a pyramid to explain the structure of many societies.

### subtopic_2 — Scribes, Soldiers and Craftspeople
**Assistant:** What did a scribe's training look like?

**Tutor:** Becoming a scribe was hard work. Boys usually started school at a young age, around five to ten years old, at a school attached to a temple or a government office. Most came from families that could afford to let their sons study instead of working. They learnt to write hundreds of signs, first by copying them over and over, often on pieces of broken pottery or flat pieces of limestone, because papyrus was expensive. They used a reed pen dipped in black or red ink. They also learnt mathematics, to measure fields and count harvests and taxes. Training could take many years. Teachers were strict, and some old writings by scribes tell boys to work hard and not to be lazy. A trained scribe had a respected, comfortable job, did not have to do heavy work in the fields, and could rise to a high position.

### subtopic_3 — Farmers, Servants, Women and Children
**Assistant:** Was everyone at the bottom of the pyramid enslaved?

**Tutor:** No. Most people at the bottom of Egyptian society were free farmers, not enslaved people. Farmers had families, homes and some belongings, and they could take a problem to court. But life was hard: they worked long hours, gave a large part of their harvest as taxes, and could be called up to work on the pharaoh's projects during the flood season. Enslaved people were fewer. Many were prisoners captured in wars, and some people became enslaved because they owed debts. They worked in homes, temples, fields and mines, and had very few rights, although some could be freed. Historians still study and discuss the details, because the situation changed over Egypt's very long history. It is important to remember that, like many ancient societies, Egypt's great achievements were built on the hard work of many ordinary people.
