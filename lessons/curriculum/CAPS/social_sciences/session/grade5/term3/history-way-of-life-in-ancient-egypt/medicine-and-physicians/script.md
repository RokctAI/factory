# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers medicine and physicians in ancient Egypt: diseases, anatomy, physiology and clinical examinations. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Welkom, in the Free State, during a visit from the school nurse. A learner who fell on the netball court sits on a chair while the nurse asks him questions: where does it hurt, how did it happen, and when? She looks carefully at his grazed knee, gently presses around it, and feels the pulse in his wrist, counting the beats while watching her watch. Then she cleans the graze, puts on some ointment and covers it with a bandage. The teacher points out that every step the nurse took, asking, looking, touching, feeling the pulse, deciding and treating, was described by physicians in Egypt more than three and a half thousand years ago, on papyrus scrolls that still exist. The big idea is that ancient Egyptian physicians were among the earliest doctors in the world to write down careful methods of examining patients and treating illness and injury, although their medicine was mixed with religion and magic, and some of their ideas about the body were wrong. Egyptian medicine is examinable.

## Subtopic: Physicians and Their Books

A physician is a doctor. Ancient Egypt had trained physicians, and they were respected members of society. Many trained at a place attached to a temple, called the House of Life, where scrolls were kept and copied.

Some physicians were specialists, who treated only one part of the body, such as the eyes, the teeth or the stomach. Records mention a woman called Peseshet, who lived more than four thousand years ago and had the title lady overseer of physicians.

The most famous physician was Imhotep, the official who designed the Step Pyramid. Long after his death, he was honoured as a god of healing.

We know about Egyptian medicine mainly from medical papyrus scrolls. The Ebers Papyrus, about three and a half thousand years old, contains hundreds of remedies and spells for many illnesses. The Edwin Smith Papyrus, which is even older, describes forty-eight cases of injuries, such as wounds and broken bones, in a very organised way. Both are named after the modern collectors who bought them.

We also learn from mummies. Scientists use X-rays and scans to study them, and they can find signs of disease, broken bones, tooth problems and even old operations.

The questions for this section are with you now: physicians, specialists and medical papyri.

## Subtopic: Diseases and Treatments

Many illnesses in ancient Egypt were linked to the environment. Eye diseases were common, because of sand, dust, bright sun and flies. Many people had worms and other parasites from drinking or wading in the water of the Nile and its canals. One of these, which causes a disease called bilharzia, has been found in mummies. Teeth were often badly worn down, because bread contained tiny bits of sand and grit from the grinding stones. Workers suffered injuries such as broken bones, wounds and back problems.

Egyptian physicians used many treatments. They cleaned wounds and closed them with stitches or strips of linen, and covered them with honey, which we now know helps to stop germs from growing. They set broken bones and held them still with splints made of wood or bark, wrapped in linen. They made medicines from plants, such as garlic, onions, juniper and willow, from minerals, and from animal products, mixed into drinks, ointments and pills. Some remedies worked well, while others did not work, or were even harmful.

Medicine was mixed with religion and magic. Egyptians believed some illnesses were caused by gods, evil spirits or the dead, so physicians often said spells and prayers along with giving medicines, and patients wore amulets, which are small charms, for protection.

The questions for this section are with you now: common diseases and how they were treated.

## Subtopic: The Body and the Examination

Anatomy means the study of the parts of the body. Physiology means the study of how the body works.

The Egyptians learnt about the inside of the body partly from mummification, when the organs were removed, and from treating injuries. They knew about the heart, lungs, liver, stomach, intestines and brain. The Edwin Smith Papyrus is one of the oldest known writings to describe the brain.

Their ideas about how the body works were partly right and partly wrong. They believed the heart was the centre of the body, and they knew that the pulse was linked to the heart: they said the heart speaks in the vessels. But they also believed the heart was where people thought and felt, and they thought the body was full of channels, carrying blood, air, tears and waste, which could become blocked and cause illness.

The clinical examination, which means examining a sick person, was carefully described. The physician asked the patient questions, looked closely at the body, felt it with the hands, felt the pulse, and sometimes smelled the wound. Then he decided what the problem was, which is called the diagnosis, and gave a treatment. The Edwin Smith Papyrus even says, for each case, whether it is an illness the physician will treat, will try to treat, or cannot treat.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking Egyptian medicine was only magic; physicians also used careful examinations and practical treatments. Two: thinking the Egyptians knew the brain was used for thinking; they believed the heart was. Three: thinking all their remedies worked; some did, but others did not or were harmful. Four: thinking only men were physicians; records mention a woman, Peseshet.

The questions for this section are with you now: anatomy, physiology and the clinical examination.

## Subtopic: Egyptian Doctors

Physicians were trained doctors. Some were specialists for eyes, teeth or the stomach. A woman, Peseshet, led physicians.

Imhotep was later honoured as a god of healing.

We learn from medical papyri, like the Ebers and Edwin Smith Papyri, and from mummies.

Keep the nurse counting the pulse in the learner's wrist in mind and try the questions for this part: physicians and their books.

## Subtopic: Illness and Cures

Sand and flies caused eye disease. Nile water gave people worms and bilharzia. Grit in bread wore down teeth.

Physicians used stitches, honey on wounds, splints for broken bones and plant medicines. They also used spells and amulets.

Hold the clean bandage on the grazed knee in mind and try this part's questions: diseases and treatments.

## Subtopic: Examining Patients

Physicians asked questions, looked, touched and felt the pulse. Then they made a diagnosis and gave treatment. They thought the heart did the thinking.
