### subtopic_1 — Weather and Climate
**Assistant:** Why do high places get colder?

**Tutor:** It seems strange, because high places are closer to the sun, but the sun is so far away that a few kilometres makes no difference. What matters is the air. The sun's rays pass through the air without heating it much. Instead, they heat the ground, and the ground then warms the air just above it. Also, air higher up is thinner, so it cannot hold heat as well. As air rises, it spreads out and cools. So, on average, the temperature drops by roughly six degrees Celsius for every thousand metres you climb. Johannesburg is about 1,750 metres above sea level, so in winter it is much colder than Durban, which is at sea level, even though Johannesburg is further north. And the top of the Drakensberg, over 3,000 metres high, is colder still, which is why it gets snow.

### subtopic_2 — Climates of the East and the Interior
**Assistant:** What does Mediterranean climate mean?

**Tutor:** The Mediterranean Sea lies between southern Europe, North Africa and western Asia. Countries around it, such as Italy, Greece, Spain, Morocco and Tunisia, have a special kind of climate: hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters. Geographers named this kind of climate after that sea. A few other places in the world have the same kind of climate, even though they are far from the Mediterranean. These include parts of California in the United States, central Chile, south-western Australia, and the south-western Cape of South Africa, including Cape Town. In all these places, winter rain comes from cold fronts, and summers are dry. That is why the Western Cape is good for growing grapes, olives, apples and wheat, which also grow well around the Mediterranean, and why its natural vegetation, fynbos, is adapted to wet winters and dry summers.

### subtopic_3 — Climates of the South and the West
**Assistant:** Is South Africa's climate changing?

**Tutor:** Scientists who study the climate have found that, around the world, average temperatures have been rising over the last hundred years or so, mostly because people burn coal, oil and gas, which add gases to the air that trap heat. This is called climate change. In South Africa, records show that average temperatures have also gone up, and hot days have become more common in many places. Scientists also expect changes in rainfall: some areas may get drier, and storms and droughts may become more severe. These changes happen slowly, over many years, which is why we use climate records, not one hot day or one storm, to study them. Many people, including scientists, governments, businesses and young people, are working on ways to reduce climate change, for example by using more solar and wind power, and on ways to adapt, such as saving water.
