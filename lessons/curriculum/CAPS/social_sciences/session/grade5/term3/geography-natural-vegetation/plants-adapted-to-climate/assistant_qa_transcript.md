### subtopic_1 — What Is Natural Vegetation?
**Assistant:** Why is fynbos so special?

**Tutor:** Fynbos is one of the most special kinds of vegetation in the world. It grows mainly in a small area of the south-western and southern Cape, yet it has an enormous number of different plant species, many thousands, and most of them grow nowhere else on Earth. Scientists have divided the world's plants into six big floral kingdoms, and the Cape Floral Kingdom, made up mostly of fynbos, is by far the smallest, but one of the richest in plant species for its size. Parts of it are a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Fynbos includes proteas, ericas, restios and many bulbs, which grow into flowers such as gladioli, freesias and lilies, many of which are now grown in gardens around the world. Fynbos is under threat from farming, towns and invasive alien plants, so it is important to protect it.

### subtopic_2 — Vegetation of Wet and Summer Rainfall Areas
**Assistant:** Why do pine trees and wattles cause problems?

**Tutor:** Pine trees, many kinds of wattle and some gum trees were brought to South Africa from other countries, mostly from Australia, Europe and the Americas, to grow wood and for shade. Some of them spread far beyond where they were planted, into the natural veld, mountains and riverbanks. They have no natural enemies here, such as the insects and diseases that keep them in check in their home countries, so they spread very fast. They grow thick and tall, shading out indigenous plants and destroying the homes of animals. They also use much more water than fynbos and grassland, so they reduce the water that flows into rivers and dams. When they burn, the fires are hotter and more damaging. South Africa has programmes that employ people to clear invasive alien plants, which helps to save water and protect indigenous plants, and creates jobs.

### subtopic_3 — Vegetation of Dry and Winter Rainfall Areas
**Assistant:** How can a plant survive in the Karoo for months without rain?

**Tutor:** Karoo plants have many clever adaptations. Succulents, such as aloes and vygies, store water in thick, fleshy leaves or stems, like a water bottle, and use it slowly during dry months. Their leaves are often small, round or covered in wax or tiny hairs, which reduce water loss and reflect sunlight. Some plants open the tiny holes in their leaves, which let water escape, only at night, when it is cool. Many have roots that spread out wide just under the surface, so they can soak up water quickly after even a short shower. Others have very deep roots. Some plants drop their leaves in very dry times and grow new ones after rain. And many small plants survive the dry season as seeds or bulbs underground, waiting for rain before they grow, flower and make new seeds very quickly, before the soil dries out again.
