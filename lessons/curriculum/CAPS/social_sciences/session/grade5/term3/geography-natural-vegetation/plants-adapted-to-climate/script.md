# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the concept of natural vegetation, and the links between natural vegetation and climate in South Africa. The anchor is a Grade 5 class on an outing to Kirstenbosch National Botanical Garden, on the slopes of Table Mountain in Cape Town. The garden is famous for growing plants that are indigenous to southern Africa, which means they grow naturally in this part of the world. The learners walk past bushes of fynbos, with tough little leaves and bright protea flowers as big as a fist. They walk across a raised walkway through the tops of tall forest trees, where it is shady and cool. Then they enter a glasshouse that feels as dry as a desert, full of thick-leaved succulents, spiky aloes and a fat-trunked baobab. The guide explains that all these plants are South African, but in the wild, they grow in very different places, because each is adapted to a different climate. The big idea is that natural vegetation is the plant life that grows on its own in a place, that South Africa has several kinds of natural vegetation, and that each kind is linked to its climate, especially its rainfall and temperature. Natural vegetation and climate are examinable.

## Subtopic: What Is Natural Vegetation?

Natural vegetation is the plant life that grows naturally in an area, without being planted or looked after by people. It includes trees, shrubs, which are bushes, grasses, flowers and succulents.

Natural vegetation is different from the plants people grow, such as crops in fields, gardens, plantations of pine and gum trees, and lawns. These are not natural vegetation, because people planted them.

Indigenous plants are plants that grow naturally in a particular country or region. Plants that people brought from other parts of the world are called alien or exotic plants. Some alien plants, such as some kinds of wattle and pine, spread quickly and push out indigenous plants, and they use a lot of water. These are called invasive alien plants.

Scientists group natural vegetation into large areas with similar plants and climate, called biomes. South Africa has several biomes, including grassland, savanna, fynbos, forest, thicket, Nama Karoo, Succulent Karoo and desert.

The natural vegetation of a place depends mostly on its climate: how much rain falls, in which season, and how hot or cold it gets. Soil, height above sea level and fire also play a part.

## Subtopic: Vegetation of Wet and Summer Rainfall Areas

Forest grows where it is wet all year and not too cold. In South Africa, natural forests are small and scattered, for example, along the Garden Route near Knysna, on parts of the escarpment, and along the KwaZulu-Natal coast. Forests have tall trees with broad leaves, which need plenty of water. The real yellowwood, South Africa's national tree, grows in these forests.

Grassland grows on the high, cool Highveld and in the Drakensberg foothills, where summers bring rain and winters are cold and dry, with frost. Grasses are adapted to this climate. In winter, the leaves dry out and turn brown, but the plants stay alive underground and in their roots. When the spring rains come, new green leaves grow from the base. Frost and fire do not kill them. Frost is one reason why few trees grow on the open grassland.

Savanna, also called bushveld, grows in the warm, summer rainfall areas of the north and east, such as Limpopo, the Lowveld and North West. It is grassland with scattered trees and bushes, such as acacias, which are thorn trees, and marulas. Many savanna trees have small leaves, which lose less water, and thorns to protect them from animals. The baobab, which grows in the hot, dry north of Limpopo, stores water in its huge, fat trunk.

## Subtopic: Vegetation of Dry and Winter Rainfall Areas

Fynbos grows in the south-western and southern Cape, where it rains in winter and summers are dry, hot and windy. Fynbos means fine bush, because many of its plants have small, thin, tough leaves. Hard, small leaves lose little water in the dry summer. Proteas, ericas, which are small heaths, and reeds called restios are typical fynbos plants. The king protea is South Africa's national flower. Fynbos is adapted to fire. Natural fires every ten to twenty years or so clear old bushes, and the seeds of many proteas are released after a fire, so that new plants grow in the ash.

The Karoo, Namaqualand and the dry west have low, scattered bushes and succulents. Succulents store water in thick, fleshy leaves or stems, so they can survive long dry periods. Many have a waxy or hairy surface, which reduces water loss. Aloes and vygies are examples. Many plants have long roots that spread wide to catch water quickly when it rains, or deep roots to reach water underground. In Namaqualand, many plants grow, flower and make seeds quickly after winter rain, which is why the area bursts into flowers in spring.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking pine plantations are natural vegetation; people planted them. Two: thinking grasses die in winter; they rest underground and grow again in spring. Three: thinking fire always destroys fynbos; many fynbos plants need fire to renew. Four: thinking succulents need lots of water; they store water to survive drought.

The questions for this section are with you now: fynbos, succulents and plants of dry places.

## Subtopic: Plants on Their Own

Natural vegetation is plants that grow on their own. Crops, gardens and plantations are not.

Indigenous plants grow naturally here. Alien plants came from elsewhere, and some spread and use too much water.

Keep the bright protea flowers in mind and try the questions for this part: what natural vegetation is.

## Subtopic: Forest, Grass and Savanna

Forest needs rain all year. Grassland grows on the cold Highveld, and turns brown in winter but grows again in spring.

Savanna is grass with scattered trees, in warm summer rain areas. Thorn trees have small leaves. The baobab stores water in its trunk.

Hold the shady walkway through the treetops in mind and try this part's questions: forest, grassland and savanna.

## Subtopic: Fynbos and Succulents

Fynbos grows where winters are wet and summers are dry. It has small, tough leaves and needs fire. Succulents in the dry Karoo store water in thick leaves, and many have deep or wide roots.
