# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers rainfall distribution maps of South Africa. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Durban, in KwaZulu-Natal, who have pen pals at a school in Springbok, in Namaqualand in the Northern Cape. The Durban learners have written about green hills, sugar cane fields, heavy summer storms and the humid air that makes their school shirts stick to their backs. The letters from Springbok describe rocky hills, low bushes, a dry riverbed that only flows after rare rains, and how everyone at school knows how to save water. The teacher pins a large rainfall distribution map of South Africa on the board, shaded in colours from dark blue and green in the east to pale yellow and orange in the west, and the class puts one sticker on Durban and another on Springbok. The big idea is that rain is not spread evenly across South Africa: the east gets much more rain than the west, and a rainfall distribution map shows this pattern clearly. Rainfall distribution maps are examinable.

## Subtopic: Reading a Rainfall Map

Distribution means how something is spread out over an area. A rainfall distribution map shows how much rain different places get in an average year.

The map is divided into areas, each shaded in a different colour or pattern. The key tells you what each colour means. Each colour stands for a range of rainfall in millimetres per year. For example, the key might show that orange means less than 200 millimetres a year, yellow means 200 to 400 millimetres, light green means 400 to 600, dark green means 600 to 1,000, and blue means more than 1,000 millimetres a year.

To read the map, find the place you want, look at its colour, and then find that colour in the key. On the class map, Durban is in a dark green area, so it gets about 1,000 millimetres a year. Springbok is in an orange area, so it gets less than 200 millimetres a year. That means Durban gets more than five times as much rain as Springbok.

When you compare two places, always use the same map and the same key, and give the amounts in millimetres, not just wet or dry.

Some rainfall maps use lines instead of colours. Each line joins places that get the same amount of rain, and the number on the line tells you how much.

The rainfall on the map is an average: the amount a place gets in a normal year, worked out from many years of records. In a particular year, it may be more or less.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading a rainfall distribution map and its key.

## Subtopic: Wet East, Dry West

The most important pattern on the map is this: rainfall decreases from east to west. The east of South Africa is much wetter than the west.

The wettest places include parts of the KwaZulu-Natal coast, the Drakensberg, and parts of the escarpment in Mpumalanga and Limpopo, where some places get more than 1,000 millimetres a year. The driest places are in the west, in Namaqualand, the Richtersveld and along the west coast, where some places get less than 200 millimetres, and a few get less than 100 millimetres a year.

Why is the east wetter? The Indian Ocean, along the east coast, is warm, so a lot of water evaporates from it into the air. Winds from the east carry this warm, moist air over the land. When the moist air is pushed up over the Great Escarpment and the Drakensberg, it cools, clouds form, and rain falls. By the time the air has crossed the country to the west, it has lost most of its moisture.

Why is the west drier? Along the west coast, the cold Benguela Current flows in the Atlantic Ocean. Cold water does not evaporate much, so the air above it is dry. Cold sea also makes fog along the coast, but few rain clouds.

The questions for this section are with you now: why rainfall decreases from east to west.

## Subtopic: A Dry Country

Overall, South Africa is a dry country. The average rainfall for the whole country is often given as about 450 millimetres a year, which is only about half of the world average.

Rainfall is also unreliable in many places. This means that the amount changes a lot from year to year: one year can bring floods, and the next year can bring drought.

Rainfall affects where and how people live. In the wetter east, there are more farms growing crops, more forests and plantations, and more large towns and cities. In the dry west, there are fewer people, and farmers mostly keep sheep and goats on large farms, because there is not enough rain for most crops without irrigation. Irrigation means bringing water to crops from rivers, dams or boreholes.

Because water is scarce, South Africa builds dams to store water, and moves water through pipes and tunnels from wetter areas to drier ones. Everyone should save water.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the west of South Africa is wetter; rainfall decreases from east to west. Two: reading the map without the key; the key tells you what each colour means. Three: thinking the map shows today's rain; it shows the average yearly rainfall. Four: thinking South Africa is a wet country; its average rainfall is only about half the world average.

The questions for this section are with you now: South Africa as a dry country and how rainfall affects people.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the pen pals in Durban and Springbok — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Map and Key

A rainfall map shows how much rain places get in an average year.

Each colour means a range of millimetres. The key tells you which.

Durban gets about 1,000 millimetres a year. Springbok gets less than 200.

Keep the two stickers on the rainfall map in mind and try the questions for this part: reading a rainfall map.

## Subtopic: East and West

The east is wet. The west is dry. Rain decreases from east to west.

The warm Indian Ocean gives the east moist air and rain. The cold Benguela Current makes the west dry.

Hold the green hills of Durban and the rocky hills of Springbok in mind and try this part's questions: wet east, dry west.

## Subtopic: Save Water

South Africa is a dry country. Its average rain is only about half of the world average. Rain can change a lot from year to year. Dams store water, and everyone should save it.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A rainfall distribution map shows average yearly rainfall, using colours explained in a key, in millimetres. In South Africa, rainfall decreases from east to west. The east is wetter because the warm Indian Ocean gives moist air that rises over the escarpment, while the west is dry because of the cold Benguela Current. South Africa is a dry country, with unreliable rain, so we must save water.

Hold the letters about the dry riverbed in Springbok in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: a dry country.
