# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the Nile River and how it influenced settlement in ancient Egypt. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Musina, in Limpopo, not far from the Limpopo River. In the classroom, the learners have filled a long tray with pale sand and dug a narrow channel down the middle to stand for the Nile. They sprinkle dark potting soil along both sides of the channel and push in a few green twigs as fields. Then, slowly, a learner pours water from a jug at one end. The water fills the channel, spills over its banks, and spreads across the low land on each side. When the water drains away, the dark soil is left wet and spread out along the river, while the sand beyond stays dry and pale. The teacher shows a satellite photograph of Egypt: a thin green ribbon of farmland winding through a vast yellow desert, widening into a green fan at the top where the river meets the sea. The big idea is that the Nile made life possible in the desert, and that its yearly flood, its rich soil, its water and its use as a highway decided where the ancient Egyptians lived and how their society grew. The Nile and settlement are examinable.

## Subtopic: A River in the Desert

The Nile is the longest river in Africa, and one of the two longest rivers in the world. It flows north through Egypt into the Mediterranean Sea.

The Nile has two main branches. The White Nile flows from the region of Lake Victoria in East Africa. The Blue Nile flows from Lake Tana in the highlands of Ethiopia. They join at Khartoum, in Sudan, and the river then flows through the desert into Egypt.

Egypt gets almost no rain. Without the Nile, it would be almost entirely desert. Along the river, there is a narrow strip of green, fertile land, often only a few kilometres wide, with desert on both sides. Near the sea, the river splits into many branches and spreads out into a wide, flat, fertile area shaped like a triangle, called the delta.

The ancient Egyptians called their land Kemet, which means the black land, because of the dark, fertile soil along the river. They called the desert Deshret, the red land. The black land was where people lived and farmed. The red land was dry, hot and dangerous, but it gave them stone, gold and other minerals, and protected them from enemies.

The Greek traveller Herodotus called Egypt the gift of the Nile, because without the river, Egypt as he knew it could not have existed.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Nile, the black land and the red land.

## Subtopic: The Yearly Flood

Every year, in summer, heavy rains fell in the highlands of Ethiopia, far to the south. The water rushed down the Blue Nile and other rivers and into the main Nile, and the river rose and flooded the land along its banks in Egypt. This is called the inundation, which means flooding.

When the floodwater went down, it left behind a layer of thick, black mud called silt, full of goodness for plants. This silt made the soil very fertile, so farmers could grow good crops year after year.

The Egyptians divided their year into three seasons, based on the river. Akhet was the flood season, when the fields were under water. Peret was the growing season, when farmers planted seeds in the wet silt and the crops grew. Shemu was the harvest season, when crops were gathered before the next flood.

The flood had to be just right. Too little water meant poor harvests and hunger. Too much water meant that villages and dykes were washed away. The Egyptians measured the height of the flood with nilometers, which were steps or marked walls going down into the river, and used the readings to predict the harvest and decide taxes.

The questions for this section are with you now: the inundation, silt and the three seasons.

## Subtopic: Living Along the River

Because of the Nile, almost all ancient Egyptians lived close to the river, in villages, towns and cities along its banks and in the delta. The capital cities, such as Memphis and Thebes, were on the river.

Farmers grew wheat and barley, which were made into bread and beer, the main foods of the people. They grew flax, which was made into linen cloth, and vegetables such as onions, garlic, lentils and beans, and fruits such as dates and figs. They kept cattle, goats, sheep, ducks and geese, and caught fish in the river.

Farmers dug canals to carry water to their fields, and used a shaduf to lift water from the river. A shaduf is a long pole on a frame, with a bucket on one end and a heavy weight on the other, so that lifting water is easier.

The Nile was also Egypt's main highway. Boats carried people, food, animals and huge stone blocks for building. Boats floated north with the current, and sailed south, because the wind usually blows from the north.

Papyrus reeds that grew along the river were used to make paper, boats, baskets, mats and sandals.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the flood was a disaster; it brought the silt that made farming possible. Two: thinking the Egyptians lived all over the desert; they lived mostly in the narrow strip along the river. Three: thinking the flood came from rain in Egypt; it came from summer rains in the Ethiopian highlands. Four: thinking Kemet meant the red land; it meant the black land, the fertile soil.

The questions for this section are with you now: farming, the shaduf, boats and papyrus along the Nile.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the sand tray in Musina — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Green in the Desert

The Nile flows north through the desert into the Mediterranean.

Along the river is a narrow strip of black, fertile soil. The Egyptians called it Kemet, the black land. The desert was Deshret, the red land.

Herodotus called Egypt the gift of the Nile.

Keep the thin green ribbon on the satellite photograph in mind and try the questions for this part: a river in the desert.

## Subtopic: The Flood

Summer rain in Ethiopia made the Nile flood every year.

The flood left rich black silt on the fields.

There were three seasons: Akhet, the flood; Peret, growing; and Shemu, harvest. Nilometers measured the flood.

Hold the water spilling over the banks of the sand tray in mind and try this part's questions: the yearly flood.

## Subtopic: Life by the River

People lived along the river. They grew wheat, barley and flax, and used a shaduf to lift water. Boats carried goods up and down the Nile, and papyrus made paper.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Nile flows north through the desert, and made a narrow strip of fertile black land, Kemet, where the Egyptians lived. Every year, summer rain in Ethiopia caused a flood, the inundation, which left rich silt. The year had three seasons: flood, growing and harvest. Farmers grew wheat, barley and flax, used canals and the shaduf, and the river was a highway for boats.

Hold the dark soil left behind when the water drained away in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: living along the river.
