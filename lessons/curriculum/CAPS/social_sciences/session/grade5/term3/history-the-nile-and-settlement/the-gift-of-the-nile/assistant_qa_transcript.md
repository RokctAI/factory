### subtopic_1 — A River in the Desert
**Assistant:** Does the Nile still flood every year?

**Tutor:** No, not any more. In the 1960s, Egypt built a huge dam across the Nile near the city of Aswan, in the south of the country, called the Aswan High Dam. It was finished in 1970. Behind it, a vast lake formed, called Lake Nasser, which stores the floodwater. The dam controls the river, so there is no longer a yearly flood in Egypt. This has good and bad results. The good: farmers have water all year, so they can grow two or three crops a year, towns are safe from floods, and the dam makes electricity. The bad: the rich silt that used to fertilise the fields is now trapped behind the dam, so farmers must use fertilisers, and some land near the sea is being worn away. Ancient temples were also at risk of being flooded by the lake, and some had to be moved.

### subtopic_2 — The Yearly Flood
**Assistant:** Why did the Egyptians have three seasons instead of four?

**Tutor:** Our four seasons, summer, autumn, winter and spring, are based mostly on temperature. But in Egypt, what mattered most for life was not temperature but the river. Egypt is warm or hot for most of the year and gets almost no rain, so the big change during the year was the rise and fall of the Nile. So the Egyptians divided their year into three seasons, each about four months long, based on what the river and the farmers were doing. Akhet was the flood, when the fields were under water and many farmers had time to work on building projects for the pharaoh. Peret was the growing season, when the water went down and seeds were sown in the wet silt. Shemu was the harvest, when crops were gathered and the river was at its lowest, before the next flood.

### subtopic_3 — Living Along the River
**Assistant:** How does a shaduf make lifting water easier?

**Tutor:** A shaduf is a simple but clever machine, a kind of lever. It has a long wooden pole balanced on a frame, a bit like a see-saw. At one end of the pole hangs a bucket, and at the other end is a heavy weight, such as a lump of clay or a stone. The farmer pulls the bucket end down into the river to fill it. Then the heavy weight on the other end does most of the work, swinging the full bucket up. The farmer then turns the pole and empties the water into a canal or a field. Because the weight helps, a farmer can lift many buckets of water in a day without getting too tired. Shadufs were used in ancient Egypt thousands of years ago, and some farmers in parts of Africa and Asia still use them today.
