### subtopic_1 — Where and When
**Assistant:** Were the ancient Egyptians really Africans?

**Tutor:** Yes. Ancient Egypt grew up in north-eastern Africa, along the Nile, and its people were Africans who had lived in the Nile Valley and the lands around it for a very long time. For many years, some writers from other parts of the world did not want to give Africa credit for this great civilisation, and they described ancient Egypt as if it were separate from the rest of the continent. Today, historians recognise that ancient Egypt was an African society, connected to its African neighbours, especially Nubia and the kingdom of Kush to the south, through trade, marriage, travel, ideas and sometimes war. Like many places, Egypt was also connected to people from western Asia and the Mediterranean, and people from different places lived there over its long history. Studying ancient Egypt is part of studying Africa's rich history, which includes Mapungubwe, Great Zimbabwe and many others.

### subtopic_2 — An African Society
**Assistant:** What does BC mean on a timeline about Egypt?

**Tutor:** Many history books count years from the traditional date of the birth of Jesus, which is about two thousand years ago. Years before that date are written with BC or BCE, which mean before Christ or before the Common Era. Years after it are written with AD or CE. The tricky part is that BC years count backwards: 3000 BC is older than 1000 BC, because it is further back in time. For example, Upper and Lower Egypt were united at about 3100 BC, which is about five thousand one hundred years ago, and Tutankhamun ruled at about 1330 BC, which is about three thousand three hundred years ago. In this topic, you can also simply say how many years ago something happened. To find out, add the BC year to the current year, roughly two thousand years.

### subtopic_3 — How We Know
**Assistant:** Why did the Egyptians build their society along the Nile?

**Tutor:** Because the Nile was the only reliable source of water in a huge, dry desert. Egypt gets almost no rain, so without the river, people could not grow crops or keep animals. Every year, the Nile flooded, and when the water went down, it left behind a layer of dark, rich mud on the land along its banks, which was perfect for farming. The river also gave fish, water birds, drinking water and papyrus reeds, and it was a natural highway: boats could float north with the current, and sail south with the wind, which usually blows from the north. So people lived in a long, narrow, green strip along the river, with desert on both sides. The desert also protected Egypt from attack, because it was hard to cross. We will learn more about the Nile in the next part of this topic.
