# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the discovery of the tomb of Tutankhamun: who, when and why. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Pretoria, in Gauteng, on the first day of a case study. The teacher hands out copies of an old newspaper page from December 1922, with a large headline about a wonderful discovery in the Valley of the Kings in Egypt. There is a grainy black-and-white photograph of a narrow stone staircase leading down into the ground, and men in hats and long robes standing beside a heap of rubble in the hot sun. The learners read the article aloud: after years of searching, an archaeologist had found a sealed tomb, full of golden treasures, belonging to a young pharaoh. One learner asks why the discovery was such big news when Egypt was full of tombs. The teacher answers that almost every royal tomb in the Valley had been robbed long ago, but this one was almost untouched. The big idea is that the discovery of Tutankhamun's tomb in 1922, by Howard Carter and his team, after years of searching, was one of the most famous discoveries in history, because it was the most complete royal tomb ever found in Egypt. The discovery of the tomb is examinable.

## Subtopic: Who Was Tutankhamun?

Tutankhamun was a pharaoh of ancient Egypt who lived about three thousand three hundred years ago. He became pharaoh when he was only about nine years old, so older officials helped him to rule. He ruled for about ten years and died when he was about eighteen or nineteen.

Before Tutankhamun, a pharaoh called Akhenaten, who was probably his father, had made big changes. He tried to make the Egyptians worship only one god, the Aten, the disc of the sun, and he moved the capital to a new city. During Tutankhamun's rule, the old gods and the old capital were restored. His name means the living image of Amun, one of the old gods.

Nobody knows for certain how Tutankhamun died. Scientists who have studied his mummy found a broken leg that had not healed, signs of malaria, and problems with one foot, and many walking sticks were found in his tomb. Because he died young and suddenly, his tomb was small for a pharaoh, and it may have been prepared quickly.

After his death, later pharaohs tried to remove his name and the names of his family from lists of kings, because they were linked to Akhenaten's changes. So, by 1922, Tutankhamun was almost forgotten.

The questions for this section are with you now: who Tutankhamun was and when he lived.

## Subtopic: The Search

The discovery was made by Howard Carter, a British archaeologist who had worked in Egypt for many years, first as an artist copying tomb paintings, and later as an inspector of ancient sites. His digging was paid for by a wealthy British man, Lord Carnarvon, who was very interested in ancient Egypt.

Why did they search for Tutankhamun? The Valley of the Kings, near the modern city of Luxor, was where many pharaohs of that time were buried, in tombs cut deep into the rock. By the early 1900s, many experts believed that all the tombs in the Valley had been found. But Carter believed that Tutankhamun's tomb was still hidden there, because a few small objects with his name on them had been found in the Valley.

Carter and his team dug for several seasons, from 1917, moving tonnes of rock and sand, but found almost nothing. Lord Carnarvon was ready to give up, but Carter persuaded him to pay for one last season.

On 4 November 1922, Carter's workers found a step cut into the rock, under the remains of ancient workers' huts. According to one story, it was found by a boy who was bringing water for the workers. They dug, and found sixteen steps leading down to a blocked doorway, with seals still on it.

The tomb had stayed hidden because its entrance had been buried under rubble when another tomb was cut above it, about two hundred years after Tutankhamun's burial.

The questions for this section are with you now: Howard Carter, Lord Carnarvon and why they searched.

## Subtopic: Wonderful Things

Carter covered the steps again and sent a telegram to Lord Carnarvon in England, who travelled to Egypt with his daughter. On 26 November 1922, they cleared the passage to a second sealed doorway. Carter made a small hole and held up a candle. Hot air escaped, making the flame flicker. As his eyes got used to the light, he saw strange animals, statues and gold, everywhere the glint of gold. Lord Carnarvon asked if he could see anything, and Carter is reported to have replied: yes, wonderful things.

The first room, called the antechamber, was crammed with objects: golden couches shaped like animals, chariots, boxes, statues and vases. Two life-size statues of the king guarded a sealed wall. Behind it, in February 1923, they found the burial chamber, almost filled by huge gilded shrines, one inside another. Inside them was a stone coffin, and inside that, three coffins, one inside the other. The innermost coffin was made of solid gold. In 1925, they finally reached the mummy of the king, wearing a magnificent golden mask.

The work took Carter and his team about ten years. Every object was photographed, drawn, numbered, described and carefully preserved before it was moved. Egyptian workers, experts and officials took part in the work, and the Egyptian government decided that the treasures should stay in Egypt.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking Tutankhamun was a famous, powerful pharaoh in his time; he was a young king who was almost forgotten. Two: thinking the tomb was found by accident; Carter searched for years. Three: thinking the tomb had never been entered; robbers had entered it soon after the burial, but took little. Four: thinking the treasures were taken out of Egypt; they stayed in Egypt.

The questions for this section are with you now: opening the tomb and recording what was found.
