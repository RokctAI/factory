### subtopic_1 — Who Was Tutankhamun?
**Assistant:** Was there really a curse on the tomb?

**Tutor:** No, there is no real evidence of a curse. The story of the curse of the pharaoh started in 1923, when Lord Carnarvon died in Egypt, only a few months after the tomb was opened. He died from an infection after a mosquito bite on his cheek became infected while he was shaving. Newspapers, which were competing to sell exciting stories, claimed that he had been killed by an ancient curse for disturbing the king's rest, and the story spread around the world. But most of the people who were present when the tomb was opened lived for many more years. Howard Carter himself, who spent ten years working in the tomb, lived until 1939. Scientists who have studied the question found that the people who entered the tomb did not die younger than other people of the time. The curse makes a good story, but that is all it is.

### subtopic_2 — The Search
**Assistant:** Who were the Egyptians who worked on the discovery?

**Tutor:** Howard Carter's name is the most famous, but the discovery depended on many people, especially Egyptians. Teams of Egyptian workers did most of the hard digging, moving tonnes of rubble in the heat, and skilled Egyptian foremen led the work teams. According to one story, the first step was uncovered by a boy who was carrying water for the workers. Egyptian officials of the Antiquities Service supervised the excavation, and in the end, the Egyptian government decided that all the objects should stay in Egypt, which was a very important decision. For many years, books and films told the story as if only the British team was involved, but historians today try to tell the fuller story and recognise the Egyptians who made the discovery possible. This is a good reminder to ask, when reading any history: whose voices are missing from this story?

### subtopic_3 — Wonderful Things
**Assistant:** How did they keep the objects from falling apart?

**Tutor:** Many objects in the tomb were very fragile after more than three thousand years. Cloth, leather, wood and beadwork could crumble when touched or when the air changed. So Carter's team worked slowly and carefully. Before anything was moved, it was photographed where it lay, by an expert photographer, and drawn and described in detail, with a number. Then a team of conservators, who are experts in preserving objects, treated fragile items, for example, by coating them with melted wax or special liquids to hold them together. Some objects were packed in cotton wool and padded boxes, and carried on trays. An empty tomb nearby was used as a laboratory. Then the objects were packed in crates and taken by boat down the Nile to the museum in Cairo. The team's careful records are still studied by archaeologists today, and are a model of how a discovery should be recorded.
