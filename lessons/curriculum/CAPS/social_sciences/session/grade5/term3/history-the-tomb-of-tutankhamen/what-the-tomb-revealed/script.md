# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers what the discovery of Tutankhamun's tomb revealed about ancient Egyptian society. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Stellenbosch, in the Western Cape, which has turned its classroom into a small museum. Each group has made a replica of one object from the tomb, using cardboard, clay, gold paint, beads and fabric. There is a golden mask with blue stripes, a model chariot, a small wooden shrine, a board game with thirty squares, a pair of sandals, a row of tiny clay figures, a little model boat, four small jars with lids shaped like heads, and a necklace with a beetle in the middle. Next to each replica is a label card with two lines: what the object is, and what it tells us about ancient Egypt. Parents walk around reading the labels, and the learners act as guides. The big idea is that the objects in Tutankhamun's tomb are like a huge library without words: they reveal what the Egyptians believed about death and the afterlife, how powerful and wealthy the pharaoh was, how skilled their craftspeople were, how far they traded, and how they lived every day. What the tomb revealed is examinable.

## Subtopic: Beliefs About the Afterlife

The tomb shows how strongly the Egyptians believed in life after death. They believed that a person's spirit could live forever in the afterlife, but only if the body was preserved and the person had everything they needed.

So the body was mummified. Embalmers removed the organs, dried the body with a special salt called natron for many weeks, then wrapped it in many layers of linen bandages, with amulets tucked between the layers for protection. Tutankhamun's organs were placed in four tiny golden coffins, kept in a stone chest, a little like the four jars in the Stellenbosch museum.

The tomb was filled with everything the king would need in the afterlife: food, drink, clothes, furniture, beds, chairs, weapons, chariots, jewellery and games. There were model boats to travel in the afterlife, and hundreds of small figures called shabtis, which were meant to come to life and do work for the king, so that he would not have to.

The walls of the burial chamber were painted with scenes of the king meeting the gods, including Osiris, god of the afterlife. Many objects have prayers and spells written on them, to protect the king on his journey.

The questions for this section are with you now: the afterlife, mummification and grave goods.

## Subtopic: Wealth, Power and Skill

The tomb reveals the great wealth and power of the pharaoh. Tutankhamun was a young king who did not rule for long, and his tomb was small, yet it held more than five thousand objects, many of them covered in gold. If a minor king had this much, the tombs of great pharaohs, which were robbed long ago, must have held even more.

The famous death mask is made of solid gold, weighing about ten kilograms, decorated with blue glass and precious stones. The innermost coffin is solid gold, weighing more than a hundred kilograms.

The objects show the amazing skill of Egyptian craftspeople. Goldsmiths hammered, shaped and decorated gold with tiny details. Jewellers set coloured stones and glass into patterns. Carpenters made furniture with fine joints, inlays of ebony and ivory, and folding stools. Weavers made fine linen, and painters and sculptors made lifelike statues.

The materials show that Egypt traded with faraway lands. The deep blue stone called lapis lazuli came from what is now Afghanistan, thousands of kilometres away. Ebony, ivory and some gold came from lands to the south, in Africa, such as Nubia. Scientists have even shown that the blade of one of Tutankhamun's daggers was made from iron that came from a meteorite, a rock that fell from space.

The questions for this section are with you now: wealth, craftsmanship and trade.

## Subtopic: Daily Life and Family

Many objects in the tomb are everyday things, which tell us about daily life, at least for the royal family.

There were clothes made of linen, including shirts, loincloths and gloves, and dozens of pairs of sandals, some made of reeds and some decorated with gold. There were walking sticks, fans made of ostrich feathers, beds, chairs, headrests, which Egyptians used instead of pillows, lamps and boxes for storing things.

There were board games, such as senet, which shows that Egyptians enjoyed playing games. There were musical instruments, such as trumpets. There was food, such as bread, meat, fruit and honey, and jars of wine, labelled with the year and the vineyard. There were hunting bows and chariots, showing that the king hunted.

The tomb also gives clues about Tutankhamun's family. A tiny coffin held a lock of hair belonging to his grandmother, Queen Tiye. Paintings and carvings show the young king with his wife, Ankhesenamun. The tomb also held the mummies of two baby daughters who did not survive.

Today, scientists still study the objects and the mummy, using modern technology, and they keep learning new things.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the grave goods were just to show off; they were for the king's life after death. Two: thinking Egypt did not trade with other lands; lapis lazuli came from far away. Three: thinking the tomb only tells us about gold; it also shows clothes, games, food and family. Four: thinking we have learnt everything from the tomb; scientists are still studying it.

The questions for this section are with you now: daily life and family.
