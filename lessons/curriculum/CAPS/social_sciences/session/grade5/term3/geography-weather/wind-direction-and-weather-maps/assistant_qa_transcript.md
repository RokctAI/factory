### subtopic_1 — Wind Direction
**Assistant:** Why does the arrow on a wind vane point into the wind?

**Tutor:** It is because of its shape. A wind vane has a thin, pointed arrowhead at one end and a big, flat tail at the other, balanced on a pole so that it can turn freely. When the wind blows, it pushes much harder on the big, flat tail than on the thin point, just as the wind pushes harder on an open umbrella than on a pencil. So the tail is pushed away, downwind, and the arrowhead swings around to face the wind. The arrow ends up pointing at the direction the wind is coming from, which is exactly how we name winds. So if the arrow points south-east, the wind is a south-easter. It is a clever, simple design, and wind vanes have been used for hundreds of years, often on top of churches, farmhouses and schools, sometimes shaped like roosters, ships or animals.

### subtopic_2 — Wind Vanes and Windsocks
**Assistant:** What makes the tablecloth on Table Mountain?

**Tutor:** The tablecloth forms when the south-easter blows towards Table Mountain. The wind has crossed False Bay, so it carries a lot of moisture from the sea. When it reaches the mountains, the air is pushed up the slopes. As air rises, it cools, and when it cools enough, the moisture in it turns into tiny water droplets, forming a cloud on the top of the mountain. The wind keeps blowing the cloud over the edge of the flat top, and as the air sinks down the other side towards the city, it warms up again, so the cloud disappears a little way down the slope. That is why the cloud looks like a white cloth hanging over the edge of a table, but never reaching the city. It is a famous sight, and it is a good example of how wind, temperature and cloud work together.

### subtopic_3 — Weather Maps in the Media
**Assistant:** Where can I find a reliable weather forecast?

**Tutor:** In South Africa, the official forecasts are prepared by the South African Weather Service, a government organisation with weather stations, satellites and radar across the country. Its forecasts are shared on its website and app, and they are used by many television and radio stations and newspapers. When there is dangerous weather, such as severe thunderstorms, floods, strong winds or heavy snow, it issues warnings, which are often shown in colours, such as yellow, orange or red, with red being the most serious. It is a good habit to check the forecast before school trips, sports days or long journeys. Remember that a forecast is a careful prediction, not a promise, so it is sometimes wrong, especially about exactly where a thunderstorm will hit. Forecasts for the next day or two are usually more accurate than forecasts for next week.
