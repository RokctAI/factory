# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers measuring temperature and rain, with the instruments and units used. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Rustenburg, in North West, at the foot of the Magaliesberg. The school has just been given a small weather station, and the class sets it up on an open patch of grass, away from the buildings and trees. There is a white wooden box on legs, with slatted sides like the shutters of a window, and inside it, a thermometer. A few steps away, a clear plastic rain gauge with markings is fixed to a short pole. On the classroom wall, the class pins up a chart with columns for the date, the time, the temperature and the rainfall. Every morning at eight o'clock, two learners will go out, read the instruments and write down the numbers. The big idea is that weather can be measured with instruments, that temperature is measured with a thermometer in degrees Celsius, and that rain is measured with a rain gauge in millimetres, and that measuring in the same way every day gives us records we can trust. Measuring temperature and rain is examinable.

## Subtopic: The Thermometer

A thermometer is the instrument used to measure temperature. A common kind has a thin glass tube with a liquid inside, usually coloured alcohol. When the air gets warmer, the liquid expands, which means it takes up more space, and rises up the tube. When the air cools, the liquid contracts, takes up less space, and goes down. A scale next to the tube shows the temperature. Many thermometers today are digital and show the number on a small screen.

Temperature is measured in degrees Celsius. Water freezes at zero degrees Celsius and boils at one hundred degrees Celsius at sea level. A comfortable room is about twenty degrees, and a very hot summer day in South Africa can be over thirty-five degrees. Temperatures below zero are written with a minus sign, and are read as minus three degrees, for example.

To read a thermometer, look at the top of the liquid with your eye level with it, and read the nearest mark on the scale. Do not hold the bulb at the bottom, because the heat of your hand will change the reading.

## Subtopic: Measuring Temperature Properly

The air temperature must be measured in the shade, not in the sun. If a thermometer stands in the sun, the sun heats the thermometer itself, and it shows a temperature higher than the air.

That is why the thermometer in Rustenburg is kept inside a white box with slatted sides, called a Stevenson screen. The white colour reflects the sun's heat. The slats let air flow through, but keep out direct sunlight and rain. The box stands on legs, so that the thermometer is about one and a half metres above the ground, away from the heat of the ground. And it stands in an open place, away from walls, which store heat.

Weather stations often record the highest temperature of the day, called the maximum, and the lowest, called the minimum. Special maximum and minimum thermometers, or digital thermometers, can keep these readings. The minimum is usually reached just before sunrise, and the maximum in the early afternoon.

Readings should be taken at the same time each day and in the same place, so that they can be compared fairly.

## Subtopic: The Rain Gauge

A rain gauge is the instrument used to measure rainfall. A simple rain gauge is a container with straight sides and a scale marked on it, open at the top to catch the rain.

Rainfall is measured in millimetres. If the rain gauge shows ten millimetres, it means that if all the rain that fell had stayed on flat ground without running away or soaking in, it would be ten millimetres deep. A light shower might give two or three millimetres, while a heavy thunderstorm can give thirty millimetres or more.

To measure properly, the rain gauge must stand in an open place, away from trees, roofs and walls, which could block the rain or drip extra water into it. Its top must be level, and it should be read at the same time every day. After reading, it must be emptied, so that the next day's rain can be measured.

By adding up the daily readings, we can find the total rainfall for a week, a month or a year. For example, if the gauge shows four millimetres on Monday, nothing on Tuesday and twelve millimetres on Wednesday, the total for those three days is sixteen millimetres.

Records like these, kept carefully for many years at weather stations all over the country, help weather experts to work out the climate of each place, and help farmers and town planners to know how much water to expect.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: putting the thermometer in the sun; air temperature is measured in the shade. Two: measuring rain in litres; rainfall is measured in millimetres. Three: forgetting to empty the rain gauge; then the next reading is wrong. Four: putting the rain gauge under a tree; it must be in the open.

A thermometer measures temperature in degrees Celsius.

The liquid goes up when it is warm and down when it is cold.

Read it with your eye level with the top of the liquid, and do not hold the bulb.

Keep the white box with slatted sides in mind and try the questions for this part: the thermometer.

## Subtopic: In the Shade

Measure air temperature in the shade, not in the sun.

The Stevenson screen is a white box with slats. It keeps out sun and rain, but lets the air in.

The highest temperature of the day is the maximum, and the lowest is the minimum. Measure at the same time every day.

Hold the learners reading the thermometer at eight o'clock in mind and try this part's questions: measuring properly.

## Subtopic: Rain Gauge

A rain gauge measures rain in millimetres. Put it in the open, away from trees and roofs. Read it every day, and then empty it. Add the readings to get the total.
