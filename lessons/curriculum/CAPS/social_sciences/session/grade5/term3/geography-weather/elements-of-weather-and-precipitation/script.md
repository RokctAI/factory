# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the elements of weather and precipitation as rain, hail and snow. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Harrismith, a town in the eastern Free State, close to the Drakensberg. It is a hot afternoon in December. In the morning the sky was clear, but by lunch time, huge white clouds have started to pile up over the mountains, like towers of cotton wool. By two o'clock, the bottoms of the clouds are dark grey, the wind suddenly blows hard and cool, and thunder rumbles. Then the rain comes down in big, heavy drops, drumming on the classroom roof, and for a few minutes, small balls of ice bounce across the playground. Twenty minutes later, the storm is over, the air smells of wet earth, and the sun comes out again. The teacher reminds the class that in July, the same mountains were white with snow. The big idea is that weather is made up of elements that we can observe and describe, temperature, wind, cloud cover and precipitation, and that precipitation can fall as rain, hail or snow. The elements of weather are examinable.

## Subtopic: Temperature and Wind

Temperature is how hot or cold the air is. It is measured in degrees Celsius. The sun heats the land, and the land heats the air above it. So temperatures are usually highest in the early afternoon and lowest just before sunrise.

Temperatures change with the seasons. In Harrismith, summer afternoons can be hot, but winter nights are very cold, often below zero degrees Celsius, which is the temperature at which water freezes. Then frost forms on the grass.

Wind is moving air. Air moves from places where it is cooler and heavier towards places where it is warmer and lighter. Wind can be gentle, like a light breeze, or very strong, like a gale. In a thunderstorm, cool air rushes down from the clouds, so the wind suddenly becomes strong and cool, just as it did in Harrismith.

We describe wind by its strength and by its direction. Wind is named after the direction it blows from. A wind that blows from the north is a north wind.

Temperature and wind work together. A windy winter day feels much colder than a still one at the same temperature, because the wind carries heat away from our bodies. On a hot day, a breeze makes us feel cooler for the same reason. That is why weather reports sometimes say how cold it feels, not only what the thermometer shows.

The questions for this section are with you now: temperature and wind as elements of weather.

## Subtopic: Clouds and Cloud Cover

Clouds are made of billions of tiny drops of water or tiny bits of ice, floating in the air. They form when warm, moist air rises, cools, and the water vapour in it turns into drops. Water vapour is water in the form of an invisible gas.

Cloud cover is how much of the sky is covered by cloud. We describe it in words such as clear, when there are no clouds; partly cloudy, when some of the sky is covered; and overcast, when the whole sky is covered.

There are different kinds of clouds. Cumulus clouds are white and fluffy, with flat bottoms, and are often seen on fine days. When they grow very tall, like the towers over the mountains near Harrismith, they become cumulonimbus clouds, or thunderclouds, which can bring heavy rain, lightning, thunder and hail. Stratus clouds are flat, grey layers that cover the sky and may bring drizzle. Cirrus clouds are thin and wispy, high in the sky, and made of ice.

Clouds affect temperature too. On a cloudy night, clouds act like a blanket and keep the land warmer. On a clear night, heat escapes and it gets colder.

The questions for this section are with you now: how clouds form, cloud cover and kinds of clouds.

## Subtopic: Rain, Hail and Snow

Precipitation is any water that falls from clouds to the ground. Rainfall is how much rain falls in a place. There are three main kinds of precipitation.

Rain is drops of liquid water. When tiny cloud droplets join together, they become big and heavy enough to fall. Rain can be light drizzle, a steady shower, or a heavy downpour in a thunderstorm.

Hail is balls or lumps of ice. Hail forms inside tall thunderclouds, where strong winds carry raindrops high up into freezing air. They freeze, fall, and are carried up again, adding layers of ice, until they are too heavy and fall to the ground. Hail is common in summer on the Highveld and in the eastern Free State, and it can damage crops, roofs and cars.

Snow is made of ice crystals that stay frozen all the way to the ground. In South Africa, snow falls mainly in winter, on high mountains such as the Drakensberg, and sometimes in other high places.

Most of the rain in the eastern Free State falls in summer, in afternoon thunderstorms like the one in Harrismith.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking wind is named after the direction it blows to; it is named after the direction it comes from. Two: thinking hail is frozen snow; it is layers of ice formed in thunderclouds. Three: thinking clouds are smoke; they are tiny drops of water or bits of ice. Four: thinking hail falls mostly in winter; in South Africa it falls mostly in summer thunderstorms.

The questions for this section are with you now: rain, hail and snow.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the thunderstorm in Harrismith — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Hot, Cold and Windy

Temperature is how hot or cold the air is. We measure it in degrees Celsius. Water freezes at zero degrees.

Wind is moving air. It is named after the direction it comes from.

Keep the sudden cool wind before the storm in mind and try the questions for this part: temperature and wind.

## Subtopic: Clouds

Clouds are tiny drops of water or bits of ice. They form when warm, wet air rises and cools.

Clear means no clouds. Overcast means the whole sky is covered. Tall thunderclouds bring storms.

Hold the towers of cloud over the mountains in mind and try this part's questions: clouds and cloud cover.

## Subtopic: Rain, Hail and Snow

Rain falls when cloud drops join and get heavy. Rain is drops of water. Hail is balls of ice from thunderclouds. Snow is ice crystals. Hail falls mostly in summer storms, and snow falls on high mountains in winter.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The elements of weather are temperature, measured in degrees Celsius, wind, named after the direction it comes from, cloud cover and precipitation. Clouds form when warm, moist air rises and cools. Cloud cover can be clear, partly cloudy or overcast, and tall thunderclouds bring storms. Precipitation falls as rain, as hail, which forms in thunderclouds, or as snow, which falls on high mountains in winter.

Hold the small balls of ice bouncing across the playground in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: rain, hail and snow.
