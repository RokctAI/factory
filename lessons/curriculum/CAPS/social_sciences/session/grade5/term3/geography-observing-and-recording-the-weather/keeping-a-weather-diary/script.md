# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers observing and recording the weather over two weeks, and commenting on wind direction, weather patterns and how weather affects daily life. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Kimberley, in the Northern Cape, at the start of their weather project. Each learner has a weather diary, a booklet with a table ruled on every page. The class has a thermometer in a shady box outside the classroom window, a home-made rain gauge made from a plastic bottle, a wind vane made from a straw, a pin and a piece of card, and a compass painted on the paving to show north, east, south and west. The teacher explains the rules: observe at the same time every day, at eight in the morning and again at one in the afternoon; record honestly, even when nothing seems to happen; and write down how the weather affected you and your family that day. On the first morning, it is cool and clear, with a light wind from the north-west. The big idea is that careful, regular observation of the weather gives us records, and records let us see patterns and explain how weather affects our lives. Observing and recording the weather is examinable.

## Subtopic: Observing and Recording

To observe means to watch carefully and notice details. To record means to write down what you observe, so that you can remember it and use it later.

A weather diary is usually a table. Each row is one observation, and each column is one element of weather. The columns are: the date and time; the temperature in degrees Celsius, read from the thermometer in the shade; cloud cover, described as clear, partly cloudy or overcast, often with a simple symbol, such as an empty circle for clear, a half-filled circle for partly cloudy, and a full circle for overcast; precipitation, with the type, rain, hail or snow, and the amount in millimetres from the rain gauge; and wind direction, from the wind vane, and wind strength, described as calm, light, strong or very strong.

There can also be a column for comments, where you write anything special, such as thunder in the afternoon, mist in the morning or frost on the grass.

Good recording follows rules. Observe at the same times every day. Use the same instruments in the same places. Write down the reading straight away, so that you do not forget it. If you miss a day, leave the space empty and say why, rather than guessing. Honest records are more useful than perfect-looking records.

The questions for this section are with you now: observing the weather and recording it in a weather diary.

## Subtopic: Graphs and Patterns

After two weeks, the class in Kimberley has twenty rows of information. Now they can turn their records into graphs, which make patterns easier to see.

Temperature is often shown on a line graph. The days go along the bottom, and the temperature goes up the side. Each reading is shown with a dot, and the dots are joined with a line. The line shows whether it was getting warmer or cooler.

Rainfall is often shown on a bar graph, with one bar for each day. The height of each bar shows how many millimetres of rain fell. Days with no rain have no bar.

Then the class looks for patterns. A pattern is something that happens again and again. They notice that the one o'clock temperature was always higher than the eight o'clock temperature, because the sun heats the land during the day. They notice that on clear nights, the next morning was colder, because clouds act like a blanket. They notice that before the one thunderstorm, the afternoon was hot and the clouds built up, and that after the storm, the temperature dropped. And they notice that the wind often came from the north-west on hot, dry days.

They also compare their records with the weather forecasts on television to see how accurate the forecasts were.

The questions for this section are with you now: line graphs, bar graphs and weather patterns.

## Subtopic: Weather and Daily Life

The last part of the project is to comment on how the weather affected daily life. Learners use the comments column of their diaries.

In Kimberley, the learners wrote about cold mornings, when they wore jerseys to school and took them off by lunch time, because the temperature rose a lot during the day. They wrote about the windy, dusty day, when washing on the line got dirty and some people with allergies sneezed. They wrote about the thunderstorm, when the electricity went off, the playground flooded, and their grandmother was happy because her vegetable garden got water. They also noticed that on hot days, people sat in the shade, drank more water and used fans.

A good comment links the weather to its effect, and explains why. For example: on Tuesday, the wind was strong from the north-west, so the air was full of dust and we stayed inside at break.

Finally, the learners write a short conclusion: a few sentences about the main patterns they saw, and what they learnt.

This kind of project is how real weather science works: careful measuring, honest recording, looking for patterns and explaining them.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: guessing a missed reading; leave it empty and explain why. Two: observing at different times each day; the times must be the same to compare fairly. Three: showing daily rainfall on a line graph; a bar graph shows separate daily amounts more clearly. Four: describing the weather without saying how it affected people; the project asks for both.

The questions for this section are with you now: how weather affected daily life and writing a conclusion.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the weather diaries in Kimberley — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Watch and Write

Observe means to watch carefully. Record means to write it down.

Use a table with columns: date and time, temperature, cloud cover, rain, and wind direction and strength.

Observe at the same times every day. Never guess a missed reading.

Keep the weather diary with its ruled table in mind and try the questions for this part: observing and recording.

## Subtopic: Graphs and Patterns

Draw temperature as a line graph. Draw daily rainfall as a bar graph.

Look for patterns. Afternoons are warmer than mornings. Clear nights make cold mornings. It gets cooler after a storm.

Hold the line on the temperature graph going up and down in mind and try this part's questions: graphs and patterns.

## Subtopic: Weather and Us

Write how the weather changed your day, and say why. Then write a short conclusion about the patterns you saw.

Pocket summary of the lesson. In the weather project, learners observe the weather at the same times every day for two weeks and record temperature, cloud cover, precipitation, and wind direction and strength in a table. They show temperature on a line graph and rainfall on a bar graph, look for patterns, and comment on how the weather affected daily life, linking each effect to its cause, and finish with a conclusion.

Hold the painted compass on the paving in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: weather and daily life.
