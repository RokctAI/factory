### subtopic_1 — Why and Where They Settled
**Assistant:** Why can't sorghum grow in the Western Cape's winter rain?

**Tutor:** Sorghum and millet are crops that come from hot parts of Africa. They need warm weather and plenty of water while they grow, and then a dry time to ripen. In the summer-rainfall east of South Africa, the rain falls during the hot months, exactly when these crops are growing, so they do well. In the Western Cape, most rain falls in the cold winter months, when it is too cold for sorghum and millet to grow well, and the summers are hot and dry, with little rain at the time the plants would need it. Long ago, the first farmers did not have irrigation canals or dams to bring water to their fields in summer. So the winter-rainfall south-west was not a good place for their crops, and they did not settle there. Later, European settlers brought wheat and grapes, which do grow well with winter rain.

### subtopic_2 — Land Belonged to Everyone
**Assistant:** Is land still given out by chiefs in South Africa today?

**Tutor:** Yes, in some places. In many rural areas, especially in the former homelands, a large amount of land is communal land. This means it is held for the community, and traditional leaders, such as chiefs and headmen, together with the community, decide who may use it for homes, fields and grazing, a little like in the past. Families who are given land usually have the right to live on it and use it, and pass it on to their children, but they do not have a title deed like people who own land in towns. Some people think this system protects communities and their way of life. Others think people should have full ownership papers, so they can, for example, get loans from banks. South Africans continue to discuss how to make the system fair, especially for women, who in the past were often not given land in their own names.

### subtopic_3 — Using the Land Carefully
**Assistant:** Why did the first farmers bury people near the cattle kraal?

**Tutor:** Among many farming communities in southern Africa, the cattle kraal was the most important and respected place in the homestead. Cattle were the family's wealth and were used in ceremonies to communicate with the ancestors. The kraal was usually in the centre of the homestead, and it was often a place where the men of the family gathered and important matters were discussed. Burying the head of the family in or near the kraal showed great respect, and kept the ancestor close to the family and its cattle, so that the ancestor could continue to watch over them. Archaeologists have found burials in and near old cattle kraals at Iron Age sites, which helps them understand how important cattle and the ancestors were. Many families today still have strong links to the land where their ancestors are buried.
