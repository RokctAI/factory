### subtopic_1 — Meeting the Khoisan
**Assistant:** How do you make the three isiXhosa clicks?

**Tutor:** Each click is made by trapping a little air with the tongue and then releasing it with a pop, without breathing out. For the c click, place the tip of your tongue just behind your top front teeth and pull it away, making a soft tut sound, like the sound people make when they are disappointed. For the q click, press the tip of your tongue against the roof of your mouth, a little further back, and pull it down sharply, making a loud, hollow pop, like a cork coming out of a bottle. For the x click, put the side of your tongue against your back teeth on one side and pull it away, making the sound people sometimes use to call a horse. Each click can also be combined with other sounds, so isiXhosa has many different click sounds. With practice, they become easy and fun.

### subtopic_2 — Trade, Work and Marriage
**Assistant:** Why were San rainmakers so respected?

**Tutor:** In southern Africa, rain is precious and often uncertain. A drought could destroy a farming community's crops and kill its cattle. The San were believed by many people to have a special relationship with the rain and the spirit world. In San beliefs, healers could enter a trance and travel to the spirit world to capture a rain animal and lead it across the land, so that rain would fall. Rock paintings show these rain animals. Farming communities, who also had their own rainmakers and rain ceremonies, often respected the San as the first people of the land, with the oldest knowledge of it. Historical records and stories tell that some farming chiefs asked San rainmakers to make rain, and paid them with cattle or other gifts. This shows how the groups valued each other's knowledge and beliefs.

### subtopic_3 — What Was Shared and What Was Lost
**Assistant:** What happened to the San in the mountains later on?

**Tutor:** In the Drakensberg and other mountain areas, San groups continued to live as hunter-gatherers for a long time after farming villages spread through the valleys below. They traded with farmers and sometimes worked for them. But in the eighteen hundreds, things became much harder. More farmers, both African and European settlers, moved into the area, game animals became scarce, and hunting grounds were fenced. Some San raided cattle and horses, and colonial forces and settlers attacked San groups, killing many people. By the end of the eighteen hundreds, the San had disappeared as separate groups from the Drakensberg, though some joined farming communities, and their descendants live on. Their paintings remain on the rock walls of the mountains, including paintings of horses, cattle and men with guns, which tell part of this sad story from the San side.
