# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the interaction between the first farmers and the Khoisan, with a focus on the generous acceptance of strangers and the integration of Khoisan people into farming societies. The anchor is a Grade 5 isiXhosa lesson in Qonce, in the Eastern Cape. The teacher writes three letters on the board: c, q and x. The class practises the three clicks: a soft tut with the tongue behind the teeth, a loud pop from the roof of the mouth, and a click at the side of the mouth, like the sound someone makes to urge on a horse. They say words such as ukucula, to sing, iqanda, an egg, and ixhego, an old man. Then the teacher explains that isiXhosa did not always have so many clicks. Many of them came from Khoisan languages, because, long ago, Xhosa-speaking farmers and Khoisan people lived together, traded and married. The big idea is that when the first farmers met the San and Khoikhoi, they often welcomed them, and over time many Khoisan people became part of farming communities, which shows in South African languages, families and beliefs today. ## Subtopic: Meeting the Khoisan

When the first farmers settled in the east of southern Africa, about one thousand eight hundred years ago, they did not arrive in an empty land. San hunter-gatherers had lived there for tens of thousands of years, and Khoikhoi herders lived in some areas too.

The groups had very different ways of life. The farmers stayed in villages, grew crops and kept cattle. The San moved with the seasons and hunted and gathered. The Khoikhoi moved with their herds. But they also had things to offer each other.

The farmers welcomed strangers. In many African farming societies, it was a strong custom that a visitor or stranger should be given food and shelter. A person who arrived and asked to stay could be accepted into the community, given land to use, and protected by the chief, as long as they respected the chief and the customs of the community. This generous acceptance of strangers helped people from different backgrounds to live together.

Archaeologists have found evidence that, in many places, farmers and hunter-gatherers lived close to each other for hundreds of years. At some San rock shelters, they have found farmers' pottery and iron, and at some farming villages, they have found San stone tools.

The questions for this section are with you now: the first meetings between the first farmers and the Khoisan, and the custom of welcoming strangers.

## Subtopic: Trade, Work and Marriage

The first farmers and the Khoisan traded with each other. San hunters had skins, ivory, ostrich eggshell beads, honey, meat from wild animals and knowledge of wild plants and medicines. Farmers had grain, pots, iron tools and arrowheads, tobacco later on, and cattle. Each group could get things it could not easily make itself.

Some San people worked for farmers, for example as hunters or herders, or helping in the fields at harvest time, and were paid in grain, cattle or other goods. Some farmers learnt hunting and tracking skills from the San.

San healers and rainmakers were highly respected. Some farming communities asked San rainmakers to help bring rain in times of drought, because the San were believed to have special power over rain.

People from the groups married each other. Some Khoisan women married into farming families, and their children grew up as part of the farming community. Over many generations, many Khoisan people became fully part of farming societies. This is called integration: when people from one group become part of another group, while also bringing their own ways and knowledge with them.

The questions for this section are with you now: trade, work, rainmakers and marriage between farmers and Khoisan.

## Subtopic: What Was Shared and What Was Lost

Integration left marks that we can still see today.

Languages are one example. isiXhosa and isiZulu have click sounds, like the ones the class in Qonce practises, which came from Khoisan languages. isiXhosa has the most clicks, because Xhosa-speaking people lived closely with Khoikhoi and San for a long time. Sesotho also has a click. Some words in these languages were also borrowed from Khoisan languages.

Families are another. Scientists who study people's ancestry have found that many South Africans who speak Nguni and Sotho-Tswana languages have Khoisan ancestors as well as farming ancestors.

Beliefs and knowledge were shared too, such as knowledge of plants, healing and rainmaking.

But the story was not only peaceful. As farming villages grew and spread, more land was used for fields and grazing. There were fewer wild animals for the San to hunt, and less open land for them to move through. Some San moved into the mountains, such as the Drakensberg, and into drier areas where farmers did not want to live. There were sometimes clashes, when hunters killed cattle or when farmers took over hunting grounds. So, over a long time, the hunter-gatherer way of life became harder to follow in the east.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the first farmers arrived in an empty land; the San and Khoikhoi were already living there. Two: thinking the groups were always at war; they often welcomed, traded with and married each other. Three: thinking the Khoisan simply disappeared; many became part of farming communities, and their descendants live today. Four: thinking the click sounds in isiXhosa and isiZulu were always there; many came from Khoisan languages.

The questions for this section are with you now: integration, language and the changes that came with farming.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the isiXhosa class in Qonce — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Welcome, Stranger

When the first farmers came, the San and Khoikhoi were already living there. The land was not empty.

The farmers often welcomed strangers and gave them food and shelter. A stranger who respected the chief could stay, and was given land to use.

For hundreds of years, farmers and hunter-gatherers lived close to each other. Farmers' pots have been found in San rock shelters.

Keep the three letters on the board in mind and try the questions for this part: meeting the Khoisan.

## Subtopic: Swapping and Marrying

They traded, worked together and married.

The San had skins, honey, meat and beads. The farmers had grain, pots and iron tools. Each group swapped what the other needed.

Some San people worked for farmers as hunters or herders. San rainmakers were respected, and farmers sometimes asked them to bring rain.

Over many years, many Khoisan people became part of farming families. This is called integration.

Hold the soft tut of the c click in mind and try this part's questions: trade and marriage.

## Subtopic: Clicks and Families

Clicks came from Khoisan languages. That is why isiXhosa and isiZulu have click sounds today. Many families have Khoisan ancestors.

But farming also changed the land. There were fewer wild animals to hunt, and some San moved into the mountains and dry areas.

Pocket summary of the lesson. When the first farmers settled in the east, the San and Khoikhoi already lived there. Farmers often generously accepted strangers, giving them food, shelter and land. The groups traded goods, worked for each other, and married. San rainmakers were respected. Over generations, many Khoisan people were integrated into farming communities, which is why isiXhosa and isiZulu have clicks. But as farming spread, hunting became harder, and there were sometimes clashes.

Hold the sound of the whole class clicking together in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: what was shared and what was lost.
