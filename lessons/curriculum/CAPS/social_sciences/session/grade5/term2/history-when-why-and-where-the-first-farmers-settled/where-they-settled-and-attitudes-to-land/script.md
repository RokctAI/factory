# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers when, why and where the first African farmers settled in southern Africa, and their attitudes to land. The anchor is a Grade 5 class from Thohoyandou, in Limpopo, on a visit to a village in the green, hilly country of Venda. Under a big shady tree near the headman's home, men and women of the village have gathered for a meeting. A young couple has asked for a piece of land to build a home and plant a field. The headman listens to the elders, asks questions, and then the group walks to a spot on a gentle slope near a stream, with good soil, where the couple may build. Nobody pays any money. The teacher explains that in many rural areas of South Africa, land is still given out in a way that goes back to the first farmers. The big idea is that the first farmers chose places to settle carefully, where they could farm and keep cattle, and that they saw land as belonging to the whole community, to be shared and used, not owned and sold. Why and where the first farmers settled, and their attitudes to land, are examinable.

## Subtopic: Why and Where They Settled

The first farmers needed certain things from the land, so they chose where to settle carefully.

They needed rain at the right time. Their main crops, sorghum and millet, grow in summer and need summer rain. In southern Africa, summer rain falls mainly in the east. So the first farmers settled in the eastern half of southern Africa: in what is now Limpopo, Mpumalanga, KwaZulu-Natal, Gauteng, North West, the Free State and the Eastern Cape. They did not settle in the dry west, or in the south-west, where most rain falls in winter, because their crops would not grow there. That is one reason why the Khoikhoi herders and San hunter-gatherers continued to live in the west.

They needed water. They built their villages near rivers and streams, for drinking, cooking, washing and for their animals.

They needed good soil for their fields. River valleys often have rich, dark soil, washed down by floods.

They needed grazing for their cattle, sheep and goats. Grassland near the village gave the animals food.

They needed wood, to build houses and cattle kraals and for fires and for making charcoal to smelt iron. And they needed iron ore, to make tools.

So the first farmers usually settled on the slopes of river valleys in the wetter east, where they could find water, good soil, grazing, wood and iron ore close together. Early farming villages have been found along rivers such as the Limpopo, the Thukela and the Olifants.

## Subtopic: Land Belonged to Everyone

The first farmers had a very different attitude to land from the one many people have today.

Today, in towns and cities, land is usually owned by a person, a company or the government. People buy and sell land, and the owner has a paper called a title deed that says it belongs to them.

For the first farmers, land was not something to buy or sell. Land belonged to the whole community. The chief looked after the land on behalf of the people, like a guardian or a trustee. He did not own it for himself.

Every family in the community had the right to land for a home, for fields to grow food, and for grazing their animals. When a young couple married and needed land, they asked the headman or chief, who, after talking with the elders, gave them a place to build and a field to plant. As long as the family used the land, it was theirs to use. If they left, the land went back to the community, to be given to someone else.

Grazing land was shared. All the families' cattle grazed together on the common pasture, often looked after by the boys of the village.

The land was also connected to the ancestors. People buried their family members close to their homes, often in or near the cattle kraal. They believed the ancestors continued to watch over and care for the family. So land was not just soil. It was home, history and family, linking the living with those who had died.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the first farmers thought about land, and how land was given out.

## Subtopic: Using the Land Carefully

The first farmers knew that land had to be looked after, so that it would keep feeding them.

Fields were dug with iron hoes, planted, weeded and harvested by hand. After some years of farming, the soil became tired and gave smaller harvests. Then farmers would clear a new field nearby, and let the old field rest, so that grass and bushes could grow back and the soil could recover. This is sometimes called shifting cultivation.

Cattle were moved to different grazing areas at different times of year, so that the grass in one place was not eaten down to the roots. Wood was cut from the forests around the village, but over many years, if a village stayed too long, the wood ran out, and the village might move to a new site.

The land in the village where the Thohoyandou class visits is still given out by the headman, under a system called communal land. Today, there are also debates in South Africa about land: who owns it, how it should be shared, and how to undo the unfairness of the past, when laws took land away from many Black South Africans. Different people have different views, and it is an important question for the country.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the first farmers settled everywhere in South Africa; they settled mostly in the summer-rainfall east. Two: thinking the chief owned all the land for himself; he looked after it for the whole community. Three: thinking land was bought and sold; families had the right to use land, not to sell it. Four: thinking land was only soil to the first farmers; it was linked to home, family and the ancestors.
