# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the topic physical features of South Africa. It explains what physical features are, how they differ from human features, and gives a first look at the shape of the land of South Africa. The anchor is a Grade 5 class from Paarl, in the Western Cape, on a hike to the top of Paarl Rock, a giant, smooth dome of grey granite that rises above the town. From the top, the view stretches for kilometres. To the east are high, rocky mountains. Below them, the Berg River winds through a green valley. On the flat land beside the river are vineyards, orchards and the roofs, roads and church spires of the town. Far to the west, on a clear day, the flat top of Table Mountain can be seen. The big idea is that the land has a natural shape, made of physical features such as mountains, valleys, plains and rivers, and that this shape affects where and how people live. Knowing what physical features are, and the difference between physical and human features, is examinable.

## Subtopic: Physical and Human Features

A feature is something that you can see in a landscape. Geographers sort features into two groups.

Physical features are made by nature. They include the shapes of the land, such as mountains, hills, valleys, plains, plateaus and cliffs, and features of water, such as rivers, lakes, waterfalls, the sea, beaches and bays. Physical features were formed over very long periods of time by natural forces, such as movements deep inside the Earth, wind, rain, rivers, ice and the sea.

Human features are made by people. They include houses, schools, roads, bridges, railways, dams, farms, factories, towns and cities.

From the top of Paarl Rock, the learners can sort what they see. The granite dome they are standing on is a physical feature. So are the mountains to the east, the valley, the flat land beside the river, and the Berg River itself. The vineyards, roads, houses, bridges and church spires are human features.

Some features are a mixture. A dam is built by people, so it is a human feature, but it holds back the water of a river, which is a physical feature. A farm is a human feature, but it is on flat land with good soil, which is part of the physical landscape.

A landform is a natural shape of the land, such as a mountain, a hill, a valley or a plain. Landforms are one kind of physical feature.

Physical features change very slowly, over thousands or millions of years. A river slowly cuts its valley deeper. The sea slowly wears away a cliff. Human features can change quickly. A new road or a new block of flats can appear in a few months. That is one more way to tell them apart: if it was here long before any people, it is almost certainly a physical feature.

The questions for this section are with you now: physical features, human features and landforms.

## Subtopic: South Africa's Shape

If you could cut South Africa in half from west to east and look at it from the side, it would look a little like an upside-down saucer, or a plate turned over.

Around the edges, along the coast, is a narrow strip of low, mostly flat land. This is the coastal plain. Behind it, the land rises steeply up a long line of mountains and cliffs, called the Great Escarpment. On top of the escarpment, the land is high and fairly flat. This is the plateau, which covers most of the interior of South Africa.

This shape means that most of South Africa is high above sea level. Large parts of the plateau, including Gauteng and the Free State, are more than one thousand metres high. Coastal cities, such as Cape Town, Durban and Gqeberha, are low, near sea level.

The highest mountains in South Africa are on the edge of the plateau, in the Drakensberg, along the border with Lesotho. Lesotho itself is very high, and its mountains are the highest in southern Africa.

The shape of the land also affects rivers. Many short rivers flow quickly from the escarpment down to the Indian Ocean in the east. The longest river, the Gariep, or Orange, flows west across the plateau to the Atlantic Ocean.

The questions for this section are with you now: the coastal plain, the escarpment and the plateau.

## Subtopic: Why Physical Features Matter

Physical features affect almost everything about how people live.

Flat land is easier for building houses, roads and railways, and for farming with machines. That is why Paarl and its farms are on the flat land beside the river, and not on the steep mountains.

Rivers give water for drinking, farming and factories, so many towns are built near rivers. The Berg River gives water to the farms and towns in its valley.

Mountains are hard to build on and to cross. Roads must climb through passes, the lowest gaps between mountains, or go through tunnels. Between Paarl and Worcester, the Huguenot Tunnel takes the main road under the mountains. Mountains also catch rain and snow, which feeds the rivers, and they are places for hiking and tourism.

The coast gives harbours, fishing, beaches and holidays. High land is usually cooler than low land.

This term, we study the physical features of South Africa in detail: high and low places, the coastal plain, escarpment and plateau, the regions of South Africa, mountains, valleys, rivers, waterfalls, capes and bays, famous features in each province, rivers and their systems, and how people use and change the landscape, especially by building dams.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a farm is a physical feature; it is made by people, so it is a human feature. Two: thinking a dam is a physical feature; it is built by people, even though it holds a river. Three: thinking most of South Africa is low and flat like the coast; most of it is a high plateau. Four: thinking physical features do not affect people; they affect where we build, farm and travel.

The questions for this section are with you now: why physical features matter to people.
