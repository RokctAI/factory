### subtopic_1 — Physical and Human Features
**Assistant:** How was Paarl Rock formed?

**Tutor:** Paarl Rock is made of granite, a hard rock that formed deep underground, many millions of years ago, from hot, melted rock that slowly cooled and hardened before it ever reached the surface. Over a very long time, the softer rocks that covered the granite were worn away by rain, rivers and wind, until the granite was exposed at the surface. Granite tends to crack and peel off in curved layers, a bit like the skin of an onion, which is why it forms smooth, rounded domes. After rain, the wet granite glistens in the sun. People say early Dutch settlers thought it looked like a giant pearl, which in Dutch is parel, and that is how the rock, and later the town, got the name Paarl. Today it is a nature reserve, and people climb it for the view.

### subtopic_2 — South Africa's Shape
**Assistant:** Why is South Africa shaped like an upside-down saucer?

**Tutor:** Scientists believe this shape goes back to a time, many millions of years ago, when southern Africa was in the middle of a giant continent that slowly broke apart. As the land split and the oceans opened up around southern Africa, the edges of the land were worn away by rivers and the sea, and the middle was pushed up. Over millions of years, rivers kept eating backwards into the high land from the coast, and this is how the steep Great Escarpment formed, as the edge of the high interior. The escarpment has moved slowly inland over time, and it is still being worn back, very, very slowly, by rivers and rain. So the saucer shape is the result of the land rising, the edges being worn away, and an incredibly long time.

### subtopic_3 — Why Physical Features Matter
**Assistant:** Is there snow in South Africa?

**Tutor:** Yes, there is. Because so much of South Africa is high above sea level, and because the higher you go the colder it gets, snow falls in some places almost every winter. The Drakensberg in KwaZulu-Natal and the Eastern Cape, the mountains of the Eastern Cape highlands, and parts of the Western Cape, such as the mountains near Ceres and the Hex River Valley, often get snow. The highlands of Lesotho, next door, get even more, and there is a ski resort there. Sometimes, in a very cold winter, a light snowfall even reaches towns on the Highveld, such as Johannesburg, though this is rare and very exciting when it happens. Coastal cities, which are low, almost never get snow. Snow in the mountains is important, because when it melts it feeds the rivers.
