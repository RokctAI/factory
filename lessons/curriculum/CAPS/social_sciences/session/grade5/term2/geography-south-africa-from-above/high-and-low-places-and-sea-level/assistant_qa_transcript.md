### subtopic_1 — Sea Level
**Assistant:** How does a phone know how high it is?

**Tutor:** Most smartphones can work out their height in one or both of two ways. The first is GPS, the same system that shows your position on a map. The phone receives signals from several satellites high above the Earth, and by working out how long each signal took to arrive, it can calculate where it is, including how high it is. The second is a tiny sensor called a barometer, which measures air pressure. Air pressure is the weight of all the air above you pressing down. At the coast, there is a lot of air above you, so the pressure is high. As you climb a mountain, there is less air above you, so the pressure drops. The phone uses the drop in pressure to estimate how high you have climbed. Neither method is perfect, so readings can be off by a few metres.

### subtopic_2 — High and Low in South Africa
**Assistant:** Why does it get colder as you go higher?

**Tutor:** It seems strange, because higher up you are a little closer to the sun. But the sun is so far away that a few kilometres makes no difference. What matters is the air. The sun's rays pass through the air without heating it much. Instead, they heat the ground, and the ground then warms the air just above it. So the air near the ground at sea level is usually the warmest. Higher up, the air is further from the warm ground at the bottom. Also, the air higher up is thinner, with less pressure. When air rises and spreads out into lower pressure, it cools down. These two things together mean that, on average, the temperature drops by roughly six degrees Celsius for every thousand metres you climb. That is why mountain tops can have snow while the valleys below are warm.

### subtopic_3 — Showing Height on a Map
**Assistant:** Is sea level going up?

**Tutor:** Yes. Scientists have measured that, on average, sea level around the world is rising slowly, a few millimetres each year, and that it has risen by more than twenty centimetres over the last hundred years or so. There are two main reasons. First, as the world gets warmer, ice on land, such as glaciers and the great ice sheets of Greenland and Antarctica, melts, and the water flows into the sea. Second, water expands, or takes up more space, when it gets warmer, so as the oceans warm up, they get a little bigger. A few millimetres a year sounds tiny, but over many years it adds up. In South Africa, rising seas can cause more flooding and damage along the coast during storms, so coastal towns need to plan carefully where to build.
