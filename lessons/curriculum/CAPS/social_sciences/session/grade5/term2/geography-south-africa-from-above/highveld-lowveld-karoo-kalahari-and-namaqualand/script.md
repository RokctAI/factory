# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the location and character of six regions of South Africa: the Highveld, the Lowveld, the Great Karoo, the Little Karoo, the Kalahari and Namaqualand. The anchor is a Grade 5 classroom in Kroonstad, in the Free State, where the class has been receiving postcards from their teacher, who is driving around South Africa during her long leave. Six postcards are now pinned up next to a big map of South Africa, each with a piece of string leading to the place it shows. One shows giraffes under thorn trees. One shows a flat, dry plain with a windmill. One shows ostriches between mountains. One shows red sand dunes. One shows a carpet of orange and white flowers. And one shows a field of maize under a stormy sky, near Kroonstad itself. The big idea is that South Africa has different regions, each with its own height, landscape, climate and way of life, and that we can find them on a map. The location of these six regions is examinable.

## Subtopic: Highveld and Lowveld

The word veld is Afrikaans for open country or natural grassland and bush. It is used in English in South Africa too.

The Highveld is the high grassland on the plateau. It covers most of Gauteng and the Free State, and parts of Mpumalanga and North West. Most of it is more than about one thousand two hundred metres above sea level, and in places it is more than one thousand eight hundred metres high. The land is wide and open, with gentle slopes and ridges. The natural vegetation is grass, with few trees. Summers are warm, with afternoon thunderstorms and sometimes hail. Winters are dry and cold, with frost at night. The Highveld is an important farming area for maize, sunflowers, wheat and cattle. It also has big cities, coal mines and power stations. Kroonstad is on the Highveld, which is why the last postcard shows a maize field.

The Lowveld is the lower land below the escarpment, in the east of Mpumalanga and Limpopo. It is mostly between about one hundred and fifty and six hundred metres above sea level. It is hot for much of the year, with summer rain, and winters are mild, with little or no frost. The natural vegetation is bushveld, with grass, thorn trees and large trees such as marula and baobab in the north. The Kruger National Park is in the Lowveld, which is why that postcard shows giraffes under thorn trees. Farmers in the Lowveld grow tropical and subtropical fruit, such as oranges, bananas, mangoes, avocados and litchis. Mbombela is the main city of the Lowveld.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Highveld and the Lowveld.

## Subtopic: The Great and Little Karoo

The word Karoo is believed to come from a Khoikhoi word meaning dry or hard place.

The Great Karoo is a large, dry region on the southern part of the plateau. It lies mostly in the Northern Cape, the Western Cape and the Eastern Cape, between the Great Escarpment to the north and the Swartberg mountains to the south. It is mostly flat, with flat-topped hills and small hills called koppies. It gets little rain, so the natural vegetation is mostly small, tough bushes, called Karoo bushes, and grass in wetter years. Summers are very hot and winters are cold, with frost. Few people live there. The main farming is sheep and goats, and windmills pump water from under the ground for the animals, as in the postcard. Beaufort West is one of its towns.

The Little Karoo, also called the Klein Karoo, is a long, narrow valley between two mountain ranges in the Western Cape: the Swartberg to the north and the Langeberg and Outeniqua mountains to the south. It is dry and hot in summer. Farmers grow fruit and vegetables along the rivers, using irrigation, and keep ostriches. Oudtshoorn, the main town, is famous for its ostrich farms, which is why the postcard shows ostriches between mountains. The Cango Caves are nearby.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Great Karoo and the Little Karoo.

## Subtopic: Kalahari and Namaqualand

The Kalahari is a large, dry region with deep red and orange sand, in the north of the Northern Cape and stretching into Botswana and Namibia. It is often called a desert, but it gets enough rain for grass, bushes and camel thorn trees to grow, so many geographers call it a semi-desert. Summers are extremely hot and winters can be very cold at night. Wild animals such as gemsbok, springbok, meerkats and black-maned lions live there. The Kgalagadi Transfrontier Park, shared with Botswana, protects a large part of it. The red dunes in the postcard are in the Kalahari.

Namaqualand is in the far north-west of South Africa, along the Atlantic coast of the Northern Cape and into the Western Cape. It is dry and rocky, and most of its little rain falls in winter. After good winter rains, in August and September, millions of wild flowers bloom, covering the land in orange, yellow, white and purple. People travel from all over the world to see them. Namaqualand also has copper and diamond mines. Springbok is its main town.

On the map in Kroonstad, the class sees a pattern. The Highveld is in the north-east of the plateau, the Lowveld is in the east, below the escarpment, the Karoo is in the south of the plateau, the Kalahari is in the north-west, and Namaqualand is in the far west.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the Lowveld is on the plateau; it is below the escarpment. Two: thinking the Little Karoo is just a small part of the Great Karoo; it is a separate valley between mountain ranges. Three: thinking the Kalahari is completely bare sand; it has grass, bushes and trees. Four: thinking Namaqualand is always covered in flowers; they bloom only for a few weeks after good winter rains.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Kalahari, Namaqualand and where the regions are.
