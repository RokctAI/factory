### subtopic_1 — Mountains, Ranges and Hills
**Assistant:** Why is the Cape of Good Hope called that?

**Tutor:** The Portuguese explorer Bartolomeu Dias was the first European known to sail around the southern tip of Africa, in 1488. He met terrible storms there, and some say he named the headland the Cape of Storms. When he returned to Portugal, the king is said to have renamed it the Cape of Good Hope, because sailing around it gave hope of finding a sea route from Europe to India and the east, for trade in spices and silk. Ten years later, Vasco da Gama did sail all the way to India. Many people think the Cape of Good Hope is the southernmost point of Africa, or the place where the Atlantic and Indian Oceans meet, but both of those are at Cape Agulhas, about one hundred and fifty kilometres to the south-east. The Cape of Good Hope is, however, an important turning point for ships.

### subtopic_2 — Valleys, Rivers and Waterfalls
**Assistant:** How long does it take a river to carve a canyon?

**Tutor:** A very, very long time, usually millions of years. A river carves its valley by wearing away the rock bit by bit. The water carries sand, pebbles and even boulders, especially during floods, and these scrape and grind against the river bed like sandpaper. Over time, the river cuts deeper and deeper. Where the land is slowly being lifted up, the river keeps cutting down to stay at its level, and a deep canyon can form. The Blyde River Canyon was carved by the Blyde River into hard sandstone and other rocks over many millions of years. Its cliffs show layers of rock, like the pages of a giant book, each laid down at a different time. We cannot see a canyon getting deeper in our lifetime, but every flood carries away a little more rock.

### subtopic_3 — Capes and Bays
**Assistant:** Why do whales come to Walker Bay?

**Tutor:** Southern right whales spend the summer far to the south, in the cold, food-rich waters near Antarctica, where they feed on tiny shrimp-like animals called krill. In winter and spring, from about June to November, many of them swim north to the warmer, sheltered bays along the South African coast, such as Walker Bay at Hermanus, False Bay and St Sebastian Bay. Here, the mothers give birth to their calves and look after them in calm water, away from the storms of the open ocean. The whales come very close to the shore, so people can watch them from the cliffs. Hermanus even had a whale crier, who blew a horn made from kelp to tell people where the whales were. Whale watching brings many tourists, showing how a bay can be important for both nature and people.
