### subtopic_1 — Where People Live
**Assistant:** Why did the railway need tunnels in the Hex River Mountains?

**Tutor:** The Hex River Valley is surrounded by steep mountains, and to reach the Karoo, the railway must climb up onto the plateau. The old railway line, built in the eighteen hundreds, zigzagged up the steep slopes of the Hex River Pass in tight curves. Trains had to go very slowly, and long, heavy goods trains needed extra engines to push them up. In 1989, a new route was opened, with a series of tunnels, including one that is more than thirteen kilometres long, one of the longest railway tunnels in Africa. The tunnels give the trains a gentler slope, so they can travel faster and carry heavier loads. It took many years and a lot of money to build, but it made the main railway line between Cape Town and Johannesburg much more efficient.

### subtopic_2 — What People Do
**Assistant:** Why are there so many windmills in the Karoo?

**Tutor:** The Karoo gets very little rain, and there are few rivers that flow all year. But under the ground, there is water stored in cracks and layers of rock, called groundwater. To reach it, farmers drill a deep, narrow hole, called a borehole, down to the water. Then they need a way to lift the water to the surface. A windmill does this using the power of the wind, which blows strongly across the open Karoo plains. As the wind turns the blades, a long rod moves up and down inside the borehole, pumping water up into a dam or a tank, from which sheep and other animals drink. Windmills need no fuel or electricity, which is important far from towns. Today, some farmers use solar-powered pumps instead, but the windmill is still a famous symbol of the Karoo.

### subtopic_3 — How People Overcome Physical Features
**Assistant:** Can people live in very dry places like the Kalahari?

**Tutor:** Yes, people have lived in the Kalahari for thousands of years, and they still do today, but it takes special knowledge and planning. The San who lived there long ago knew where to find water in roots, melons and under the sand, and moved with the seasons. Today, people in the Kalahari live in small towns and on large farms. They use boreholes and pipelines for water, and farm cattle, sheep, goats and game animals that can survive on the natural vegetation. Some people work in tourism, in places like the Kgalagadi Transfrontier Park, or in mines, such as the iron and manganese mines near Kathu and Hotazel. Because the land is so dry, each farm needs a very large area of land to feed its animals, so people live far apart, and towns are small and distant.
