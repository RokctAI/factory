# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a case study of how human activities change landscapes, focusing on the impact of dams on the physical environment. The anchor is a Grade 5 class from Colesberg, in the Northern Cape, on a visit to the Gariep Dam, on the Gariep, or Orange, River, where the Free State, the Eastern Cape and the Northern Cape meet. They walk along the road on top of the curved concrete dam wall. On one side, a huge lake of blue water stretches away for many kilometres, with sailing boats in the distance. On the other side, far below, the river flows on out of the dam through the hydro-electric power station, into a rocky gorge. The guide explains that before the dam was built, there were farms, roads and riverbanks where the lake is now. The big idea is that dams bring great benefits to people, such as water and electricity, but they also change the physical environment, both upstream and downstream, and these changes must be weighed carefully. The impact of dams on the environment is examinable.

## Subtopic: What a Dam Is and Why We Build Them

A dam is a wall built across a river to hold back its water. The water collects behind the wall and forms a lake, called a reservoir. Water can then be let out of the dam slowly, when it is needed.

South Africa is a dry country, and its rainfall is uneven. In some years there are floods, and in others there are droughts. Most rain falls in one season. So South Africa has built hundreds of dams to store water from the wet times for the dry times.

Dams are built for several reasons. They store drinking water for towns and cities. They provide water for irrigation, so farmers can grow crops in dry areas. Some dams make hydro-electric power, by letting water rush through turbines, which spin and make electricity. Dams can help control floods, by holding back flood water. And dams are used for recreation, such as fishing, sailing and camping.

The Gariep Dam was completed in 1971. It is South Africa's largest reservoir, holding more water than any other dam in the country. Its wall is about eighty-eight metres high and more than nine hundred metres long. Water from the Gariep Dam is used to irrigate farms along the river, and it is also sent through a tunnel more than eighty kilometres long, under the mountains, into the Fish River in the Eastern Cape, for farms and towns there. Its power station makes electricity when it is most needed.

The questions for this section are with you now: what dams are and why people build them.

## Subtopic: Changes Upstream

Building a dam changes the land upstream, behind the wall. Upstream means towards the source of a river, against the flow.

When the dam fills, a large area of land is flooded. At the Gariep Dam, the reservoir covers more than three hundred square kilometres when it is full. Everything in that area was drowned: farmland, grazing, riverbanks, trees, roads and buildings. Families who lived there had to move, and some lost homes and farms that had been in their families for generations. When the Lesotho Highlands dams were built more recently, many villages had to be moved, and people still discuss whether they were treated fairly.

Plants and animals that lived along the river lose their habitat, the natural home where they find food and shelter. Animals that cannot move away may die.

A river becomes a lake. The water is still and deep, instead of flowing. This suits some fish and birds, but not the ones that need flowing water.

Rivers carry a lot of mud and sand. When the river reaches the still water of the reservoir, the mud and sand sink to the bottom, a process called siltation. Over many years, the dam slowly fills up with silt, and holds less water.

A large reservoir also loses a lot of water to evaporation, because its wide surface is exposed to the hot sun and wind.

The questions for this section are with you now: how dams change the land upstream.

## Subtopic: Changes Downstream

A dam also changes the river below it, downstream. Downstream means towards the mouth of the river, with the flow.

Less water flows downstream, because water is stored and taken out for farms and cities. Floods, which used to spread water and fresh soil over the land beside the river, become smaller and rarer. Some wetlands and floodplains that depended on floods dry out.

The water that is let out of a dam is often clear and cold, because the mud has settled in the reservoir and the water comes from deep down. Without new mud and sand, the riverbed and riverbanks below the dam can be worn away. At the mouth of the river, less sand reaches the sea, which can change the beaches and the estuary.

The flow of the river also becomes more even. Below the Gariep and other dams on the river, the steady flow has helped tiny biting flies called blackflies to breed in huge numbers, which bother farm animals.

Dams are barriers to fish. Some fish, such as eels, need to swim up and down rivers to breed, and a dam wall blocks their way.

But there are benefits downstream too. Farmers get a reliable supply of water. Towns get drinking water. Floods that once damaged homes and farms are controlled.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a dam is a physical feature; it is built by people, so it is a human feature that changes physical features. Two: thinking dams only bring benefits; they also flood land and change rivers. Three: thinking dams only change the land under the lake; they also change the river all the way downstream to the sea. Four: thinking dams last forever without care; they slowly fill with silt.

The questions for this section are with you now: how dams change rivers downstream, and weighing benefits and costs.
