### subtopic_1 — What a Dam Is and Why We Build Them
**Assistant:** Why was the Gariep Dam's name changed?

**Tutor:** When the dam was completed in 1971, it was named the Hendrik Verwoerd Dam, after a prime minister of South Africa who had been one of the main planners of apartheid, the system of laws that kept people apart by race and treated most South Africans unfairly. After South Africa became a democracy in 1994, many people felt that it was wrong for an important public place, used by everyone, to honour someone linked to apartheid. In 1996, the dam was renamed the Gariep Dam, using the old Khoikhoi name for the river, which is believed to mean great river. The town next to the dam was also renamed Gariep Dam. Changing names of places has been an important and sometimes debated part of South Africa's journey, as people think about whose history is remembered and honoured.

### subtopic_2 — Changes Upstream
**Assistant:** How does a dam make electricity?

**Tutor:** A hydro-electric power station uses the energy of falling water. The water in a dam is high up, behind the wall. When gates are opened, the water rushes down through large pipes inside or beside the wall. At the bottom, it flows through a turbine, which is like a giant wheel with curved blades. The rushing water pushes the blades and makes the turbine spin very fast. The turbine is joined to a generator, a machine with magnets and coils of wire, which turns the spinning movement into electricity. The water then flows out into the river below. Hydro-electric power does not burn fuel or make smoke, which is good for the air. The Gariep Dam power station is often used at busy times of the day, when people need the most electricity, because it can be switched on quickly.

### subtopic_3 — Changes Downstream
**Assistant:** Can a dam ever be removed?

**Tutor:** Yes. In some countries, especially in the United States and Europe, people have removed old dams that were no longer useful, were unsafe, or were doing a lot of harm to rivers. When a dam is removed, the river can begin to flow freely again. Fish can swim up and down to breed, mud and sand travel down to the sea again, and over the years the riverbanks and plants can recover. But removing a big dam is difficult and expensive, and it must be done carefully, because the silt that built up behind the wall can rush downstream all at once. In South Africa, most dams are still very important for water supply, so instead of removing them, people work to reduce their harm, for example by releasing extra water at certain times to imitate natural floods, which helps the river and its plants and animals downstream.
