# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the links between physical features, where people live and what they do. The anchor is a Grade 5 class from Cape Town on a long-distance passenger train to Johannesburg, a journey of more than a day. Leaving the city, the train passes the harbour, then crosses flat farmland and climbs through mountain passes into the green Hex River Valley, full of vineyards. Then it crawls up onto the plateau, and for hours there is only the wide, dry Karoo, with small bushes, windmills, sheep and tiny towns far apart. After a night of sleeping on the train, the class wakes up on the Highveld, with huge maize fields, mine dumps and then the tall buildings of Johannesburg. The big idea is that physical features, such as the shape of the land, rivers, the coast and the climate, help to decide where people live and the kinds of work they do. The links between physical features and human activities are examinable.

## Subtopic: Where People Live

People settle where they can meet their needs. Physical features make some places easier to live in than others.

Flat land is easy to build on, so towns, roads and railways are usually built on plains, plateaus and valley floors, not on steep mountainsides. Water is essential, so towns are often built near rivers, springs or dams. Good soil and enough rain attract farmers, and where there are farms there are also towns, to sell goods and provide services. The coast attracts people because of harbours, fishing and trade, and the mild climate near the sea.

Mountains, very dry areas and very hot or cold places usually have fewer people. It is hard to grow food, find water or build roads there.

In South Africa, the population is not spread evenly. Most people live in the wetter east and north-east, and in the big cities of Gauteng and the coast. The dry west, the Karoo, the Kalahari and Namaqualand have very few people. From the train, the class can see this. Near Cape Town and in the Hex River Valley, there are many farms, houses and towns. In the Karoo, the towns are small and far apart, sometimes more than one hundred kilometres from each other. On the Highveld, near Johannesburg, the towns and suburbs run together into one huge built-up area.

The questions for this section are with you now: how physical features affect where people live.

## Subtopic: What People Do

Physical features also affect the work people do.

In valleys with good soil and water, people farm. The Hex River Valley has rich soil, water from the mountains and hot, dry summers, so it is perfect for growing table grapes, which are sent all over the world. The Breede River valley and the area around Ceres grow apples, pears and other fruit.

In dry areas, crops need too much water, so farmers keep animals that can survive on natural vegetation. In the Karoo, farmers keep sheep and goats, and windmills pump water from under the ground.

On the Highveld, the flat land, deep soils and summer rain are good for maize, sunflowers and wheat, and for cattle. The plateau also has rich minerals, such as gold, coal and platinum, so many people work in mining. Johannesburg grew because of the gold of the Witwatersrand ridge, and coal mines and power stations are found on the Highveld of Mpumalanga.

On the coast, people work in harbours, fishing, shipping and tourism. Cape Town and Durban have big harbours. In the mountains, people work in tourism, forestry and nature conservation, and mountains collect water for dams that supply the cities.

In the warm, wet Lowveld and on the coast of KwaZulu-Natal, people grow sugar cane, bananas, citrus and other subtropical crops.

From the train window, the class can also see how jobs change along the way. In the Hex River Valley, workers are pruning vines and packing grapes into boxes. At small Karoo stations, there are sheep in pens and trucks loading wool. On the Highveld, there are grain silos, tall towers that store maize and wheat, next to the railway line, and then the headgears and dumps of the mines near Johannesburg. Each place has its own work, shaped by its land.

The questions for this section are with you now: how physical features affect the work people do.

## Subtopic: How People Overcome Physical Features

People do not only adapt to physical features. They also find ways to overcome them.

Where mountains are in the way, people build passes, tunnels and bridges. The railway that the class travels on climbs out of the Hex River Valley through long tunnels. Where there is not enough water, people build dams, pipelines and canals to bring it from far away. Gauteng has no big river of its own, so it gets much of its water from the Vaal Dam and from the mountains of Lesotho, through tunnels. Where the land is dry, farmers use irrigation, bringing water from rivers or boreholes to their fields, so that crops can grow even in the Karoo and along the Gariep. Where the coast is rocky or shallow, people build harbour walls and dredge deep channels for ships.

But overcoming physical features costs a lot of money and energy, and can harm the environment. Not every place can be changed, and people must think carefully about whether the benefits are worth the costs.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking people live everywhere equally; most South Africans live in the wetter east and in the big cities. Two: thinking towns are built anywhere by chance; they are usually built on flat land near water. Three: thinking the same crops grow everywhere; each area's soil, water and climate decide what is farmed. Four: thinking people can always change the land with no cost; tunnels, dams and irrigation are expensive and can harm the environment.

The questions for this section are with you now: how people overcome physical features, and the costs.
