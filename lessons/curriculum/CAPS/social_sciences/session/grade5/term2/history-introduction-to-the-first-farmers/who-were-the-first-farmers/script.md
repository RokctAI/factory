# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the topic the first farmers in southern Africa. It links back to hunter-gatherers and herders, and explains who the first farmers were, when and how they came, and what was new about their way of life. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Mashishing, also known as Lydenburg, a town in the hills of Mpumalanga. At the local museum, the learners crowd around a glass case holding copies of the Lydenburg Heads. These are seven hollow clay heads, made by people who lived in the valley about one and a half thousand years ago. Pieces of them were found by a boy on a farm nearby, many years ago, and archaeologists later dug up the rest. The heads have raised lines across the forehead, small ears and neat bands of pattern, and two are big enough to have been worn like masks. The original heads are kept at a museum in Cape Town. Next to the case is a broken clay pot, an iron hoe and some burnt seeds. The big idea is that the first farmers brought new ways of living to southern Africa: growing crops, keeping cattle, making iron tools and living in settled villages. Who the first farmers were and what was new about their way of life is examinable.

## Subtopic: From Hunting to Farming

Remember the people we studied before. The San were hunter-gatherers, who got all their food from the wild and moved with the seasons. The Khoikhoi were herders, who kept sheep and cattle and moved with their herds to find grass and water.

The first farmers lived in a different way. They grew crops, such as sorghum, millet, beans and pumpkins, in fields that they dug and planted. They also kept cattle, sheep, goats and chickens. They are sometimes called mixed farmers, because they did both: crop farming and livestock farming.

Growing crops meant staying in one place. A farmer has to clear the land, plant the seeds, look after the plants and wait months for the harvest. So the first farmers built settled homes and villages, where they lived for many years, instead of moving often.

Farming could produce more food from a small area of land than hunting and gathering. Grain could be stored for the dry season. So farming villages could support more people, and families could be larger.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the first farmers' way of life was different from that of the hunter-gatherers and herders.

## Subtopic: When and How They Came

The first farmers came into southern Africa from further north, slowly, over hundreds of years.

Historians and archaeologists believe that, long before, farming people lived in West and Central Africa, in the area around present-day Cameroon and Nigeria. Over a very long time, more than two thousand years, some of their descendants spread south and east across Africa, a few kilometres at a time, family by family, as children grew up and set up new villages near their parents. It was not one big march, but a slow spread over many generations.

These people spoke related languages, which belong to a big language family called Bantu languages. Many of the languages spoken in South Africa today come from this family, such as isiZulu, isiXhosa, Sesotho, Setswana, Sepedi, Tshivenda, Xitsonga, siSwati and isiNdebele. That is why historians can trace the spread of farming by studying how these languages are related. During apartheid, the word Bantu was misused as an insulting label for Black South Africans, so today historians use it only to describe the language family.

Farming communities had reached what is now Limpopo, Mpumalanga and KwaZulu-Natal by about one thousand eight hundred years ago. Over the next centuries, they settled across the eastern half of southern Africa.

Archaeologists know this from what they dig up: pieces of pottery with special patterns, iron tools and slag, the waste left after iron is made, burnt seeds, cattle bones and the floors of houses. The Lydenburg Heads are among the most famous finds from this time.

The questions for this section are with you now: where the first farmers came from, how they spread and how we know.

## Subtopic: Iron and the Iron Age

The first farmers knew how to make iron, so their time in southern Africa is often called the Iron Age.

Iron is a metal found in certain rocks, called iron ore. To get the iron out, the farmers built a clay furnace, a kind of very hot oven. They filled it with charcoal and crushed iron ore, and used bellows, bags made from animal skin, to pump air into the fire to make it extremely hot. The iron slowly separated from the rock. Then a skilled metalworker, called a smith, heated the iron and hammered it into tools.

Iron tools were much stronger and longer-lasting than stone tools. With iron hoes, farmers could dig fields more easily. With iron axes, they could cut down trees to clear land and build houses. They also made iron spears, knives and arrowheads. Iron was so valuable that it was traded and given as gifts.

The first farmers also made clay pots, for cooking, for storing grain and water, and for brewing sorghum beer. They decorated their pots with patterns, and each community had its own style. Archaeologists use these patterns, like fingerprints, to tell different groups apart.

In this topic, we ask: why and where did the first farmers settle? How did they treat the land? How did they live with the San and Khoikhoi? What were their homesteads and villages like? How were their chiefdoms organised? What did men, women, boys and girls do? And why were the chief and cattle so important?

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the first farmers arrived in one big march; they spread slowly, family by family, over many generations. Two: thinking the first farmers only grew crops; they also kept cattle, sheep and goats. Three: thinking farmers moved often like hunter-gatherers; they lived in settled villages. Four: thinking the word Bantu describes a race; historians use it only for a family of languages.
