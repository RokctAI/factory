# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers where rivers begin and end, and why rivers flow from high areas down towards the sea. The anchor is a Grade 5 class from Bergville, in KwaZulu-Natal, on a two-day hike to the top of Mont-aux-Sources, in the northern Drakensberg. After a long climb, including two steel chain ladders bolted to the cliff, they reach a wide, high plateau, more than three thousand metres above sea level. It is cold and windy, and the grass is wet and spongy underfoot. Little streams trickle out of the marshy ground and join together. A short walk away, one of these streams, the young Thukela River, reaches the edge of the escarpment and plunges over the cliff, falling hundreds of metres in a series of drops called the Tugela Falls. The guide tells them that from this mountain, rivers flow in different directions: one east to the Indian Ocean, and others west, all the way to the Atlantic. The big idea is that rivers begin in high places, flow downhill, and end in the sea, a lake or another river. Where rivers begin and end, and their direction of flow, are examinable.

## Subtopic: The Source

The place where a river begins is called its source.

Many rivers begin high in the mountains, where a lot of rain and snow falls. Some begin at a spring, a place where water from under the ground flows out onto the surface. Some begin in a wetland, a marshy area of wet, spongy ground that soaks up rain like a sponge and slowly releases it. Some begin where snow melts. Often, many small streams join together to start a river. On the Mont-aux-Sources plateau, the wet, spongy grassland is a wetland, and the little streams that trickle out of it are the beginnings of rivers.

The name Mont-aux-Sources is French and means mountain of the sources, because several rivers begin there. The Thukela River begins there and flows east across KwaZulu-Natal to the Indian Ocean. On the other side of the plateau, streams flow into Lesotho and the Free State, and their water eventually joins the Gariep, or Orange, River, which flows west to the Atlantic Ocean.

Near its source, a river is usually small and narrow, and it flows quickly down steep slopes. It may tumble over rocks in rapids and waterfalls. The young Thukela, plunging over the escarpment, is a good example.

A high place where rain falls and runs off in different directions, into different rivers, is called a watershed or divide. The Drakensberg is one of South Africa's most important watersheds.

The questions for this section are with you now: sources, springs, wetlands and watersheds.

## Subtopic: Flowing Downhill

Water always flows downhill. This is because of gravity, the force that pulls everything towards the centre of the Earth. So a river always flows from higher land to lower land, and it keeps going until it reaches the lowest place it can, which is usually the sea.

As a river flows away from its source, it changes. In its upper course, near the source, it is narrow and fast, in a steep, V-shaped valley. In its middle course, it gets wider and deeper, because more streams have joined it, and it flows across gentler slopes, making bends. In its lower course, near the end, it is wide and slow, flowing across flat land, often winding in big loops called meanders.

A river carries more than water. It carries mud, sand, pebbles and even boulders, which it has worn away from the land. Fast water can carry big stones. When the water slows down, it drops the sand and mud.

The sides of a river are its banks. The bottom of a river is its bed. If you stand in a river and face downstream, the way the water flows, the bank on your left is the left bank, and the bank on your right is the right bank.

The questions for this section are with you now: why rivers flow downhill, and the upper, middle and lower course.

## Subtopic: The Mouth

The place where a river ends is called its mouth.

Most rivers end by flowing into the sea. Some end by flowing into a lake. Many smaller rivers end by flowing into a bigger river, and become part of it. In very dry areas, some rivers never reach the sea, but dry up or sink into the sand.

Where a river flows into the sea, fresh river water mixes with salt sea water. In many places the mouth becomes an estuary, a wide, sheltered area where the river meets the tides. Estuaries are rich places for fish, birds and plants. In some places, a river drops so much sand and mud at its mouth that it builds new land, spreading out into many channels. This is called a delta.

In South Africa, the shape of the land decides which way rivers flow. Rivers that begin on the eastern side of the escarpment, in the Drakensberg and the mountains of the east, have a short, steep journey to the Indian Ocean, so they flow east. The Thukela is one of these. Its mouth is on the coast of KwaZulu-Natal, north of Durban. Rivers that begin on the plateau mostly flow west across the gently sloping interior, join the Gariep, and reach the Atlantic Ocean at Alexander Bay. A few rivers in the north flow into the Limpopo, which reaches the Indian Ocean through Mozambique.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a river's mouth is where it begins; the source is where it begins, and the mouth is where it ends. Two: thinking rivers can flow uphill; they always flow downhill, because of gravity. Three: thinking all rivers end in the sea; some end in lakes or other rivers, or dry up. Four: thinking all South African rivers flow east; rivers on the plateau mostly flow west, to the Atlantic.

The questions for this section are with you now: river mouths, estuaries, deltas and directions of flow in South Africa.

The mouth is where a river ends, usually at the sea.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A river begins at its source, high up, in a spring, a wetland or melting snow. It always flows downhill, because of gravity: fast and narrow near the source, slow and wide near the end. It ends at its mouth, usually in the sea, sometimes in a lake or another river. In South Africa, rivers east of the escarpment flow east to the Indian Ocean, and rivers on the plateau mostly flow west to the Atlantic.
