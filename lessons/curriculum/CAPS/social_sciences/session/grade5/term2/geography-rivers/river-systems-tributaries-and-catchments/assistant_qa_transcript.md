### subtopic_1 — Tributaries and Confluences
**Assistant:** Why is the Gariep also called the Orange River?

**Tutor:** The river has had several names. For a very long time, Khoikhoi people called it the Gariep, which is believed to mean great river or big water. People in Lesotho call its upper part the Senqu. In 1779, a Dutch officer called Robert Gordon, who was exploring the area, named it the Orange River, after the Dutch royal family, the House of Orange. It was not named after the colour orange, even though the water can look brownish-orange when it carries a lot of mud after rain. Today, both Gariep and Orange are used, and many maps show both. Using the name Gariep is a way of recognising the people who lived along the river and named it long before European explorers arrived. The big dam on the river is called the Gariep Dam.

### subtopic_2 — River Systems
**Assistant:** How is water moved from one catchment to another?

**Tutor:** Sometimes people build tunnels, canals and pipelines to move water from a catchment that has plenty of water to one that needs more. This is called an inter-basin transfer. South Africa has several big transfer schemes. The Lesotho Highlands Water Project collects water in dams high in the mountains of Lesotho, and sends it through long tunnels into the Vaal system, to supply Gauteng. The Orange-Fish tunnel carries water from the Gariep Dam under the mountains into the Fish River in the Eastern Cape, for farms and towns there. Water is also pumped from the Thukela in KwaZulu-Natal up over the escarpment into the Vaal system. These schemes help the dry interior and the big cities, but they are very expensive, use a lot of energy for pumping, and can harm the rivers that lose water.

### subtopic_3 — Catchment Areas
**Assistant:** Can one person really help a whole river?

**Tutor:** Yes, because a river system is made up of millions of small actions. Every drop of water that reaches a river has crossed someone's land, street or yard first. If people throw litter in the street, rain washes it into drains, which empty into streams and then rivers. If people pour oil, paint or chemicals down the drain, these can end up in a river too. So keeping your street clean, putting rubbish in bins, and never dumping waste near water all help. Saving water at home means less water has to be taken from rivers. Schools and communities can also join river clean-ups, plant indigenous trees along riverbanks, and test the health of a stream by looking for small creatures that live only in clean water. When many people in a catchment do these things, the whole river benefits.
