### subtopic_1 — Rivers Flowing West
**Assistant:** How did the Dusi Canoe Marathon begin?

**Tutor:** The race began in 1951, when a man named Ian Player and a few others paddled from Pietermaritzburg to Durban, following the uMsunduzi and the uMngeni rivers. It took them about six days, and only Player finished. Since then, the race has grown into one of the biggest canoe races in the world, with thousands of paddlers each year, and it now takes three days. The paddlers must deal with rapids, weirs and low water, and in some places they have to get out and run with their canoes on their shoulders, which is called portaging. Ian Player later became a famous conservationist, who helped to save the white rhino from dying out. The race also helps people notice the health of the rivers, and in some years it has been affected by pollution in the water.

### subtopic_2 — Rivers Flowing East and South
**Assistant:** Why does the Mzimvubu not have a big dam?

**Tutor:** The Mzimvubu flows through the Eastern Cape, an area with a lot of rain but where many people have struggled to get reliable water and electricity. For many years, people have discussed building a big dam on the Mzimvubu, to supply water for farming and homes and to make hydro-electric power. Planning started, and the government has announced the project more than once, but building has been delayed many times, because of the cost, questions about whether it is the best plan, and other problems. Some people say a dam would bring jobs and development. Others worry about the effect on the river, the estuary at Port St Johns, and people who would have to move. So for now, the Mzimvubu is one of the few big rivers in South Africa still flowing mostly freely to the sea.

### subtopic_3 — The Limpopo and Reading the Map
**Assistant:** Does the Gariep really reach the sea, if the west is so dry?

**Tutor:** Yes, it does, but it carries much less water to the sea than it would naturally, and its flow changes a lot during the year. Most of the Gariep's water comes from rain and snow far away in the high mountains of Lesotho and from the Vaal catchment on the Highveld. As the river crosses the dry west, the hot sun evaporates some water, and farms along the river take water for irrigation, which is why the land along the river at places like Upington is green with vineyards. Big dams upstream also store water and release it slowly. At its mouth, at Alexander Bay, the river forms an estuary and a wetland that is important for birds. Scientists worry that taking too much water could harm this wetland, so some water must be left to flow to the sea.
