# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers river systems: tributaries, confluences and catchment areas. The anchor is a Grade 5 class from Douglas, a small farming town in the Northern Cape, on an outing to a viewpoint just outside town, where two great rivers meet. From the left comes the Vaal River, flowing in from the north-east after a long journey across the Highveld. From the right comes the Gariep, or Orange, River, flowing in from the east, all the way from the mountains of Lesotho. Where they meet, the two rivers join into one wide river, which carries on westwards towards Namibia and the Atlantic Ocean. Around the meeting place, green fields of lucerne, wheat and pecan nut trees are watered by the rivers, while the land beyond is dry and dusty. The big idea is that rivers do not flow alone: smaller rivers join bigger ones to form a river system, and all the land that drains into that system is its catchment area. River systems, tributaries and catchments are examinable.

## Subtopic: Tributaries and Confluences

A tributary is a smaller river or stream that flows into a bigger river. The bigger river is called the main river. A tributary does not reach the sea on its own. Instead, it adds its water to the main river.

The place where two rivers meet and join together is called a confluence. At Douglas, the class is looking at the confluence of the Vaal and the Gariep. The Vaal is the biggest tributary of the Gariep. After the confluence, there is only one river, and it keeps the name Gariep, or Orange.

Tributaries can have their own tributaries. For example, the Caledon, or Mohokare, River is a tributary of the Gariep. The Wilge River is a tributary of the Vaal, which is itself a tributary of the Gariep. Small streams flow into tributaries, which flow into bigger tributaries, which flow into the main river. It is a bit like the branches of a tree: twigs join small branches, small branches join big branches, and big branches join the trunk.

On a map, you can tell which way tributaries flow by looking at the angle where they join the main river. Tributaries usually join pointing downstream, in the same direction as the main river flows.

Some confluences are easy to see, because the two rivers have different colours. One may be muddy brown after rain, while the other is clearer. For a short distance after they meet, the two colours flow side by side before they mix.

The questions for this section are with you now: tributaries, main rivers and confluences.

## Subtopic: River Systems

A main river together with all its tributaries is called a river system. If you drew a river system from above, it would look like a tree lying on its side, with the main river as the trunk and the tributaries as branches, all leading towards the mouth.

The Gariep-Vaal system is the biggest river system in South Africa. The Gariep begins as the Senqu in the high mountains of Lesotho, and flows west across South Africa. Along the way, the Caledon joins it, and then, near Douglas, the Vaal joins it. The Vaal begins on the Highveld in Mpumalanga, and flows west across the plateau, past the south of Gauteng, collecting tributaries such as the Wilge, the Klip and the Harts. Further west, a few other rivers join the Gariep, including the Fish River from Namibia. Finally, the Gariep reaches the Atlantic Ocean at Alexander Bay.

Another important system is the Thukela system in KwaZulu-Natal. The Thukela begins in the Drakensberg, and tributaries such as the Buffalo, the Mooi and the Bushmans join it before it reaches the Indian Ocean.

The Limpopo system in the north collects water from rivers such as the Crocodile, the Marico, the Mogalakwena and the Olifants, and flows through Mozambique to the Indian Ocean.

The questions for this section are with you now: river systems, and the Gariep-Vaal, Thukela and Limpopo systems.

## Subtopic: Catchment Areas

A catchment area is all the land that drains into a river system. When rain falls anywhere in a catchment area, the water that runs off the land flows into streams, then into tributaries, and finally into the main river. A catchment area is also called a drainage basin.

The edge of a catchment area is a line of high ground called a watershed, or divide. Rain that falls on one side of the watershed flows into one river system, and rain that falls on the other side flows into a different system. The Drakensberg is a watershed: rain that falls on its eastern side flows into rivers that go to the Indian Ocean, and rain on its western side flows into the Gariep-Vaal system, towards the Atlantic.

The catchment area of the Gariep-Vaal system is huge. It covers most of the plateau of South Africa, all of Lesotho, and parts of Namibia and Botswana. That means rain falling in Gauteng, in the Free State, and in the mountains of Lesotho can all end up flowing past Douglas.

Catchments matter because everything that happens in a catchment affects the river. If a factory or a town upstream pollutes a tributary, the pollution flows downstream to everyone below it. If wetlands near the source are destroyed, the river may flood more in summer and dry up more in winter. If too much water is taken out upstream for farms and cities, there is less for people and animals downstream. So everyone who lives in a catchment shares the river and must look after it together.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a tributary flows away from the main river; it flows into it. Two: thinking a confluence is where a river ends in the sea; it is where two rivers meet. Three: thinking a catchment area is only the land right next to the river; it is all the land that drains into the river system. Four: thinking pollution stays where it is dumped; it flows downstream through the whole system.

The questions for this section are with you now: catchment areas, watersheds and why catchments matter.
