# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the roles of men, women, boys and girls in early farming societies, children's economic activity, and initiation into adulthood. The anchor is a Grade 5 class near Mthatha, in the Eastern Cape, on a history project. Two elders from the village have come to the classroom. The grandfather tells them that when he was a small boy, he looked after the goats and calves, and when he was older, he took the cattle out to graze in the hills, with a stick in his hand and a bag of food on his back. The grandmother tells them that as a girl she carried her baby brother on her back, fetched water from the river in a clay pot on her head, and stood in the sorghum field from early morning, shouting and clapping to chase away the birds. The learners write down everything, and they notice that the elders' childhoods were full of work as well as play. The big idea is that in early farming societies, everyone, including children, had a role, and that roles were usually divided by age and by whether a person was male or female. These roles are examinable.

## Subtopic: Men and Women

In early farming societies, work was usually divided between men and women. The details were different from one community to another, but there was a common pattern.

Men were mostly responsible for the cattle. They cared for the herds, milked the cows in many communities, and looked after the kraal. Men cleared new fields by cutting down trees and bushes, built the wooden frames of houses and kraals, hunted, and defended the community. Men worked iron, carved wood and worked skins. Senior men took part in the council and public meetings.

Women were mostly responsible for growing crops. They hoed, planted, weeded and harvested the fields. They ground grain, cooked, brewed beer, fetched water and firewood, and looked after young children. Women plastered the walls and smeared the floors of houses, and in many communities did the thatching. They made clay pots, baskets and mats.

Both roles were important, and the community needed both. The food from the fields, grown mostly by women, fed the family every day, while the cattle, cared for mostly by men, gave milk and wealth.

In those societies, men usually held the positions of leadership, but some women, such as senior wives, mothers of chiefs and healers, were very respected and influential.

The questions for this section are with you now: the roles of men and women in early farming societies.

## Subtopic: Boys and Girls at Work

Children were an important part of the workforce. From a young age, they helped their families to produce food. This is called children's economic activity.

Boys started by looking after goats, sheep and calves near the homestead. As they grew older, they took the cattle out to graze, sometimes far from home, and brought them back in the evening. While herding, boys learnt about the land, grass and water, practised stick fighting, hunted birds and small animals, and learnt about each animal in the herd by name.

Girls helped their mothers in the fields and at home. They carried and looked after younger brothers and sisters, fetched water and firewood, helped to grind grain and cook, and learnt to make pots, mats and baskets. Both girls and boys helped to chase birds away from the ripening grain.

By working alongside adults, children learnt the skills they would need as grown-ups. There were no schools as we know them, so the homestead, the fields and the grazing land were the classrooms, and the elders were the teachers. Children also learnt from songs, games, riddles and stories told around the fire in the evening.

The questions for this section are with you now: children's work and how children learnt.

## Subtopic: Becoming an Adult

In many farming societies, the step from childhood to adulthood was marked by initiation. Initiation is a set of ceremonies and teachings that marks a young person becoming an adult member of the community.

Initiation usually happened around the teenage years. Boys of a similar age were often initiated together, as a group, and spent a period of time away from the homestead, with older men who taught them. Girls also had their own initiation, with older women as their teachers. Different communities had different names and customs for these ceremonies, and many communities still practise them today.

During initiation, young people were taught about the history and customs of their people, how to behave as adults, their duties to their families and community, and respect for elders. Initiation was a time of lessons and of testing courage and patience.

After initiation, young people were treated as adults. They could marry, and young men could start to build up their own herds. Boys who were initiated together often formed an age group, and stayed close friends and helpers for life.

These ceremonies are respected and private traditions, so many details are kept within the community.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking children did no work; they helped to herd, fetch water, scare birds and care for younger children. Two: thinking women did unimportant work; their crops fed the family every day. Three: thinking initiation was just a party; it was a time of teaching and growing into adult duties. Four: thinking roles were exactly the same everywhere; they differed from community to community.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the two elders near Mthatha — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Men and Women

Men looked after cattle, cleared fields, built frames, hunted and worked iron.

Women grew crops, cooked, fetched water, plastered walls and made pots.

Both jobs were needed. The crops fed the family every day, and the cattle gave milk and wealth.

Roles were not exactly the same in every community.

Keep the grandfather's herding stick in mind and try the questions for this part: men and women.

## Subtopic: Children at Work

Boys herded goats, then calves, then cattle.

Girls helped in the fields, fetched water and cared for babies.

Children chased birds from the grain. They learnt by working with adults.

Hold the grandmother clapping in the sorghum field in mind and try this part's questions: boys and girls at work.

## Subtopic: Initiation

Initiation marked the step from child to adult. Young people were taught their duties and customs by older men and older women.

After initiation, they could marry. Boys initiated together stayed friends for life.
