### subtopic_1 — Building a Home
**Assistant:** Why did people use cattle dung on their floors?

**Tutor:** It might sound strange, but cattle dung is an excellent building material. Cattle eat grass, so their dung is mostly chewed-up plant fibres. When it is mixed with earth and water and spread on a floor, the fibres bind the earth together, a bit like the steel rods in concrete, and it dries hard and smooth. Once it is dry, it does not smell. It helps to stop dust, it fills cracks, and many people believe it helps to keep insects such as fleas away. The floor can be polished until it shines, and it is cool in summer and warm in winter. It also needs regular care: women would smear a fresh layer every week or two, which kept the floor clean and neat. Many people in rural South Africa still use dung floors today, and they are proud of a well-kept, shining floor.

### subtopic_2 — The Homestead
**Assistant:** Why was the cattle kraal in the middle of the homestead?

**Tutor:** There were several good reasons. First, safety. Cattle were a family's most valuable possession, so keeping them in the middle, surrounded by the houses, protected them from wild animals, such as lions and hyenas, and from thieves at night. Second, importance. Cattle were not only wealth, they were also used in ceremonies to honour the ancestors and in marriages, so the kraal was a respected, almost sacred place, often where the men met and important decisions were made. Third, storage. Grain pits dug in the kraal floor were hidden and protected, because the floor was trampled hard by the cattle and covered in dung, which kept the pits dry and hard to find. Archaeologists call this pattern, with the kraal in the centre and houses around it, the Central Cattle Pattern, and they find it at many farming sites across southern Africa.

### subtopic_3 — From Homestead to Village
**Assistant:** How do archaeologists find old villages?

**Tutor:** Old farming villages leave clues, even after the houses have fallen down and been covered by soil. Archaeologists look for pieces of broken pottery on the surface of the ground, especially in fields after ploughing or along eroded riverbanks. They look at aerial photographs and satellite images, because old kraals and stone walls can show up as circles in the grass, where plants grow differently. When they find a promising site, they dig carefully in small squares, layer by layer, recording everything. They find the hard, burnt floors of houses, which they call daga floors, as well as pots, grindstones, iron slag, beads and the bones of animals. Burnt seeds tell them which crops were grown. By dating charcoal from the fireplaces, they can find out how old the village is. Local people who know the land often help archaeologists to find sites.
