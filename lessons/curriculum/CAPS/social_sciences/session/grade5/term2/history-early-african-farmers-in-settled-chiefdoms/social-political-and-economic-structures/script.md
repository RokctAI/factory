# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the social, political and economic structures of early African farming societies. The anchor is a Grade 5 class from Mahikeng, in North West, on a visit to a village where the community still has a kgotla. A kgotla is a traditional meeting place in Setswana-speaking communities. This one is an open space under a huge old tree, with a low, curved wall of stones and poles around it. Benches stand in a half circle, and at one end is a chair for the kgosi, the chief. An elderly councillor welcomes the class and explains that, for hundreds of years, people have met in places like this to discuss the problems of the community, settle arguments and make decisions. He tells them a well-known Setswana saying, which means a chief is a chief because of the people. The big idea is that early farming communities were well organised: people were linked by family and clan, they were led by chiefs who governed with councils and the people, and they made a living by farming, crafts and trade. Social, political and economic structures are examinable.

## Subtopic: Families and Clans

Social structure means the way people in a society are linked to each other and arranged into groups.

The basic group was the family. Families in early farming societies were large. They included parents, children, grandparents, uncles, aunts and cousins, who often lived close together and helped each other.

Families who shared a common ancestor from long ago formed a clan. Members of a clan often shared a clan name, and many South Africans today still use their clan names, such as isiduko in isiXhosa or isibongo in isiZulu, as a sign of respect. In many communities, a person could not marry someone from their own clan.

Respect for elders was very important. Older people were believed to have wisdom and experience, and young people listened to them. Ancestors, the spirits of family members who had died, were believed to still care for the living, and were honoured in ceremonies.

Children were taught the history, values and customs of their people through stories, songs, praise poems and by working alongside adults.

The questions for this section are with you now: families, clans, elders and ancestors.

## Subtopic: Chiefs and Councils

Political structure means how a society is governed: who leads, who makes decisions and how rules are kept.

At the head of a chiefdom was the chief. The position was usually passed down in a royal family, often from father to son.

The chief did not rule alone. He was advised by a council of senior men, often his relatives and respected elders. Below him were headmen, who looked after villages or districts and reported to the chief.

Important matters were discussed at public meetings, such as the kgotla, or an imbizo among Zulu-speaking people. Men of the community could speak their minds, and the chief listened before making a decision. The chief and his council also acted as a court, where disputes were settled and people who broke the rules were judged. Fines were often paid in cattle.

The chief had duties: to look after the land and give it out to families, to protect the people, to care for the poor and hungry, and to lead important ceremonies. If a chief was cruel or unfair, people could leave and join another chief, which encouraged chiefs to rule well.

The questions for this section are with you now: chiefs, headmen, councils and public meetings.

## Subtopic: Making a Living

Economic structure means how people produce, share and exchange goods.

Most people were farmers. Each family grew most of its own food and kept its own animals. Cattle were the main form of wealth.

Some people were specialists, with special skills. Ironworkers smelted iron ore in clay furnaces and made hoes, axes, spears and knives. Their skills were often kept within certain families. Potters, usually women, made clay pots for cooking, storing and brewing. Others carved wood, worked skins, made baskets or wove mats. Healers and rainmakers were also specialists.

People traded, usually by barter, which means swapping goods without using money. A family with extra grain could swap it for a pot, a hoe or a goat. Iron hoes, cattle and beads were valuable in trade.

There was also long-distance trade. Ivory, animal skins and later gold were carried to the east coast, where traders who sailed across the Indian Ocean exchanged them for glass beads and cloth. Glass beads from far away have been found at early farming sites in southern Africa.

People also gave gifts of grain or cattle to the chief, called tribute, and the chief shared food with the poor and in times of hunger.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the chief ruled alone; he governed with councillors, headmen and public meetings. Two: thinking early farmers used money; they bartered and used cattle as wealth. Three: thinking a clan is the same as a household; a clan is a large group with a shared ancestor. Four: thinking people could never leave a bad chief; they could move to another chief.

The questions for this section are with you now: farming, specialists, barter, trade and tribute.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the kgotla near Mahikeng — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Family and Clan

Families were large. Families with the same ancestor from long ago formed a clan, with a clan name.

People respected their elders and honoured the ancestors. Ancestors are the spirits of family members who have died.

Children learnt the history and customs of their people from stories, songs and praise poems, and by working with adults.

Keep the half circle of benches in mind and try the questions for this part: families and clans.

## Subtopic: Chief and Council

The chief led the chiefdom. A council of elders advised him, and headmen looked after villages.

People met at the kgotla to talk, and the chief listened before deciding. He also settled arguments, like a judge, and fines were often paid in cattle.

The chief had to care for his people. If he was cruel or unfair, people could leave and follow another chief.

Hold the chair under the big old tree in mind and try this part's questions: chiefs and councils.

## Subtopic: Work and Trade

Most people farmed, and cattle were their wealth.

Specialists made iron tools, pots and baskets. People swapped goods without money. This is called barter.

Traders on the east coast swapped ivory, skins and later gold for glass beads and cloth. People also gave gifts to the chief, called tribute.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Early farming societies were built on large families and clans, with respect for elders and ancestors. A chief led the chiefdom, advised by a council, with headmen in the villages, and important matters were discussed at public meetings such as the kgotla. Most people were farmers, while specialists made iron tools and pots. People bartered goods, traded with the east coast and gave tribute to the chief.

Hold the words a chief is a chief because of the people in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: making a living.
