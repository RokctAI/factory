# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers agriculture in early African farming societies: the crops they grew and the livestock they kept. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Giyani, in Limpopo, during a morning in the school garden. The learners have planted a small field of sorghum, with tall stems and heavy heads of reddish-brown seed, a row of millet, some cowpeas, which are a kind of bean, and a few melons. Two learners are on bird duty, waving their arms and clapping to chase away the small birds that try to steal the ripening grain. At the gate, a farmer from the village stands with two Nguni cattle, one brown and white, one speckled black, with long, curved horns. Next to the garden, the teacher has laid out a flat grindstone, a round stone for grinding, and an old iron hoe borrowed from the farmer. The big idea is that early African farmers lived mainly from crops they grew and animals they kept, using iron tools, and that farming gave them a steady supply of food so that they could live in settled villages. Agriculture, crops and livestock, is examinable.

## Subtopic: Growing Crops

The most important crop of the early farmers was sorghum, a grain that grows well in hot, fairly dry places. Another grain was millet, which has small seeds. Both sorghum and millet were first grown in Africa, long before the first farmers came to southern Africa.

The farmers also grew cowpeas and jugo beans, also called Bambara groundnuts, as well as melons, gourds and leafy vegetables. Gourds are plants with a hard shell, which were dried and used as bottles, bowls and spoons. Maize, which is the main grain in South Africa today, came much later. It was first grown in the Americas and was brought to Africa by the Portuguese, and it spread across southern Africa only in the last few hundred years.

Farming followed the seasons. Fields were cleared in winter and spring, often by cutting and burning bushes. When the first summer rains came, the soil was dug with iron hoes and the seeds were planted. Through summer, the fields were weeded, and children chased birds away from the ripening grain. The harvest came in late summer and autumn.

After some years, the soil in a field became tired and gave smaller harvests. Then the farmers cleared a new field and let the old one rest, so that the soil could recover.

The questions for this section are with you now: the crops, the farming year and caring for the soil.

## Subtopic: From Field to Food

After the harvest, the grain had to be dried, threshed and stored. Threshing means beating the heads of grain to knock the seeds loose. The seeds were then tossed in the air in flat baskets, so that the wind blew away the light husks.

Grain was stored in pits dug in the ground, often in the floor of the cattle kraal, or in granaries, which are large baskets or clay containers raised off the ground on stones or poles, to keep out rats, damp and insects. Good storage meant that a family could eat all year, until the next harvest, and keep seed to plant.

To make food, women ground the grain on a flat grindstone, rubbing it with a smaller, round stone until it became flour. The flour was cooked with water to make porridge, which was the main daily food. Sorghum was also used to brew beer, which was drunk at work parties and ceremonies, and was an important part of social life.

The farmers did not stop hunting and gathering. They still hunted wild animals, fished and collected wild fruits, honey and plants, which gave them extra food, especially in hard times.

The questions for this section are with you now: harvesting, storing and preparing food.

## Subtopic: Cattle, Sheep and Goats

The early farmers kept cattle, sheep and goats, as well as dogs, and later chickens.

Cattle were the most valued animals. They gave milk, which was often left to go sour in gourds or containers, and eaten as thick sour milk, like amasi. Meat was usually eaten only at special occasions, such as weddings, funerals and ceremonies for the ancestors, because a family did not want to kill its wealth. Hides were used to make clothing, blankets, bags and shields. Horns were made into containers, and dung was used for floors, plaster and fuel.

The Nguni cattle at the school gate belong to a hardy breed that many people believe is descended from the cattle of early farmers. They can live on poor grazing, survive heat and dry seasons, and resist many diseases carried by ticks.

Sheep and goats were easier to keep than cattle, because they eat a wider range of plants and need less water. They gave meat, milk and skins.

Herding was a daily task. In the morning, the cattle were taken out to graze, often by boys, and in the evening, they were brought back to the kraal to be safe from wild animals.

The farmers did not use ploughs. Fields were dug by hand with iron hoes. Ploughs pulled by oxen came to South Africa much later.

Two: thinking farmers ploughed with oxen; they dug their fields by hand with iron hoes. # Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the school garden in Giyani — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Sorghum and Millet

The farmers grew sorghum and millet. They also grew beans, melons and gourds.

They planted when the summer rains came, and harvested in late summer and autumn. They dug with iron hoes.

When a field was tired, they let it rest. Maize came much later.

Keep the heavy reddish-brown heads of sorghum in mind and try the questions for this part: growing crops.

## Subtopic: Grinding and Storing

They dried the grain and stored it in pits or raised granaries, so that there was food all year.

Women ground the grain on a grindstone to make flour for porridge. Sorghum was also used to make beer.

They still hunted and gathered wild food too.

Hold the flat grindstone in mind and try this part's questions: from field to food.

## Subtopic: Cattle

Cattle gave milk, hides, horns and dung. Meat was eaten at special occasions. Sheep and goats gave meat, milk and skins. Boys herded the cattle.
