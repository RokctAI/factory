# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how early African farmers lived in settled chiefdoms, focusing on homesteads and villages. The anchor is a Grade 5 class from Eshowe, in KwaZulu-Natal, on a visit to a heritage village in the green hills nearby, where a traditional homestead has been rebuilt to show how people lived long ago. The homestead stands on a gentle slope. Round houses with thatched roofs are arranged in a wide circle. In the middle is a large circular cattle kraal, with a fence of thick wooden poles. Inside one house, it is cool and dim, with a smooth, shiny floor and a fireplace in the centre. A guide shows the class how the floor is made, how the poles of the walls are woven together, and where each member of the family slept. The big idea is that the homestead was the basic unit of farming society: a family's home, built from local materials and carefully arranged around the cattle kraal, and that homesteads grouped together formed villages and chiefdoms. Homesteads and villages are examinable.

## Subtopic: Building a Home

The first farmers built their houses from materials they found around them: wood, grass, mud, stones and cattle dung.

Most early houses were round. One common way of building was wattle and daub. First, builders pushed a ring of wooden poles into the ground. Then they wove thin, flexible sticks in and out of the poles, like a basket. This woven framework is called the wattle. Then they plastered the wattle, inside and out, with a thick mixture of mud, clay and often cattle dung, which dried hard. This plaster is called the daub. The roof was made of a cone of poles, covered with thick grass thatch, which kept out the rain and kept the house cool in summer and warm in winter.

Another style, used in parts of KwaZulu-Natal, was a beehive-shaped house, with a dome made of bent saplings, which are young, thin trees, completely covered with thatch, from the ground to the top.

Floors were made of earth mixed with cattle dung, beaten flat and polished until they were hard and smooth. Cattle dung, once dry, does not smell, and it helped to keep insects away and stop dust. The floor was re-smeared regularly to keep it clean and smooth.

Inside, there was a fireplace in the middle for cooking and warmth, with the smoke escaping through the thatch. People slept on mats woven from reeds or grass, and kept clay pots, baskets and tools along the walls.

Building was a shared job. Men usually cut and set the poles and built the frame, while women often plastered the walls, made the floors and, in many communities, did the thatching.

The questions for this section are with you now: how houses were built and what they were made of.

## Subtopic: The Homestead

A homestead was the home of one family, made up of several houses. In isiZulu, a homestead is called an umuzi, and in Sesotho and Setswana a motse, though these words can also mean a village.

A family was often large. The head of the homestead was usually the father, and the family included his wife or wives, their children, and sometimes grandparents, unmarried relatives and others who were attached to the family. Each wife usually had her own house, where she lived with her young children, and her own fields and kitchen garden. Older boys and girls might have separate houses.

The homestead was laid out in a careful pattern. The houses were arranged in a circle or a horseshoe shape. In the centre was the cattle kraal, an enclosure made of poles, thorn branches or stones, where the cattle were kept safe at night. Grain was often stored in deep pits dug in the floor of the kraal, sealed with clay, where it stayed dry and safe from thieves and insects, or in raised baskets or small granaries on stilts.

The main house, belonging to the senior wife or the head of the family, was usually at the top of the slope, opposite the entrance to the kraal. The entrance to the homestead was usually at the lower end of the slope. Building on a slope helped rainwater to drain away.

Fields were outside the homestead, often some distance away, and grazing land was beyond that.

The questions for this section are with you now: the homestead, the family and the layout around the cattle kraal.

## Subtopic: From Homestead to Village

Several homesteads near each other formed a village. Families in a village were often related, for example brothers and their families, or cousins.

Early farming villages were usually built on the slopes of river valleys, near water, fields and grazing. Archaeologists have found the remains of many villages, with the hard floors of houses, broken pots, iron tools, grindstones and the bones of cattle, sheep and goats.

Villages of the same area were linked by family ties, marriage, trade and shared customs. They were led by headmen, who in turn followed a chief. A chief, his people and the land they lived on formed a chiefdom.

Later, from about a thousand years ago, some farming communities on the Highveld and in the north built houses and kraals with stone walls instead of wood, because there were few trees. The remains of many stone-walled settlements can still be seen in Mpumalanga, the Free State and North West.

Today, many rural homesteads in South Africa still follow parts of the old pattern, with round houses, a cattle kraal, and floors smeared with dung, although many people now also build square houses with corrugated iron roofs and bricks.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the first farmers lived in square brick houses; most early houses were round, made of wood, mud and thatch. Two: thinking a homestead was one house; it was a group of houses for one large family. Three: thinking the cattle kraal was outside the homestead; it was usually in the centre. Four: thinking cattle dung floors were dirty; dried and polished, they were hard, clean and smooth.

The questions for this section are with you now: villages, chiefdoms and stone-walled settlements.
