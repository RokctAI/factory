### subtopic_1 — Families and Clans
**Assistant:** What does a chief is a chief because of the people mean?

**Tutor:** It means that a chief's power comes from the people who follow him. A chief with no followers is not really a chief at all. In early farming societies, people were not stuck with one chief. If a chief was cruel, greedy or unfair, families could pack up, take their cattle, and move to the land of another chief who would welcome them. A chief who lost many followers lost his strength and wealth. So a good chief had to listen to his council and his people, settle disputes fairly, share food in times of hunger and look after the land. That is why public meetings, like the kgotla and the imbizo, were so important. People could speak openly there, and the chief heard what they thought before making a decision. Many people see these meetings as an early form of public discussion, a little like a community meeting today.

### subtopic_2 — Chiefs and Councils
**Assistant:** Why did people use cattle instead of money?

**Tutor:** Coins and paper money did not exist in early farming societies in southern Africa. Instead, cattle did many of the jobs that money does today. They were a way of storing wealth: a family with many cattle was rich. They were used to pay for important things, such as marriage, when the groom's family gave cattle to the bride's family, and to pay fines in the chief's court. Unlike money, cattle could also grow, because cows had calves, and they gave milk, hides and dung every day. Smaller things were bartered: a basket of grain could be swapped for a clay pot or a hoe. Iron hoes and beads were also valued in trade. So people had a working system for exchanging goods and showing wealth, even without coins. Cattle are still important in many South African families today, especially for weddings and ceremonies.

### subtopic_3 — Making a Living
**Assistant:** How do we know there was trade across the Indian Ocean?

**Tutor:** The best evidence comes from objects that archaeologists have dug up. At many early farming sites in southern Africa, they have found small glass beads. Glass was not made in southern Africa at that time, so the beads must have come from far away. By studying how the beads were made and what they are made of, scientists can tell that many came from places around the Indian Ocean, such as India, the Middle East and other lands. At some sites, they have also found pieces of pottery from those lands, and, at later sites, cowrie shells from the Indian Ocean. There are also old writings by travellers and traders from those lands, describing the gold and ivory trade of the east African coast. Together, these sources show that people in southern Africa were connected to a huge trading world, long before Europeans arrived.
