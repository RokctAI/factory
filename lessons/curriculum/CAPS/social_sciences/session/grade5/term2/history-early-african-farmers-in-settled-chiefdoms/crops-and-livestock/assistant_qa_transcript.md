### subtopic_1 — Growing Crops
**Assistant:** Why didn't the first farmers grow maize?

**Tutor:** Because maize was not yet in Africa. Maize was first grown by farmers in Mexico and Central America, thousands of years ago, and it was unknown in Africa, Europe and Asia until about five hundred years ago. After European sailors reached the Americas, maize was carried across the ocean. The Portuguese, who traded along the African coast, brought it to Africa. From the coast it spread inland, slowly, from farmer to farmer, over the next few hundred years. Many African farmers took it up, because it can give a big harvest. Today maize is South Africa's main grain, and pap is eaten every day in many homes. But the first farmers, about one thousand eight hundred years ago, grew sorghum and millet, grains that were first grown in Africa itself. Sorghum is still grown and eaten in South Africa, for porridge and traditional beer.

### subtopic_2 — From Field to Food
**Assistant:** Why did the farmers burn the bushes before planting?

**Tutor:** Clearing land by hand is very hard work, especially with only iron axes and hoes. Burning was a quicker way to get rid of dry bushes, grass and fallen branches. The ash left behind also acted like a fertiliser, putting goodness back into the soil for the new crops. This way of farming is sometimes called slash and burn, or shifting farming. After a few years, the soil in the field became tired and the harvests got smaller. Then the farmers moved to a new patch, cleared and burnt it, and let the old field rest, so that bushes grew back and the soil recovered. This worked well when there was plenty of land and not too many people. Today, with many more people, burning is controlled carefully, because uncontrolled fires can damage the land, kill animals and spread to homes.

### subtopic_3 — Cattle, Sheep and Goats
**Assistant:** Why are Nguni cattle so tough?

**Tutor:** Nguni cattle have lived in southern Africa for a very long time, and many people believe they are descended from the cattle of early farmers. Over hundreds of years, the animals that survived best in the local conditions, with heat, dry seasons, poor grazing and diseases carried by ticks, had more calves, and these calves were tough too. Farmers also chose the best animals to breed from. So the breed slowly became well suited to southern Africa. Nguni cattle are medium-sized, so they need less food than big breeds, and their skin helps them to resist ticks. They are also famous for their beautiful hides, with many different colours and patterns, and in isiZulu there are many special names for these patterns. Today, Nguni cattle are still kept by many farmers in South Africa, and some farmers are breeding more of them again because they are so hardy.
