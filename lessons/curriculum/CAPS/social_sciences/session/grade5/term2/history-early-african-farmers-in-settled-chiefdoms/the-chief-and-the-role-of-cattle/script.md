# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the role of the chief and the role of cattle in early African farming societies. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Ulundi, in KwaZulu-Natal, on a visit to a farmer who keeps a herd of Nguni cattle on the hills outside town. The cattle come in every colour: white with black spots, red and white, brown speckled with white, grey and almost blue. The farmer tells the class that in isiZulu there are many special names for the colours and patterns of cattle hides, and that, as a boy, he could name every animal in his father's herd. He shows them the kraal, made of strong poles, and explains that, in the old days, it was the heart of the homestead, where men met and where the ancestors were honoured. Then he says that, long ago, if you wanted to know how important a man was, you looked at his cattle. The big idea is that in early farming societies, the chief was the leader, judge and protector of his people, and that cattle were wealth, a link between families, a way of honouring the ancestors and a source of the chief's power. The role of the chief and the role of cattle is examinable.

## Subtopic: The Role of the Chief

The chief was the leader of a chiefdom. His position was usually inherited, passed down in the royal family.

The chief had many roles. He was the leader of the people, making decisions on important matters with the advice of his council. He was the judge in the highest court, where serious disputes were settled. He was in charge of the land: it belonged to the whole community, but the chief, through his headmen, gave each family land for a homestead, fields and grazing. He led the defence of the chiefdom and called up the young men in times of war.

The chief also had religious roles. He was seen as a link between the people and the royal ancestors, and he led important ceremonies, such as rain ceremonies in times of drought and first fruits ceremonies at harvest time. In some communities, nobody could eat the new crops until the chief had tasted the first fruits in a special ceremony.

The chief received tribute: gifts of grain, cattle and labour from his people, for example, working in his fields. In return, he was expected to be generous, to feed visitors, to care for widows, orphans and the poor, and to share food in times of hunger.

The questions for this section are with you now: the roles of the chief as leader, judge, guardian of the land and religious leader.

## Subtopic: Cattle as Wealth and as a Link

Cattle were much more than food. They were the main form of wealth. A family with a large herd was rich and respected.

Cattle were central to marriage. When a couple married, the groom's family gave cattle to the bride's family. In isiZulu and isiXhosa this is called lobola, in Sesotho bohadi, and in Setswana bogadi. This gift was not buying a wife. It thanked the bride's family, showed that the groom could support a family, and joined the two families together in a lasting bond. If there were problems in the marriage, the families would meet to help.

Cattle were also important in religion. When a family wanted to honour or thank the ancestors, or ask for their help, an animal was slaughtered in the kraal, and the meat was shared by the family and neighbours. Cattle were slaughtered at important events such as weddings, funerals and the birth of a child.

Cattle were used to pay fines in court, and they were given as gifts to make friendships and alliances.

The questions for this section are with you now: cattle as wealth, in marriage and in ceremonies.

## Subtopic: Cattle and the Power of the Chief

The chief usually had the largest herds in the chiefdom. Some cattle came from tribute, some from fines paid in his court, and some from raids in times of war.

A chief used his cattle to build support. In a system called mafisa in Sesotho and Setswana, and ukusisa in isiZulu, a wealthy person, often the chief, loaned cattle to a poorer family. The poorer family looked after the cattle and could use the milk, and sometimes kept some of the calves. The cattle still belonged to the owner. This helped poorer families, and it made them loyal to the chief, because they owed him thanks.

The chief also used cattle to feed people at gatherings and in times of hunger, to reward loyal followers and brave warriors, and to make alliances with other chiefs through marriage.

So cattle and power went together: a chief with many cattle could feed and help many people, and a chief who helped many people had many followers. But a greedy chief who did not share could lose his followers to another chief.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking lobola was buying a wife; it joined two families and showed respect. Two: thinking the chief did whatever he wanted; he was expected to listen, be generous and care for the poor. Three: thinking cattle were only for food; they were wealth, a link between families and part of religion. Four: thinking mafisa gave cattle away for ever; the cattle were loaned and still belonged to the owner.

The questions for this section are with you now: how cattle gave the chief power and support.
