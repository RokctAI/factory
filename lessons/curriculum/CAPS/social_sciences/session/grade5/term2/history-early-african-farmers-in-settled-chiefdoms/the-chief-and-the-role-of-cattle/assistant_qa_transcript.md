### subtopic_1 — The Role of the Chief
**Assistant:** Is lobola still practised today?

**Tutor:** Yes, lobola is still an important custom in many South African families today, among isiZulu, isiXhosa, Sesotho, Setswana, Sepedi and other language communities. In the old days, it was paid in cattle. Today, it is often paid partly or fully in money, with the amount still counted in cattle: a family might agree on a certain number of cows, with each cow given a money value. The two families meet, often with uncles or other representatives speaking for each side, to discuss and agree. The meaning is the same as long ago: it shows respect for the bride's family, thanks them for raising her, and joins the two families together. Many people see it as an important part of their heritage. Some people today have different opinions about how it should be practised, and families decide for themselves how they follow the custom.

### subtopic_2 — Cattle as Wealth and as a Link
**Assistant:** Why did nobody eat the new crops before the first fruits ceremony?

**Tutor:** In many farming communities, the first fruits ceremony was one of the most important events of the year. It happened when the new crops were ripening, usually in late summer. The chief, as the link between the people and the royal ancestors, tasted the first of the new harvest in a special ceremony, often with special medicines, to thank the ancestors and ask for their blessing on the harvest and on the people. Until this was done, people were not allowed to eat the new crops. The ceremony also brought the whole chiefdom together. People gathered at the chief's place, sang, danced and renewed their loyalty to the chief. So it was religious, political and social at the same time. Among Zulu-speaking people, this ceremony was called umkhosi wokweshwama, and versions of it are still held at the royal homestead in KwaZulu-Natal today.

### subtopic_3 — Cattle and the Power of the Chief
**Assistant:** Why did the chief lend his cattle instead of keeping them?

**Tutor:** Lending cattle was a clever way to share wealth and build loyalty at the same time. A chief might have more cattle than his own family could look after, and grazing land around his homestead was limited. By lending cattle to families in different villages, he spread his herds across the land, so if disease or raiders struck one area, he did not lose everything. The families who looked after the cattle gained milk and sometimes calves, which helped them a lot. In return, they were grateful and loyal to the chief, and would support him in council and in times of war. The cattle were also a sign that the chief trusted them. This system, called mafisa in Sesotho and Setswana and ukusisa in isiZulu, linked rich and poor together, and wealthy commoners also used it to build support.
