# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the impact of mining on the environment: water and air pollution. The anchor is a Grade 5 class from Mogale City, also known as Krugersdorp, on the West Rand of Gauteng, on a science outing with a local environmental group. They walk to a small stream near some old gold mines. The water is a strange orange-red colour, and the stones in the stream are coated with orange slime. A guide dips a strip of test paper into the water, and it turns a colour that shows the water is acidic, a little like vinegar. She explains that this water has come out of old, flooded mine tunnels underground. Back at school, the teacher shows a photograph taken on a winter morning in Mpumalanga, with power station chimneys and a thick, brownish haze hanging over the land, and a photograph of dust blowing off a mine dump on a windy day. The big idea is that mining can pollute water and air, harming people, animals and plants, and that it is important to prevent pollution, clean it up, and make sure mining companies take responsibility. Water and air pollution are examinable.

## Subtopic: Polluting the Water

Pollution means making the environment dirty or harmful, by adding substances that damage it.

Mining can pollute water in several ways. One of the most serious is acid mine drainage. Many rocks in gold and coal mines contain a mineral called pyrite, also known as fool's gold, because it looks golden. When pyrite is exposed to air and water, it reacts and forms acid. The acid dissolves harmful metals from the rocks, such as iron, manganese and even small amounts of uranium. When old mines close, the pumps that kept the tunnels dry are switched off, and the tunnels slowly fill with water. When they are full, this acidic, metal-rich water flows out onto the surface, into streams and rivers. On the West Rand, this began to happen in about 2002.

Acid mine drainage is often orange, because of the iron in it. It can kill fish and water plants, make water unsafe for people and animals to drink, damage crops that are watered with it, and eat away at pipes and concrete.

Water can also be polluted by chemicals used to process ore, by rain washing dust and chemicals off mine dumps into rivers, and by mud from mines making rivers muddy. Coal mines in Mpumalanga have polluted rivers such as the Olifants River, which flows on to the Kruger National Park.

Mines also use large amounts of water, in a country that is already dry.

The questions for this section are with you now: acid mine drainage and other water pollution from mining.

## Subtopic: Polluting the Air

Mining also pollutes the air.

Dust is a big problem. Blasting rock with explosives, crushing ore, driving trucks on dirt roads, and wind blowing over mine dumps all send dust into the air. Fine dust can be breathed in deep into the lungs, causing coughing, asthma and other lung problems, especially for children and older people living close to mines. Dust also settles on plants, houses and washing.

Smelters, which are factories that melt ore to get the metal out, can release smoke and harmful gases, such as sulphur dioxide. Coal-fired power stations, which burn coal from the mines, release smoke, ash and gases. Mpumalanga's Highveld, with many coal mines and power stations close together, has some of the most polluted air in South Africa, and the government has named it a priority area for cleaning up the air.

Some of these gases mix with water in the clouds and fall as acid rain, which can harm plants, soils, lakes and buildings.

Burning coal also releases a lot of carbon dioxide, a gas that traps heat and adds to climate change.

In winter, the air near the ground is often still and cold, and the pollution gets trapped, which is why the haze is thickest on winter mornings.

The questions for this section are with you now: dust, smoke, gases and acid rain.

## Subtopic: Reducing Pollution

Pollution can be prevented and reduced, and there are laws in South Africa that require mines to protect the environment.

To protect water, mines can line dams so that polluted water does not leak into the ground, treat polluted water before it is released, and keep clean rainwater away from polluted areas. On the Witwatersrand, the government has built plants to pump out and treat acid water from old mines. Some mine water is cleaned so well that it can be used for drinking water.

To reduce dust, mines spray water on roads and rock piles, cover conveyor belts, and plant grass and trees on old mine dumps, so that their roots hold the sand. To reduce smoke and gases, power stations and smelters can use filters and special equipment in their chimneys. Using more solar and wind power, instead of coal, also reduces air pollution.

Inspectors check whether mines follow the law, and mines that pollute can be fined or closed. Communities and environmental groups, like the one in Mogale City, test water and air, report pollution, and speak up for their rights. South Africa's Constitution says everyone has the right to an environment that is not harmful to their health.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking pollution stops when a mine closes; old mines can pollute water for many years. Two: thinking orange water is just mud; it can be acidic and full of metals. Three: thinking dust from mines is harmless; fine dust can harm the lungs. Four: thinking nothing can be done about pollution; it can be prevented, treated and reduced.

The questions for this section are with you now: how pollution can be reduced and who is responsible.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the orange stream in Mogale City — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Dirty Water

Pollution makes the environment dirty or harmful.

Acid mine drainage happens when old mines fill with water. The water becomes acidic, full of metals, and often orange. It flows into streams and rivers.

It can kill fish and make water unsafe. Mines also use lots of water.

Keep the orange stones in the stream in mind and try the questions for this part: polluting the water.

## Subtopic: Dirty Air

Blasting, crushing, trucks and mine dumps make dust, which harms the lungs.

Smelters and coal power stations release smoke and gases, which can cause acid rain.

The haze is thickest on still, cold winter mornings.

Hold the brownish haze over the Mpumalanga Highveld in mind and try this part's questions: polluting the air.

## Subtopic: Cleaning Up

Mines must follow laws. They can treat dirty water, spray water to stop dust, plant grass on dumps and use filters in chimneys. Communities can test and report pollution.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Mining can pollute water, especially through acid mine drainage, when old mines fill with water that becomes acidic and full of metals, and flows into rivers. Mining pollutes the air with dust, smoke and gases from blasting, mine dumps, smelters and coal power stations, causing lung problems and acid rain. Pollution can be reduced by treating water, controlling dust, using filters and following the law.

Hold the test paper changing colour in the stream in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: reducing pollution.
