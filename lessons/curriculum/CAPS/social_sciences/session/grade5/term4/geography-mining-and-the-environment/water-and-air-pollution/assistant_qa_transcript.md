### subtopic_1 — Polluting the Water
**Assistant:** Why is acid mine drainage hard to stop?

**Tutor:** Because the old mines are enormous and deep, and the problem continues long after mining ends. Under the Witwatersrand, there are hundreds of kilometres of old tunnels and shafts, connected to each other, dug over more than a hundred years. While the mines were working, pumps removed the underground water to keep the tunnels dry. When the mines closed, the pumps were switched off, and the tunnels began to fill up, like a huge underground bath. The water reacts with the pyrite in the rocks for many years, so it keeps becoming acidic. Many of the companies that dug the old mines no longer exist, so it is hard to make anyone pay. To stop the acid water from flowing out, it must be pumped out and treated, day and night, possibly for decades, which costs a great deal of money. Scientists are also studying cheaper, natural ways to clean the water, such as using wetlands.

### subtopic_2 — Polluting the Air
**Assistant:** Is it safe to live near a mine dump?

**Tutor:** It depends on the dump and how it is managed. Many old mine dumps on the Witwatersrand are made of fine sand that can blow in the wind, especially in the dry, windy months of late winter and early spring. People living close by can breathe in the dust, which can make lung problems such as asthma worse. Some dumps also contain small amounts of harmful metals and uranium, and children sometimes play on them, which is not safe. Today, laws require mines to control dust, and many dumps have been covered with grass, or are being removed and reprocessed to recover leftover gold, which can be a good thing in the long run, although the work can make extra dust while it happens. If you live near a mine dump, it is wise not to play on it, to keep windows closed on very dusty days, and to report dust problems to the local municipality.

### subtopic_3 — Reducing Pollution
**Assistant:** How can communities help to stop pollution?

**Tutor:** Communities have an important role. People who live near mines often notice pollution first, such as a change in the colour or smell of a stream, dead fish, or dust on their houses. They can keep records, take photos, and test the water or air, sometimes with help from schools, universities or environmental groups. They can report pollution to the municipality, to the government departments responsible for water, the environment and mining, and to the mine itself. Before a new mine is allowed to open, the law says there must be a study of its possible effects on the environment, and the public must be given a chance to comment. Communities can attend meetings, ask questions and raise concerns. Some communities and organisations have gone to court to make mines and power stations follow the law. Schools can start environmental clubs to monitor their local rivers.
