# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the impact of mining on the environment: destruction of vegetation and wildlife, and waste and waste disposal. The anchor is a Grade 5 class near Richards Bay, in KwaZulu-Natal, on a nature walk. They follow a guide along a sandy path through coastal dune forest, with tangled trees, monkeys chattering in the branches, and the sound of the sea beyond the dunes. The guide surprises them: about twenty years ago, this place was bare sand. The dunes here contain heavy minerals, such as titanium, so the vegetation was cleared and the sand was mined. Afterwards, the sand was shaped back into dunes, the stored topsoil was spread on top, and indigenous plants were planted. Slowly, the forest has returned, and so have many birds, insects and small animals. Back at school, the teacher shows contrasting photographs: a huge grey heap of waste rock near a mine, and a wide, flat tailings dam with a cracked, pale surface. The big idea is that mining destroys natural vegetation and the habitats of wildlife and produces huge amounts of waste, but that careful waste disposal and rehabilitation can reduce the harm. Vegetation, wildlife and waste are examinable.

## Subtopic: Vegetation and Wildlife

To build a mine, the natural vegetation must usually be cleared from the land. Trees, bushes and grass are removed to make way for open pits, strip mines, roads, buildings, dumps, dams and railway lines. Surface mining and open pit mining destroy the most vegetation, because they disturb large areas.

When vegetation is destroyed, wildlife loses its habitat. A habitat is the natural home of an animal or plant, where it finds food, water and shelter. Some animals die, and others are forced to move away. Animals may also be disturbed by noise from blasting, machines and trucks, by bright lights at night, and by dust and polluted water. New roads and fences can cut across the paths animals use to move between areas.

Without plant roots to hold it, the soil can be washed away by rain or blown away by wind. This is called soil erosion. Wetlands, which are like natural sponges that store and clean water, can be drained or damaged by mining, especially coal mining in Mpumalanga, where many rivers begin.

Some places have rare plants and animals found nowhere else. If a mine is built there, they can be lost forever. That is why some special areas, such as national parks, World Heritage Sites and important wetlands, are protected, and people argue strongly about whether mining should be allowed near them.

The questions for this section are with you now: how mining destroys vegetation and wildlife habitats.

## Subtopic: Mining Waste

Mining produces enormous amounts of waste, because the valuable mineral is often only a tiny part of the rock that is dug up.

Waste rock is rock that is removed to reach the ore but contains little or no valuable mineral, such as the overburden in a surface mine. It is piled into large heaps, called waste rock dumps.

Tailings are the fine, sandy or muddy waste left after the ore has been crushed and processed to remove the mineral. They are mixed with water and pumped into tailings dams, also called slimes dams, where the solids settle and the water can be reused. When they dry, they form wide, flat-topped dumps, like the yellow mine dumps around Johannesburg. Tailings can contain chemicals and harmful metals.

Coal-fired power stations produce huge amounts of ash, which is stored in ash dumps.

Mining waste can be dangerous. Dust blows off dry dumps. Rain can wash chemicals from dumps into rivers. If the wall of a tailings dam is badly built or poorly looked after, it can break, sending a flood of mud over the land. In 2022, a tailings dam at Jagersfontein, in the Free State, collapsed, and the mud destroyed homes and farmland, and people lost their lives.

Old, abandoned mines can also leave open shafts and holes that are dangerous to people and animals.

The questions for this section are with you now: waste rock, tailings, ash and the dangers of mining waste.

## Subtopic: Waste Disposal and Rehabilitation

Waste disposal means getting rid of waste safely. Mines should store waste in well-planned places, away from rivers and homes, with strong, well-checked dam walls, linings to stop chemicals leaking into the ground, and covers of soil and plants to stop dust and erosion.

Some mining waste can be reused or recycled. Waste rock can be crushed and used to build roads. Old tailings dumps can be reprocessed to recover leftover gold. Ash from power stations can be used to make cement and bricks.

Rehabilitation means repairing the land after mining, so that it is safe and can be used again. Before mining starts, the topsoil is removed and stored, because it contains seeds and living things. After mining, the land is reshaped, the topsoil is spread back, and indigenous plants are planted, as in the dune forest near Richards Bay. Rehabilitated land can be used for farming, grazing, nature reserves or even sports fields.

In South Africa, the law says that mining companies must plan for rehabilitation before they start mining, and must set aside money to pay for it, so that the land is not left damaged if the company closes.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking only the mine itself destroys vegetation; roads, dumps and dams also clear land. Two: thinking mining waste is just harmless sand; it can contain chemicals and harmful metals. Three: thinking tailings dams can never fail; badly built or poorly kept walls can collapse. Four: thinking mined land can never be used again; rehabilitation can bring plants and animals back.

The questions for this section are with you now: safe waste disposal, recycling and rehabilitation.

## Subtopic: Plants and Animals

Mines clear the natural vegetation for pits, roads, dumps and dams.

Animals lose their habitat, their home. Noise, lights and dust disturb them.

Without plants, soil washes or blows away. Wetlands can be damaged.

Keep the monkeys chattering in the replanted forest in mind and try the questions for this part: vegetation and wildlife.

## Subtopic: Mining Waste

Waste rock is piled in dumps. Tailings are the fine waste left after processing, stored in tailings dams.

Waste can blow as dust, wash chemicals into rivers, or burst out if a dam wall breaks.

Old open shafts are dangerous.

Hold the huge grey heap of waste rock in mind and try this part's questions: mining waste.

## Subtopic: Fixing the Land

Store waste safely, and reuse some of it. Keep the topsoil, and after mining, put it back and plant indigenous plants. Mines must pay for this.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Mining clears natural vegetation for pits, roads, dumps and dams, destroying habitats, disturbing wildlife and causing soil erosion. Mining produces waste rock, tailings stored in tailings dams, and ash, which can cause dust, pollution and dangerous dam failures. Waste must be stored safely and can sometimes be reused. Rehabilitation reshapes the land, replaces topsoil and replants indigenous plants, and the law says mines must pay for it.
