### subtopic_1 — What Is Mining?
**Assistant:** How was the Big Hole dug by hand?

**Tutor:** When diamonds were found at Kimberley in 1871, thousands of people rushed there. The land was divided into thousands of tiny claims, small squares of ground, each owned or worked by different people. Miners dug down with picks and shovels, filling buckets with rock and earth. Because the claims were next to each other and dug at different speeds, the hole became a maze of deep, narrow pits with walls between them, and roadways collapsed. To lift the rock out, miners built a huge web of ropes and pulleys from the edge of the hole, with buckets going up and down. The rock was then sorted by hand to find the diamonds. Many of the workers were black men from across southern Africa, who did the hardest work for low pay. Over time, small claim holders were bought out, and by 1888 one company controlled all the mines, using machines and, later, underground shafts.

### subtopic_2 — Surface and Open Pit Mining
**Assistant:** How big are the trucks in an open pit mine?

**Tutor:** The trucks in big open pit mines are some of the largest vehicles in the world. They are called haul trucks. A big haul truck can be about as tall as a two-storey house, and its tyres alone are taller than an adult person. Some can carry more than two hundred tonnes of rock in one load, which is about the weight of a hundred and fifty cars. The drivers sit high up in a small cab and climb a staircase to get in. Because they are so big, the drivers cannot see small vehicles near them, so mines have strict rules about where people and smaller vehicles may go, and the trucks have cameras, lights and warning systems. Some mines now use trucks that drive themselves, controlled by computers. The trucks drive up and down the spiral roads of the pit all day and night, carrying ore to the crusher and waste rock to the dumps.

### subtopic_3 — Shaft and Deep-Level Mining
**Assistant:** Why is it so hot deep down in a mine?

**Tutor:** The inside of the Earth is very hot. At the centre, it is thousands of degrees Celsius. Some of this heat moves outwards towards the surface. So the deeper you go into the Earth, the hotter the rock becomes. Near the surface, the temperature of the rock is about the same as the average air temperature. But in the deep gold mines of the Witwatersrand, the rock at about four kilometres deep can be about sixty degrees Celsius, hotter than the hottest day ever recorded in South Africa, and hot enough to burn your hand if you touched it for long. The air in the tunnels is also squeezed by the weight of the air above it, which makes it warmer. To make it possible for people to work there, deep mines use huge fans and refrigeration plants, and even pump ice down the shafts, to cool the air.
