### subtopic_1 — Where the Mines Are
**Assistant:** Why are minerals found only in some places?

**Tutor:** Minerals form under special conditions, so they are found only where those conditions existed. Different minerals form in different ways. The gold of the Witwatersrand was carried by ancient rivers, about three billion years ago, and settled in layers of gravel at the edge of a huge ancient lake or sea, which later became the gold reefs. Platinum and chrome formed when a huge mass of molten rock cooled slowly underground, about two billion years ago, in the area now called the Bushveld, and heavy minerals sank and settled in layers. Coal formed in the swampy areas of the Karoo Basin, about 250 to 300 million years ago. Diamonds formed very deep in the Earth and were blasted up through kimberlite pipes only in certain places. So geologists, who study rocks, use their knowledge of how minerals form to work out where to search for them.

### subtopic_2 — Reading a Mining Map
**Assistant:** What was life like in early Johannesburg?

**Tutor:** When gold was discovered on the Witwatersrand in 1886, Johannesburg did not exist. Within a few months, thousands of people arrived, from other parts of southern Africa and from all over the world, hoping to get rich or find work. They lived in tents, wagons and tin shacks, and the streets were dusty, crowded and noisy. Water was scarce, and diseases spread easily. The town grew amazingly fast, and within about ten years, it was the biggest town in South Africa. But life was very different for different people. Mine owners and some business people became very rich. Most of the hard and dangerous work underground was done by black mineworkers, many of whom came from rural areas and neighbouring countries, on contracts, lived in crowded compounds, and were paid far less than white workers. Johannesburg's story is one of great wealth, hard work and deep inequality.

### subtopic_3 — Mining and Settlement
**Assistant:** What happens to a town when its mine closes?

**Tutor:** When a mine closes, usually because the ore has run out or become too expensive to mine, it can be very hard for the town. Many people lose their jobs, not only mineworkers, but also people in shops, transport and other businesses that depended on the mine and its workers. Some people move away to look for work elsewhere, so the town's population shrinks, and schools, shops and services may close. Some towns become ghost towns. But some towns find new ways to survive. Pilgrim's Rest, in Mpumalanga, became a tourist town, where visitors can see old buildings and learn about the gold rush. Kimberley turned its huge old diamond mine, the Big Hole, into a tourist attraction and museum. Other towns develop farming, factories, or new businesses. That is why planners say mining towns should prepare for the future before the mine closes.
