# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers coal as a non-renewable resource: how coal is formed and its uses. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in eMalahleni, in Mpumalanga. The name eMalahleni comes from isiZulu and means place of coal, and the town is surrounded by coal mines. From the school field, the learners can see the huge, curved cooling towers of a power station on the horizon, with white steam rising from them, and long trains of coal trucks rumbling past. In the classroom, the teacher passes around a flat piece of dark grey rock that was found near a coal mine. On its surface is the clear print of a leaf, with a long middle vein and many small side veins. It is the fossil of a plant that lived millions of years ago, long before the dinosaurs. Next to it, the teacher places a lump of shiny black coal. The teacher explains that these two things are connected: the coal was made from plants like this one. The big idea is that coal formed from the remains of plants that grew in ancient swamps, buried and squeezed over millions of years, that it is non-renewable, and that it is used mainly to make electricity, but burning it causes pollution. How coal is formed and used is examinable.

## Subtopic: Swamp Forests Long Ago

About 250 to 300 million years ago, much of what is now southern Africa looked very different. There were large, wet, swampy areas, with forests of strange plants, such as giant ferns, horsetails and tall trees with fern-like leaves. One of the most common plants was called Glossopteris, and its leaf prints are often found in rocks near coal mines, like the fossil in the eMalahleni classroom.

The climate was cool and wet. Plants grew, died and fell into the shallow swamp water, year after year, for thousands of years. Because the water had very little oxygen, the dead plants did not rot away completely, as plants do on dry land. Instead, they piled up in thick layers of soggy, partly rotted plant material.

This brown, spongy material is called peat. Peat still forms today in some wet places, such as bogs and marshes. When peat is dried, it can be burnt as a fuel, but it is not yet coal.

The questions for this section are with you now: the ancient swamp forests and how peat forms.

## Subtopic: From Peat to Coal

Over time, rivers carried mud and sand into the swamps, and these covered the layers of peat. More and more layers piled up on top, sometimes hundreds of metres thick.

The weight of all these layers squeezed the peat, pressing out water and gases. The deeper it was buried, the hotter it became, because the inside of the Earth is hot. Over millions of years, this pressure and heat slowly changed the peat into coal. The mud and sand on top turned into rock, such as shale and sandstone.

The longer and deeper the plant remains were buried, the harder the coal became, and the more heat it gives when burnt. Soft, brown coal is younger and gives less heat. Hard, black, shiny coal has been squeezed more.

Coal is found in layers called seams, between layers of other rock, like the filling in a sandwich. In South Africa, most coal seams are fairly close to the surface, often less than three hundred metres deep, which makes them easier to mine.

Because coal took millions of years to form, and because the swamps that made it no longer exist in the same way, coal is non-renewable. Once it is burnt, it is gone forever.

The questions for this section are with you now: how peat turned into coal, coal seams and why coal is non-renewable.

## Subtopic: Uses and Problems

Coal is very important to South Africa. Most of South Africa's electricity is made in coal-fired power stations, many of them in Mpumalanga, close to the coal mines. In a power station, coal is crushed into powder and burnt to heat water into steam. The steam spins huge machines called turbines, which make electricity. The cooling towers cool the steam back into water, which is why white steam rises from them.

Coal is also used to make steel. It is heated to make coke, which is used in furnaces to melt iron ore. Coal is used to make liquid fuel, such as petrol and diesel, and chemicals for plastics and fertilisers, at a big plant at Secunda, in Mpumalanga. Some families use coal for cooking and heating, and South Africa sells coal to other countries.

But burning coal causes problems. It releases smoke, dust and gases into the air, which can make people sick, especially with breathing problems. Some gases mix with water in the air to form acid rain, which harms plants, soil and buildings. Burning coal also releases a lot of carbon dioxide, a gas that traps heat in the air and adds to climate change. Coal mining can also pollute water and damage land.

That is why South Africa is slowly building more solar and wind power stations, which use renewable energy.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking coal formed from dinosaurs; it formed from ancient plants. Two: thinking coal forms in a few years; it took millions of years. Three: thinking peat is the same as coal; peat is the first stage, not yet coal. Four: thinking coal is clean to burn; it releases smoke, gases and carbon dioxide.

The questions for this section are with you now: how coal is used and the problems it causes.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the fern fossil in eMalahleni — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Ancient Swamps

About 250 to 300 million years ago, southern Africa had swamp forests with ferns and trees.

Plants died and fell into the water. They did not rot away completely, and piled up as peat.

Peat is soft, brown and spongy. It is not yet coal.

Keep the print of the leaf on the dark grey rock in mind and try the questions for this part: swamp forests long ago.

## Subtopic: Squeezed Into Coal

Mud and sand covered the peat. The weight and heat squeezed it.

After millions of years, the peat became coal, in layers called seams.

Coal takes millions of years to form, so it is non-renewable.

Hold the lump of shiny black coal next to the fossil in mind and try this part's questions: from peat to coal.

## Subtopic: Using Coal

Coal is burnt in power stations to make most of our electricity. It is also used for steel, fuel and chemicals. But burning it pollutes the air and adds to climate change.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Coal formed from plants in swamp forests about 250 to 300 million years ago. Dead plants piled up in the water as peat, were buried under mud and sand, and over millions of years were squeezed and heated into coal, found in seams. Coal is non-renewable. It is used mainly to make electricity, and also for steel, fuel and chemicals, but burning it causes air pollution, acid rain and climate change.

Hold the white steam rising from the cooling towers on the horizon in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: uses and problems.
