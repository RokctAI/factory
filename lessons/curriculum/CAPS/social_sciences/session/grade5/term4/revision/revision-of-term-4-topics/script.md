# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the Term 4 topics. In Geography, the topic was minerals and mining in South Africa: minerals as non-renewable resources, gold, diamonds and coal and their uses, how coal formed, where mines are and how they shaped settlements, types of mining, mining's impact on water, air, vegetation and wildlife, mining waste, and the challenges, health and safety of miners. In History, the topic was a heritage trail through the provinces of South Africa: what heritage is, the Cradle of Humankind, the golden objects of Mapungubwe, the Castle of Good Hope and changing identities, the aloe and indigenous medicine, and San rock art in the Drakensberg. The anchor is a revision morning for a Grade 5 class in Kimberley, in the Northern Cape, a city that grew around the diamond mines. The class has set up three tables. Table 1 has rock samples, a mining map of South Africa and a model of an open pit. Table 2 has a hard hat, a cap lamp, a dust mask, and photographs of an orange stream and a tailings dam. Table 3 has a heritage passport with nine stamps, a replica golden rhinoceros, a picture of the Castle, an aloe leaf and a copy of an eland painting. The big idea is that minerals have shaped South Africa's economy, towns and environment, and that our heritage, in places, objects, knowledge and art, must be valued and protected. The whole of Term 4 is examinable.

## Subtopic: Minerals and Mines

At Table 1, the rock samples remind the class that minerals are natural, solid substances in the Earth's crust, and that rock rich in a mineral is called ore. Minerals are non-renewable, because they take millions of years to form and can run out, unlike sunlight and wind.

Gold, which never rusts, is used for jewellery, wealth and electronics, and is mined in the Witwatersrand Basin. Diamonds, pure carbon and the hardest natural substance, are used as gems and in cutting tools, and were found near Hopetown and Kimberley from 1867. Coal formed from ancient swamp plants, squeezed and heated over millions of years, and is mined mainly in Mpumalanga to make most of our electricity. Platinum is mined around Rustenburg and in Limpopo, and iron ore in the Northern Cape.

Many towns grew because of mining, such as Johannesburg after gold was found in 1886, Kimberley and eMalahleni. When mines close, towns can shrink.

The type of mining depends on depth: surface or strip mining for shallow coal, open pits for large ore bodies near the surface, like the Big Hole and Sishen, and shaft and deep-level mining for deep ore, like gold mines about four kilometres deep.

The questions for this section are with you now: minerals, their uses, mine locations and types of mining.

## Subtopic: Mining, the Environment and People

At Table 2, the photographs remind the class that mining affects the environment. Acid mine drainage happens when old mines fill with water that becomes acidic and full of metals, and flows into rivers. Dust from blasting and mine dumps, and smoke and gases from smelters and coal power stations, pollute the air and cause acid rain. Mining clears vegetation and destroys habitats, and produces waste rock and tailings. Pollution can be reduced, and land can be rehabilitated by replacing topsoil and replanting, which the law requires mines to pay for.

The hard hat and cap lamp remind the class about miners. In deep gold mines, the rock is very hot, so ventilation and cooling are needed. Rock falls and rockbursts are dangerous, so the rock is supported. Silica dust can cause silicosis and makes TB more likely, and noise can cause hearing loss. The Mine Health and Safety Act requires protective equipment, self-rescuers, refuge bays and training, and miners have the right to leave a dangerous place.

The questions for this section are with you now: pollution, waste, rehabilitation, and miners' health and safety.

## Subtopic: The Heritage Trail

At Table 3, the heritage passport reminds the class that heritage is what we inherit from the past, value and pass on. It can be natural or cultural, and tangible, which we can touch, or intangible, such as languages and songs.

The Cradle of Humankind, in Gauteng, has fossils such as Mrs Ples and Little Foot, which show that humankind began in Africa. Mapungubwe, in Limpopo, was a wealthy African kingdom about eight hundred years ago, famous for its golden rhinoceros and trade across the Indian Ocean. The Castle of Good Hope, in the Western Cape, built from 1666 to 1679, meant power for the Dutch East India Company, but loss and suffering for the Khoikhoi and enslaved people, and today tells everyone's stories, showing how identities change. The aloe, in the Eastern Cape, shows that indigenous knowledge of medicine is valuable heritage that must be shared fairly. San rock art in the Drakensberg, linked to San beliefs, is fragile and must be protected.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking minerals are renewable; they can run out. Two: thinking coal formed from dinosaurs; it formed from ancient plants. Three: thinking heritage is only old buildings; it includes knowledge, art, languages and nature. Four: thinking the golden rhinoceros is solid gold; it is gold foil over wood.

The questions for this section are with you now: heritage and the stops on the heritage trail.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the three revision tables in Kimberley — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Minerals and Mines

Minerals are non-renewable. Gold never rusts, diamonds are hardest, and coal makes electricity.

Towns grew around mines. Surface mines and open pits are near the surface, and shafts go deep.

Kimberley itself is a good reminder. The town grew quickly because people came to dig for diamonds, and once a mineral is dug out, it cannot grow back.

Keep the rock samples and the model open pit on Table 1 in mind and try the questions for this part: minerals and mines.

## Subtopic: Mining's Effects

Old mines can make acid water. Dust and smoke pollute the air. Mines clear plants and make waste, and must repair the land.

Deep mines are hot and dangerous. Miners need safety gear, and they can leave a dangerous place.

So mining brings jobs and useful things, but it also has costs for the land, the water and the people who do the work.

Hold the hard hat and the photograph of the orange stream in mind and try this part's questions: mining, the environment and people.

## Subtopic: Heritage

Heritage is what we inherit, value and pass on. Our trail: the Cradle, Mapungubwe, the Castle, the aloe and Drakensberg rock art.

Each stop tells a different story, and all of these stories belong to South Africa. We look after heritage so that people in the future can learn from it too.

Pocket summary of the term. Minerals are non-renewable resources, such as gold, diamonds, coal and platinum, found in particular areas, and towns grew around mines. Mining can be surface, open pit or deep shaft mining. It can pollute water and air, destroy habitats and create waste, and miners face serious risks. Heritage is what we inherit and value: the Cradle of Humankind, Mapungubwe, the Castle, the aloe and San rock art.

Hold the heritage passport with all nine stamps in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: the heritage trail.
