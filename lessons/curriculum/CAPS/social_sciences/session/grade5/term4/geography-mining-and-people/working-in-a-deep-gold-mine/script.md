# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the challenges of working in a deep gold mine: ventilation, heat, rock falls and dust. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Carletonville, on the West Rand of Gauteng, a town surrounded by some of the deepest gold mines in the world. They visit a mine training centre, where a retired miner who worked underground for more than thirty years meets them. He shows them a tall model of a mine: a shaft going straight down from the headgear on the surface, with levels of tunnels branching off it, deeper and deeper. Then he takes them to a practice tunnel built inside the centre. It is dark and narrow, with a low roof held up by wooden packs and steel props. The learners switch on headlamps and crawl along on their hands and knees. A heater blows hot air, and a loud fan roars. After a few minutes, they come out sweaty and dusty. The miner smiles and says that real miners do this for a whole shift, kilometres underground, where it is much hotter. The big idea is that working in a deep gold mine is very challenging and dangerous, because of the depth, heat, need for fresh air, risk of rock falls and dust, and that mines must use special equipment and methods to make work possible and safer. Working in a deep gold mine is examinable.

## Subtopic: Going Deep

On the Witwatersrand, the gold-bearing rock, called the reef, lies in a thin layer that slopes down deep into the Earth. The easy, shallow gold was mined long ago, so today's gold mines must go very deep. Some mines near Carletonville reach about four kilometres below the surface, among the deepest mines in the world.

Each shift starts at the shaft. Miners collect their lamps, helmets and equipment, and enter the cage, a steel lift that can carry many people. The cage drops down the shaft very fast. In the deepest mines, miners may change to a second shaft further down, and then walk or ride a small train through tunnels to reach the working area. The journey can take an hour or more each way.

The working area where the ore is mined is called a stope. Because the gold reef is often only about a metre thick, or even less, the stope is very low, so miners often work bent over, kneeling or lying down, in a sloping space, with heavy drills.

It is completely dark underground, except for the miners' cap lamps and lights along the main tunnels. It is also very noisy, with drills, fans, machines and trains.

The questions for this section are with you now: the depth of gold mines, the journey down and the stope.

## Subtopic: Heat and Ventilation

The deeper you go into the Earth, the hotter the rock becomes. In the deepest gold mines, the rock can be about sixty degrees Celsius, hot enough to burn skin. Without cooling, the air in the tunnels would be far too hot for people to survive. Working hard in great heat can cause heat exhaustion and heatstroke, which can be deadly.

Ventilation means bringing fresh air into a place and taking used air out. Underground, miners need fresh air to breathe, and ventilation also carries away heat, dust, fumes from explosives and machines, and dangerous gases.

Mines have special ventilation shafts. Huge fans on the surface pull used air out through one set of shafts, which draws fresh air down another set of shafts and through the tunnels. To cool the air, deep mines use giant refrigeration plants, like enormous fridges, which chill water that is sprayed into the air or pumped through coolers underground. Some mines even send ice down the shafts, which melts and cools the air.

Mines check the temperature and air quality all the time. New workers go through heat tolerance training, so that their bodies get used to working in the heat, and miners must drink plenty of water during their shift.

The questions for this section are with you now: heat underground and how ventilation and cooling work.

## Subtopic: Rock Falls and Dust

Rock falls are one of the biggest dangers in deep mines. The weight of kilometres of rock above presses down on the tunnels and stopes. Rock can crack and fall from the roof or sides. Sometimes, the pressure causes a rockburst, when rock suddenly bursts out of the walls with great force, like a small earthquake. Deep mining can even cause small earth tremors that people on the surface can feel.

To support the rock, miners use steel props, wooden packs, which are stacks of timber, rock bolts, which are long steel rods drilled into the rock to hold it together, and steel mesh. Before working, miners check the roof by tapping it with a bar and listening: a hollow sound can mean loose rock.

Dust is another danger. Drilling and blasting hard rock creates fine dust containing silica, which comes from the mineral quartz. Breathing in silica dust over many years can cause a serious lung disease called silicosis, which makes it hard to breathe and increases the risk of tuberculosis. To reduce dust, miners spray water while drilling, wet down the rock after blasting, and wear dust masks.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking gold mines are cool underground; the deeper they go, the hotter the rock. Two: thinking ventilation is just for comfort; miners need it to breathe and to remove heat, dust and gases. Three: thinking rock falls only happen in old mines; the pressure of rock is a danger in all deep mines. Four: thinking mine dust is harmless; silica dust can cause silicosis.

The questions for this section are with you now: rock falls, rockbursts, support and dust.

## Subtopic: Deep Down

Gold mines near Carletonville go about four kilometres down.

Miners ride a fast cage down the shaft, then walk or take a train. It can take an hour.

The stope, where they mine, is low, dark and noisy, so they often kneel or lie down to work.

## Subtopic: Hot Rock, Fresh Air

Deep rock is very hot, about sixty degrees Celsius.

Ventilation brings fresh air in and takes used air, heat and dust out. Huge fans and refrigeration plants cool the air, and some mines use ice.

Miners drink lots of water and are trained to cope with heat.

Hold the hot air and loud fan in the practice tunnel in mind and try this part's questions: heat and ventilation.

## Subtopic: Rock and Dust

The weight of rock can cause rock falls and rockbursts. Props, packs, rock bolts and mesh hold the rock. Silica dust can cause silicosis, so miners spray water and wear masks.
