### subtopic_1 — Going Deep
**Assistant:** What is the headgear on top of a mine shaft?

**Tutor:** The headgear is the tall steel or concrete tower you see on top of a mine shaft, often with big wheels at the top. It is one of the most recognisable sights in mining towns on the Witwatersrand. Inside a building next to it is the winder, a huge engine with a giant drum, which winds thick steel cables in and out. The cables run up over the wheels at the top of the headgear and down the shaft, where they hold the cages, which carry miners, and the skips, which carry ore. When the drum turns one way, the cage goes down; when it turns the other way, the cage comes up. The winder driver controls it very carefully, using signals from the shaft, because the lives of the miners depend on it. Cages in deep mines can travel very fast, many metres every second, so the ride down is quick.

### subtopic_2 — Heat and Ventilation
**Assistant:** Why does deep mining cause earth tremors?

**Tutor:** When miners remove ore deep underground, they leave empty spaces in the rock. The enormous weight of the rock above, kilometres thick, presses down on these spaces, and the rock around them is squeezed and stressed. Sometimes the stress builds up until the rock suddenly cracks or slips, releasing energy as shaking, just like a small earthquake. Underground, this can cause a rockburst, which is very dangerous to miners nearby. On the surface, people may feel a short tremor, and sometimes buildings crack. On the West Rand and in other mining areas, such tremors happen fairly often, though most are small. Scientists use instruments called seismometers, placed all over the mines, to record these tremors and learn where stress is building up. This helps mines to plan safer ways of mining, such as leaving pillars of rock to hold up the roof, and using stronger supports.

### subtopic_3 — Rock Falls and Dust
**Assistant:** Where did the miners come from?

**Tutor:** From the beginning of gold mining on the Witwatersrand, most of the miners who did the hardest and most dangerous work underground were black men, who came from rural areas across South Africa, especially the Eastern Cape, and from neighbouring countries, such as Lesotho, Mozambique, Eswatini, Botswana and Malawi. Most came as migrant workers, on contracts lasting months, leaving their families at home and sending money back. They lived in crowded single-sex hostels or compounds near the mines, often for many years, and were paid much less than white miners, who held the better jobs because of unfair laws. Many miners became sick with silicosis and tuberculosis, and some carried these diseases home. Mineworkers also organised trade unions and fought for better pay, safety and living conditions. Today, conditions have improved, the unfair laws have ended, and many miners live with their families, but mining is still hard work.
