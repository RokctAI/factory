# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers health and safety risks for miners and the rules that protect them. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Rustenburg, in North West, an area with many platinum mines, at a mine safety open day. A safety officer meets them under a large banner that says zero harm. The learners take turns to try on a full set of protective equipment: a hard hat with a cap lamp clipped to the front, a battery pack on a belt, safety glasses, ear muffs, a dust mask, gloves, heavy boots with steel toecaps, and overalls with bright reflective strips. The safety officer shows them a small metal box on the belt, called a self-rescuer, which can give a miner oxygen to breathe if there is smoke or gas. Then she shows them a model of a refuge bay, a sealed room underground with air, water and a telephone, where miners can shelter in an emergency. She tells them that every miner has the right to stop work and leave if a place is dangerous. The big idea is that miners face serious health and safety risks, and that laws, rules, equipment, training and workers' rights help to protect them. Health and safety of miners is examinable.

## Subtopic: Health Risks

Mining can harm miners' health over time, even when there are no accidents.

Lung diseases are a major risk. Breathing in silica dust from hard rock, over many years, can cause silicosis, a disease that scars the lungs and makes breathing difficult. It cannot be cured. Miners with silicosis are also much more likely to get tuberculosis, or TB, a serious infectious lung disease. Crowded living conditions in old mine hostels helped TB to spread, and miners sometimes carried it home to their families. Coal miners can get a lung disease from coal dust.

Noise is another risk. Drills, fans and machines are very loud, and years of loud noise can cause hearing loss, which cannot be repaired.

Heat can cause heat exhaustion and heatstroke. Hard physical work, carrying heavy equipment and working bent over in low stopes can cause back, knee and joint injuries.

Working far from home, in a dangerous place, can also cause stress and affect miners' mental health and family life.

To protect miners' health, mines must test the air and noise levels, and give miners regular medical check-ups, including chest X-rays, so that diseases can be found and treated early.

The questions for this section are with you now: lung diseases, hearing loss and other health risks.

## Subtopic: Safety Risks

Safety risks are dangers that can cause accidents and sudden injury or death.

Underground, the main safety risks include rock falls and rockbursts, which are among the main causes of serious accidents; accidents with machines, trains, trucks and conveyor belts; falls down shafts or slopes; explosions, from explosives or, in coal mines, from methane gas and coal dust; fires, which fill tunnels with smoke; flooding; and dangerous gases or a lack of oxygen.

In surface mines, risks include accidents with giant trucks and machines, rock falling from the walls of open pits, and blasting.

In the past, mining in South Africa was extremely dangerous, and many thousands of miners died in accidents over the years. Safety has improved a great deal since the 1990s, and the number of miners killed each year has fallen sharply, but accidents still happen, and every death is a tragedy for a family and a community.

That is why mining companies, workers, unions and the government work together on safety, with the goal of zero harm, which means no one should be injured or made sick by their work.

The questions for this section are with you now: the main safety risks in mines.

## Subtopic: Rules and Rights

In South Africa, the Mine Health and Safety Act, passed in 1996, sets out the rules to protect miners. Government inspectors visit mines to check that the rules are followed, investigate accidents, and can stop work or close a mine that is unsafe.

Mines must provide protective equipment: hard hats, cap lamps, safety boots, gloves, glasses, ear protection, dust masks and reflective clothing, as well as self-rescuers. They must support the rock, control dust and noise, provide ventilation and cooling, and have refuge bays and emergency plans. Workers must be trained before they start, and regularly afterwards. Mines keep a record of every person who goes underground, so they know who is there in an emergency. Specially trained mine rescue teams are ready to help.

Miners also have rights and duties. They have the right to be trained, to be told about dangers, to choose health and safety representatives who speak for them, and to refuse to work in, or to leave, a place they believe is dangerous, without being punished. They have a duty to wear their protective equipment, follow safety rules and report dangers.

Miners who become sick or injured because of their work have the right to compensation, which is money to help them and their families.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking only accidents harm miners; dust and noise cause diseases over many years. Two: thinking a miner must keep working in a dangerous place; miners have the right to leave it. Three: thinking safety equipment is optional; mines must provide it and miners must wear it. Four: thinking mines have always been as safe as today; mining was far more dangerous in the past.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the safety open day in Rustenburg — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Health

Silica dust can cause silicosis, which scars the lungs. It also makes TB more likely.

Loud noise can cause hearing loss. Heat and heavy work can hurt the body.

Miners get regular check-ups and chest X-rays, so that illness is found early.

Keep the dust mask and ear muffs from the open day in mind and try the questions for this part: health risks.

## Subtopic: Safety

Dangers include rock falls, machines, explosions, fires, floods and bad air.

Mining was very dangerous in the past. It is safer now, but accidents still happen.

The goal is zero harm: nobody hurt or made sick by their work.

Hold the banner that says zero harm in mind and try this part's questions: safety risks.

## Subtopic: Rules and Rights

The Mine Health and Safety Act protects miners. Mines must give safety equipment and training. Miners can leave a dangerous place, and must wear their equipment.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Miners face health risks, such as silicosis and TB from dust, hearing loss from noise, and heat illness, and safety risks, such as rock falls, machine accidents, explosions, fires and floods. The Mine Health and Safety Act of 1996 requires mines to provide protective equipment, self-rescuers, refuge bays, training and check-ups. Inspectors check mines. Miners have the right to refuse dangerous work and a duty to follow safety rules.
