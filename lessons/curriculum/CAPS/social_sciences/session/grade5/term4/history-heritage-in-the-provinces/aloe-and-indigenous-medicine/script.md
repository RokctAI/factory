# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers heritage and indigenous medicine, using the example of the aloe in the Eastern Cape. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Makhanda, in the Eastern Cape, on a winter morning visit to a community garden on the edge of town. In the middle of the garden stands a tall aloe, taller than the learners, with a single thick stem, a crown of long, grey-green leaves edged with sharp reddish spines, and tall spikes of bright orange-red flowers, where sunbirds dart in and out to drink the nectar. A local herbalist, who learnt about plants from her grandmother, greets the class. She carefully cuts one lower leaf, and shows them the yellow, bitter juice that drips from the cut edge, and then the clear, cool gel inside. She explains that her family, and many families before them, have used this plant to treat burns, wounds and stomach problems. She also warns the class never to use any medicine, even a plant, without asking an adult or a health worker. The big idea is that indigenous knowledge of plants and medicine, such as the uses of the aloe, is valuable heritage, passed on from generation to generation, which must be respected, protected and shared fairly. The aloe and indigenous medicine are examinable.

## Subtopic: The Aloe

There are many kinds of aloe in South Africa. One of the best known is the bitter aloe, also called the Cape aloe or Aloe ferox. Ferox means fierce, because of its sharp spines. It grows naturally in the Eastern Cape, and also in parts of the Western Cape, KwaZulu-Natal and the Free State, often on rocky hillsides and in dry bushveld.

The bitter aloe can grow two to three metres tall. Its thick, fleshy leaves store water, which helps it to survive long dry periods, and the old, dry leaves hang down around the stem like a skirt, protecting it from fire and heat. In winter, it produces tall spikes of orange-red flowers, which are an important source of food for sunbirds, bees and other animals when few other plants are flowering.

The aloe leaf has two useful parts. Just under the green skin is a bitter, yellow juice, also called sap. Inside the leaf is a clear, slippery gel.

## Subtopic: Indigenous Knowledge and Medicine

Indigenous knowledge is the knowledge that communities have built up over many generations, through living closely with their environment. It includes knowledge of plants, animals, weather, farming, building, healing and much more. Indigenous knowledge systems, often called IKS, are the ways this knowledge is organised, used and passed on.

The Khoikhoi and San, and later many other communities in South Africa, learnt the healing uses of the aloe. The bitter yellow sap was used as a strong medicine to clear the stomach and bowels. The gel was put on burns, cuts, rashes and insect bites to soothe and heal the skin. Some people used aloe for eye problems, and for sick animals.

Indigenous medicine is used by many South Africans, often alongside modern medicine. Traditional healers, such as herbalists, who know about medicinal plants, and diviners, play an important role in many communities.

Indigenous knowledge is usually passed on orally, which means by speaking: through teaching, stories, and showing young people how to recognise, collect and prepare plants, as the herbalist's grandmother did. If it is not passed on, it can be lost.

Some medicinal plants can be harmful in the wrong amount, and the bitter sap is very strong. That is why plant medicines should only be used with the guidance of a knowledgeable adult or a health worker.

## Subtopic: Heritage, Trade and Fairness

The bitter aloe has been harvested and traded for a long time. Since the 1700s, the dried bitter sap, called Cape aloes, has been sent from South Africa to other countries, where it is used in medicines. The gel is also used in creams, soaps and cosmetics.

Aloe is harvested by hand in a traditional way that does not kill the plant. Harvesters cut some of the lower leaves and arrange them in a circle around a hollow in the ground, lined with a skin or plastic, with the cut ends facing inwards. The bitter sap drips out into the hollow. It is then boiled until it becomes thick and dark, and it hardens into brown crystals. Because only some leaves are cut, the plant keeps growing, and can be harvested again. This is called sustainable harvesting.

Indigenous knowledge is heritage, and it can be valuable to companies that make medicines and products. It is important that communities who hold this knowledge are respected, and share fairly in the benefits when it is used to make money. South Africa has laws about this. For example, in 2019, an agreement was made that the San and Khoi communities would share in the money earned from rooibos, a plant whose uses they discovered long ago.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking indigenous knowledge is not real knowledge; it is built on generations of careful observation. Two: thinking any plant medicine is safe in any amount; some can be harmful. Three: thinking harvesting aloe kills the plant; traditional harvesting cuts only some leaves. Four: thinking companies may use indigenous knowledge freely; communities should share in the benefits.
