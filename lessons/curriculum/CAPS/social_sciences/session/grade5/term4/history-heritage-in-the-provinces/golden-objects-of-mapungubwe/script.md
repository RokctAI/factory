# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers heritage in objects: the golden objects at Mapungubwe, in Limpopo. The anchor is a Grade 5 class from Makhado, in Limpopo, on a trip to Mapungubwe National Park, in the far north of South Africa, where the Limpopo and Shashe rivers meet, and where South Africa, Botswana and Zimbabwe come together. The land is hot and dry, with huge baobab trees and red sandstone hills. A guide leads the class up a narrow path and a steep wooden staircase to the flat top of Mapungubwe Hill. From there, they can see across the river valley into two other countries. The guide shows them a photograph of a small golden rhinoceros, about as long as a pencil, with one horn, made of thin gold sheets fixed with tiny gold nails. She tells them that it was found here, in a royal grave on top of this hill, and that the king who was buried with it lived about eight hundred years ago. The big idea is that the golden objects of Mapungubwe are precious heritage, because they show that a wealthy, organised African kingdom, with skilled craftspeople and trade links across the Indian Ocean, existed in southern Africa long before Europeans arrived. The golden objects of Mapungubwe are examinable.

## Subtopic: The Kingdom of Mapungubwe

Mapungubwe was the capital of a powerful African kingdom, which was at its greatest about eight hundred years ago, from about 1220 to about 1290. Many communities in the region today, including Venda-speaking and Shona-speaking people, see the people of Mapungubwe as their ancestors. They were farmers who grew crops such as sorghum and millet, and kept cattle, sheep and goats.

Before Mapungubwe Hill became the capital, people lived in a large town nearby, at a site that archaeologists call K2. As the community grew richer through trade, the leaders moved to the top of Mapungubwe Hill.

Mapungubwe was one of the first places in southern Africa where society was divided into classes. The king and the royal family lived on top of the hill, separated from ordinary people, who lived in the valley below. The king was seen as a sacred leader, linked to rainmaking. Archaeologists found that the royal houses on the hill were built with fine stone walls and had better materials.

About five thousand people may have lived at Mapungubwe at its height.

After about 1300, Mapungubwe declined. The climate became drier and cooler, making farming harder, and trade and power moved north, to Great Zimbabwe, which became the next great kingdom in the region.

The questions for this section are with you now: the kingdom of Mapungubwe, its people and its social classes.

## Subtopic: The Golden Objects

In 1933, after local people showed a group of men a hidden path up the steep hill, graves with gold objects were found on top of Mapungubwe Hill. Archaeologists from the University of Pretoria then studied the site.

The most famous object is the golden rhinoceros. It is about fifteen centimetres long, and was made by hammering thin sheets of gold around a carved wooden core, and fixing them with tiny gold tacks. The wood has rotted away, but the gold remains. The rhinoceros has one horn, which is unusual, because African rhinos have two. It may have been a symbol of the king's power and leadership.

Other gold objects include a golden bowl, a golden sceptre, which is a rod carried by a ruler, and thousands of gold beads, bangles and pieces of gold foil. Some of the gold was found with the remains of royal people, which shows that gold was used by the rulers and was a sign of status.

The objects show that Mapungubwe's craftspeople were very skilled goldsmiths, who knew how to find, melt, hammer, shape and decorate gold. Gold mined from the region was also traded.

The golden rhinoceros has become a national symbol. South Africa's highest award for achievement, given by the President, is called the Order of Mapungubwe, and it includes an image of the rhinoceros.

The questions for this section are with you now: the golden rhinoceros and the other golden objects.

## Subtopic: Trade and Heritage

Mapungubwe was part of a huge trading network. Its people sent gold, ivory, animal skins and copper to the east coast of Africa, to trading towns such as Sofala, in what is now Mozambique. From there, traders sailed across the Indian Ocean to Arabia, India and even China. In return, Mapungubwe received glass beads, cloth and other goods. Archaeologists have found thousands of glass beads at Mapungubwe, and even pieces of Chinese pottery, called celadon.

This shows that Mapungubwe was connected to the wider world long before European ships reached southern Africa.

For many years, the story of Mapungubwe was not widely told, and some people did not want to believe that Africans had built such a wealthy kingdom. Today, Mapungubwe is proudly recognised. It became a World Heritage Site in 2003, and the area is a national park. The golden objects are kept in a museum at the University of Pretoria. In 2007, the human remains that had been dug up were respectfully reburied on the hill, after discussions with communities who see the people of Mapungubwe as their ancestors.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking there were no wealthy African kingdoms before Europeans arrived; Mapungubwe was one. Two: thinking the golden rhinoceros is solid gold; it is gold foil over a wooden core. Three: thinking Mapungubwe was cut off from the world; it traded across the Indian Ocean. Four: thinking everyone at Mapungubwe lived the same way; the royal family lived apart on the hill.
