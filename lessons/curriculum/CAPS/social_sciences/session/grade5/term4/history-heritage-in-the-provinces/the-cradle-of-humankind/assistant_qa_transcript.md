### subtopic_1 — A Site of Significance
**Assistant:** Are we descended from Mrs Ples?

**Tutor:** Not exactly, and scientists are careful about this. Mrs Ples belonged to a group of early human relatives called Australopithecus, who lived in Africa more than two million years ago. They walked upright like us, but had small brains and could still climb trees well. Scientists think that the ancestors of modern humans were related to Australopithecus, or came from a similar group, but they are still studying which species led to which, because the family tree of human ancestors is more like a bush with many branches, and some branches died out. So it is better to say that Mrs Ples was one of our early relatives, a bit like a very distant great-great-aunt, rather than a direct great-grandmother. What scientists agree on is that the human family tree has deep roots in Africa, and that fossils from the Cradle are very important for understanding it.

### subtopic_2 — Famous Discoveries
**Assistant:** How did the Little Foot skeleton take twenty years to dig out?

**Tutor:** Little Foot was not lying in soft soil. Its bones were set inside very hard rock called breccia, a kind of natural concrete made of stones, sand and bones cemented together over millions of years, in a dark, deep part of the Sterkfontein Caves. The bones were also very fragile, and if the scientists had used heavy tools or worked too quickly, they could have smashed them. So the team worked very slowly and carefully, using small tools such as little chisels, needles and air scribes, which are like tiny drills, often lying on the cave floor in poor light, removing the rock grain by grain. Pieces of the rock block containing bones were carefully lifted out and cleaned in a laboratory. Only after about twenty years was the skeleton finally freed, cleaned and put together, and shown to the public in 2017.

### subtopic_3 — Heritage for All Humanity
**Assistant:** How did the scientists reach the Homo naledi fossils?

**Tutor:** The fossils of Homo naledi were found deep inside the Rising Star Cave, in a chamber that could only be reached by crawling through a narrow, twisting passage and then squeezing down a very tight crack, called the chute, only about twenty centimetres wide at its narrowest point, about the width of a school ruler. Cavers who were exploring the cave first found the bones and reported them. The project leader, Lee Berger, was too big to fit through, so he advertised for scientists who were slim, had experience of caving and knew how to excavate fossils. A team of young scientists was chosen, and they became known as the underground astronauts. They squeezed into the chamber, dug carefully, and passed the fossils up through the crack, while the rest of the team watched on screens from the surface, through cameras placed in the cave.
