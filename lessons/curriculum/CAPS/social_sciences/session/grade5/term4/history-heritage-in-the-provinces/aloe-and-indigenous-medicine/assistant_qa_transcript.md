### subtopic_1 — The Aloe
**Assistant:** Is it safe to put aloe on a burn?

**Tutor:** Many people use the clear gel from aloe leaves on small, minor burns, such as a mild sunburn or a small burn from touching something hot, because it feels cool and soothing, and some studies suggest it can help minor burns to heal. But there are important safety rules. First, for any burn, the first thing to do is to cool it under cool, clean running water for several minutes. Second, serious burns, large burns, burns on the face or hands, or burns that blister badly need a nurse or doctor straight away, not home treatment. Third, use only the clear gel, not the bitter yellow sap, which can irritate the skin and is a strong medicine if swallowed. Fourth, some people are allergic to aloe. So always ask an adult before using aloe, and never swallow aloe sap. Plant medicine is powerful, which is exactly why it must be used carefully.

### subtopic_2 — Indigenous Knowledge and Medicine
**Assistant:** How can indigenous knowledge be protected?

**Tutor:** There are several ways. First, by passing it on: elders can teach young people, and families and communities can keep practising their knowledge, so that it stays alive. Second, by recording it carefully, with the community's permission, for example in books, recordings or databases controlled by the community, so that it is not lost. Third, by respecting the people who hold it: traditional healers, farmers and elders should be recognised as experts. Fourth, through laws and agreements: in South Africa, there are laws that say that if a company wants to use indigenous knowledge or indigenous plants to make products, it must get permission from the communities who hold the knowledge, and share the benefits fairly. Fifth, by protecting the plants themselves, through sustainable harvesting and conservation, because if a plant disappears, the knowledge about it becomes useless. Universities and research centres in South Africa also study and promote IKS.

### subtopic_3 — Heritage, Trade and Fairness
**Assistant:** What other South African plants come from indigenous knowledge?

**Tutor:** Many useful plants were first known through indigenous knowledge. Rooibos, from the Cederberg area in the Western Cape, was used as a tea by Khoi and San people long before it became a popular drink around the world. Honeybush is another tea plant from the Cape. Buchu, a small fynbos shrub, was used by the Khoikhoi for healing and as a scented powder, and is now used in medicines and flavours. Hoodia, a succulent from the Kalahari, was chewed by San hunters to keep away hunger and thirst on long hunts, and companies became interested in it, which led to one of the first benefit-sharing agreements with the San. African potato, wild ginger and pelargoniums are also used in traditional medicine. Many of these plants are now protected, because people collected too many from the wild. Each one is a reminder that indigenous knowledge is valuable heritage.
