### subtopic_1 — The Kingdom of Mapungubwe
**Assistant:** Why does the golden rhinoceros have only one horn?

**Tutor:** Nobody knows for certain, and that is part of the mystery. Both kinds of rhinoceros in Africa, the white rhino and the black rhino, have two horns, one behind the other. Some experts think the craftsperson simply chose to show one horn, perhaps to make the shape simpler or more striking, as artists often do. Others think the one horn may have had a special meaning, perhaps linked to the power and leadership of the king, who may have been seen as standing alone, above all others. Some people have pointed out that one-horned rhinos live in India and Indonesia, and wondered whether ideas or images travelled along the trade routes, but there is no evidence for this. What we do know is that the rhinoceros was made with great skill, buried with royalty, and must have been very important. It has become one of South Africa's best-known symbols of heritage.

### subtopic_2 — The Golden Objects
**Assistant:** Why was Mapungubwe's story not told for many years?

**Tutor:** When the gold objects were found in 1933, South Africa was ruled by a government that did not give black South Africans equal rights. Many people in power at the time believed, wrongly, that Africans could not have built wealthy, organised kingdoms with gold and international trade. Some even claimed that such places must have been built by outsiders. So, for many years, the discoveries at Mapungubwe were not included in school history books, and few South Africans knew about them. The objects were studied by scientists, but the site was not celebrated. After South Africa became a democracy in 1994, this changed. Mapungubwe was recognised as an important part of African history and heritage, it became a World Heritage Site and a national park, and the golden rhinoceros became a proud national symbol. Its story reminds us to question who tells history, and why.

### subtopic_3 — Trade and Heritage
**Assistant:** How did Mapungubwe's people trade with China without going there?

**Tutor:** Mapungubwe's people did not need to travel all the way to China. Instead, they were part of a chain of trade. Goods from Mapungubwe, such as gold, ivory and animal skins, were carried, probably by river and overland, to trading towns on the east coast of Africa, such as Sofala, in what is now Mozambique, and further north, to Kilwa, in what is now Tanzania. These towns were busy ports, where Swahili-speaking traders met traders who sailed across the Indian Ocean in wooden ships called dhows, using the seasonal monsoon winds. The ships carried African goods to Arabia, Persia and India, and some goods were traded on to China. Goods from those faraway places, such as glass beads, cloth, and Chinese pottery, travelled back along the same chain to Mapungubwe. So a celadon bowl might have passed through many hands before it reached the hill.
