# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers heritage in sites of significance: the Cradle of Humankind in Gauteng. The anchor is a Grade 5 class from Pretoria on an outing to the Sterkfontein Caves, in the Cradle of Humankind, about fifty kilometres north-west of Johannesburg. The land above the caves is rolling grassland with rocky ridges and scattered trees. The learners put on hard hats and follow a guide down steep steps into the cool, dim caves, where the walls glisten and a still underground lake lies in the darkness. The guide stops at a spot where a famous skull was found, and shows them a replica: a small skull with a low forehead and a forward-jutting face. Later, at the visitor centre, they see casts of fossil bones laid out on a table, from tiny teeth to a whole skeleton, and a timeline painted along a long wall, millions of years long. The big idea is that the Cradle of Humankind is a site of world significance, because fossils found there show that Africa is where humankind began, and that this makes it part of the heritage not only of South Africa, but of all people on Earth. ## Subtopic: A Site of Significance

A site of significance is a place that is important to people, because of what happened there, what was found there, or what it means to them.

The Cradle of Humankind is an area of about four hundred and seventy square kilometres, mostly in Gauteng, with a small part in North West. Its rocks are made of dolomite, a kind of limestone, which is slowly dissolved by water, forming many caves. Over millions of years, the bones of animals and of early human ancestors fell or were washed or carried into these caves, where they were covered by sand and stones and became hardened into rock, which preserved them as fossils.

A fossil is the remains or trace of a living thing from long ago, preserved in rock, such as bones, teeth, shells or footprints.

The area is called the Cradle of Humankind because so many fossils of early human ancestors have been found there, more than at almost any other place in the world. A cradle is a bed for a baby, so the name means the place where humankind was born, like a baby in a cradle.

In 1999, it became a World Heritage Site, together with other fossil sites in South Africa, such as Taung in North West, where the famous Taung Child skull was found in 1924.

## Subtopic: Famous Discoveries

Many important fossils have been found in the Cradle of Humankind.

In 1947, at Sterkfontein, the scientists Robert Broom and John Robinson found a nearly complete skull of an adult early human ancestor, more than two million years old. It became known as Mrs Ples. It belonged to a kind of early human ancestor called Australopithecus, which walked upright on two legs, like us, but had a small brain, about a third of the size of ours, and long arms for climbing.

Also at Sterkfontein, scientists found an almost complete skeleton, nicknamed Little Foot, because the first bones identified were small foot bones. The skeleton was found in 1997 by two members of the team, Stephen Motsumi and Nkwane Molefe, after the scientist Ron Clarke had spotted the foot bones in a box of fossils. It took about twenty years to carefully free the skeleton from the hard rock. It is one of the most complete skeletons of an early human ancestor ever found, and it is millions of years old.

In 2013, in the Rising Star Cave, a team led by Lee Berger found more than a thousand pieces of bone from a new kind of human relative, which they named Homo naledi. Naledi means star in Sesotho and Setswana. The fossils were in a deep chamber that could only be reached through a very narrow gap, so slim scientists had to squeeze through.

Some of the earliest evidence of the use of fire by human ancestors has also been found in the Cradle, at a cave called Swartkrans.

## Subtopic: Heritage for All Humanity

Scientists who study fossils of early humans and their ancestors are called palaeoanthropologists. Fossils from the Cradle, and from other parts of Africa, show that the earliest human ancestors lived in Africa, and that modern humans, people like us, first appeared in Africa. From Africa, people later spread to other continents. This means that every person on Earth has ancestors who lived in Africa.

That is why the Cradle of Humankind is heritage for all humanity. It tells the story of where we all come from, and reminds us that all people share a common origin, whatever their language, appearance or country.

The Cradle is also important for South Africa. It attracts scientists from around the world, as well as tourists, which creates jobs. The visitor centre at Maropeng, whose name means returning to the place of origin in Setswana, teaches visitors about human origins.

The Cradle must be protected. Fossils are rare and fragile, and the law protects them. Only trained scientists with permits may dig for fossils, and visitors must never remove rocks or bones. Mining, pollution and development can damage the area, and acid water from old gold mines nearby has been a concern.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the fossils are of modern people; most are of early human ancestors and relatives. Two: thinking the Cradle is in the Western Cape; it is mainly in Gauteng. Three: thinking anyone may take fossils home; fossils are protected by law. Four: thinking the Cradle matters only to South Africa; it is heritage for all humanity.
