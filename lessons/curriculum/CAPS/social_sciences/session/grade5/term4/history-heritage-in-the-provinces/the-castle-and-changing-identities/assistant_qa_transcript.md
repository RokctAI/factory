### subtopic_1 — Building the Castle
**Assistant:** Why is the Castle shaped like a star?

**Tutor:** The star shape, or pentagon with pointed bastions, was a popular design for forts in Europe at the time, after cannons became common in war. Older castles with round towers and straight walls had blind spots, where attackers could hide close to the wall and not be seen or shot by defenders. With pointed bastions sticking out at each corner, soldiers standing on one bastion could see and fire along the walls to the next bastion, so there was nowhere for attackers to hide. The angled walls also made cannonballs more likely to glance off rather than smash straight through. The thick, low walls, with earth packed behind them, could absorb the impact of cannon fire. A moat around the walls made it harder to reach them. Interestingly, the Castle of Good Hope was never attacked in battle, but its design was meant to protect the company against attacks from European rivals.

### subtopic_2 — Many Meanings
**Assistant:** Who was Krotoa?

**Tutor:** Krotoa was a Khoikhoi woman who lived at the Cape in the early years of the Dutch East India Company's station, in the 1650s and 1660s. As a young girl, she went to live and work in the household of Jan van Riebeeck. She learnt to speak Dutch and Portuguese very well, as well as her own Khoikhoi language, and became an important interpreter between the Khoikhoi and the Dutch, helping with trade and negotiations. The Dutch called her Eva. She married a Danish surgeon who worked for the company, and they had children. After her husband died, her life became very difficult. She was treated harshly by the company, and was banished to Robben Island several times. She died in 1674. Today, many people see Krotoa as a symbol of the meeting, and the pain, of different cultures at the Cape, and many South African families can trace their ancestry to her.

### subtopic_3 — Changing Identities
**Assistant:** How did slavery shape the Cape?

**Tutor:** From 1658 until slavery ended in the British Empire in 1834, with a further period of forced apprenticeship until 1838, many thousands of enslaved people were brought to the Cape. They came from places such as Indonesia, India, Sri Lanka, Madagascar and Mozambique. They worked in homes, on farms and vineyards, in workshops and in building projects such as the Castle, without pay and without freedom, and they were often punished harshly. Families could be split up and sold. Despite this suffering, enslaved people kept and created culture. They brought skills in building, cooking, crafts and music. Islam came to the Cape partly through enslaved and exiled people from Asia, and Cape Malay cooking, such as bobotie and koeksisters, has roots in their traditions. Many words in Afrikaans came from the languages they spoke. Today, the Iziko Slave Lodge museum in Cape Town tells their story. December the 1st is remembered by many as Emancipation Day.
