### subtopic_1 — Art in the Mountains
**Assistant:** How do we know what San rock art means?

**Tutor:** We cannot ask the artists, because the San who painted in the Drakensberg are no longer living there as hunter-gatherers. But researchers use several sources together. First, in the 1870s, Wilhelm Bleek and Lucy Lloyd recorded thousands of pages of stories, beliefs and explanations from |Xam San people in Cape Town, in the San people's own words. Second, in the late 1800s, a San man named Qing, from the Maloti mountains, explained some paintings to a visitor. Third, researchers have studied San communities in the Kalahari in the 1900s, who still held trance dances. By comparing these sources with the paintings, researchers such as David Lewis-Williams found many links, such as the importance of the eland, figures with bleeding noses, bent-forward postures and part-animal shapes, which match descriptions of trance. Researchers still discuss and debate meanings, and some say we should be careful about claiming to know everything an artist intended.

### subtopic_2 — How and Why It Was Made
**Assistant:** Are there San people today?

**Tutor:** Yes. San people still live in southern Africa today, mainly in Botswana, Namibia and South Africa, as well as in Angola, Zambia and Zimbabwe. In South Africa, San communities include the ‡Khomani San in the southern Kalahari, in the Northern Cape, where their land and cultural landscape became a World Heritage Site in 2017, and the !Xun and Khwe communities at Platfontein, near Kimberley. Very few San today live only by hunting and gathering. Most live in villages or towns, and work in many different jobs, but many keep their languages, stories, crafts, knowledge of plants and animals, and dances alive. San communities have faced great hardship, including the loss of their land, and many have fought for their rights, their land and recognition. Some San communities are involved in protecting rock art, and in sharing the benefits of their traditional knowledge, such as rooibos and hoodia.

### subtopic_3 — Protecting Rock Art
**Assistant:** Why is the eland so important in the paintings?

**Tutor:** The eland is the largest antelope in Africa. It is heavy, strong and gentle, with twisted horns and a dewlap, a fold of skin hanging from its neck. For the San, the eland was very important for food, because one eland provided a lot of meat and fat for the whole band, and its fat was especially valued. But it was much more than food. In San beliefs, the eland was the animal most loved by the trickster god, and it was believed to have great spiritual power, or potency. It played a part in important ceremonies, such as when a girl became a woman, and when a boy killed his first eland. Healers in trance were believed to draw on the eland's power. That is why the eland is painted more often, and more carefully, than any other animal in the Drakensberg, often with beautiful shading and detail.
