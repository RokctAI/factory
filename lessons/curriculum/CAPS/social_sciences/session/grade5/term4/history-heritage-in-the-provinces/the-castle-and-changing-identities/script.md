# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers heritage and changing identities, using the example of the Castle of Good Hope in the Western Cape. The anchor is a Grade 5 class from Mitchells Plain, in Cape Town, on an outing to the Castle of Good Hope, in the centre of the city, not far from the station and the Grand Parade. From the outside, the Castle looks like a giant star with five points, surrounded by a moat, with thick stone walls painted a pale yellow. The learners walk in through a heavy gateway with a bell tower above it, into a wide courtyard. A guide shows them a balcony with steps, where announcements were once read to the people, a small dark prison cell, rooms full of old furniture and paintings, and displays about the Khoikhoi, enslaved people and soldiers who lived and worked at the Cape. The guide says that the same building has meant very different things to different people over more than three hundred years. The big idea is that heritage sites can have changing meanings, and that the Castle, once a symbol of colonial power, is now a heritage site that tells many stories, linked to the changing identities of the people of the Cape and of South Africa. The Castle and changing identities are examinable.

## Subtopic: Building the Castle

In 1652, the Dutch East India Company, a powerful trading company from the Netherlands, set up a station at the Cape, led by Jan van Riebeeck. Its purpose was to grow fresh food and supply water and meat to the company's ships sailing between Europe and Asia. The company first built a small fort of clay and wood, called the Fort de Goede Hoop.

Between 1666 and 1679, the company replaced it with a much stronger fort of stone, the Castle of Good Hope. It is the oldest surviving colonial building in South Africa.

The Castle has the shape of a pentagon, a shape with five sides, with a pointed bastion at each of its five corners. Bastions are parts of a fort that stick out, so that soldiers could fire along the walls at attackers from many directions. The five bastions were named after titles of an important Dutch prince.

The Castle was built with stone from the area around Table Mountain and from Robben Island, and with the hard work of soldiers, sailors and enslaved people.

Inside, there were living quarters for the governor and officials, offices, stores, barracks for soldiers, a church, a bakery and prison cells.

The questions for this section are with you now: why and how the Castle was built.

## Subtopic: Many Meanings

For the Dutch East India Company, the Castle was the centre of its power at the Cape. The governor lived and worked there, laws and announcements were read from its balcony, and the company's officials ruled the growing colony from there.

For the Khoikhoi, who had lived at the Cape for a very long time, the Castle was a symbol of the arrival of people who took their land and grazing, and of conflict and loss. Some Khoikhoi people worked at the Castle, traded there, or acted as interpreters, while others resisted the company.

For the enslaved people, brought by the company from places such as Indonesia, India, Madagascar and Mozambique, the Castle was a place of forced labour and harsh punishment. Its dark prison cells held people who broke the company's rules.

In 1795, and again in 1806, Britain took control of the Cape, and the Castle became the headquarters of the British army. Later, it was used by the South African army.

So the same building had very different meanings for different people: power and safety for some, and loss, fear and suffering for others.

The questions for this section are with you now: what the Castle meant to different groups of people.

## Subtopic: Changing Identities

Identity is the sense of who you are, and which groups you belong to, such as your family, language, culture, community and country. Identities can change over time.

At the Cape, the identities of people changed a great deal. Khoikhoi, Europeans, enslaved people from Africa and Asia, and others lived side by side, and over generations, many families came to include ancestors from several of these groups. New communities, new ways of life, new foods, new music and a new language, Afrikaans, developed. The Cape Muslim community also grew, with roots among enslaved and exiled people from Asia.

The identity of the Castle changed too. After South Africa became a democracy in 1994, the Castle was no longer seen only as a symbol of colonial power. Today, it is a heritage site and a museum, open to everyone. It tells the stories of all the people linked to it, including the Khoikhoi and the enslaved, not only of governors and soldiers. Ceremonies and events are held there that honour the Khoi and San, and the army still has a presence there.

This shows that heritage is not fixed. People can look at the same place in new ways, include voices that were left out, and use it to build understanding.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the Castle was built in 1652; the first fort was clay and wood, and the stone Castle was built from 1666 to 1679. Two: thinking the Castle meant the same to everyone; it meant power to some and loss to others. Three: thinking identities never change; they change over time. Four: thinking the Castle tells only the story of governors; it now tells the stories of the Khoikhoi and enslaved people too.

The questions for this section are with you now: identity and how the Castle's meaning has changed.
