# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers heritage in art: San rock art in the Drakensberg, in KwaZulu-Natal. The anchor is a Grade 5 class from Estcourt, in KwaZulu-Natal, on a hike in the Kamberg area of the Drakensberg. With an accredited guide in front, they climb a grassy slope for about an hour, crossing a stream, until they reach a long rock overhang, called Game Pass Shelter, with a view across green valleys to the high, blue mountains. On the smooth sandstone wall, there are paintings in red, brown, white and black: a herd of eland, large antelope with heavy bodies and twisted horns, and small, graceful human figures, some running and some carrying bows. The guide points to one famous painting, a dying eland with its head lowered and its legs crossing, and next to it, a figure holding the eland's tail, who seems to be part human and part animal. She reminds them not to touch the paintings, and not to splash water on them. The big idea is that San rock art in the Drakensberg is some of the finest rock art in the world, linked to San beliefs, and that it is precious heritage for South Africa and the world, which must be protected. ## Subtopic: Art in the Mountains

The Drakensberg, called uKhahlamba in isiZulu, which means barrier of spears, is the highest mountain range in South Africa, along the border of KwaZulu-Natal and Lesotho. Its many sandstone shelters and caves contain one of the largest and most concentrated collections of rock paintings in Africa, with tens of thousands of images at hundreds of sites.

The uKhahlamba-Drakensberg Park became a World Heritage Site in 2000, recognised both for its natural beauty and for its rock art. It is one of only a few places in the world recognised for both nature and culture.

The paintings were made by San hunter-gatherers, who lived in and around the Drakensberg for thousands of years. Many of the paintings are hundreds of years old, and some may be a few thousand years old. The most recent paintings, from the 1800s, show horses, cattle, wagons and people with guns, which tells us that the San were painting when farmers and colonists arrived in the area.

The paintings show many animals, especially the eland, as well as hartebeest, rhebok, elephants, lions and birds. They show people hunting, dancing, gathering, travelling and resting, and strange figures that are part human and part animal.

## Subtopic: How and Why It Was Made

San artists made paint from natural materials. Red, brown and yellow came from ochre, a kind of earth or stone rich in iron. White came from white clay, and black came from charcoal or minerals. They ground these into powder and mixed them with a liquid to make paint, possibly blood, animal fat, egg, plant juice or water. They painted with brushes made from feathers, animal hair, reeds or thin sticks, and with their fingers.

The paintings are very skilful. Artists used shading to show the round shapes of animals' bodies, and showed animals from different angles, even from the front and behind, which is very difficult.

Why did the San paint? Researchers have studied the paintings together with records of San beliefs, written down in the 1800s from San people themselves, and observations of San communities in the Kalahari. Many researchers believe that much of the art is linked to the San spirit world. San healers danced until they entered a trance, a state like a deep dream, in which they believed they could travel to the spirit world, heal the sick, make rain and guide animals. The eland was believed to hold great spiritual power. The painting of the dying eland at Game Pass Shelter, with its crossed legs and lowered head, may show the power of the eland being linked to a healer in trance, who is shown as part human, part animal. For this reason, Game Pass Shelter has been called the Rosetta Stone of rock art, because it helped researchers to understand the meaning of many other paintings.

## Subtopic: Protecting Rock Art

Rock art is fragile and cannot be replaced. Once a painting is damaged, it is lost forever.

Natural threats include water seeping through the rock, rain, sun, wind, dust, smoke from veld fires, and insects and birds building nests. Over time, these make paintings fade, flake or wash away.

People are an even bigger threat. Some visitors touch the paintings, which leaves oil from their skin. Some splash water on them to make the colours brighter for photographs, which washes away the paint. Some scratch names or draw graffiti. Some have even tried to cut paintings out of the rock to steal them.

In South Africa, the law protects all rock art. It is a crime to damage, change or remove it. Many sites can only be visited with an accredited guide, and some sites are kept secret to protect them. Rock art centres, such as the one at Kamberg, teach visitors about the art and its meaning. Researchers record paintings with photographs and careful copies, so that their images are kept even if the paintings fade.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking rock art is just decoration; much of it is linked to San beliefs and the spirit world. Two: thinking water helps to clean the paintings; it washes paint away. Three: thinking the paintings are all exactly the same age; they range from very old to the 1800s. Four: thinking rock art can be repainted if it fades; once lost, it is lost forever.
