# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the topic minerals and mining in South Africa, with a focus on minerals as non-renewable resources. The anchor is a Grade 5 class in Germiston, on the East Rand of Gauteng. From the classroom window, the learners can see a flat-topped hill of pale yellow sand on the edge of town. It is not a natural hill. It is a mine dump, made from crushed rock left over after gold was taken out of it, many years ago. On the desks, the teacher has set out a box of rock samples: a piece of shiny black coal, a lump of rusty red iron ore, a grey rock with tiny gold-coloured flecks, a pale crystal of quartz, and a small, dull stone labelled as a rough diamond. Next to the box is a picture of a wind turbine and a solar panel. The teacher asks the class to sort the samples and pictures into two groups: things that can run out, and things that will not. The big idea is that minerals are valuable natural resources found in the Earth's crust, that they are non-renewable because they take millions of years to form and can be used up, and that mining minerals has shaped South Africa's history, towns and economy. Minerals as non-renewable resources are examinable.

## Subtopic: What Are Minerals?

A mineral is a natural, solid substance found in the rocks of the Earth's crust. The crust is the hard, outer layer of the Earth, which we live on. Minerals are not made by people, plants or animals. They form over a very long time, through heat, pressure and the movement of water and molten rock deep underground.

There are many kinds of minerals. Some are metals, such as gold, platinum, iron, copper, chrome and manganese. Others are not metals, such as diamonds, salt and quartz. Coal is a fuel that formed from ancient plants. It is not strictly a mineral, but it is dug out of the ground like one, so it is usually studied with minerals.

A rock that contains enough of a valuable mineral to be worth mining is called an ore. For example, iron ore is rock that is rich in iron.

Mining is the process of digging or drilling minerals out of the ground. The minerals are then processed, which means they are separated from the rest of the rock and made pure enough to use.

We use minerals every day, often without noticing. The steel in cars, bridges and buildings is made from iron ore. Copper carries electricity in wires. Cellphones contain small amounts of gold, copper and many other minerals. Salt is a mineral we eat.

The questions for this section are with you now: what minerals, ores and mining are.

## Subtopic: Renewable and Non-Renewable

A natural resource is anything from nature that people use, such as water, soil, plants, animals, sunlight, wind and minerals.

Renewable resources can be replaced naturally, in a fairly short time, or will never run out. Sunlight and wind are renewable, because the sun will keep shining and the wind will keep blowing. Forests, fish and farmland can be renewable, if people use them carefully and give them time to recover.

Non-renewable resources cannot be replaced in a human lifetime. Minerals are non-renewable, because they took millions of years to form. Once a mineral has been dug up and used, it is gone, and there is only a limited amount in the ground. Coal, oil and natural gas are also non-renewable.

This means that one day, the mines will run out. Some mines in South Africa have already closed because the ore was used up or became too difficult and expensive to reach. That is why it is important to use minerals wisely, to recycle metals such as steel, copper, aluminium and gold, and to use more renewable resources, such as solar and wind power, for energy.

The questions for this section are with you now: renewable and non-renewable resources.

## Subtopic: A Mining Country

South Africa is one of the richest countries in the world in minerals. It has very large amounts of platinum, chrome and manganese, as well as gold, diamonds, coal, iron ore, copper and many other minerals.

Mining has shaped South Africa's history. The discovery of diamonds near Kimberley from 1867, and of gold on the Witwatersrand in 1886, brought huge changes. Towns and cities grew quickly, railways were built, and people came from all over southern Africa and the world to work on the mines. Johannesburg grew from a mining camp into South Africa's biggest city in a few decades.

Mining is important to the economy. It provides jobs for hundreds of thousands of people, brings money into the country when minerals are sold to other countries, and supplies coal for most of South Africa's electricity.

But mining also has costs. It can damage the land, pollute water and air, and be dangerous for workers. In the past, many mineworkers worked in harsh and unfair conditions, far from their families. In this topic, we will learn about all of these sides of mining.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking minerals are made by people; they form naturally in the Earth's crust. Two: thinking minerals are renewable; they take millions of years to form and can run out. Three: thinking a mine dump is a natural hill; it is waste rock left by mining. Four: thinking mining has only good effects; it also harms land, water, air and people.
