### subtopic_1 — What Are Minerals?
**Assistant:** Is coal really a mineral?

**Tutor:** Strictly speaking, no. Scientists define a mineral as a natural, solid substance that was not formed by living things and has a particular chemical make-up and structure, often forming crystals. Coal does not fit this definition exactly, because it formed from the remains of plants that lived millions of years ago, and it is a mixture of many substances. Scientists call it a fossil fuel, along with oil and natural gas, because they all formed from the remains of ancient living things. But because coal is dug out of the ground in mines, like gold and iron ore, and because it is non-renewable, it is usually included when people talk about mineral resources and mining. That is why your textbook groups coal with minerals. In a test, you can say coal is a fossil fuel and a non-renewable resource that is mined.

### subtopic_2 — Renewable and Non-Renewable
**Assistant:** What is a mine dump made of, and why is it yellow?

**Tutor:** A mine dump is a pile of waste material left over after mining. On the Witwatersrand, gold is found in tiny amounts spread through hard rock. To get the gold out, the rock was crushed into fine sand and treated with chemicals that dissolve the gold. What was left over, the crushed rock, was piled up in huge heaps or flat-topped dumps, also called tailings. Many of the old dumps around Johannesburg and the East Rand are pale yellow or white, because of the colour of the crushed quartz rock and the minerals in it. On windy days, dust blows off them, which can be a problem for people living nearby. Some old dumps are now being reprocessed, because modern methods can get out gold that was missed long ago, and some have been planted with grass to stop dust. Many people have grown up seeing them as part of the landscape.

### subtopic_3 — A Mining Country
**Assistant:** Will South Africa run out of minerals?

**Tutor:** Some minerals in some places have already run out, or have become too deep and expensive to mine. For example, many of the old gold mines on the Witwatersrand have closed, and South Africa now produces much less gold than it did fifty years ago, even though there is still gold deep underground. Other minerals, such as platinum, chrome, manganese and coal, still have very large amounts left, enough for many years. But because all minerals are non-renewable, they will run out one day. That is why it is important to plan for the future: to use minerals wisely, recycle metals, develop other jobs and industries in mining towns, and use more renewable energy, such as solar and wind power, instead of coal. Towns that depend on one mine can struggle when the mine closes, so planning ahead matters for people too.
