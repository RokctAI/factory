# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers where coniferous forests are found and what their climate is like. The anchor is a Grade 6 class at a school in Sabie, a town in the mountains of Mpumalanga, surrounded by forestry plantations. On a cool, misty morning, a forester takes the class through a pine plantation. The tall trees grow in straight rows, and the ground is soft with a thick carpet of fallen brown needles. The forester picks up a woody cone and shows the learners the seeds hidden between its scales. He explains that these pine trees are not indigenous to South Africa. Pines and similar trees were brought here from the Northern Hemisphere and planted for timber and paper, because they grow fast and straight. Their wild relatives, he says, grow in enormous natural forests in very cold places far to the north, where snow covers the ground for months. One learner asks how a tree can survive being frozen for half the year. The big idea is that coniferous forests are found in a wide belt across the cold northern parts of the world, with long, freezing winters and short, cool summers, and that their trees are suited to the cold. The location and climate of coniferous forests are examinable.

## Subtopic: Where Coniferous Forests Are

Coniferous forests are forests made up mainly of conifers, which are trees that carry their seeds in cones. Most conifers, such as pine, spruce and fir, are evergreen and have long, thin leaves called needles.

The world's great natural coniferous forest forms a huge belt across the northern parts of North America, Europe and Asia. It is sometimes called the boreal forest, from a word meaning northern, or the taiga, a Russian word. It is the largest area of forest on land in the world.

The belt lies roughly between fifty and seventy degrees north of the equator, south of the Arctic tundra, where it is too cold for trees to grow, and north of the mild, temperate lands. It stretches across Alaska and Canada in North America, across Norway, Sweden and Finland in northern Europe, and across Russia, especially the vast region of Siberia, all the way to the Pacific Ocean.

There is almost no natural coniferous forest of this kind in the Southern Hemisphere. This is because, at the same distance south of the equator, there is mostly ocean, with very little land. South Africa lies much closer to the equator, so it is far too warm for this type of forest. The pine plantations of Mpumalanga, KwaZulu-Natal and the Cape are farmed forests, not natural ones.

## Subtopic: A Cold Climate

Coniferous forests have a cold climate, with two very different seasons.

Winters are long and very cold. For about six months of the year, the average temperature is below freezing. In the coldest places, especially in Siberia, winter temperatures can fall to minus forty degrees Celsius or even lower. Snow covers the ground for months, rivers and lakes freeze, and the days are very short, because these places are far from the equator.

Summers are short and cool to mild, lasting only about three or four months. Average summer temperatures are often around ten to twenty degrees, although some days can be warm. The days are very long in summer, and plants grow quickly while they can.

Because the winters are so cold and the summers are mild, the annual temperature range is very large, often more than thirty degrees, and in parts of Siberia much more.

Precipitation is low to moderate, often between three hundred and nine hundred millimetres a year. Much of it falls as rain in summer, and in winter it falls as snow. Because it is cold, little water evaporates, so the ground does not dry out easily.

On a climate graph for a place in the coniferous forest, the temperature line looks like a tall hill. Because the forest is in the Northern Hemisphere, the line is lowest in January, far below zero, rises steeply to its highest point in July and then falls again. The rainfall bars are mostly short, a little taller in summer. Compare this with a rainforest graph, with a flat temperature line and tall bars.

## Subtopic: Trees Suited to the Cold

Conifers are well suited to the cold climate.

Most conifers are evergreen, so they do not need to grow new leaves each spring. As soon as it is warm enough, they can start making food, which is important in a short summer. Their needles are thin and have a waxy coating, so they lose very little water. This matters because in winter, the water in the ground is frozen and the trees cannot take it up. Many conifers are shaped like a cone, with sloping, bendy branches, so that heavy snow slides off instead of breaking them. Their dark green colour helps them absorb the sun's heat. One conifer, the larch, is different: it loses its needles in winter.

Few kinds of trees grow in coniferous forests, so large areas are covered by just one or two kinds. The forests are home to animals such as moose, brown bears, wolves, lynx and beavers. Some animals, such as bears, sleep through much of the winter, and many birds fly south to warmer places.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking South Africa's pine plantations are natural forests; they are farmed trees from the Northern Hemisphere. Two: thinking coniferous forests are found in both hemispheres; the great belt is almost all in the north. Three: thinking all conifers lose their leaves in winter; most are evergreen. Four: thinking the coniferous forest is hot in summer like a desert; summers are short and mostly cool to mild.
