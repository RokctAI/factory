### subtopic_1 — Where Coniferous Forests Are
**Assistant:** Why are pine plantations a problem for some South African rivers?

**Tutor:** Pine and gum trees from other countries grow fast and use a lot of water, especially when they are planted in large numbers near streams and in the mountain areas where many of our rivers begin. This can mean that less water flows into the rivers and dams downstream. Some of these trees also spread outside the plantations, where they become invasive alien plants that crowd out indigenous plants such as fynbos and grassland. To deal with this, plantations need a permit, and South Africa runs programmes, such as Working for Water, that employ people to clear invasive alien trees from river banks and mountain catchments. At the same time, plantations provide many jobs and the timber and paper that we use every day, so they must be managed carefully.

### subtopic_2 — A Cold Climate
**Assistant:** How do animals survive the winter in the coniferous forest?

**Tutor:** Animals use several strategies. Some, such as brown bears, eat as much as they can in summer and autumn to build up fat, then sleep for much of the winter in a den, using very little energy. Many birds migrate, flying south to warmer places for the winter and returning in spring. Animals that stay active, such as wolves, lynx and moose, grow thick winter coats, and some, such as the snowshoe hare, turn white to hide in the snow. Lynx and hares have large, furry feet that work like snowshoes, so they do not sink into deep snow. Squirrels and some birds store seeds and nuts in autumn to eat in winter. Beavers store branches underwater near their lodges, so they can feed even when the pond is frozen over.

### subtopic_3 — Trees Suited to the Cold
**Assistant:** Is the coniferous forest important for the whole world?

**Tutor:** Yes. Because it is so huge, the boreal forest stores an enormous amount of carbon in its trees, its soils and its frozen peat bogs. Scientists believe it may hold more carbon than tropical rainforests, mainly because so much is stored in its cold soils, where dead plants rot very slowly. If the forest is cut down or burned, or if the frozen ground thaws because of a warming climate, some of this carbon can be released into the air as gases that add to climate change. The forest also provides timber and paper for many countries and is home to indigenous peoples, such as many First Nations communities in Canada. In recent years, large wildfires in Canada and Siberia have worried scientists.
