# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the Term 3 History topic, democracy and citizenship. The anchor is a Grade 6 class at a primary school in Soweto, in Gauteng, on the day of the election for the Representative Council of Learners, the group of learners who speak for the learners of the school. Three candidates from the class have made posters and given short speeches about what they will do: one wants more books in the library, one wants a vegetable garden, and one wants a cleaner playground. In the hall, each learner receives one ballot paper, goes behind a cardboard screen to mark it in secret, and drops it into a sealed box. After school, two teachers and an observer from each candidate count the votes in front of everyone. The winner thanks everyone, and the other candidates congratulate her. The teacher tells the class that they have just taken part in democracy. The big idea is that democracy is a way of governing in which the people choose their leaders and have a say in how they are ruled, and that citizens have rights and responsibilities. The meaning of democracy and citizenship is examinable.

## Subtopic: What Democracy Means

The word democracy comes from two old Greek words: demos, meaning the people, and kratos, meaning power or rule. So democracy means rule by the people.

The idea began more than two thousand years ago in the city of Athens, in ancient Greece. There, the citizens met in a large open-air assembly to discuss and vote on important matters themselves. But Athenian democracy was limited. Only free men born in Athens counted as citizens. Women, enslaved people and people from other places could not vote.

In a big country with millions of people, it is impossible for everyone to meet in one place and vote on every decision. So most modern democracies are representative democracies. The people vote in elections to choose representatives, who make laws and decisions on their behalf. If the people are unhappy with them, they can vote for someone else at the next election.

A democracy has several important features. There are free and fair elections, held regularly, in which every adult citizen has one vote and votes in secret. There are different political parties, and people may support whichever they choose. People have rights, such as freedom of speech, which means they may criticise the government without being punished. The majority decides, but the rights of smaller groups, or minorities, are protected. And everyone, including the leaders, must obey the law.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of democracy and its main features.

## Subtopic: Other Kinds of Government

Democracy is only one way of governing. Throughout history, there have been other kinds of government.

In a monarchy, a king or queen rules, and the position is usually passed down within one family. In the past, many monarchs had almost total power. Today, some countries, such as the United Kingdom, Lesotho and Eswatini, still have monarchs. In some, like the United Kingdom, the monarch has little real power and the country is governed by an elected parliament. In others, such as Eswatini, the king has a great deal of power.

In a dictatorship, one person or a small group takes and keeps power, often by force, and the people cannot freely choose or remove them. Dictators often ban other political parties, control the newspapers and radio, and punish people who disagree with them.

South Africa itself was not a democracy for most of its history. Under colonial rule, and later under apartheid, the government was chosen only by white South Africans. From 1948, the apartheid government passed laws that separated people by race and treated black, coloured and Indian South Africans unfairly. Most South Africans were not allowed to vote for the national government. Many people struggled for many years, and many suffered, to win the right to vote for everyone.

On the twenty-seventh of April 1994, South Africa held its first democratic election, in which all adult South Africans could vote. We celebrate that day every year as Freedom Day.

The questions for this section are with you now: other kinds of government and South Africa's road to democracy.

## Subtopic: What It Means to Be a Citizen

A citizen is a person who legally belongs to a country. Most people become citizens of the country where they are born, or because their parents are citizens, and some people become citizens later in life. South African citizens can have a South African identity document and a passport.

Citizenship brings rights, which are things that every citizen is entitled to. In South Africa, these include the right to vote from the age of eighteen, the right to equality, the right to education and many others written in the Constitution.

Citizenship also brings responsibilities, which are things that citizens should do. Good citizens obey the law, respect the rights of others, pay taxes when they earn money, vote in elections, and care for their community and environment. Even children, who cannot vote yet, can be active citizens by going to school, helping others, taking part in school elections and learning about how their country is governed.

This term, we will learn about South Africa's first democratic government, the elections of 1994, parliament and the Constitution, rules and laws, the courts, the rights and responsibilities of citizens and children, and our national symbols.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking democracy means the leaders can do whatever they want once elected; they must obey the law and face the next election. Two: thinking ancient Athens let everyone vote; only free men born there could. Three: thinking South Africa has always been a democracy; the first democratic election was in 1994. Four: thinking citizens have only rights; they also have responsibilities.
