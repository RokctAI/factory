### subtopic_1 — What Democracy Means
**Assistant:** Why is the secret ballot so important?

**Tutor:** A secret ballot means that nobody can see how you voted, and you do not have to tell anyone. This protects voters from pressure, threats or bribes. If votes were not secret, a powerful person, an employer or even family members might punish people who did not vote the way they wanted, or pay people to vote a certain way and then check that they did. With a secret ballot, people can vote according to their own beliefs without fear. That is why, in South African elections, each voter marks the ballot paper alone behind a screen, folds it and puts it into a sealed box. It is the same reason the Soweto class used a cardboard screen in their school election.

### subtopic_2 — Other Kinds of Government
**Assistant:** Can a democracy make bad decisions?

**Tutor:** Yes. Democracy does not guarantee that every decision will be wise, because people and their representatives can make mistakes. But democracy has ways of correcting mistakes. People can criticise decisions openly, journalists can report on problems, citizens can protest peacefully, courts can stop the government if it breaks the Constitution, and voters can choose different leaders at the next election. Protecting the rights of minorities also stops a majority from treating smaller groups unfairly, which is one reason a democracy needs a strong constitution and independent courts, not only elections. In a dictatorship, it is much harder to correct mistakes, because people who point them out can be punished.

### subtopic_3 — What It Means to Be a Citizen
**Assistant:** Who could vote in South Africa before 1994?

**Tutor:** The rules changed over time, and they were different in different parts of the country. In the old Cape Colony, some black and coloured men who owned property or earned enough could vote, but these rights were reduced and later taken away. When the Union of South Africa was formed in 1910, only white people could be elected to parliament, and almost all voters were white men. White women won the vote in 1930. In the following decades, the remaining black and coloured voters in the Cape lost their right to vote on the same voters' roll as white voters. In 1984, separate houses of parliament were created for coloured and Indian South Africans, but black South Africans, the large majority, still had no vote for parliament. Only in 1994 could all adults vote.
