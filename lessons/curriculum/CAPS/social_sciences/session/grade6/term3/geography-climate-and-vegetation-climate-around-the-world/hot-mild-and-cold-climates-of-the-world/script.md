# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the hot, mild and cold climates of the world and how to read January and July temperature maps. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Polokwane, the capital of Limpopo, on a hot morning in late January. Their school has a partner school in Norway, in the far north of Europe, and the two classes have arranged a video call. On the screen, the Norwegian children wave from a classroom with snow piled against the windows. Outside, they say, it is minus ten degrees, and the sun rises late and sets in the middle of the afternoon. In Polokwane, the classroom fans are turning and it is already thirty degrees. The Norwegian children are amazed to hear that in Limpopo it is the middle of summer. On the wall behind the Polokwane class are two world maps, one coloured to show temperatures in January and one for July. The big idea is that temperatures change with distance from the equator, so the world has hot, mild and cold climate zones, and the seasons are opposite in the Northern and Southern Hemispheres. Climate zones and temperature maps are examinable.

## Subtopic: Why Some Places Are Hot and Others Cold

The main reason for differences in temperature around the world is the shape of the Earth. Because the Earth is round, the sun's rays do not hit every place in the same way.

Near the equator, the sun is high in the sky for most of the year, and its rays strike the Earth almost straight on. The energy is concentrated on a small area, so the ground and air heat up a lot. Near the poles, the sun is always low in the sky, and its rays strike the Earth at a low slant. The same amount of energy is spread over a much bigger area, so each part gets less heat. The rays also pass through more of the atmosphere on their slanted path, which weakens them further.

You can test this with a torch. Shine it straight down onto a sheet of paper, and you get a small, bright circle. Tilt the torch, and the light spreads into a bigger, dimmer oval. The equator gets the bright circle, and the poles get the dim oval.

This gives the world three broad temperature zones. The hot zone, or tropical zone, lies between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn, around the equator. It is hot all year. The two cold zones, or polar zones, lie beyond the Arctic Circle and the Antarctic Circle. They are cold all year, with long, dark winters. Between them lie the two mild zones, also called temperate zones, with warm summers and cool or cold winters. Most of South Africa lies in the southern mild zone, just south of the Tropic of Capricorn, which passes through Limpopo, not far from Polokwane.

The questions for this section are with you now: why temperatures change from the equator to the poles, and the three temperature zones.

## Subtopic: Reading January and July Temperature Maps

A temperature map uses colours to show the average temperature in different parts of the world during one month. Usually, reds and oranges show hot places, yellows and greens show mild places, and blues and purples show cold places. Always check the key to see which colour stands for which temperature.

Geographers often compare a map for January with a map for July, because these are usually the hottest and coldest months. On the January map, the Southern Hemisphere is in summer. Southern Africa, Australia and South America south of the equator are coloured in warm reds and oranges, while North America, Europe and northern Asia are coloured in cold blues and purples, especially Canada and Siberia, in Russia.

On the July map, the colours swap. Now the Northern Hemisphere is in summer, and places such as North Africa, the Middle East and the south of the United States are very hot. Southern Africa is in winter, and the southern parts of South America and Australia are cool. Near the equator, the colours look almost the same on both maps, because it is hot all year. Near the poles, especially Antarctica, the colours stay cold.

The seasons are opposite because the Earth is tilted. As it travels around the sun during the year, first the Southern Hemisphere leans towards the sun and has summer, and then the Northern Hemisphere leans towards the sun and has summer. That is why the children in Norway are in winter while Polokwane is in summer.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading temperature maps and why the seasons are opposite.

## Subtopic: Other Things That Affect Temperature

Distance from the equator is the main reason for temperature differences, but other factors matter too.

Height above sea level, or altitude, makes a big difference. The higher you go, the colder it gets. Mount Kilimanjaro in Tanzania is close to the equator, but its top is so high that it has snow and ice. In South Africa, Johannesburg, on the high plateau, has colder winters than Durban on the coast, even though they are not far apart.

Distance from the sea also matters. The sea heats up and cools down more slowly than land, so places near the coast have milder temperatures, with cooler summers and warmer winters. Places far inland, such as Upington or Bloemfontein, have hotter summers and colder winter nights. Ocean currents matter too. The warm Agulhas Current along the east coast makes Durban warmer, while the cold Benguela Current makes the west coast of South Africa cooler.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking places are hot because they are closer to the sun; it is because the sun's rays hit them more directly. Two: thinking the whole world has summer at the same time; the seasons are opposite in the two hemispheres. Three: thinking all places near the equator are hot; high mountains such as Kilimanjaro have snow. Four: reading a temperature map without the key; always check the colours.

The questions for this section are with you now: altitude, the sea and ocean currents.

High places are colder. Coastal places are milder than places far inland.
