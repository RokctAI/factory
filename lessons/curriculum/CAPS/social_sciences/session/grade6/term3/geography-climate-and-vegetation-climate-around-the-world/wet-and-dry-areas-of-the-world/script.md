# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the wet and dry areas of the world and how to read an annual rainfall map. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Mbombela, the capital of Mpumalanga, in the green, subtropical Lowveld, where bananas, mangoes and macadamia nuts grow. The class has a pen-pal school in Port Nolloth, a small fishing town on the west coast of the Northern Cape, close to the border with Namibia. Each school keeps a rain gauge and sends its monthly totals to the other. By the end of the summer, the Mbombela gauge has been filled again and again by afternoon thunderstorms, while the Port Nolloth class writes that their gauge has hardly been used. Instead, they say, thick fog rolls in from the sea on many mornings and wets the windows. The Mbombela learners are puzzled that two places in the same country can be so different. Their teacher opens the atlas to a world map of annual rainfall. The big idea is that rainfall is spread very unevenly around the world, and that the wettest and driest areas follow patterns that we can explain. Wet and dry areas and annual rainfall maps are examinable.

## Subtopic: Reading an Annual Rainfall Map

Annual rainfall is the total amount of rain and other precipitation that falls in a place in one year, measured in millimetres. An annual rainfall map shows this with colours or shading. Usually, dark blues and greens show very wet areas, and pale yellows and browns show very dry areas. As with every map, always read the key first.

On a world annual rainfall map, the wettest areas, with more than two thousand millimetres a year, lie in a band near the equator. They include the Amazon Basin in South America, the Congo Basin in central Africa and the islands of Southeast Asia, such as Indonesia. These are the areas of tropical rainforest. Some of the wettest places on Earth are in north-east India, where moist monsoon winds meet steep hills.

The driest areas, with less than two hundred and fifty millimetres a year, lie roughly along the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn. They include the Sahara Desert and the Arabian Desert in the north, and the Namib Desert, the Kalahari region, the deserts of Australia and the Atacama Desert in Chile in the south. The Atacama is one of the driest places on Earth, and some weather stations there have recorded almost no rain for many years.

Between the very wet and very dry areas lie places with moderate rainfall, such as much of Europe and most of South Africa. The cold polar areas are also very dry, because cold air holds little moisture. Antarctica is so dry that it is sometimes called a cold desert.

## Subtopic: Why Some Places Are Wet and Others Dry

Rain forms when warm, moist air rises and cools. As the air cools, the water vapour in it condenses into tiny drops that form clouds. When the drops grow big and heavy, they fall as rain.

Near the equator, the sun heats the land and sea strongly all year. Warm, moist air rises every day, cools and forms tall thunderclouds, and heavy rain falls, often in the afternoon. This is why the equator is so wet.

The air that rose at the equator spreads out high up and then sinks back down near the tropics. Sinking air becomes warmer and drier, so clouds cannot form. This is why many of the world's great hot deserts lie near the tropics.

Mountains affect rainfall too. When moist winds blow towards a mountain, the air is forced to rise, cools and drops rain on that side, called the windward side. By the time the air crosses over the top, it has lost most of its moisture, so the other side, the leeward side, is much drier. This dry area is called a rain shadow.

Ocean currents matter as well. Cold ocean currents cool the air above them, so it does not rise to form rain clouds. Instead, it often forms fog. The cold Benguela Current along the west coast of southern Africa helps to explain why the Namib Desert and Port Nolloth are so dry and foggy. Places far inland, a long way from the sea, are often drier too, because the winds have lost their moisture before reaching them.

## Subtopic: Rainfall in South Africa

South Africa is a dry country. Its average annual rainfall is well below the world average, and water is precious. But rainfall is very uneven. In general, rainfall decreases from east to west.

The east of the country is wetter. Moist winds blow in from the warm Indian Ocean and drop rain on the eastern coast and on the eastern slopes of the Drakensberg and the escarpment. Parts of Mpumalanga, KwaZulu-Natal and the Eastern Cape get more than one thousand millimetres a year. Mbombela gets around eight hundred millimetres, mostly in summer.

The west is much drier. By the time the winds from the east have crossed the high plateau, they have dropped most of their moisture. On the west coast, the cold Benguela Current keeps the air dry. Port Nolloth gets well under one hundred millimetres a year. The south-western Cape, around Cape Town, is an exception, because it gets winter rain from cold fronts that blow in from the Atlantic Ocean.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the hottest places are always the wettest; hot deserts near the tropics are very dry. Two: thinking deserts are only hot; Antarctica is a cold desert. Three: thinking both sides of a mountain get the same rain; the leeward side is in a rain shadow. Four: thinking rainfall is spread evenly across South Africa; it decreases from east to west.
