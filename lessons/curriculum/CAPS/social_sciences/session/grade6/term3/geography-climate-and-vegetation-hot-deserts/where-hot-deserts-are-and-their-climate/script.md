# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers where hot deserts are found, why they form and what their climate is like. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Klerksdorp, in North West. During the holidays, their teacher drove with her family across the border into Namibia and visited the Namib Desert. Now she shows the class her photographs on the classroom screen. There are huge red sand dunes, some more than three hundred metres high, with people looking like tiny ants as they climb the ridges. There is a white, cracked clay pan surrounded by dead trees, and a sky so blue that it looks painted. She tells the class that the sand was too hot to walk on barefoot at midday, but that she needed a thick jacket and a blanket at night. She also says that in the whole week she was there, it did not rain once, and the guide said some years get almost no rain at all. The learners are surprised that a hot place can be so cold at night. The big idea is that hot deserts are found mostly near the tropics, and that their climate has very little rain, very hot days and cold nights. ## Subtopic: Where Hot Deserts Are

A desert is an area that gets very little rain, usually less than two hundred and fifty millimetres a year. A hot desert is a desert that is hot for most or all of the year.

Most of the world's hot deserts lie in two bands, roughly along the Tropic of Cancer in the Northern Hemisphere and the Tropic of Capricorn in the Southern Hemisphere, about fifteen to thirty degrees north and south of the equator. Many are found on the western sides of continents.

In the Northern Hemisphere, the largest hot desert is the Sahara, which stretches across North Africa from the Atlantic Ocean to the Red Sea. It is roughly as big as the whole of the United States. East of it lies the Arabian Desert, and further east the Thar Desert in India and Pakistan. In North America, there are hot deserts in the south-west of the United States and in Mexico.

In the Southern Hemisphere, the Namib Desert runs along the Atlantic coast of Namibia, from southern Angola to near the border with South Africa. It is thought to be one of the oldest deserts in the world. Inland from it lies the Kalahari, which covers much of Botswana and parts of Namibia and the Northern Cape. Many geographers call the Kalahari a semi-desert, because many parts get a little more rain than a true desert and have grass and thorn trees. Other southern deserts are the large deserts of central and western Australia and the Atacama Desert in Chile, one of the driest places on Earth.

Not all deserts are sandy. Only a part of the Sahara is covered by sand dunes. Much of it is bare rock, stony plains covered with gravel, and mountains.

The questions for this section are with you now: where the world's hot deserts are found.

## Subtopic: Why Hot Deserts Form

There are several reasons why some places get so little rain.

The main reason is sinking air. Remember that near the equator, hot air rises and makes rain. That air spreads out high up and sinks back down near the tropics. As it sinks, it warms up and becomes very dry, so clouds cannot form. This is why many deserts lie in bands near the tropics.

Cold ocean currents also play a part. Along the west coasts of southern Africa and South America, cold currents, such as the Benguela Current, cool the air above them. Cool air near the ground does not rise to form rain clouds, so the coast stays dry, often with fog. This helps to explain the Namib and the Atacama.

Some deserts lie in a rain shadow, on the dry side of high mountains that block moist winds. Others, such as the middle of the Sahara and the deserts of central Australia, are very far from the sea, so the winds have lost their moisture before they arrive.

## Subtopic: The Climate of a Hot Desert

The climate of a hot desert has three main features.

First, there is very little rain, usually less than two hundred and fifty millimetres a year, and in some places much less. The rain does not fall regularly. A place might have no rain for months or even years, and then a sudden heavy storm that causes flash floods in the dry riverbeds.

Second, the days are very hot, especially in summer. Afternoon temperatures often rise above forty degrees Celsius in the Sahara and the Arabian Desert.

Third, the nights can be cold. Because there are few clouds, the sun's heat reaches the ground easily during the day, but at night the heat escapes quickly into the clear sky. The difference between the day and night temperatures, called the daily temperature range, can be twenty degrees or more.

On a climate graph for a hot desert, such as the town of Aswan in southern Egypt, the rainfall bars are so small that they can hardly be seen, because Aswan gets almost no rain at all. The temperature line rises from a mild winter of around fifteen degrees in January to over thirty degrees in the hottest months of June and July. Compare this with a rainforest graph, with its tall rain bars and flat temperature line.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking all deserts are covered in sand; many are rocky or stony. Two: thinking deserts are hot day and night; the nights can be cold. Three: thinking deserts are near the equator; most hot deserts lie near the tropics. Four: thinking it never rains in a desert; rain is rare but can fall in sudden storms.
