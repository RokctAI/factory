# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how people live in hot deserts, with examples of different lifestyles. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Kuruman, a town in the dry Northern Cape, on the edge of the Kalahari. On an outing, the class visits the Eye of Kuruman, a natural spring where clear water bubbles out of the ground into a pond full of fish, even in the driest months. A guide tells them that the spring produces millions of litres of water every day, and that people have depended on it for hundreds of years, long before the town was built. Without it, he says, there would be no town here at all. The learners look out at the dry, flat land beyond the pond and begin to understand that in dry places, people live wherever they can find water. The teacher asks them to imagine living in a much drier place, like the Sahara or the heart of the Kalahari, and to think about four questions: where would you get water, what would you eat, what kind of home would you build, and what would you wear? The big idea is that people in hot deserts have developed clever ways of life to cope with heat and lack of water. Desert lifestyles are examinable.

## Subtopic: Nomadic Herders

Nomads are people who do not live in one place, but move from place to place with their animals to find water and grazing. Many desert peoples have lived this way for centuries.

The Tuareg live in the Sahara, in countries such as Niger, Mali, Algeria and Libya. Traditionally, they kept camels, goats and sheep, and moved between wells and oases as the grazing changed. Camels can carry heavy loads and go many days without water, so in the past the Tuareg led long camel caravans across the Sahara, carrying goods such as salt to trade with people in the south. The Bedouin of the Arabian Desert and parts of North Africa lived in a similar way.

Nomadic herders live in tents that can be packed up and carried by camels. The tents are often made of animal skins or woven goat hair. Their food comes mostly from their animals, such as milk, cheese and sometimes meat, as well as dates and grain traded at oases.

Desert clothing is cleverly designed. Many Tuareg and Bedouin people wear long, loose robes that cover the whole body. Loose clothing lets air move around the body, and covering the skin protects it from the burning sun and blowing sand. Tuareg men traditionally wear a long cloth wrapped around the head and face, which protects them from sun and sand.

Today, many nomads have settled in towns or villages, but some still travel with their herds for part of the year.

## Subtopic: Hunter-Gatherers of the Kalahari

The San are the descendants of some of the earliest people of southern Africa. For thousands of years, groups of San lived in the Kalahari as hunter-gatherers, which means they found all their food by hunting wild animals and gathering wild plants.

The San had a deep knowledge of the desert. Men hunted antelope with bows and poisoned arrows, and were famous for their skill at tracking animals by their footprints. Women gathered most of the food, such as roots, berries, nuts and insects, and knew which plants were safe to eat. They found water in surprising ways. They dug up roots and gathered wild melons, called tsamma melons, which are full of water. They could also suck water from damp sand through a hollow reed, and they stored water in ostrich eggshells, which they sealed and buried in the sand to keep cool, so that they could find it later.

Because they moved often, they built simple shelters from branches and grass. They wore clothing made from animal skins.

Today, most San people no longer live only by hunting and gathering. Many lost access to their land when it was taken for farms and game reserves, and many now live in settled communities in Botswana, Namibia and South Africa. Some still keep their knowledge and skills alive, and some work as trackers and guides.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the San lived as hunter-gatherers in the Kalahari.

## Subtopic: Oases and Modern Desert Life

Some desert people are settled farmers who live at oases or along rivers. At an oasis, water from underground reaches the surface, and people grow date palms, vegetables and grain, watering them from wells and channels. Along the River Nile, which flows through the desert in Egypt, people have farmed the land for thousands of years. Many traditional desert houses have thick mud-brick walls and small windows, which keep the inside cool during the hot day and warm during the cold night, and flat roofs for sleeping on in hot weather.

Today, many millions of people live in modern desert cities, such as Cairo in Egypt, Riyadh in Saudi Arabia, Dubai in the United Arab Emirates and Phoenix in the United States. They use air conditioning, pump water from deep underground or from far away, and in some places remove the salt from sea water to make fresh water. People work in deserts in mining, for example for copper in the Atacama, diamonds in the Namib and oil in Arabia, and in tourism. Near Upington, large solar power plants use the strong desert sunshine to make electricity.
