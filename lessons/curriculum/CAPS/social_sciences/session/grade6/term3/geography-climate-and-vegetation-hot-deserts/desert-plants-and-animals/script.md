# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the natural vegetation and wildlife of hot deserts. The anchor is a Grade 6 class from Springbok, the largest town in Namaqualand, in the dry north-west of the Northern Cape. The area is a semi-desert, not as dry as the Namib, but dry enough that plants and animals must be very good at saving water. On a field trip to a nature reserve near the town, a ranger leads the class along a rocky path. She shows them fat, juicy-leaved succulents growing between the rocks, and a quiver tree with a smooth, pale trunk and thick branches. She kneels and points to what look like small pebbles, which turn out to be tiny plants called stone plants, hiding from the sun and from hungry animals. Then she shows them the burrows of ground squirrels and the tracks of a gemsbok in the sand. The learners wonder how anything can live in a place with so little water. The ranger says that every desert plant and animal has its own tricks, called adaptations. The big idea is that desert plants and animals are specially adapted to save water and to survive great heat. Desert vegetation and wildlife are examinable.

## Subtopic: How Desert Plants Survive

Desert plants face two big problems: there is very little water, and the sun is very hot. They have three main ways of surviving: storing water, finding water and losing as little water as possible.

Many desert plants store water. Plants with thick, juicy leaves or stems that store water are called succulents. In southern Africa, there are thousands of kinds of succulents, including aloes, the stone plants of Namaqualand and the quiver tree, which stores water in its trunk and branches. In the deserts of North and South America, cacti store water in their thick, swollen stems.

Desert plants find water in different ways. Some, such as many cacti, have shallow roots that spread out wide just under the surface, to soak up as much rain as possible after a shower. Others, such as the camel thorn tree of the Kalahari, have very long roots that reach deep down to water far below the ground.

Desert plants also lose as little water as possible. Plants lose water through their leaves, so many desert plants have very small leaves, or no leaves at all. On cacti, the leaves have become spines, which also protect the plant from animals that want to eat it or drink its water. Many desert plants have a thick, waxy skin that keeps water in. Some, such as the quiver tree, have a pale or white surface that reflects the sun's heat.

Some desert plants survive the dry times as seeds. After a good rain, the seeds sprout, grow, flower and make new seeds within a few weeks, then die. The seeds can wait in the soil for years until the next rain. In good years, this is what makes the famous spring flowers of Namaqualand, when the veld is covered in orange, yellow and white daisies.

The questions for this section are with you now: how desert plants store, find and save water.

## Subtopic: Special Desert Plants

Some desert plants are truly remarkable.

The welwitschia grows only in the Namib Desert, in Namibia and southern Angola. It has just two long, leathery leaves that keep growing from the base for the whole of the plant's life, while their ends become torn and frayed by the wind. Some welwitschias are thought to be many hundreds of years old, and a few may be more than a thousand. It is believed to take in some moisture from the fog that drifts in from the cold sea.

The quiver tree grows in Namaqualand and southern Namibia. It is really a giant aloe. The San people are said to have hollowed out its branches to make quivers to carry their arrows, which gave the tree its name.

At an oasis, a place in the desert where water from underground reaches the surface, date palms grow. Their roots reach the water, and people have grown dates at oases in the Sahara and Arabia for thousands of years.

The questions for this section are with you now: the welwitschia, the quiver tree and the date palm.

## Subtopic: How Desert Animals Survive

Desert animals are also adapted to survive heat and lack of water.

Many desert animals are nocturnal, which means they sleep in cool burrows or in the shade during the hot day and come out at night when it is cooler. Meerkats, ground squirrels, many rodents, snakes and scorpions shelter underground. Some animals get almost all the water they need from the plants, seeds or insects they eat, and hardly ever drink.

The camel of the Sahara and Arabia can go for many days without drinking. Its hump stores fat, not water, which it can use for energy. It has wide feet that do not sink into the sand, long eyelashes, and nostrils it can close to keep out blowing sand. The fennec fox of the Sahara has huge ears that give off body heat and help it to hear prey underground. The gemsbok of the Kalahari and Namib can survive very high temperatures and gets much of its water from plants such as wild melons and roots.

Some birds have clever tricks too. The male sandgrouse flies to a waterhole, soaks its special belly feathers in water and flies back, sometimes many kilometres, so that its chicks can drink from its feathers.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a camel's hump stores water; it stores fat. Two: thinking cacti are native to South Africa; cacti come from the Americas, and our own water-storing plants are mainly other succulents such as aloes. Three: thinking desert animals are active in the midday heat; many are nocturnal. Four: thinking desert plants all have deep roots; some have shallow, wide roots instead.
