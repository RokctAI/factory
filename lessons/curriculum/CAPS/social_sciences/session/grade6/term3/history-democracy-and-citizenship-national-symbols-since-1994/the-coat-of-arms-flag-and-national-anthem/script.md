# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers South Africa's national symbols since 1994: the coat of arms, the national flag and the national anthem. The anchor is a Grade 6 class at a school in Mahikeng, the capital of North West. Every Monday morning, the school holds an assembly in the quad. This term, the Grade 6 class has been chosen to lead it. Two learners carefully raise the national flag up the pole while the rest of the school stands straight and quiet. Then the whole school sings the national anthem, starting softly in isiXhosa and isiZulu, moving into Sesotho, then Afrikaans, and ending loudly in English. On the wall of the school hall behind them hangs a large, colourful coat of arms. After assembly, a Grade 4 learner asks the Grade 6 class why the anthem has so many languages and what the strange words at the bottom of the coat of arms mean. The Grade 6 learners realise they are not sure, so their teacher sets them the task of finding out. The big idea is that national symbols represent a country and its values, and that South Africa's symbols since 1994 were designed to unite a diverse nation. The national symbols are examinable.

## Subtopic: The National Flag

The national flag was first flown on the twenty-seventh of April 1994, the day of the first democratic election. It replaced the old flag that had been used since 1928. The flag was designed by Frederick Brownell, who was the State Herald, the official in charge of designing national symbols. It was chosen in a hurry, only weeks before the election, and was meant to be temporary, but it became so popular that it was kept.

The flag has six colours: chilli red, white, green, gold, black and blue. There is no official meaning for each colour, so people should not say that a particular colour stands for something. The main design is a horizontal Y shape, in which two bands come together from the left and flow into one band to the right. This is usually understood to show different parts of society coming together and moving forward as one, in unity.

There are rules about how to treat the flag with respect. It should not touch the ground, it should not be used as a tablecloth or a covering, and it must be flown with the red band at the top. When the flag is hung vertically, the red band should be on the left as you look at it.

Our flag is one of the few in the world with six colours, and many South Africans wave it proudly at sports events, such as rugby, soccer and cricket matches.

The questions for this section are with you now: the national flag and its design.

## Subtopic: The Coat of Arms

A coat of arms is a design that represents a country, an organisation or a family. South Africa's coat of arms appears on official documents, passports, birth certificates and coins. The new coat of arms was launched on Freedom Day, the twenty-seventh of April 2000, replacing the old one from 1910.

It is built up in layers, like a picture of growth. At the bottom is the motto, written in the /Xam language, once spoken by San people in the Northern Cape and no longer spoken today. The motto means diverse people unite, or unity in diversity. It shows respect for the San, who are among the earliest people of southern Africa.

Above the motto are two crossed elephant tusks, symbols of wisdom, strength and moderation, and ears of wheat, symbols of fertility and growth. In the middle is a shield showing two human figures taken from a famous San rock painting called the Linton Stone. They face each other in greeting, a sign of unity. Above the shield are a spear and a knobkierie, a fighting stick, lying down, which represents peace and authority. Above that is a protea, South Africa's national flower, a symbol of beauty. At the top are a secretary bird with its wings raised, which represents protection and growth, and a rising sun, a symbol of a new beginning and the source of life.

The questions for this section are with you now: the coat of arms and the meaning of its parts.

## Subtopic: The National Anthem

The national anthem joins two songs. The first is Nkosi Sikelel' iAfrika, which means God bless Africa. It was composed in 1897 by Enoch Sontonga, a teacher and choir leader at a mission school in Johannesburg. It became a hymn of hope and was sung at meetings of the liberation movement for decades. The second is Die Stem van Suid-Afrika, meaning the call of South Africa, a poem written by C. J. Langenhoven and set to music, which had been used as the national anthem of the old South Africa.

From 1994, both songs were sung as national anthems. Then, in 1997, a shortened version combining parts of both was made the single national anthem. It uses five of South Africa's official languages: isiXhosa, isiZulu, Sesotho, Afrikaans and English. By joining songs that had belonged to different sides of the country's divided past, the anthem was meant to show reconciliation and unity.

When the anthem is sung, people should stand up straight, face the flag if there is one, stay quiet and still, and men should remove their hats. South Africa also has other national symbols, such as the springbok, the blue crane, the king protea, the real yellowwood tree and the galjoen fish.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking each colour of the flag has an official meaning; it does not. Two: thinking the coat of arms motto is in English; it is in the /Xam San language. Three: thinking the anthem is a single old song; it combines two songs and five languages. Four: thinking the flag was always meant to be permanent; it was first chosen as a temporary flag.
