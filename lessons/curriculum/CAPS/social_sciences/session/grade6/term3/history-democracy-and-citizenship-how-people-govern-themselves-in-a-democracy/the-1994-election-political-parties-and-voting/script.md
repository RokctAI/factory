# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers South Africa's first democratic election in 1994, political parties and how voting works. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Mthatha, in the Eastern Cape, working on an oral history task. Each learner has interviewed an older family member about the election of April 1994. In class, the learners share their stories. One grandmother remembers waking before sunrise, putting on her church clothes and walking to a school, where the queue already stretched far down the road. She waited for most of the day in the heat, but nobody complained, she says, because people were singing, sharing food and helping the old and sick to the front. A grandfather remembers his hands shaking as he made his cross, because he was sixty years old and voting for the first time in his life. Another learner's uncle says it was the proudest day of his life. The learners notice that every story is full of joy. The big idea is that the 1994 election was South Africa's first in which all adults could vote, and that political parties and free and fair voting are at the heart of democracy. ## Subtopic: Political Parties

A political party is an organised group of people who share similar ideas about how a country should be run, and who want to win elections so that they can form the government and put their ideas into practice.

Before an election, each party writes a manifesto, a document that explains what the party will do if it wins, for example about jobs, schools, health, housing or crime. Parties campaign to win support. They hold rallies, put up posters, speak on radio and television, and go from door to door to talk to voters.

In a democracy, people are free to form parties and to support any party they choose. Having several parties gives voters a real choice, and parties that are not in government, called opposition parties, watch the government closely and criticise it when it makes mistakes.

In the 1994 election, nineteen parties appeared on the national ballot paper. The main parties included the African National Congress, or ANC, led by Nelson Mandela; the National Party, led by F. W. de Klerk; the Inkatha Freedom Party, or IFP, led by Mangosuthu Buthelezi; the Freedom Front; the Democratic Party; and the Pan Africanist Congress, or PAC. The IFP decided to take part only about a week before the election, after the ballot papers had already been printed, so a sticker with its name was added to millions of ballot papers.

The questions for this section are with you now: what political parties are and the parties of 1994.

## Subtopic: How People Voted in 1994

The election was organised by the Independent Electoral Commission, or IEC, which was set up to run the election fairly and not to take the side of any party. It was a huge task, because many people had never voted before, and there were no up-to-date lists of voters.

Voting took place over several days. On the twenty-sixth of April 1994, special voting was held for elderly, sick and disabled people and for South Africans living in other countries. The main voting day was the twenty-seventh of April, and voting was extended in some areas because of the long queues and shortages of ballot papers.

To vote, a person had to be a South African citizen aged eighteen or older and show an identity document or a temporary voter's card. Each voter's hand was checked under a special light, and then marked with invisible ink, so that nobody could vote twice. Each voter received two ballot papers: one for the national parliament and one for their provincial government.

Because many people could not read, each party on the ballot paper had its name, its symbol and a photograph of its leader. The voter went behind a screen and made a cross next to the party of their choice, in secret, then folded the papers and put them into sealed ballot boxes.

Almost twenty million people voted. In spite of fears of violence before the election, the voting days were mostly peaceful, and the election was accepted as free and fair. Observers from many countries watched.

## Subtopic: The Results and Elections Today

South Africa uses a system called proportional representation. Voters vote for a party, not a single person. Each party then gets a share of the four hundred seats in the National Assembly that matches its share of the votes. The party chooses which of its members fill those seats from a list.

In 1994, the ANC won about sixty-two per cent of the votes, the National Party about twenty per cent, and the IFP about ten per cent. The Freedom Front, the Democratic Party, the PAC and the African Christian Democratic Party also won seats. The ANC won the most seats and formed the government, with Nelson Mandela as President, in the Government of National Unity.

Since 1994, South Africa has held national and provincial elections every five years, as well as local elections for municipal councils. Today, citizens must register on the voters' roll before they can vote, and the IEC still runs elections. Every vote counts, and the twenty-seventh of April is remembered every year as Freedom Day.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking voters in South Africa vote directly for the president; they vote for a party, and parliament elects the president. Two: thinking the 1994 election was held on one single day everywhere; voting took place over several days. Three: thinking a party with sixty-two per cent of the votes gets all the seats; seats are shared in proportion to votes. Four: thinking the IEC belongs to the governing party; it is independent.
