### subtopic_1 — How Parliament Is Made Up
**Assistant:** Why does South Africa have three capital cities?

**Tutor:** The three capitals go back to 1910, when four separate British colonies joined together to form the Union of South Africa. The leaders of the different colonies could not agree on one capital, so they shared out the important parts of government as a compromise. Pretoria, in the old Transvaal, became the seat of the executive, Cape Town, in the old Cape Colony, became the seat of Parliament, and Bloemfontein, in the old Orange Free State, became the seat of the highest court of appeal. Pietermaritzburg, the capital of the fourth colony, Natal, received some money as compensation. This arrangement has continued in democratic South Africa, although people have sometimes debated whether it would be cheaper to have just one capital.

### subtopic_2 — What Parliament Does
**Assistant:** Can ordinary people watch Parliament at work?

**Tutor:** Yes. Parliament is meant to be open to the public, because members of Parliament represent the people. Many sittings of the National Assembly and the NCOP are broadcast on a special television channel and streamed online, so anyone can watch debates and question sessions. Members of the public may usually also visit Parliament and watch from the public gallery, and schools can arrange tours. People can also follow the work of committees, many of which hold their meetings in public, and send written comments on bills. Being able to see what Parliament does helps citizens hold their representatives accountable, which means making sure they do their jobs properly.

### subtopic_3 — The Constitution: The Highest Law
**Assistant:** Where can I read the Constitution?

**Tutor:** The Constitution is a public document, and every South African has the right to read it. You can find it on the websites of Parliament, the government and the Constitutional Court, and printed copies are often available at libraries. There are short, pocket-sized versions, and it has been translated into all of South Africa's official languages. There are also illustrated versions written for children and young people, which explain the rights in simple words and pictures. Some schools and organisations hold reading clubs or competitions about the Constitution. Reading it, even just the preamble and the Bill of Rights, is a good way to understand your rights and the values on which South Africa's democracy is built.
