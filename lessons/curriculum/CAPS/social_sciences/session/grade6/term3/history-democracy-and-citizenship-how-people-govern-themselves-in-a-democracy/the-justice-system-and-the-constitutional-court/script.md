# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the justice system, equality under the law and the Constitutional Court. The anchor is a Grade 6 class from a school in Ekurhuleni, east of Johannesburg, on an outing to Constitution Hill in Braamfontein, Johannesburg. A guide first takes them through the old prison buildings. She explains that for about a hundred years this was a prison complex, where many people were locked up, some for real crimes, but many for breaking unjust laws such as the pass laws. Mahatma Gandhi and Nelson Mandela were both held here at different times. The learners see the tiny, cold cells and read the words of former prisoners on the walls. Then the guide leads them to a bright, modern building next door: the Constitutional Court. Inside, sunlight falls through high windows onto colourful art. The guide points out that some of the walls were built with bricks from an old prison block that was pulled down. She says this was done on purpose, to show that a place of injustice has become a place of justice. The big idea is that the justice system in a democracy must treat everyone equally and fairly, and that the Constitutional Court protects the Constitution and the rights of every person. The justice system and the Constitutional Court are examinable.

## Subtopic: How the Justice System Works

The justice system is the set of people and organisations that uphold the law, investigate crimes, and settle disputes fairly. It has several parts that work together.

When a crime is reported, the police investigate. They collect evidence, interview witnesses and may arrest a suspect. Next, a prosecutor from the National Prosecuting Authority decides whether there is enough evidence to charge the person with a crime, and if so, presents the case in court on behalf of the state.

The accused person has the right to a lawyer to defend them. If they cannot afford one, Legal Aid South Africa can provide a lawyer for free in many cases. In court, a magistrate or judge listens to the evidence from both sides and decides whether the accused is guilty. If the person is found guilty, the magistrate or judge decides on the sentence, such as a fine, community service or prison.

Courts also deal with civil cases, which are disputes between people or organisations, for example about money owed, damage to property or divorce, rather than crimes.

There are different levels of courts. Magistrates' courts hear most everyday cases. High courts hear more serious cases and appeals from lower courts. The Supreme Court of Appeal, in Bloemfontein, hears appeals from the high courts. At the top is the Constitutional Court in Johannesburg, the highest court in the country.

The questions for this section are with you now: the parts of the justice system and the levels of courts.

## Subtopic: Equality and a Fair Trial

Section 9 of the Bill of Rights says that everyone is equal before the law and has the right to equal protection and benefit of the law. This means that the law must treat every person in the same way, whether they are rich or poor, powerful or ordinary, and whatever their race, gender, religion or language. The same rules apply to a president and to a learner.

The Constitution also gives every accused person the right to a fair trial. An accused person is presumed innocent until proven guilty, which means the state must prove the case, and the person does not have to prove they are innocent. They have the right to be told what they are accused of, to remain silent, to have a lawyer, to have the trial within a reasonable time, and to appeal to a higher court. Courts must be independent and impartial, which means judges must not take sides and must not be told what to decide by the government or anyone else.

Under apartheid, many people did not receive equal treatment. Laws themselves were unequal, and some people were held in prison for long periods without a trial. The Constitution was written to make sure this could not happen again.

The questions for this section are with you now: equality before the law and the right to a fair trial.

## Subtopic: The Constitutional Court

The Constitutional Court was created in 1994 to protect the Constitution. It is the highest court in South Africa. It has eleven judges, including the Chief Justice, who is the head of the judiciary, and the Deputy Chief Justice. In 2024, Mandisa Maya became the first woman to be Chief Justice of South Africa.

The court decides cases about the Constitution and the Bill of Rights. It can declare that a law passed by Parliament, or an action by the President or the government, is unconstitutional and therefore invalid. This is how it protects people's rights and keeps the other branches of government within their limits.

One of its first big cases, in 1995, was about the death penalty. The court decided that putting people to death went against the rights to life and to human dignity, so the death penalty was abolished. In other cases, it has ruled that the government must provide medicine to stop mothers passing HIV to their babies, and that people's rights to housing must be taken seriously.

The court's symbol shows people sitting together under a tree, because in many African communities, disputes were traditionally settled by elders meeting under a tree. Its motto is Justice under a tree.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the police decide whether someone is guilty; the courts decide. Two: thinking an accused person must prove they are innocent; they are presumed innocent until the state proves guilt. Three: thinking judges must do what the President says; courts are independent. Four: thinking Parliament can pass any law it likes; the Constitutional Court can strike down unconstitutional laws.
