### subtopic_1 — How the Justice System Works
**Assistant:** What is it like inside the Constitutional Court building?

**Tutor:** The Constitutional Court building on Constitution Hill was designed to feel open and welcoming, not frightening. It opened in 2004. Light pours in through windows and slanted openings in the roof, which are meant to suggest light coming through the leaves of a tree. The court contains a large collection of South African art, including paintings, tapestries and beadwork. In the courtroom, the judges sit at the same level as the people in the room, rather than high above them, to show that justice belongs to everyone. The walls of the courtroom include bricks from the old awaiting-trial block of the prison, and windows at ground level let people walking outside see feet passing by. Members of the public, including school groups, can visit the court and sometimes watch a hearing.

### subtopic_2 — Equality and a Fair Trial
**Assistant:** Why were Gandhi and Mandela held at the Old Fort prison?

**Tutor:** Mahatma Gandhi lived in South Africa for about twenty years as a young lawyer and activist. In 1908, he was arrested in Johannesburg for leading a campaign of peaceful resistance against a law that forced Indian people in the Transvaal to register and carry passes, and he was held at the Old Fort prison complex. Nelson Mandela was held there in 1962, after he was arrested for leaving the country without a passport and for encouraging workers to strike, before he was later sentenced to life imprisonment in another trial. Many thousands of ordinary people were also held at the prison complex over the years, often for breaking unjust laws. Today, these prisons are a museum.

### subtopic_3 — The Constitutional Court
**Assistant:** What is the difference between a judge and a magistrate?

**Tutor:** Both magistrates and judges are judicial officers who listen to evidence and make decisions in court, and both must be independent and fair. Magistrates work in the magistrates' courts, which are the lower courts found in most towns. They hear most everyday criminal and civil cases, such as theft, assault, traffic offences and smaller money disputes, and there are limits on the sentences they may give. Judges work in the higher courts: the high courts, the Supreme Court of Appeal and the Constitutional Court. They hear more serious cases, such as murder, and appeals from lower courts. Judges are appointed by the President on the advice of a body called the Judicial Service Commission.
