### subtopic_1 — Political Parties
**Assistant:** Why were people so willing to wait all day in the queues?

**Tutor:** For most South Africans, the 1994 election was the first time in their lives that they were allowed to vote for the government of their own country. Under apartheid, black South Africans, the large majority of the population, had been denied this right, and many people had struggled, gone to prison or even died fighting for it. So voting was not just a duty, but a deeply emotional moment of dignity and freedom. Many people later said that they felt like full citizens for the first time. Some elderly people had waited their whole lives for this day. Stories of people sharing food and water, letting the elderly go first and singing in the queues show how proud and hopeful people felt.

### subtopic_2 — How People Voted in 1994
**Assistant:** What does it mean for an election to be free and fair?

**Tutor:** A free election means that all citizens who are allowed to vote can register and vote without fear, threats or bribes, and that parties can campaign freely and share their ideas. A fair election means that the rules are the same for everyone, the voting is secret, the votes are counted honestly and correctly, and the results reflect what the voters actually chose. Independent organisations, such as the IEC, run the elections, and observers from parties and other organisations watch the voting and counting. In 1994, there were problems, such as shortages of ballot papers and some disputes in certain areas, but the IEC and observers accepted that the result reflected the will of the people.

### subtopic_3 — The Results and Elections Today
**Assistant:** How are votes counted today?

**Tutor:** After voting stations close, the ballot boxes are opened in front of party agents and observers. The election officials count the ballot papers by hand, sort them by party, and record the results on official forms, which the party agents can check and sign. Spoilt ballot papers, for example ones where a voter marked more than one party, are not counted for any party. The results from each voting station are captured into the IEC's computer system, checked and added up. The IEC shows the results as they come in at results centres and online. When all the results are in and checked, and any objections have been dealt with, the IEC announces the final results and the number of seats each party has won.
