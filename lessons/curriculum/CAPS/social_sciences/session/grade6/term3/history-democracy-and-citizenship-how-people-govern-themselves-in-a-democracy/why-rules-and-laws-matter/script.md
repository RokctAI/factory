# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the importance of rules and laws. The anchor is a Grade 6 class at a school in Rustenburg, a mining town in North West. At the start of the term, the teacher asks the class to write their own classroom rules. In groups, the learners argue about what is most important and finally agree on five rules, including listen when someone else is speaking, keep the classroom clean, and no bullying of any kind. They also agree on what will happen if someone breaks a rule. The next day, a visitor arrives: the mother of one of the learners, who is a traffic officer. In her uniform, she explains the laws of the road, such as speed limits, wearing seatbelts and stopping at red traffic lights. She tells the class that she once stopped a driver who was speeding past a school and gave him a fine, and that if people ignored traffic laws, the roads would be full of crashes. A learner asks what the difference is between their classroom rules and the laws she enforces. The big idea is that rules and laws help people live together safely and fairly, and that laws in a democracy must themselves be fair. Rules and laws are examinable.

## Subtopic: Rules and Laws

Rules and laws are both instructions about how people should behave, but they are different.

Rules are made for a particular group or place, such as a family, a school, a club or a sports team. Families have rules about bedtime or chores. Schools have rules about uniform and behaviour, and each public school must have a code of conduct, drawn up by the school governing body after consulting learners, parents and teachers. Sports have rules too, such as the offside rule in soccer. Rules are made by the people in charge or are agreed by the members of the group. If you break a rule, the consequence might be detention, losing a privilege or a yellow card, but rules apply only to that group.

Laws are rules made by government that apply to everyone in the country, or in a province or municipality. In South Africa, Parliament makes national laws, provincial legislatures make some laws for their provinces, and municipal councils make by-laws for their towns, for example about noise or littering. Laws are enforced by the police, traffic officers and other officials, and people who break the law can be taken to court. The punishment might be a fine, community service or even prison.

So the classroom rules in Rustenburg apply only to that class, but the traffic laws apply to every driver in South Africa.

The questions for this section are with you now: the difference between rules and laws.

## Subtopic: Why We Need Rules and Laws

Without rules and laws, life would be confusing, unfair and dangerous. Imagine a soccer match where players could pick up the ball, push others over and score however they liked. Nobody would enjoy it. Imagine a road where every driver decided which side to drive on.

Rules and laws matter for several reasons. They keep people safe, for example traffic laws and laws against violence. They protect people's rights and property, for example laws against theft and laws that protect children from abuse. They create order, so that people know what to expect from one another. They help settle disputes peacefully, through courts instead of fighting. And they protect people who are weaker or more vulnerable from those who are stronger.

In a democracy, there is also the important idea of the rule of law. This means that everyone, including the President, ministers, police officers and the rich and powerful, must obey the law, and that nobody is above it. It also means that people can be punished only according to the law, after a fair process, and not just because a powerful person wants to punish them.

The questions for this section are with you now: why societies need rules and laws, and the rule of law.

## Subtopic: Fair and Unfair Laws

A good law should be fair. It should apply equally to everyone, be clear and publicly known, and respect people's rights. In South Africa today, every law must also obey the Constitution.

But laws are not always fair. Under apartheid, the government passed many unjust laws based on race. Pass laws forced black South Africans to carry a document called a pass, and those found without one could be arrested. The Group Areas Act of 1950 decided where people of different race groups were allowed to live, and many families were forced out of their homes. The Bantu Education Act gave black children a poorer education. These laws were legal at the time, because they were passed by Parliament, but they were deeply unfair, because they took away the rights and dignity of most South Africans.

Many people resisted unjust laws. In the Defiance Campaign of 1952, thousands of volunteers deliberately broke apartheid laws, for example by using facilities marked for whites only, and were arrested. Today, the Constitution protects people's rights, and the courts can strike down any law that goes against it.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking rules and laws are the same; rules apply to a group, laws to everyone in a country, province or town. Two: thinking powerful people do not have to obey the law; under the rule of law, nobody is above the law. Three: thinking that because something is a law it must be fair; apartheid laws were legal but unjust. Four: thinking learners have no say in school rules; the code of conduct is drawn up after consulting learners.

The questions for this section are with you now: fair laws, unjust apartheid laws and resistance.
