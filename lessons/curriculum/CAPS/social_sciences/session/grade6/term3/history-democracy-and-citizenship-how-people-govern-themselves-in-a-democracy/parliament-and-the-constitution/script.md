# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the role of Parliament and the purpose of the Constitution. The anchor is a Grade 6 class at a school in George, on the Garden Route in the Western Cape. Their teacher switches on the classroom television to show part of a debate in Parliament. The learners see members of the National Assembly taking turns to speak about a proposed law on school safety. Some speak strongly in favour, others raise objections, and the Speaker, sitting in a high chair, keeps order and tells one member to sit down when time is up. At the end, the members vote. Then the teacher hands round a small, pocket-sized book with a green cover: the Constitution of the Republic of South Africa. She tells the learners that this little book is more powerful than the President, more powerful than Parliament, and more powerful than any law, because every law and every action of the government must obey it. The learners find that hard to believe. The big idea is that Parliament makes the laws of South Africa on behalf of the people, and that the Constitution is the highest law, which protects everyone's rights and limits the power of the government. Parliament and the Constitution are examinable.

## Subtopic: How Parliament Is Made Up

South Africa has three capital cities, each with a different part of government. Pretoria is the seat of the executive, the President and the government departments. Cape Town is the seat of Parliament, which makes the laws. Bloemfontein is the seat of the Supreme Court of Appeal, one of the highest courts.

This division is part of an important idea called the separation of powers. The power of the state is split into three branches, so that no one branch has too much power. The legislature, which is Parliament, makes the laws. The executive, which is the President, the Deputy President and the ministers in the cabinet, carries out the laws and runs the country. The judiciary, which is the courts, decides what the laws mean and settles disputes, and can check that the other two branches obey the Constitution.

Parliament has two houses. The National Assembly has four hundred members, elected by the voters every five years through proportional representation. The National Council of Provinces, or NCOP, has ninety members, ten from each of the nine provinces, so that the provinces have a voice in national law-making.

The National Assembly is led by the Speaker, who keeps order during debates. In the past, the main chamber was in the Parliament buildings in Cape Town, but a fire in January 2022 badly damaged the National Assembly building, and for some time the members have met in other venues while it is being repaired.

The questions for this section are with you now: the separation of powers and the two houses of Parliament.

## Subtopic: What Parliament Does

Parliament has several important jobs.

Its first job is to make laws. A proposed law is called a bill. A bill is usually drawn up by a government department, although members of Parliament can also propose bills. Parliamentary committees, small groups of members, study the bill in detail, and members of the public can send in comments or speak at public hearings. The bill is then debated and voted on in the National Assembly and considered by the NCOP. If both houses pass it, it goes to the President, who signs it. Once signed, it becomes an Act of Parliament, which is a law. If the President thinks the bill goes against the Constitution, he or she can send it back to Parliament or to the Constitutional Court.

Its second job is to elect the President. After each national election, the members of the National Assembly elect one of their members as President.

Its third job is to keep watch over the executive. Ministers must answer questions from members in Parliament, and committees check how government departments spend money and do their work. This is called oversight.

Its fourth job is to approve the national budget, the plan for how the government will raise and spend public money each year.

The questions for this section are with you now: how laws are made and the other jobs of Parliament.

## Subtopic: The Constitution: The Highest Law

A constitution is a set of basic rules that sets out how a country is governed, what powers the government has, and what rights the people have.

South Africa's Constitution was written by the Constitutional Assembly after the 1994 election. President Nelson Mandela signed it at Sharpeville on the tenth of December 1996, and it came into effect in February 1997. It is called the supreme law, or highest law, of the land. This means that any law or action of the government that goes against the Constitution is invalid, and the courts can strike it down.

The Constitution begins with a preamble, an introduction that starts with the words We, the people of South Africa. It recognises the injustices of the past and commits the country to healing those divisions and building a society based on democratic values, social justice and human rights. The founding values include human dignity, equality, freedom, non-racialism and non-sexism, and the rule of law.

Chapter 2 of the Constitution is the Bill of Rights, which lists the rights of everyone in South Africa. The Constitution also recognises official languages. In 2023, South African Sign Language became the twelfth official language. Changing the Constitution is deliberately difficult: most changes need the support of two-thirds of the National Assembly.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the President is above the law; the Constitution is the highest law. Two: thinking Parliament has only one house; it has the National Assembly and the NCOP. Three: thinking a bill is already a law; it becomes a law only when passed and signed. Four: thinking the Constitution is easy to change; most changes need a two-thirds majority.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Constitution and why it is the highest law.
