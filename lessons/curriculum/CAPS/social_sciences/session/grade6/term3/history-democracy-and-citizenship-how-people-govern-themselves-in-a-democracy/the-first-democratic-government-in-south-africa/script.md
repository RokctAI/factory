# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers South Africa's first democratic government, formed after the election of 1994. The anchor is a Grade 6 class from a school in Pretoria, on an outing to the Union Buildings, the home of the offices of the President, on a hill overlooking the city. The learners walk across the wide, sloping lawns and stop in front of a huge bronze statue of Nelson Mandela, about nine metres tall, with his arms spread wide as if to embrace the whole country. Their guide tells them that the statue was unveiled in 2013, soon after Mandela died, and that it stands on the very spot where, in May 1994, a crowd of many thousands gathered to watch him become president. The guide describes the day: world leaders sat on the stage, choirs sang, and air force jets flew overhead, trailing smoke in the colours of the new flag. People cried with joy. A learner asks what the new government actually did after the celebrations ended. The big idea is that South Africa's first democratic government brought together former enemies to govern for all South Africans, and faced the huge task of healing and rebuilding the country. The first democratic government is examinable.

## Subtopic: Mandela Becomes President

In the election of April 1994, the African National Congress, or ANC, won the most votes, about sixty-two per cent. The National Party, which had governed under apartheid, came second, and the Inkatha Freedom Party, or IFP, came third.

The members of the new National Assembly met in Cape Town and, on the ninth of May 1994, elected Nelson Mandela, the leader of the ANC, as President. On the tenth of May 1994, he was sworn in at the Union Buildings in Pretoria. He was South Africa's first democratically elected president and its first black president.

Nelson Mandela had spent twenty-seven years in prison, many of them on Robben Island, for fighting against apartheid. He was released in 1990. Instead of seeking revenge, he called on all South Africans to work together. In his speech at the Union Buildings, he spoke of building a society in which all South Africans, black and white, could walk tall without fear, at peace with themselves and the world. He called the new South Africa a rainbow nation at peace with itself and the world.

The questions for this section are with you now: the 1994 result and Mandela becoming president.

## Subtopic: The Government of National Unity

The rules agreed for the first democratic government, in the interim, or temporary, Constitution of 1993, said that for the first five years, the main parties would share power in a Government of National Unity. Any party that won enough seats in the National Assembly could have ministers in the cabinet, the group of senior ministers who run government departments.

So the first cabinet included ministers from the ANC, the National Party and the IFP. Nelson Mandela was President. There were two Deputy Presidents: Thabo Mbeki of the ANC and F. W. de Klerk of the National Party, the last president under apartheid, who had released Mandela and helped negotiate the end of apartheid. Mangosuthu Buthelezi, the leader of the IFP, became Minister of Home Affairs.

Sharing power was meant to help former enemies learn to work together and to give all groups confidence in the new democracy, after years of violence and fear. It was not always easy. In 1996, the National Party left the Government of National Unity, but the government continued.

The country was also reorganised. The old four provinces and the separate homelands created under apartheid were replaced by nine new provinces, each with its own elected government, and a new national flag and anthem were introduced.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Government of National Unity and how power was shared.

## Subtopic: Healing and Rebuilding

The new government faced enormous challenges. Apartheid had left deep divisions and great inequality. Millions of people lived in poverty, without proper houses, clean water, electricity, good schools or clinics.

The government launched the Reconstruction and Development Programme, or RDP, to meet people's basic needs. Over the following years, more than a million low-cost houses were built, often called RDP houses, and millions of people got clean running water and electricity for the first time. Free health care was introduced for young children and pregnant women at public clinics.

Schools were opened to learners of all races, and the separate education departments for different race groups were joined into one system. Land reform began, to return land to some of the people who had been forced off it under apartheid laws, or to pay them compensation. These changes were slow and difficult, and many problems remained for years to come.

The government also worked for reconciliation, which means bringing people together after conflict. In 1996, the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, or TRC, led by Archbishop Desmond Tutu, began hearings. Victims of human rights abuses under apartheid told their stories, and people who had committed political crimes could ask for amnesty, or freedom from punishment, if they told the full truth.

Sport helped too. In 1995, South Africa hosted and won the Rugby World Cup. Mandela wore a Springbok jersey to hand the trophy to the captain, Francois Pienaar, which many saw as a powerful sign of unity. A new Constitution was signed in 1996. In 1999, after one term, Mandela stepped down, and Thabo Mbeki became President.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking Mandela became president by taking power; he was elected by the National Assembly after a democratic election. Two: thinking only the ANC was in the first government; the ANC, National Party and IFP shared power. Three: thinking all problems were solved in 1994; poverty and inequality were huge challenges. Four: thinking the TRC was a court that sent people to prison; it uncovered the truth and could give amnesty.

The questions for this section are with you now: the RDP, the TRC and building a united nation.
