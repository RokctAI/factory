### subtopic_1 — Where Tropical Rainforests Are
**Assistant:** Why do clothes never dry properly in the rainforest?

**Tutor:** Clothes dry when the water in them evaporates into the air. In a rainforest, the air is already very humid, which means it is already holding almost as much water vapour as it can. Because the air is so full of moisture, water evaporates from wet clothes very slowly. Under the thick trees there is also little wind and little direct sunlight, which would normally help clothes to dry. People who work in rainforests often find that their clothes, shoes and books stay damp, and that mould grows quickly on leather and paper. Scientists at research stations sometimes keep cameras and equipment in sealed boxes with special packets that soak up moisture, to stop them from being damaged by the damp.

### subtopic_2 — A Hot and Wet Climate
**Assistant:** Is the Amazon River the biggest river in the world?

**Tutor:** The Amazon carries more water than any other river in the world, by a very long way. Its basin, the area of land drained by the river and all its tributaries, is also the largest of any river. In length, the Amazon and the Nile in Africa are both about six and a half thousand kilometres long. Geographers have long argued about which is longer, because it depends on where you decide each river begins and ends, so many books say the Nile is the longest and some say the Amazon is. Either way, the Amazon is a giant river, and the huge amount of rain that falls on the rainforest is what keeps it so full.

### subtopic_3 — Reading a Rainforest Climate Graph
**Assistant:** Does it ever stop raining in a rainforest?

**Tutor:** Yes, it does not rain all day every day. Rain usually falls in heavy bursts, often in the afternoon, and there can be sunny mornings and clear evenings. Some rainforests have a few months that are a bit drier than the others, but even then some rain usually falls. In many parts of the Amazon, even the drier months get more rain than Pietermaritzburg gets in its dry winter months. In recent years, scientists have recorded some serious droughts in parts of the Amazon, when rivers dropped very low. They are studying whether cutting down the forest and climate change may be making such droughts more likely, which is something we will learn more about when we study deforestation.
