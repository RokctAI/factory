# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the natural vegetation and wildlife of tropical rainforests. The anchor is a Grade 6 class at a school in East London, on the coast of the Eastern Cape, preparing for an open day. The class has decided to build a model of a tropical rainforest that reaches from the floor to the ceiling of their classroom. They stack cardboard boxes as tree trunks, hang string as climbing vines, and cut hundreds of leaves out of green paper. Groups make animals out of clay and paper for different heights: frogs, snakes and a gorilla near the bottom, monkeys and parrots near the top. As they work, the learners notice that the top of the model is crowded with leaves, while the bottom is dark and shady. Their teacher explains that a real rainforest is just like that, but on a giant scale, with trees as tall as tall buildings. The big idea is that rainforest vegetation grows in layers, as plants compete for sunlight, and that rainforests are home to an enormous variety of animals, each adapted to its own layer. ## Subtopic: The Layers of the Rainforest

Because rainforests are hot and wet all year, plants can grow all year round, and they grow very thickly. Most plants need sunlight, so there is a constant race towards the light. This creates four main layers, like the floors of a building.

The emergent layer is the top layer. It is made up of a few giant trees that grow above all the others, sometimes fifty metres tall or more, about as high as a fifteen-storey building. Their tops stand in full sunlight and strong wind. Large birds of prey, such as eagles, nest and hunt here.

The canopy is the next layer down, about twenty-five to forty metres above the ground. The tops of most of the trees grow so close together that they form a thick roof of leaves, branches, flowers and fruit. The canopy catches most of the sunlight and rain. Most rainforest animals live in the canopy, because there is so much food there.

The understorey lies below the canopy. It is dim and still, because the canopy blocks most of the light. Young trees, shrubs and plants with very large leaves grow here, spreading their leaves wide to catch the little light that gets through. Many young trees wait here for years, until an old tree falls and opens a gap in the canopy.

The forest floor is the bottom layer. It is dark, warm and damp, because only a very small part of the sunlight reaches the ground. Few plants can grow here. Instead, the floor is covered with a thin layer of fallen leaves, fruit and dead wood, with fungi, insects and worms that break it down.

## Subtopic: How Rainforest Plants Are Adapted

Rainforest plants have special features, called adaptations, that help them survive.

Most rainforest trees are evergreen, which means they keep their leaves all year, because there is no cold or dry season. Many leaves are broad, to catch light, and have pointed ends called drip tips, which let the heavy rain run off quickly so that the leaves do not rot or grow mould. Many trees have thin, smooth bark, because they do not need protection from cold or drought.

The soil in a rainforest is surprisingly thin and poor. Dead leaves fall all year and rot very quickly in the heat and damp, and the nutrients are taken up again by plant roots almost at once. So most roots spread out near the surface. Very tall trees often have huge roots that spread out from the bottom of the trunk like walls, called buttress roots, which help hold up the heavy tree in the shallow soil.

Some plants have found clever ways to reach the light. Lianas are woody climbing plants that root in the ground and wind up the tree trunks to the canopy. Epiphytes, such as many orchids and ferns, grow on the branches of trees, high up in the light, and take water from the damp air and rain rather than from the soil.

## Subtopic: Rainforest Wildlife

Tropical rainforests have more kinds of plants and animals than any other natural region on land. Many scientists estimate that around half of all the world's kinds of plants and animals live in tropical rainforests, and many have not yet been named. This huge variety of life is called biodiversity.

Each rainforest has its own animals. In the Amazon, there are jaguars, sloths that hang upside down in the canopy, colourful toucans and macaws, tiny poison dart frogs, anacondas and many kinds of monkeys. In the Congo Basin, there are gorillas, chimpanzees, bonobos, forest elephants, okapi, which are relatives of the giraffe, and African grey parrots. In Southeast Asia, there are orangutans, which live only on the islands of Borneo and Sumatra, as well as tigers, hornbills and flying lizards.

Rainforest animals are adapted to their layer. Monkeys have long arms, and some have tails that can grip branches. Sloths move slowly to save energy. Many frogs and insects are brightly coloured to warn that they are poisonous, while others are camouflaged to hide. Rainforest plants also give people many useful products, including rubber, cocoa, bananas and medicines.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking rainforest soil is rich and deep; it is thin and poor. Two: thinking the forest floor is bright and full of plants; it is dark, with few plants. Three: thinking most animals live on the ground; most live in the canopy. Four: thinking orangutans live in Africa; they live only on Borneo and Sumatra.
