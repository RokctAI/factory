# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers deforestation, its causes and consequences, with a case study of the Amazon rainforest in Brazil. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Knysna, on the Garden Route in the Western Cape, a town surrounded by indigenous forest. The class has just come back from a walk among tall yellowwood and stinkwood trees in the Knysna forest, where a ranger told them that in the eighteen hundreds, huge numbers of these trees were cut down for timber, and that the forest is now protected. Back in the classroom, the teacher shows two satellite photographs of the same part of the Amazon rainforest. The first, from the nineteen-seventies, is solid dark green. The second, from many years later, shows a long road with side roads branching off like the bones of a fish, with brown, cleared land along each one. The learners gasp. They want to know who cut down the forest, and why. The big idea is that rainforests are being cleared for many reasons, mainly to make money from the land, and that this has serious consequences for nature, people and the climate. Deforestation, its causes, its consequences and the case study are examinable.

## Subtopic: What Deforestation Is and Why It Happens

Deforestation is the cutting down or clearing of forests, so that the land can be used for something else. When a forest is cleared and turned into farms, roads or mines, it usually does not grow back.

The Amazon rainforest is the largest tropical rainforest in the world, and most of it lies in Brazil. From the nineteen-seventies onwards, the government of Brazil built long roads into the forest, such as the Trans-Amazonian Highway, and encouraged people to move there and farm. Along the roads, people cleared the forest on both sides, which created the fishbone pattern that can be seen from space, especially in the state of Rondônia.

There are several main reasons for deforestation in the Amazon. The biggest reason is cattle ranching. Large areas of forest are cleared, often by cutting and burning, to make grassland for cattle, and the beef is sold in Brazil and to other countries. The second reason is farming crops, especially soya beans, much of which is exported and used to feed animals. The third reason is logging, which means cutting down valuable hardwood trees, such as mahogany, for timber and furniture. Logging roads then open the forest to farmers. Other reasons include mining for gold, iron ore and other minerals, building dams for hydroelectric power, and building new towns and roads.

In Southeast Asia, large areas of rainforest have also been cleared to plant oil palms, whose oil is used in many foods, soaps and other products.

## Subtopic: The Consequences of Deforestation

Deforestation has many serious consequences.

The first is the loss of habitats and species. When a forest is cleared, the animals and plants that lived there lose their homes. Because so many species live in rainforests, and some live only in a small area, some may disappear completely. When a species dies out for ever, we say it has become extinct.

The second consequence is for indigenous people, the people whose ancestors have lived in the rainforest for thousands of years, such as the Yanomami and many other groups in the Amazon. They depend on the forest for food, medicine and shelter. When outsiders, such as illegal miners or loggers, move onto their land, they can lose their homes and way of life, and there has sometimes been violence and disease.

The third consequence is damage to the soil. Remember that rainforest soil is thin and poor, and that the nutrients are mostly in the plants. When the trees are removed, heavy rain washes the soil away, which is called soil erosion. After a few years, the land may become too poor even for cattle, so farmers clear more forest. The soil washed away also makes rivers muddy.

The fourth consequence is a change in climate. Trees take in water from the soil and release water vapour into the air through their leaves, which helps to make rain. With fewer trees, some areas may become drier. Trees also store carbon. When forests are burned, this carbon goes into the air as carbon dioxide, a gas that traps heat and adds to climate change.

The questions for this section are with you now: the consequences of deforestation for nature, people and the climate.

## Subtopic: Case Study: Saving the Amazon

By many estimates, close to a fifth of the Brazilian Amazon rainforest has already been cleared, an area larger than many countries. But people are working to protect it.

Brazil uses satellites to watch the forest from space and spot new clearing, so that officials can act quickly against illegal cutting. In the years after about 2004, stronger laws, satellite monitoring and more protected areas helped to bring the rate of deforestation down a great deal. Since then, the rate has gone up and down, depending on government policies and how strictly the laws are enforced.

Large parts of the Amazon are now national parks or reserves for indigenous people, where clearing is not allowed. Some timber companies practise sustainable logging, which means cutting only some trees and planting new ones, and their wood can be given a special label. Some farmers practise agroforestry, growing crops such as cocoa among living trees. Around the world, shoppers can help by choosing products with labels showing that they come from well-managed forests.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking deforestation is mainly caused by people collecting firewood; in the Amazon, cattle ranching is the biggest cause. Two: thinking cleared rainforest makes rich farmland for ever; the thin soil soon becomes poor. Three: thinking deforestation affects only animals; it also affects indigenous people, soil, rain and the climate. Four: thinking nothing can be done; satellite monitoring, laws and protected areas have slowed it before.
