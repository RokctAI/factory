# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers where tropical rainforests are found and what their climate is like. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Pietermaritzburg, the capital of KwaZulu-Natal. One learner, Sipho, has a cousin who is a scientist working at a research station in the rainforest of the Congo Basin, in central Africa. For the past week, the cousin has sent voice notes that the teacher plays to the class. Each morning, the cousin says, it is already hot and the air is so damp that clothes never quite dry. By lunchtime, huge clouds build up over the trees. In the afternoon, a thunderstorm pours down for an hour or two, so loud that people have to shout. By evening, the forest drips and steams, full of the sounds of frogs and insects. The next day, the same thing happens again. The class is amazed that the weather can be so similar every day of the year. The teacher puts up a world map and a climate graph from a rainforest station. The big idea is that tropical rainforests are found near the equator, where the climate is hot and wet all year round. The location and climate of tropical rainforests are examinable.

## Subtopic: Where Tropical Rainforests Are

Tropical rainforests are thick, tall forests that grow in hot, wet places near the equator. They are found between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn, and most lie close to the equator itself.

There are three main areas of tropical rainforest in the world. The largest is the Amazon rainforest in South America. It covers the huge basin of the Amazon River, mostly in Brazil, but also in Peru, Colombia and several other countries. The second largest is the rainforest of the Congo Basin in central Africa, which stretches across the Democratic Republic of the Congo, the Republic of the Congo, Gabon, Cameroon and nearby countries. The third main area is in Southeast Asia, on islands such as Borneo, Sumatra and New Guinea, in countries such as Indonesia, Malaysia and Papua New Guinea.

There are also smaller areas of tropical rainforest in Central America, in West Africa, on the east coast of Madagascar and in the north-east of Australia.

South Africa does not have tropical rainforest, because it lies too far south of the equator and much of it is too dry. It does have some small forests, such as the Knysna forests in the southern Cape and coastal forests in KwaZulu-Natal, but these are different kinds of forest.

Although tropical rainforests cover only a small part of the Earth's land, less than a tenth, they are home to a very large share of the world's kinds of plants and animals. We will learn more about these in the next lesson.

The questions for this section are with you now: where the world's tropical rainforests are found.

## Subtopic: A Hot and Wet Climate

Tropical rainforests have a hot, wet climate all year round. There are no real summer and winter seasons as we know them in South Africa.

Rainforests are hot because they are near the equator, where the sun's rays strike the Earth almost straight on all year. The average temperature in most months is about twenty-five to twenty-eight degrees Celsius. The difference between the hottest month and the coolest month, called the annual temperature range, is very small, often only one to three degrees. In fact, the temperature changes more between day and night than between the months of the year.

Rainforests are also very wet. Most get more than two thousand millimetres of rain a year, and some get much more. Rain falls in every month, although some months are wetter than others. The air is very humid, which means it holds a lot of water vapour, so it feels sticky and damp.

The daily pattern of weather is often the same. In the morning, the sun heats the ground and the forest, and water evaporates from the wet leaves, soil and rivers. The warm, moist air rises, cools and forms tall thunderclouds. In the afternoon, heavy rain falls, often with thunder and lightning. This kind of rain, caused by rising warm air, is called convectional rain. By evening, the sky may clear, and the cycle starts again the next day.

The questions for this section are with you now: the hot, wet climate of tropical rainforests and the daily pattern of rain.

## Subtopic: Reading a Rainforest Climate Graph

A climate graph shows the average temperature and rainfall of a place for each month of the year. The months are written along the bottom, usually as letters from J for January to D for December. The rainfall is shown as bars, read against the scale in millimetres on one side. The temperature is shown as a line, read against the scale in degrees Celsius on the other side.

On a climate graph for a rainforest station, you will notice two things. First, the temperature line is almost flat, staying close to twenty-six or twenty-seven degrees all year. Second, the rainfall bars are tall in every month, often between one hundred and fifty and three hundred millimetres, with no truly dry month.

Compare this with a climate graph for Pietermaritzburg. There, the temperature line rises in summer and falls in winter, with a range of about eight to ten degrees, and the rainfall bars are tall in summer but very short in winter, when there is little rain.

To describe a climate graph, give the hottest and coolest months and temperatures, work out the range, name the wettest and driest months, and add up or read off the total annual rainfall.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking rainforests have cold winters; they are hot all year. Two: thinking South Africa has large tropical rainforests; it is too far south and too dry. Three: reading the temperature line against the rainfall scale; each has its own scale. Four: thinking rainforests get rain only in one season; rain falls in every month.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading and describing a rainforest climate graph.
