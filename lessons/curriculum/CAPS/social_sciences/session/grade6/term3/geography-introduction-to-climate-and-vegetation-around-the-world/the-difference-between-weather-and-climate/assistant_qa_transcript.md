### subtopic_1 — What Weather Is
**Assistant:** Is climate change the same as a change in the weather?

**Tutor:** No. The weather changes all the time, from hour to hour and day to day, and that is normal. Climate change means a long-term change in the usual pattern of weather over many years, across large parts of the world. Scientists have measured that the average temperature of the Earth has risen over the past hundred years or so, and most scientists agree that this is mainly caused by people burning coal, oil and gas, which adds gases to the air that trap heat. Climate change can bring more heatwaves, changes in rainfall and stronger storms in some places. Because climate is about long-term patterns, one cold day or one hot summer on its own does not prove or disprove climate change. Scientists look at records over many decades.

### subtopic_2 — What Climate Is
**Assistant:** Why is a thermometer kept inside a white box?

**Tutor:** A thermometer in direct sunlight would heat up and show a temperature higher than the real temperature of the air. So weather stations keep thermometers in a special box, often called a Stevenson screen. It is painted white to reflect sunlight, has slatted sides like a louvre so that air can move freely through it, and stands on legs above the ground, at about the same height as a person's chest. This way, the thermometer measures the temperature of the air in the shade, and readings from different weather stations around the world can be compared fairly, because they are all measured in the same way.

### subtopic_3 — Why Climate Matters
**Assistant:** How does the Weather Service predict tomorrow's weather?

**Tutor:** The South African Weather Service collects readings from many weather stations across the country, from weather balloons that measure the air high above the ground, from radar that shows where rain is falling, and from satellites that photograph clouds from space. All these measurements are fed into powerful computers that use mathematics and the laws of science to work out how the air is likely to move and change over the next few days. Forecasters then study the results and use their experience to write the forecast. Forecasts for tomorrow are usually quite accurate, but the further ahead they look, the less certain they become, because the atmosphere is always changing.
