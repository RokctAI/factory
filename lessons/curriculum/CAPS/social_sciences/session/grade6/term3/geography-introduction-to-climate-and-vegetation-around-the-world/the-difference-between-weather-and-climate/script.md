# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the Term 3 Geography topic, climate and vegetation around the world, and explains the difference between weather and climate. The anchor is a Grade 6 class at a school in Upington, in the Northern Cape, close to the Orange River and the edge of the Kalahari. The class has a small weather station in a corner of the playground: a thermometer inside a white wooden box, a rain gauge on a pole and a wind vane on the roof of the storeroom. Every morning for a week, two learners take readings and write them on a chart in the classroom. On Monday it is thirty-five degrees and sunny. On Tuesday a strong wind blows dust across the playground. On Wednesday afternoon, a thunderstorm drops eleven millimetres of rain, and everyone is surprised. A learner asks whether Upington now has a wet climate. The teacher smiles and says that one rainy day tells us about the weather, but not about the climate. ## Subtopic: What Weather Is

In Grade 5, you learned that weather is the condition of the air, or atmosphere, at a particular place and time. Weather can change from hour to hour and from day to day. A sunny morning can become a stormy afternoon.

We describe weather using its elements. The main elements are temperature, which tells us how hot or cold the air is, rainfall or precipitation, which includes rain, hail and snow, wind, which has a direction and a speed, cloud cover, and humidity, which is the amount of water vapour in the air.

We measure weather with instruments. A thermometer measures temperature in degrees Celsius. A rain gauge collects rain so that we can measure how much fell, in millimetres. A wind vane shows which direction the wind is blowing from, and a wind sock or an instrument called an anemometer shows how strong it is. Weather stations, like the one at the Upington school, record these readings every day.

Weather forecasters, such as those at the South African Weather Service, use readings from many weather stations, satellites and computers to predict the weather for the next few days. Farmers, pilots, fishermen and people planning a wedding all need to know the weather.

The questions for this section are with you now: the elements of weather and how we measure them.

## Subtopic: What Climate Is

Climate is the average or usual pattern of weather in a place over a long time, usually about thirty years or more. To find the climate of a place, scientists collect weather readings every day for many years and then work out averages for each month.

Climate tells us what to expect. Upington has a hot, dry climate, with very hot summers, cool to cold winter nights and little rain, mostly falling in summer and autumn thunderstorms. One rainy Wednesday does not change that pattern.

Different parts of South Africa have different climates. Cape Town has a winter rainfall climate, with cool, wet winters and warm, dry summers. Gauteng has a summer rainfall climate, with afternoon thunderstorms in summer and dry, sunny winters with cold nights. Durban, on the warm east coast, is warm and humid for most of the year.

When we describe a climate, we usually talk about two things. The first is temperature: how hot the summers are, how cold the winters are, and the difference between them, which is called the temperature range. The second is rainfall: how much rain falls in a whole year, called the annual rainfall, and in which season most of it falls. Upington, for example, gets less than two hundred millimetres of rain in an average year, while parts of the Drakensberg get well over one thousand millimetres.

Climate is often shown on a climate graph. The monthly average rainfall is shown as bars, and the monthly average temperature is shown as a line. By reading a climate graph, we can see which months are hottest, coldest, wettest and driest in a place. We will use climate graphs all term.

The questions for this section are with you now: what climate is and how it differs between places in South Africa.

## Subtopic: Why Climate Matters

Weather and climate are easy to mix up. A good way to remember the difference is this: weather is what you get today, and climate is what you expect over many years.

Climate affects almost everything about a place. It decides which natural vegetation grows there, such as forests, grasslands or desert plants, and so it decides which animals can live there. Climate affects which crops farmers can grow, what kind of houses people build, what clothes they wear and even which sports and holidays are popular. Around Upington, farmers use water from the Orange River to irrigate grapes, because there is too little rain for them to grow on rainfall alone.

This term, we will travel around the world to study the hot, mild and cold climates and the wet and dry areas of the world, and then three very different natural regions: tropical rainforests, hot deserts and coniferous forests. For each one, we will look at where it is, its climate, its plants and animals, and how people live there.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking weather and climate mean the same thing; weather is day to day, climate is the pattern over many years. Two: thinking one rainy day gives a place a wet climate; climate uses about thirty years of records. Three: thinking a rain gauge measures wind; it measures rainfall in millimetres. Four: thinking the whole of South Africa has the same climate; Cape Town, Gauteng and Upington are very different.
