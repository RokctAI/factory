# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the rights and responsibilities of citizens in a democracy, with a case study of Pius Langa. The anchor is a Grade 6 class at a school in Durban, in KwaZulu-Natal, in the week before Human Rights Day. Their teacher has divided a long classroom wall into two columns. On the left, learners stick cards with rights from the Bill of Rights: the right to equality, the right to dignity, the right to education, the right to a safe environment, freedom of expression. On the right, they must stick a matching responsibility for each one. At first, the learners find the rights column easy and the responsibilities column hard. After some discussion, they write treat everyone equally, respect other people, come to school and work hard, do not litter, and do not spread hurtful lies about people. Then the teacher tells them about a man from Durban who spent his life defending people's rights: Pius Langa, who rose from working in a factory to become the Chief Justice of South Africa. The big idea is that everyone in South Africa has rights protected by the Constitution, that rights come with responsibilities, and that people like Pius Langa worked hard to protect them. Rights, responsibilities and the case study are examinable.

## Subtopic: Our Rights

A right is something that every person is entitled to, simply because they are human. In South Africa, our rights are written in the Bill of Rights, which is Chapter 2 of the Constitution. The Bill of Rights protects everyone in South Africa, not only citizens, although some rights, such as the right to vote, belong only to citizens.

Some of the most important rights are the right to equality, which means that nobody may be treated unfairly because of their race, gender, religion, disability, language or other reasons; the right to human dignity, which means that every person must be treated with respect; and the right to life.

Other rights include freedom of religion, belief and opinion; freedom of expression, which includes freedom of the press; the right to gather and protest peacefully; the right to vote and to form or join political parties; the right to education; the right to use your own language and take part in your own culture; and the right to an environment that is not harmful to your health. There are also rights to have access to housing, health care, food, water and social security, which the government must work towards providing within the resources it has. Children have their own special rights, which we will study in the next lesson.

The questions for this section are with you now: what rights are and the rights in the Bill of Rights.

## Subtopic: Rights and Responsibilities

Rights are not unlimited. Your rights must not be used to harm the rights of others. For example, freedom of expression does not protect hate speech that encourages harm against people because of their race, religion or gender. The Constitution allows rights to be limited in some cases, if the limit is reasonable and fair in an open and democratic society.

Every right comes with responsibilities, which are duties we have towards others. If you have the right to equality, you have the responsibility to treat others equally and not to discriminate. If you have the right to dignity, you must respect the dignity of others, which means no bullying or insulting people. If you have the right to education, you have the responsibility to attend school, work hard and not disrupt other learners. If you have the right to a healthy environment, you must not litter or pollute. If you have freedom of expression, you must not spread hurtful lies or hate.

When people take their responsibilities seriously, everyone's rights are better protected. When people ignore them, the rights of others suffer. In a democracy, citizens also have responsibilities to the country, such as obeying the law, paying taxes and voting.

The questions for this section are with you now: why rights have limits and how each right comes with responsibilities.

## Subtopic: Case Study: Pius Langa

Pius Langa was born in 1939 in Bushbuckridge, in what is now Mpumalanga, and later lived and worked in KwaZulu-Natal. His family did not have much money, and he left school early. As a young man, he worked in a factory, and he completed his schooling by studying privately.

He then worked for many years in the Department of Justice, starting as an interpreter and messenger in the courts, which was one of the few court jobs open to black South Africans at the time. While working, he studied law part-time through the University of South Africa. It took him many years, but he earned his law degrees.

In 1977, he became an advocate, a lawyer who argues cases in the higher courts, and he worked in Durban. During apartheid, he defended many people, including activists who were fighting against apartheid and ordinary people treated unfairly under unjust laws. He helped to lead an organisation of lawyers who worked for democracy and human rights.

In 1994, President Mandela appointed him as one of the first judges of the new Constitutional Court. He later became its deputy head, and from 2005 to 2009, he was the Chief Justice of South Africa, the head of the judiciary. He believed that the Constitution should not just be words on paper, but should change people's daily lives, for example by helping them to get housing, education and fair treatment. He died in 2013.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking rights are unlimited; they must not harm the rights of others. Two: thinking the Bill of Rights protects only citizens; it protects everyone in South Africa, though some rights are for citizens. Three: thinking rights come without responsibilities; every right has matching duties. Four: thinking Pius Langa had an easy path; he left school early, worked and studied part-time for years.

The questions for this section are with you now: the life and work of Pius Langa.

Every right comes with a responsibility, and rights must not harm others.
