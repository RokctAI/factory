### subtopic_1 — Step by Step down Africa
**Assistant:** Why did so many ships sink off the South African coast?

**Tutor:** The South African coast is one of the most dangerous coastlines in the world for ships. Strong winds blow for much of the year, especially in winter, when powerful storms come in from the Southern Ocean. Along the east and south coasts, the fast Agulhas Current flows south-west, and when strong winds blow against it, they can build up enormous waves, sometimes called freak waves, that can break a ship. The coast has many rocky reefs and headlands, and fog is common on the west coast. Before lighthouses, accurate charts and modern weather forecasts, sailors often did not know exactly where they were. Thousands of shipwrecks lie along the coast, from early Portuguese ships to modern vessels. Lighthouses, such as the ones at Cape Point and Cape Agulhas, were built to warn ships away from danger.

### subtopic_2 — Rounding the Cape
**Assistant:** How did sailors find water and food on the African coast?

**Tutor:** Ships carried barrels of water and stores of food, but on voyages lasting many months, these ran low or went bad. So sailors looked for safe bays with fresh water, such as springs, streams or rivers near the shore. Mossel Bay, for example, had a freshwater spring close to the beach. They also needed fresh meat and, if possible, fruit and vegetables. They caught fish and sometimes hunted, and they tried to trade with the people living near the coast. In southern Africa, the Khoikhoi herders had large herds of cattle and sheep, so sailors offered goods such as iron, copper, beads and cloth in exchange for animals. These exchanges were sometimes peaceful, but misunderstandings and conflict were common, especially when sailors took water or animals without permission. ### subtopic_3 — The Route to the East
**Assistant:** How did the Suez Canal change the route?

**Tutor:** The Suez Canal is a long, man-made waterway in Egypt that connects the Mediterranean Sea with the Red Sea, which leads to the Indian Ocean. Before it opened in 1869, ships travelling between Europe and Asia had to sail all the way around Africa, past the Cape. With the canal, ships could take a much shorter route through the Mediterranean, the canal and the Red Sea, saving thousands of kilometres and many weeks of sailing. After 1869, fewer ships between Europe and Asia needed to stop at the Cape. However, the route around the Cape is still used today, especially by very large ships that are too big for the canal, and whenever the canal is blocked or the area is unsafe. So the waters around southern Africa remain an important shipping route.
