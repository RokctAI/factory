# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers Vasco da Gama's voyage to India and the Dutch East India Company's refreshment station at the Cape. The anchor is a Grade 6 class from Cape Town on an outing to the Company's Garden, the long green park in the middle of the city, near Parliament and the museums. The learners walk under tall old trees. Their teacher stops them at a small vegetable patch and asks why a park in the centre of Cape Town is called the Company's Garden. Nobody knows. She explains that the garden began more than three hundred and seventy years ago as a vegetable garden, planted to feed the sailors of a Dutch trading company whose ships sailed past the Cape to the East. To understand why ships came past the Cape at all, she says, we must go back to a Portuguese captain called Vasco da Gama. The big idea is that da Gama opened the sea route to the East around southern Africa, and that later the Dutch built a station at the Cape to serve that route, which changed the lives of the Khoikhoi. The voyage of da Gama and the Dutch station at the Cape are examinable.

## Subtopic: Vasco da Gama Sails to India

After Bartolomeu Dias sailed around the southern tip of Africa in 1488, the King of Portugal knew that a sea route to the East was possible. It took nearly ten years to prepare the next voyage. Dias helped to design the ships, and the king chose Vasco da Gama, a nobleman and an experienced sailor, to lead it.

Da Gama left Lisbon in July 1497 with four ships and roughly one hundred and seventy men. Two of the ships, the São Gabriel and the São Rafael, were bigger, square-rigged ships, built to carry more cargo and stores on a long ocean voyage. There was also a caravel and a supply ship full of food and stores.

Instead of hugging the African coast as Dias had done, da Gama sailed in a great curve far out into the Atlantic Ocean to use the ocean winds. His ships were out of sight of land for about three months, much longer than any earlier European voyage. In November 1497 they reached land at St Helena Bay, north of where Cape Town is today. Here the sailors met Khoikhoi people. At first they exchanged small items, but the meeting turned into a fight, and da Gama was wounded in the leg.

The ships then rounded the Cape of Good Hope and anchored at Mossel Bay, which Dias had visited nine years before. This time, the meeting with the Khoikhoi began peacefully. According to a journal kept by one of the sailors, the Khoikhoi played music on flutes and danced, the Portuguese played trumpets and danced too, and the sailors bought an ox in exchange for bracelets. Later, the mood changed. The Portuguese put up a padrão and a cross, and as they sailed away, they saw Khoikhoi people knocking it down.

The questions for this section are with you now: the start of da Gama's voyage and his meetings with the Khoikhoi.

## Subtopic: Along the East Coast to Calicut

Da Gama sailed on up the east coast of southern Africa. On Christmas Day 1497, the ships were passing a green coast, and da Gama named it Natal, from the Portuguese word for Christmas. Today the province of KwaZulu-Natal still carries that name.

Further north, da Gama reached the busy trading towns of the East African coast. Remember from Term 1 that Swahili towns such as Kilwa, Mombasa and Malindi had traded across the Indian Ocean for hundreds of years. At Mozambique Island and at Mombasa, the Muslim rulers and merchants were suspicious of the Portuguese strangers, and there was conflict. At Malindi, the ruler was friendly, because he was a rival of the ruler of Mombasa. He gave da Gama an experienced pilot who knew the monsoon winds of the Indian Ocean. With the pilot's help, the ships crossed the ocean in less than a month.

In May 1498, da Gama reached Calicut, a rich spice port on the west coast of India. This was the first time a European fleet had sailed all the way from Europe to India by sea. The ruler of Calicut was not impressed by da Gama's simple gifts, such as cloth, hats and honey, and the Muslim merchants who controlled the spice trade did not want competition. Still, da Gama managed to load pepper, cinnamon and other spices.

The journey home was terrible. Crossing the Indian Ocean against the winds took about three months, and many sailors died of scurvy, a disease caused by a lack of fresh fruit and vegetables. Da Gama returned to Lisbon in 1499 with only two ships and fewer than half of his men. Yet the spices he brought back were worth many times the cost of the voyage, and Portugal now controlled the sea route to the East. For about a hundred years, Portuguese ships used this route and built forts and trading posts along it.

The questions for this section are with you now: da Gama's route along the east coast to Calicut and the results of his voyage.

## Subtopic: The Dutch at the Cape

Around 1600, the Dutch and English began to challenge Portugal for the spice trade. In 1602, Dutch merchants formed the Dutch East India Company, known by its Dutch initials as the VOC. It was a trading company, not a government, but it was allowed to build forts, keep soldiers and make treaties in Asia. It became one of the richest companies in the world, trading spices from the islands that are now Indonesia.

VOC ships sailing between the Netherlands and Asia took many months, and sailors suffered terribly from scurvy. Ships often stopped at Table Bay to take on fresh water and to trade with the Khoikhoi for cattle and sheep. In 1647, a Dutch ship called the Haarlem was wrecked in Table Bay. Its crew lived at the Cape for about a year, grew vegetables and traded with the Khoikhoi. When they returned home, they reported that a station at the Cape would be useful.

So in 1652, the VOC sent Jan van Riebeeck with three ships to Table Bay. He arrived in April 1652. His task was not to start a colony but to set up a refreshment station: to build a small fort, plant a vegetable garden, and trade with the Khoikhoi for meat, so that passing ships could get fresh food and water. The Company's Garden in Cape Town is what remains of that first garden.

For the Khoikhoi, this was a turning point. At first they traded cattle for copper, beads and tobacco. A young Khoikhoi woman called Krotoa worked in van Riebeeck's household and became an important interpreter. But from 1657, the VOC gave land along the Liesbeek River to some of its men to farm. These farms took over grazing land and water that the Khoikhoi had used for generations, and war broke out in 1659. Over time, the Khoikhoi lost much of their land and cattle.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking da Gama was the first European to sail around the Cape; Dias did it first, in 1488. Two: thinking da Gama discovered India; Indian, Arab and African traders already knew the sea routes well. Three: thinking the VOC was a country or a government; it was a trading company. Four: thinking van Riebeeck came to start a big colony; he came to set up a refreshment station.

The questions for this section are with you now: the VOC, van Riebeeck's station and its effect on the Khoikhoi.
