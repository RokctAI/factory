# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the European trade route to the East via southern Africa: how Portuguese sailors found it, the dangers and importance of the Cape, and how the route worked. The anchor is a Grade 6 class from Simon's Town, on the False Bay coast of the Cape Peninsula. They visit Cape Point, in the Table Mountain National Park, and climb up to the old lighthouse. Far below, huge waves smash against the cliffs, and the wind is so strong that the learners must hold onto the railings. Their teacher points out to sea and tells them that, more than five hundred years ago, small wooden ships from Portugal struggled through storms like this, trying to find a way around Africa to India. She asks: if you were a sailor on one of those ships, how would you have felt when you finally saw the coast turning north-east? The big idea is that the Portuguese found a sea route from Europe to the East around southern Africa, step by step, and that this route changed trade, and the history of southern Africa, for centuries. The trade route and its importance are examinable.

## Subtopic: Step by Step down Africa

The Portuguese did not reach the southern tip of Africa in one voyage. It took them more than fifty years, from the 1430s to 1488, sailing a little further south each time.

In 1434, Gil Eanes sailed past Cape Bojador, which had frightened sailors. Over the following decades, Portuguese ships reached the coasts of what are now Senegal, Sierra Leone and Ghana. In the 1470s, they crossed the equator, and found that the sea did not boil. In the early 1480s, a captain called Diogo Cão sailed further south, reaching the mouth of the Congo River and later the coast of what is now Namibia. In about 1486, he placed a padrão at a place still called Cape Cross, on the Namibian coast.

Each voyage brought back new knowledge: maps of the coast, information about winds and currents, safe harbours, places to find water, and contact with the people who lived there. Each captain built on the work of the ones before.

Sailing south along the west coast of southern Africa was hard. The coast of Namibia is mostly desert, with little fresh water, and a cold ocean current, called the Benguela Current, flows northwards along it, against ships sailing south. Winds also often blew from the south. So Portuguese sailors learnt to swing far out into the Atlantic Ocean, away from the coast, to find better winds, and then turn back towards Africa further south.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the Portuguese explored the west coast of Africa step by step.

## Subtopic: Rounding the Cape

In 1487, Bartolomeu Dias left Lisbon with two caravels and a supply ship. Early in 1488, somewhere off the west coast of southern Africa, a powerful storm blew his ships far south for many days, out of sight of land. When the storm ended, Dias sailed east, looking for the coast, but found nothing. Then he turned north and reached land, and found that the coast now ran from west to east. Without seeing it, he had already passed the southern tip of Africa.

Dias sailed on along the south coast, landing at Mossel Bay and going as far as the area near the mouth of the Bushmans River and Kwaaihoek. His tired crew refused to go further, and he turned back. On the way home, he saw the great cape near the end of the Cape Peninsula. According to an old Portuguese account, Dias called it the Cape of Storms, but the King of Portugal renamed it the Cape of Good Hope, because it gave hope that a sea route to India had been found.

The seas around the Cape are some of the stormiest in the world. Strong winds blow for much of the year, and the warm Agulhas Current, flowing south along the east coast, meets the cold waters of the Atlantic and the strong winds, creating huge waves. Over the centuries, many ships have been wrecked along the coast of southern Africa.

The questions for this section are with you now: Dias's voyage and the naming of the Cape of Good Hope.

## Subtopic: The Route to the East

After Dias, the whole sea route to the East became clear. Ships left Lisbon, sailed south past the Canary and Cape Verde islands, swung far out into the Atlantic, came back towards southern Africa, rounded the Cape, sailed north-east along the east coast of Africa to the Swahili towns such as Mozambique Island, Mombasa and Malindi, and then crossed the Indian Ocean with the monsoon winds to India. A voyage from Lisbon to India took about six months or more.

In 1497 and 1498, Vasco da Gama followed this route all the way to India, which we study in a later lesson.

The Cape became very important to this route. It was roughly halfway between Europe and India. Ships stopped at bays along the southern African coast to find fresh water, to get fresh meat by trading with the Khoikhoi, to repair damage and to wait for good winds. This brought European sailors into contact, and often conflict, with the Khoikhoi.

The route around the Cape remained the main sea route between Europe and Asia for nearly four hundred years, until the Suez Canal in Egypt opened in 1869 and gave ships a much shorter way.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the Portuguese reached the Cape in one voyage; it took more than fifty years of step-by-step voyages. Two: thinking Dias saw the tip of Africa on his way out; a storm blew him past it out of sight of land. Three: thinking Dias reached India; he turned back in the Eastern Cape. Four: thinking the Cape of Good Hope is the most southern point of Africa; that is Cape Agulhas.
