# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers what happened when Bartolomeu Dias and his crew met the Khoikhoi at Mossel Bay in 1488, and how historians look at the event from different points of view. The anchor is a Grade 6 class from George, in the Western Cape, on a visit to the Dias Museum in Mossel Bay. In a large hall stands a full-size copy of a caravel, built in Portugal and sailed to Mossel Bay in the late 1980s, to mark five hundred years since Dias's voyage. The learners climb on board and are amazed at how small it is for a crew sailing so far. Outside, the guide shows them a freshwater spring, still flowing near the shore, and an old milkwood tree. She explains that this spring was the reason Dias came ashore, and that what happened here in 1488 was the first recorded meeting between Europeans and the people of southern Africa. The big idea is that this first meeting was a clash between strangers who did not understand each other, and that historians must look at it from both sides. The events at Mossel Bay and the different points of view are examinable.

## Subtopic: The Khoikhoi at Mossel Bay

The Khoikhoi were herders who had lived in southern Africa for a long time, probably arriving in the area about two thousand years ago. They kept large herds of fat-tailed sheep and cattle, and their wealth was counted in animals. They lived in groups led by chiefs, and moved with the seasons to find good grazing and water for their animals, carrying their portable mat houses with them.

The land around Mossel Bay, with its grass and freshwater springs near the coast, was good grazing land. In summer, the Khoikhoi brought their herds to the coast to graze. Water was precious, especially in the dry season, and springs were very important to the people and their animals. The Khoikhoi did not think of land and water as something to be bought and owned by one person, but they did expect strangers to ask permission and to respect who used a place.

The Khoikhoi had probably never seen ships like the Portuguese caravels or people like the Portuguese sailors, with their pale skins, strange clothes, metal weapons and a language nobody could understand. We do not have written records from the Khoikhoi themselves, so we must imagine their point of view from what we know about their way of life.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Khoikhoi way of life and why the Mossel Bay area was important to them.

## Subtopic: What Happened in 1488

After the storm that carried his ships past the southern tip of Africa, Dias sailed north until he saw land. On the third of February 1488, his ships anchored in a bay. Because it was the feast day of a Christian saint, Saint Blaise, the Portuguese called it the watering place of Saint Blaise, in Portuguese Aguada de São Brás. Today it is called Mossel Bay.

The sailors had been at sea for months and badly needed fresh water. They saw Khoikhoi herders with many cattle near the shore. When the Portuguese rowed in and landed, the herders were alarmed and moved their animals away, inland.

The Portuguese went to the spring near the beach and began to fill their water barrels. The Khoikhoi gathered on a hill above the spring and threw stones to drive the strangers away. According to Portuguese accounts, Dias then fired a crossbow at them and killed one of the Khoikhoi men. After this, the Khoikhoi moved away, and the Portuguese finished taking water and sailed on.

This was the first recorded meeting between Europeans and people of southern Africa, and it ended in the first recorded killing. It was a sign of the conflict over land, water and cattle that would follow in later centuries.

Dias sailed further east, placed a padrão near Kwaaihoek, and then turned back because his crew refused to go further.

The questions for this section are with you now: the events at Mossel Bay in February 1488.

## Subtopic: Two Points of View

Historians must think about whose point of view a source shows. Almost all the written sources about this event were written by the Portuguese. So we must ask how the Khoikhoi might have seen it.

From the Portuguese point of view, they were tired, thirsty sailors who needed water, which seemed free for anyone to take. When stones were thrown at them, they felt attacked and defended themselves with weapons.

From the Khoikhoi point of view, strangers arrived in huge floating houses, without warning or permission, and began taking water from a spring that their people and animals depended on, in the dry season. The strangers did not speak their language, follow their customs or offer anything in return. Throwing stones was a way to protect their water and their cattle and to drive the strangers off. Then one of their people was killed by a strange weapon.

Neither side understood the other. There was no shared language, no trust and no agreement about who could use the water. This is a common pattern in history when very different peoples first meet.

Today, the Dias Museum Complex in Mossel Bay remembers the arrival of the Portuguese, and many people believe it should also tell the story of the Khoikhoi who lived there first.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the Khoikhoi attacked for no reason; they were protecting their water and cattle. Two: thinking the land was empty when Dias arrived; Khoikhoi herders were living there. Three: thinking we have Khoikhoi written records of this event; the written sources are Portuguese. Four: thinking Dias named the bay Mossel Bay; he called it the watering place of Saint Blaise.
