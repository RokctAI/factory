# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers two more reasons why Europeans explored: spreading the Christian religion, and crossing the sea to find a route to the East. The anchor is a Grade 6 class from Kenton-on-Sea, a small coastal town in the Eastern Cape, between the Bushmans and Kariega Rivers. On a windy morning, they walk along the beach and over the dunes to a rocky headland called Kwaaihoek. On top stands a stone cross on a tall pillar. A sign explains that this is a copy of a padrão, a stone pillar with a cross, that Bartolomeu Dias placed near here in 1488, on his voyage around southern Africa. Broken pieces of the original were found in the twentieth century, and the restored original is now kept at a university in Johannesburg. The teacher asks the class: why would sailors on a dangerous voyage carry heavy stone crosses all the way from Portugal? The big idea is that religion and the search for a sea route to the East were, together with curiosity and profit, important reasons for European exploration. These reasons are examinable.

## Subtopic: Spreading Christianity

In the 1400s, most people in Portugal and Spain were Christians, belonging to the Roman Catholic Church, led by the Pope in Rome. Religion was a very important part of life, and many people believed it was their duty to spread their faith to others.

Portugal and Spain had a long history of conflict with Muslim kingdoms. For centuries, large parts of Spain and Portugal had been ruled by Muslim rulers. Christian kingdoms had fought for a long time to take this land, and in 1492 the last Muslim kingdom in Spain, Granada, was conquered. The Ottoman Empire, which was Muslim, was also powerful in the east of Europe and controlled many trade routes. So Portuguese and Spanish rulers saw exploration partly as a way to spread Christianity and to weaken their Muslim rivals.

The Pope supported the voyages and gave Portugal and Spain the right to claim lands that they reached. Priests often sailed on the ships.

Europeans also believed an old legend about a powerful Christian king called Prester John, who was said to rule a kingdom somewhere in Asia or Africa. The Portuguese hoped to find him and join forces with him. Later, they linked the legend to Ethiopia, in East Africa, which really did have an ancient Christian kingdom, Christian since about the fourth century AD.

The padrões that Portuguese explorers placed along the coast of Africa carried a cross and the coat of arms of the King of Portugal. They showed both the Christian faith of the explorers and a claim to the land for Portugal, even though the land already belonged to the people who lived there.

The questions for this section are with you now: why religion was a reason for exploration and what a padrão was.

## Subtopic: Crossing the Sea to Find the East

Europeans called the lands of India, China and South-East Asia the East, or the Indies. Finding a sea route to the East became the great goal of exploration, because it would bring spices, silk and riches directly to Europe.

Two countries tried two different routes.

Portugal chose to sail south, down the west coast of Africa, hoping to sail around the southern end of Africa and then north-east across the Indian Ocean to India. This took many years, as Portuguese ships went a little further with each voyage. In 1488, Bartolomeu Dias rounded the southern tip of Africa. In 1498, Vasco da Gama finally reached India.

Spain chose to sail west. Christopher Columbus, an Italian sailor working for the King and Queen of Spain, believed that the world was round and that he could reach Asia by sailing west across the Atlantic Ocean. In 1492, he reached islands in the Caribbean. He believed he had reached the Indies, which is why the islands are still called the West Indies and the people who lived there were wrongly called Indians. In fact, he had reached the Americas, which Europeans had not known about. The world was much bigger than he thought.

In 1494, Spain and Portugal signed an agreement, called the Treaty of Tordesillas, with the support of the Pope, which drew a line on the map and divided newly reached lands outside Europe between them. Nobody asked the people who lived in those lands.

The questions for this section are with you now: the search for a sea route to the East and the routes of Portugal and Spain.

## Subtopic: God, Gold and Glory

Historians sometimes sum up the reasons for European exploration in three words: God, gold and glory.

God stands for religion: spreading Christianity, finding Christian allies and weakening rival Muslim powers.

Gold stands for wealth: trade in spices, silk and gold, and the profits that kings and merchants hoped to make.

Glory stands for fame and power: kings wanted their countries to become great and powerful, and explorers wanted to be famous and rewarded.

Together with curiosity, these reasons explain why people were willing to risk their lives on long, dangerous voyages into unknown seas. Usually, several reasons were mixed together. A captain might be a sincere Christian, want to become rich and famous, and be curious about the world, all at the same time.

For the people who lived in the lands that Europeans reached, exploration often brought serious harm: the loss of land, war, disease, forced labour and slavery, and attempts to replace their own beliefs and cultures. Historians today try to understand the explorers' reasons while also remembering these consequences.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking religion had nothing to do with exploration; spreading Christianity was an important reason. Two: thinking Columbus reached Asia; he reached the Americas. Three: thinking a padrão was only a religious symbol; it was also a claim to land for Portugal. Four: thinking explorers had only one reason each; most had several reasons mixed together.

The questions for this section are with you now: God, gold and glory and the consequences of exploration.

God, gold and glory, plus curiosity.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Portuguese and Spanish rulers wanted to spread the Christian religion, weaken their Muslim rivals and find the legendary Christian king Prester John. Explorers placed stone crosses called padrões to show their faith and claim land, such as the one Dias placed near Kwaaihoek in 1488. Europeans wanted a sea route to the East: Portugal sailed around Africa, reaching India in 1498, while Columbus sailed west for Spain in 1492 and reached the Americas.
