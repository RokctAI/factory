# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers two reasons why Europeans explored in the 1400s and 1500s: curiosity about the world, and the search for trade and profit. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Durban, in KwaZulu-Natal, on a visit to a spice stall in a busy city market. Large bowls are heaped with spices of every colour: black peppercorns, sticks of cinnamon bark, brown cloves, round nutmegs, bright yellow turmeric and red chilli powder. The stall owner tells the class that some of these spices came to Durban by ship from India and Indonesia, and that many families in Durban, especially families of Indian descent, have used them in cooking for generations. The teacher then tells the class that, in Europe about five hundred years ago, spices like these were so expensive that only the rich could afford them, and that they were one of the main reasons why sailors risked their lives on long voyages. The big idea is that European exploration was driven by curiosity and by the desire for trade and profit. These reasons for exploring are examinable.

## Subtopic: Curiosity

Curiosity means wanting to know and learn about something. In the 1400s, Europeans knew very little about large parts of the world, including most of Africa, the Americas and the far side of Asia.

Renaissance ideas encouraged people to ask questions, observe and find out for themselves. Scholars, mapmakers and rulers wanted to fill in the empty spaces on their maps. They wanted to know how far Africa stretched to the south, whether it was possible to sail around it, and what peoples, animals and plants lived in distant lands.

There were also many frightening stories. Some sailors believed that the sea near the equator was so hot that it boiled, that huge sea monsters lived in the deep ocean, or that ships that sailed too far would never return. For a long time, Portuguese sailors were afraid to sail past a dangerous headland on the west coast of Africa called Cape Bojador, where strong winds and currents made sailing difficult. In 1434, a Portuguese captain called Gil Eanes finally sailed past it and returned safely, which showed that many of the fears were not true.

A Portuguese prince, often called Prince Henry the Navigator, who lived from 1394 to 1460, encouraged and paid for many of these early voyages down the west coast of Africa. He did not sail on them himself, but he gathered information from sailors and mapmakers and sent ship after ship further south.

The questions for this section are with you now: curiosity, the fears of sailors and Prince Henry the Navigator.

## Subtopic: Spices and Silk

The most important reason for exploration was trade, and the profit it could bring.

Europeans wanted spices from Asia, such as pepper from India, cinnamon from Sri Lanka, and cloves and nutmeg from small islands in what is now Indonesia. Spices were used to flavour food, in medicines and perfumes, and as a sign of wealth. Europeans also wanted silk and porcelain from China, cotton cloth from India and precious stones.

These goods travelled to Europe along long routes. Some came overland, by camel caravans along the Silk Roads across Central Asia. Others came by sea across the Indian Ocean to ports in Arabia and Egypt, and then overland or by sea to the Mediterranean. Along the way, they passed through the hands of many traders, including Indian, Arab, Persian and Turkish merchants, and finally Italian merchants from cities such as Venice. Each trader added a profit, and rulers along the way charged taxes. By the time spices reached Portugal, they cost many times more than in India.

In 1453, the Ottoman Turks captured Constantinople, an important city on the trade routes between Asia and Europe. The Ottomans now controlled many routes to the East. Europeans who wanted spices had to deal with them and with the Venetian merchants who bought from them.

So countries such as Portugal, on the far western edge of Europe, wanted to find their own sea route to the East. If they could sail around Africa to India, they could buy spices directly, cut out the middlemen and make huge profits.

The questions for this section are with you now: spices, silk and the trade routes from Asia to Europe.

## Subtopic: Gold and Profit

The Portuguese also wanted gold. As they sailed down the west coast of Africa, they began trading for gold from West African kingdoms. In 1482, they built a fortress called Elmina, on the coast of what is now Ghana, to control this trade.

Sadly, profit also led to great cruelty. From the 1440s, Portuguese sailors began capturing and buying African people and taking them to Europe as enslaved workers. Over the next centuries, this grew into the transatlantic slave trade, in which millions of Africans were taken across the Atlantic Ocean to the Americas by many European countries. This is one of the most terrible chapters in human history.

Voyages of exploration were very expensive. Ships, sailors, food and weapons cost a lot of money. Kings, princes and rich merchants paid for them because they hoped that successful voyages would bring back far more money than they cost. A voyage that returned with a cargo of spices could make enormous profits. Kings also hoped to gain power, land and fame for their countries.

Sailors and captains had their own reasons too. Many joined voyages because they were poor and hoped for wages, a share of the cargo or a reward from the king. Some hoped to become famous. Some were prisoners who were offered freedom in return for joining a dangerous voyage.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking explorers sailed only for adventure; trade and profit were the main reasons. Two: thinking spices were cheap in Europe; they were very expensive because of the long chain of traders. Three: thinking Prince Henry the Navigator sailed on the voyages himself; he organised and paid for them. Four: thinking the search for profit harmed nobody; it led to the slave trade and great suffering.

The questions for this section are with you now: gold, the slave trade and why voyages were paid for.
