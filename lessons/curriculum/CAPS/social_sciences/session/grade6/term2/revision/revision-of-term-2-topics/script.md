# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the Term 2 topics. In Geography, the topic was trade: what trade is, why people trade, what people trade, how value is added to resources, and fair trading. In History, the topic was explorers from Europe find southern Africa: the reasons for European exploration, the Renaissance and new inventions, and the trade route to the East around southern Africa. The anchor is a revision afternoon for a Grade 6 class in Kimberley, in the Northern Cape, a city that grew up around diamond mining. The class turns the school hall into a small market with three stalls. Stall 1 has empty packets and labels from products made in South Africa and in other countries, and a set of old kitchen scales. Stall 2 has a cocoa pod drawn on a card, a chocolate wrapper, a picture of a gold ring and a box of rooibos tea with a fair trade label. Stall 3 has a model of a caravel, a compass and a large map of Africa with the routes of Dias and da Gama drawn in red. Groups move from stall to stall, answering the questions on each card. The big idea is that trade links people and places all over the world, and that the search for new trade routes brought Europeans to southern Africa. The whole of Term 2 is examinable.

## Subtopic: Trade and Why People Trade

At Stall 1, the class remembers the basics. Trade is the buying and selling, or exchange, of goods and services. Goods are things you can touch, such as bread or shoes. Services are things people do for us, such as teaching, nursing or fixing a car. Producers make goods or provide services, and consumers buy and use them. Trade happens locally in a community, nationally inside a country and internationally between countries, and it creates jobs.

People trade because nobody can make everything they need, and different places have different resources. When people produce more than they need, the extra is a surplus that they can trade. Long ago, people used barter, which means swapping goods without money. Barter was difficult, because you had to find someone who wanted what you had and had what you wanted, and many goods are hard to divide, heavy or spoil quickly. Money made trade easier.

Goods sold to other countries are exports, and money flows in. South Africa exports minerals such as platinum, gold, coal and iron ore, as well as citrus fruit, wine and cars. Goods bought from other countries are imports, such as crude oil and fuel, machines, phones and medicines. China is one of South Africa's biggest trading partners.

Raw materials come straight from nature through farming, mining, fishing and forestry. They are primary products. Manufactured goods are made from raw materials in factories, and they are secondary products. Services are the third kind of work. One product, such as bread, involves all three: the farmer, the bakery and the shop.

The questions for this section are with you now: trade, barter, exports and imports, and raw materials and manufactured goods.

## Subtopic: Value and Fair Trade

At Stall 2, the class remembers value. Value is what something is worth. Each stage of work, using skills, machines and energy, adds value, so manufactured goods are usually worth much more than the raw materials they are made from. Wheat becomes flour and then bread. Iron ore becomes steel and then a car.

Cocoa trees grow in hot, wet places near the equator. Most of the world's cocoa comes from West Africa, especially Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana, where it is grown on small family farms. The beans are fermented, dried and shipped to factories, where they are roasted and ground, then mixed with sugar and milk to make chocolate. The farmers usually earn only a small share of the price of a chocolate bar.

Gold was found on the Witwatersrand in 1886. Mines there are some of the deepest in the world, more than three kilometres deep, and each tonne of rock holds only a few grams of gold. The ore is crushed, chemicals separate the gold, it is melted into bars and refined, and then jewellers turn it into rings and chains. Most jewellery is not pure gold, because pure gold is too soft.

Unfair trade happens when farmers get very low prices, workers get very low wages, or trade rules favour rich countries. Exploitation means using people unfairly, and it includes child labour. In fair trade, farmers get a fair minimum price and an extra amount called a premium for community projects, with no child or forced labour and care for the environment. Rooibos grows naturally only in a small area around the Cederberg, and small-scale farmers there have formed cooperatives to sell fair trade rooibos.

The questions for this section are with you now: adding value, cocoa and gold, and unfair and fair trade.

## Subtopic: Explorers from Europe

At Stall 3, the class remembers History. Long before Europeans arrived, southern Africa was linked to the world through the Indian Ocean trade of places such as Mapungubwe. In Europe, spices and silk from the East were very expensive, because they passed through many middlemen.

From about the 1400s, the Renaissance, meaning rebirth, began in Italy, in Florence. People studied Greek and Roman learning again, valued human ability and made new art and science. Leonardo da Vinci, born in 1452, was a painter, inventor and scientist who filled notebooks with ideas. Gutenberg's printing press spread new ideas quickly. Gunpowder and the compass came to Europe from China, and with the astrolabe, the caravel and the lateen sail, sailors could travel further.

Europeans explored for curiosity, for trade and profit, to spread Christianity and to find a sea route to the East. Prince Henry the Navigator paid for Portuguese voyages down the west coast of Africa. In 1488, Bartolomeu Dias was the first European to sail around the southern tip of Africa. At Mossel Bay, sailors took water from a spring, the Khoikhoi threw stones, and Dias killed a Khoikhoi man. In 1497 to 1498, Vasco da Gama sailed around the Cape, named Natal and, with a pilot from Malindi, reached Calicut in India. In 1652, Jan van Riebeeck set up a refreshment station at the Cape for the Dutch East India Company, and the Khoikhoi slowly lost their land.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: mixing up exports and imports; exports go out and bring money in. Two: thinking the cocoa farmer earns most of the price of chocolate; the farmer usually earns a small share. Three: thinking Europeans first linked southern Africa to the world; Indian Ocean trade had done so for centuries. Four: thinking da Gama was first around the Cape; Dias did it first, in 1488.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Renaissance, reasons for exploring and the sea route to the East.
