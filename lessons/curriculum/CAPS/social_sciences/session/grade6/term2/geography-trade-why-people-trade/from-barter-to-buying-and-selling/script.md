# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers why people trade, trade as the exchange of goods, and how buying and selling goods for money works. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Pietermaritzburg, in KwaZulu-Natal. The teacher has set up a swap market in the classroom. Each learner gets a card showing goods they own, such as a bag of oranges, a loaf of bread, three pencils, a pair of socks or a bunch of carrots, and a card showing what they need. The rule is simple: no money allowed, only swaps. At first the class is noisy and cheerful. Then the problems begin. A learner called Thabo has oranges and needs a pencil, but the learner with pencils wants bread, not oranges. Another learner has a whole loaf of bread and wants just one pencil, which does not seem fair. After ten minutes, many learners are still holding the wrong goods. Then the teacher hands out paper money, and suddenly everyone can trade. The big idea is that people trade because they cannot produce everything they need, and that money makes trade much easier than swapping. Why people trade, barter and money are examinable.

## Subtopic: Why People Trade

No person, family, community or country can produce everything it needs and wants. That is the main reason people trade.

Think about a family that grows maize. They can grow plenty of maize, but they also need salt, cooking oil, soap, school shoes, medicine and electricity. They cannot make all of these themselves. So they sell some of their maize, or the money they earn from work, to buy the other things.

Different places have different resources. Resources are the things that people can use, such as fertile soil, water, minerals, forests, fish, sunshine and the skills of people. The Western Cape has a climate good for growing grapes, apples and citrus. Mpumalanga has coal. The ocean along our coast has fish. Countries in Asia have huge factories making electronics. Each place is good at producing certain things, and trade lets everyone share them.

When people produce more than they need, the extra is called a surplus. Farmers with a surplus of maize can sell it. A country with a surplus of coal can sell it to other countries.

People also specialise. To specialise means to focus on one kind of work and become very good at it, such as a baker, a mechanic or a nurse. Specialists produce more and better goods and services, and then trade with each other.

The questions for this section are with you now: why people trade, resources, surplus and specialisation.

## Subtopic: Barter

Long before money was invented, people traded by bartering. Barter means exchanging goods or services directly for other goods or services, without using money. For example, a farmer might swap a goat for a few bags of grain, or a potter might swap clay pots for a sheep.

Barter was used all over the world, including in southern Africa. In earlier times, farming communities exchanged cattle, grain, iron tools, salt and pottery with each other, and herders traded animals with hunter-gatherers and farmers.

Barter still happens today, in small ways. Children swap stickers or cards. Neighbours exchange vegetables from their gardens. A plumber might fix a mechanic's tap in return for a car service.

But barter has problems, as the swap market in Pietermaritzburg showed.

First, you must find someone who has what you want, and who also wants what you have. Thabo needs a pencil seller who wants oranges. This is difficult and takes a long time.

Second, goods are often hard to divide fairly. If a cow is worth more than one bag of grain but less than ten, how do you pay for two bags of grain with part of a cow?

Third, some goods spoil or are hard to store. Oranges rot, and milk goes sour, so you cannot keep them for long to trade later.

Fourth, some goods are heavy or hard to carry, like cattle or sacks of grain, so trading over long distances is difficult.

The questions for this section are with you now: what barter is and the problems with barter.

## Subtopic: Buying and Selling with Money

Money solves most of the problems of barter. Money is anything that people accept as payment for goods and services. In the past, people used shells, beads, salt, metal rods and coins as money. In South Africa today, our money is the rand, with coins and notes, and much of our money moves electronically, through bank cards and cellphones. The rand was introduced in 1961, and it takes its name from the Witwatersrand, the ridge of gold-bearing rock around Johannesburg.

With money, trade happens in two steps instead of one. Thabo sells his oranges to anyone who wants oranges, and receives money. Then he uses the money to buy a pencil from the pencil seller. He no longer needs to find one person who has a pencil and wants oranges.

Money has many advantages. It is easy to carry. It is easy to divide, so you can pay exactly the right amount, down to cents. It does not rot, so you can save it and use it later. And it lets us compare the value of different goods, because each has a price.

When we buy goods, we pay money to the seller. When we sell goods, we receive money from the buyer. The seller usually tries to sell for more than it cost to produce or buy the goods, and the extra is called profit. Businesses need profit to pay their workers and keep going.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking barter is the same as buying; buying uses money, and barter swaps goods. Two: thinking money has always existed; people bartered for thousands of years first. Three: thinking only poor countries trade; every country trades, because none can produce everything. Four: thinking profit means cheating; a fair profit pays for work and keeps businesses running.

The questions for this section are with you now: money, buying and selling, and profit.
