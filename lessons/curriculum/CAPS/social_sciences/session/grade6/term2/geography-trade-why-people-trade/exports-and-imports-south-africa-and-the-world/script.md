# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers exports and imports between South Africa and the world. The anchor is a Grade 6 class from Durban, in KwaZulu-Natal, on a Saturday outing to a lookout point on the Bluff, a long ridge beside the entrance to Durban harbour. Below them, a huge container ship, stacked high with rows of red, blue and green steel boxes, is being guided out to sea by small tugboats. On the other side of the harbour, another ship waits to come in. Cranes, as tall as buildings, lift containers on and off ships at the quayside, and long lines of trucks wait to collect them. The teacher explains that Durban is South Africa's busiest port for containers, and asks the class: what do you think is inside the boxes leaving, and what is inside the boxes arriving? The big idea is that South Africa sells goods to other countries and buys goods from other countries, and that this international trade is vital for jobs and for the goods we use every day. The meaning of exports and imports, and South Africa's main exports and imports, are examinable.

## Subtopic: Exports

Exports are goods and services that a country sells to other countries. When South Africa sells goods to other countries, money flows into South Africa.

South Africa is rich in minerals, and many of our biggest exports come from mining. We are the world's largest producer of platinum, a precious metal used in jewellery and in car exhaust systems that reduce pollution. We also export gold, coal, iron ore, manganese, chrome and diamonds.

South Africa also exports farm products. We are one of the world's biggest exporters of citrus fruit, such as oranges, lemons and naartjies, mostly grown in Limpopo, the Eastern Cape and the Western Cape. We export grapes, apples, pears, wine, wool and sugar.

We export manufactured goods too. Several car factories in South Africa, in provinces such as the Eastern Cape, Gauteng and KwaZulu-Natal, build vehicles that are shipped to countries in Europe and elsewhere. We also export machinery, chemicals and steel.

Services can be exports as well. When tourists from other countries visit Kruger National Park or Table Mountain and pay for hotels, food and tours, South Africa earns money from abroad, just as if it had sold them goods.

Exports are very important because they bring money into the country and create jobs in mines, farms, factories, transport and ports.

The questions for this section are with you now: what exports are and South Africa's main exports.

## Subtopic: Imports

Imports are goods and services that a country buys from other countries. When South Africa buys goods from other countries, money flows out of South Africa.

South Africa imports goods that it cannot produce at all, or cannot produce enough of, or that other countries can make more cheaply. One of our biggest imports is crude oil, and petrol and diesel made from it, because South Africa has very little oil of its own. We also import machinery and equipment for factories and mines, electronic goods such as cellphones, computers and televisions, vehicle parts, medicines, clothing and some foods. For example, South Africa grows very little rice, so almost all the rice we eat is imported.

Imports are important too. They give us goods we need, such as fuel for transport, medicines for hospitals and machines for factories. They also give consumers more choice.

The countries a country trades with most are called its trading partners. China has been one of South Africa's biggest trading partners for many years, both for exports and imports. Other important partners include the United States, Germany, India, the United Kingdom and Japan. In Africa, our neighbours, such as Botswana, Namibia, Mozambique, Zimbabwe, Lesotho and Eswatini, buy many South African goods. South Africa, Botswana, Lesotho, Namibia and Eswatini belong to a customs union, which makes trade between them easier.

The questions for this section are with you now: what imports are, South Africa's main imports and its trading partners.

## Subtopic: How Goods Cross Borders

Most of South Africa's exports and imports travel by sea, in ships. South Africa has several important commercial ports. Durban is the busiest container port. Richards Bay, also in KwaZulu-Natal, has a large terminal for exporting coal. Saldanha Bay, on the west coast, exports iron ore that comes by a long railway line from mines in the Northern Cape. Cape Town handles fruit and containers, and the ports at Gqeberha and nearby Ngqura also handle containers and cars.

Containers have changed world trade. A container is a large steel box of a standard size, which can be lifted from a ship onto a truck or a train without unpacking. This makes loading fast and cheap, and protects goods.

Some goods travel by air, especially goods that are light, valuable or spoil quickly, such as fresh flowers, medicines and some electronics. Goods for our neighbours often travel by road and rail across land borders, through border posts such as Beitbridge, on the border with Zimbabwe. Beitbridge is one of the busiest border posts in southern Africa, and long lines of trucks often wait there to have their goods checked. Delays at borders and ports make goods slower to arrive and more expensive, so making ports and border posts work faster helps trade and saves money for everyone.

The difference between the value of a country's exports and imports is called its balance of trade. If a country sells more than it buys, money flows in. If it buys more than it sells, more money flows out. Countries try to export a lot, so that they earn money to pay for their imports.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: mixing up exports and imports; exports go out and imports come in. Two: thinking imports are always bad; we need imported fuel, medicines and machines. Three: thinking South Africa only exports gold; platinum, coal, iron ore, fruit and cars are big exports too. Four: thinking services cannot be exported; tourism earns money from other countries.

The questions for this section are with you now: ports, containers and the balance of trade.
