# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a case study of the contribution of Leonardo da Vinci to the Renaissance. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Rustenburg, in North West. The teacher hands each group a photocopy of a page from one of Leonardo's notebooks. It is covered in neat, tiny writing and small drawings of machines and birds, but nobody can read it. Then she gives each group a small mirror. When they hold the page up to the mirror, the writing turns around, and the letters become clearer, although they are in Italian. Leonardo often wrote from right to left, in mirror writing. Next, the class follows a simple drawing from his notebooks to build a small pyramid-shaped parachute out of paper and string, and they drop it from the top of the stairs. It floats down slowly. The big idea is that Leonardo da Vinci was one of the greatest examples of the Renaissance spirit: a person who combined art, science and invention, and who learnt by observing the world closely. His life and contribution are examinable.

## Subtopic: Leonardo's Life

Leonardo da Vinci was born in 1452, in or near the small town of Vinci, in the hills of Tuscany, not far from Florence, in Italy. Da Vinci simply means from Vinci. He grew up in the countryside, where he loved to watch animals, plants, rivers and rocks.

As a teenager, Leonardo became an apprentice in the workshop of a well-known artist in Florence, called Andrea del Verrocchio. An apprentice is a young person who learns a skill by working with an expert. In the workshop, Leonardo learnt to paint, draw, sculpt and work with metals, and he soon became a master himself.

Leonardo worked for many powerful patrons. He spent many years in Milan, working for its ruler, the Duke of Milan, as a painter, engineer and designer of festivals. He also worked in Florence and Rome. Near the end of his life, the King of France invited him to live in France, where he died in 1519, at the age of sixty-seven.

Leonardo was curious about almost everything. People today often call him a Renaissance man, which means a person with great knowledge and skill in many different subjects. He had little formal education in Latin, the language of scholars at the time, so he learnt mostly by looking, drawing, asking questions and experimenting. In his notebooks he wrote long lists of questions to find answers to, such as why the sky is blue and how a woodpecker's tongue works.

The questions for this section are with you now: where and when Leonardo lived and how he learnt.

## Subtopic: Leonardo the Artist

Leonardo is most famous as a painter. He used Renaissance techniques to make his paintings look real and alive. He used perspective to give depth, and he blended light and shadow very softly, so that faces seemed to glow and edges looked soft, as in real life.

His most famous painting is the Mona Lisa, a portrait of a woman with a mysterious smile, painted in the early 1500s. Today it hangs in the Louvre Museum, in Paris, in France, and millions of people visit it every year.

Another famous work is The Last Supper, a large wall painting in Milan that shows Jesus and his twelve disciples at a meal. Leonardo tried a new way of painting on the dry wall, but the paint began to flake off within his lifetime, and the painting has had to be carefully restored many times.

Leonardo also made thousands of drawings. One famous drawing, called the Vitruvian Man, shows a man with his arms and legs stretched out inside a circle and a square, to show the proportions of the human body.

Leonardo finished fewer paintings than many other artists, partly because he worked slowly, kept improving his work and was always distracted by new interests. But the few he finished are among the most admired paintings in the world.

The questions for this section are with you now: Leonardo's art and his most famous works.

## Subtopic: Leonardo the Scientist and Inventor

Leonardo filled thousands of pages in his notebooks with drawings and notes on almost every subject. Many of these pages still survive. He often wrote in mirror writing, from right to left. Leonardo was left-handed, and some people think this made it easier to write without smudging the ink, while others think he wanted to keep his ideas private. Nobody knows for sure.

He studied the human body. He examined muscles, bones and organs very carefully and drew them in detail, including the heart and the skeleton. His drawings were far more accurate than most of the time.

He studied nature: how birds fly, how water flows and swirls, how plants grow and how rocks form.

He designed inventions, many of them far ahead of his time: flying machines with flapping wings, a kind of parachute, a screw-shaped flying machine that looks a little like a helicopter, an armoured vehicle, diving equipment, bridges, canals and machines for lifting heavy loads. Most of them were never built in his lifetime, and some would not have worked, because the materials and engines needed did not yet exist. But they show his imagination and his habit of testing ideas on paper.

Leonardo's contribution was to show how art and science can work together, and how careful observation can unlock the secrets of the world. This spirit of curiosity and testing was the same spirit that encouraged European explorers to sail into the unknown.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking Leonardo was only a painter; he was also a scientist, engineer and inventor. Two: thinking all his inventions were built and used; most stayed on paper. Three: thinking da Vinci was his surname; it means from Vinci. Four: thinking mirror writing means he could not write properly; he chose to write that way.

The questions for this section are with you now: Leonardo's notebooks, science and inventions, and his contribution.
