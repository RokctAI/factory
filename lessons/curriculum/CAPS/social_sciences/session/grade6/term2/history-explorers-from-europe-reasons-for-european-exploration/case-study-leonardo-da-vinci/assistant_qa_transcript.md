### subtopic_1 — Leonardo's Life
**Assistant:** Why is the Mona Lisa so famous?

**Tutor:** There are several reasons. First, it is a masterpiece of Renaissance painting. Leonardo used very soft shading, so the edges of the face and the smile seem to melt into the shadows, which makes her expression hard to read. Some people say her smile seems to change depending on where you look. Second, the background, with winding paths and misty mountains, uses perspective in a new way, making the landscape seem to fade into the distance. Third, the painting has an interesting history. In 1911, it was stolen from the Louvre by a worker, and it was missing for about two years before it was found in Italy, which made it famous all over the world. Today, so many visitors want to see it that it is protected behind special glass, and people often queue for a long time just for a quick look.

### subtopic_2 — Leonardo the Artist
**Assistant:** Did Leonardo's flying machines ever fly?

**Tutor:** As far as we know, Leonardo never flew in any of his machines, and most of them were never built in his lifetime. Some of his designs, such as machines with flapping wings powered by a person, could not have worked, because human muscles are not strong enough to lift a person and a heavy machine into the air. But Leonardo studied birds and the air very carefully, and some of his ideas were clever. In modern times, people have built models of some of his designs. In 2000, a British skydiver built a parachute based on Leonardo's drawing, using materials that might have been available in his time, and jumped with it from a hot-air balloon. It worked, although he used a modern parachute for the last part of the landing. Real aeroplanes were only invented more than four hundred years after Leonardo died.

### subtopic_3 — Leonardo the Scientist and Inventor
**Assistant:** Why did Leonardo study dead bodies?

**Tutor:** Leonardo wanted to paint and draw people as accurately as possible, and he was also deeply curious about how the human body works. To really understand muscles, bones, the heart and other organs, he believed he had to see them for himself. With permission from hospitals in Florence and Milan, he studied the bodies of people who had died, carefully cutting them open and making detailed drawings. This kind of careful study is called anatomy. It was difficult and unpleasant work, especially without fridges to keep bodies cool. His anatomy drawings were among the most detailed of the time, and some of his observations about the heart were only fully understood by scientists much later. Today, medical students still study anatomy, and Leonardo's drawings are admired by doctors as well as artists.
