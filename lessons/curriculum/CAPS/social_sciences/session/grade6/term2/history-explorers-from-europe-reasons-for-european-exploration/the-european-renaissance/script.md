# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the European Renaissance of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, the 1400s and 1500s, and why it was a turning point in European history. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Makhanda, in the Eastern Cape. In an art lesson, the teacher asks the class to draw a straight road stretching away into the distance. Most learners draw two straight lines side by side, and the road looks flat, like a ribbon standing up on the page. Then the teacher shows them a trick. She draws a line across the page for the horizon, puts a dot on it, and draws the two edges of the road so that they come closer together and meet at the dot. Suddenly the road seems to stretch far away. She explains that this trick, called perspective, was worked out carefully by artists in Italy about six hundred years ago, during a time called the Renaissance. The big idea is that the Renaissance was a time of new ideas and curiosity in Europe that changed art, learning and science, and helped to make the voyages of exploration possible. The Renaissance as a turning point is examinable.

## Subtopic: What the Renaissance Was

The word Renaissance comes from French and means rebirth. Historians use it to describe a period in European history, roughly from the 1300s to the 1600s, when there was a great flowering of art, learning and new ideas. In this topic, we focus on the 1400s and 1500s, the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries.

The Renaissance began in Italy, especially in the city of Florence, and then spread to other parts of Europe, such as France, Germany, the Netherlands, England, Spain and Portugal.

Why was it called a rebirth? During the Renaissance, scholars in Europe became very interested in the learning of ancient Greece and Rome, from more than a thousand years earlier. They searched for old books, copied and translated them, and studied their ideas about philosophy, mathematics, science, medicine, architecture and art. Many of these ancient books had been kept, studied and added to by scholars in the Arab and Islamic world and in the Byzantine Empire, centred on Constantinople, and they reached Italy through trade and travel. When Constantinople was captured by the Ottoman Turks in 1453, some scholars moved to Italy and brought more books and knowledge with them.

Italian cities, such as Florence and Venice, were rich from trade and banking. Rich families, such as the Medici family in Florence, paid artists, architects and scholars to work for them. Someone who pays for and supports artists in this way is called a patron.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of Renaissance, when and where it began and why it was a rebirth.

## Subtopic: New Ideas in Art and Learning

During the Renaissance, people began to place more value on human abilities, on learning and on studying the world around them. This way of thinking is called humanism. Humanists believed that education could help people to live better lives and to understand the world.

Art changed greatly. Renaissance artists tried to make their paintings and sculptures look real and alive. They used perspective, so that scenes seemed to have depth, and they used light and shadow to make figures look solid. Some artists studied the human body carefully, even its muscles and bones, so that they could draw people accurately. Famous Renaissance artists include Leonardo da Vinci, Michelangelo, who painted the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel in Rome, and Raphael.

Learning changed too. Around the 1440s and 1450s, in the town of Mainz, in Germany, a goldsmith called Johannes Gutenberg developed a printing press with movable metal letters. Before this, books in Europe were mostly copied by hand, which took months, so books were rare and expensive. With the printing press, many copies could be made quickly and more cheaply. Printing had been developed even earlier in China and Korea, but Gutenberg's press spread very fast across Europe. Soon, books on every subject, including maps and travel stories, were printed in thousands, and new ideas spread faster than ever.

The questions for this section are with you now: humanism, Renaissance art and the printing press.

## Subtopic: A Turning Point

A turning point is a time when important changes happen that set history in a new direction. The Renaissance is seen as a turning point in European history because it changed how many people thought.

Before the Renaissance, many people in Europe accepted what they were told by old books and by religious leaders, without asking questions. During the Renaissance, more people began to ask questions, observe carefully and test ideas for themselves. This was the beginning of modern science. For example, in 1543, an astronomer called Nicolaus Copernicus published a book arguing that the Earth moves around the sun, and not the sun around the Earth, as most people then believed. Later, Galileo Galilei used a telescope to support this idea.

This new curiosity and confidence also encouraged exploration. People wanted to know what lay beyond the edges of their maps. Mathematicians and astronomers improved ways of measuring the stars, which helped sailors find their position at sea. Mapmakers used new information from sailors to draw better maps, and printing spread these maps widely. Rich rulers and merchants were willing to pay for voyages that might bring knowledge, fame and profit.

Not everyone in Europe shared in the Renaissance. Most people were poor farmers who could not read, and their daily lives changed slowly. The new ideas were strongest among wealthy, educated people in the cities. But over time, printing and schooling spread these ideas more widely.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the Renaissance began in Portugal; it began in Italy. Two: thinking Renaissance people invented all their ideas from nothing; they built on Greek, Roman, Arab and other knowledge. Three: thinking Gutenberg invented printing everywhere; printing existed earlier in China and Korea. Four: thinking the Renaissance was only about art; it also changed learning, science and exploration.

The questions for this section are with you now: why the Renaissance was a turning point and how it encouraged exploration.
