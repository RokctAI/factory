### subtopic_1 — How Fair Trade Works
**Assistant:** How can I tell if something is fair trade?

**Tutor:** Look for a fair trade label or logo on the packaging. The best-known label is a round mark from the international Fairtrade organisation, but there are a few other trusted fair trade and ethical labels too. These labels mean that an independent organisation has checked that the producers meet certain standards, such as fair prices, no harmful child labour and safe working conditions. Common fair trade products include tea, coffee, cocoa and chocolate, sugar, bananas, flowers and wine. In South Africa, you can find fair trade rooibos, wine and some fruit. Be careful with words like natural or ethical on their own, without a proper label, because anyone can print those words. If you are not sure, you can look up the label or the company, or ask a librarian or teacher to help.

### subtopic_2 — The Rooibos Story
**Assistant:** Why does rooibos only grow in one small area?

**Tutor:** Rooibos is a fynbos plant, part of the special plant life of the Cape region, which has a huge number of plants found nowhere else in the world. Rooibos has adapted over a very long time to the conditions in the Cederberg and Bokkeveld: hot, dry summers, cool, wet winters and poor, sandy, acidic soils. Its long roots reach deep into the ground to find water during the dry season, and its roots work together with tiny living things in the soil that help it get nutrients. People in other countries have tried to grow rooibos, but it has generally not grown well elsewhere. In 2021, rooibos was officially registered in the European Union as a protected name linked to its region, a little like champagne, which helps protect South African farmers.

### subtopic_3 — What Fair Trade Changed
**Assistant:** Does fair trade help the environment too?

**Tutor:** Yes, caring for the environment is one of the aims of fair trade. Fair trade standards ask farmers to protect soil and water, reduce and carefully manage pesticides and fertilisers, avoid the most harmful chemicals, manage waste and protect wild plants and animals. Many fair trade farmers also grow crops organically, and the higher prices help them afford to do so. For rooibos farmers, this matters a lot, because the Cederberg and Bokkeveld are home to rare fynbos plants, and the farms sit right next to wild land. Climate change is a big worry for rooibos, because hotter, drier summers and longer droughts can kill the plants. Some fair trade cooperatives have used their premium or other support to learn ways of farming that conserve water and soil and help the plants survive dry years.
