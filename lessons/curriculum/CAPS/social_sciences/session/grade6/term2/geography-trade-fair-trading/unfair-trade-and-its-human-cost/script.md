# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the concepts of fair and unfair trade, and the human cost of unfair trade, including low pay, poor working conditions, child labour and exploitation. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Paarl, in the Western Cape. The teacher holds up a plain T-shirt that she bought for a very low price at a discount shop. She asks the class to read the label, which says that it was made in a country in Asia. Then she draws a chain on the board: cotton farmer, cotton buyer, spinning mill, clothing factory, shipping company, importer, shop and customer. She asks the learners to guess how the money from the T-shirt might be shared along the chain. Most guess that the people at the start of the chain, who grew the cotton and sewed the shirt, earned the most, because they did the most work. The teacher explains that it is often the other way around. The big idea is that trade can be fair or unfair, and that unfair trade has a real human cost for workers and farmers, even when the price in the shop looks cheap. The meaning of fair and unfair trade and the human cost of unfair trade are examinable.

## Subtopic: Fair and Unfair Trade

Trade is fair when everyone involved is treated fairly and receives a fair share for their work, goods or services. In fair trade, producers are paid a price that covers their costs and lets them live decently, workers earn fair wages and work in safe conditions, and nobody is forced to work.

Trade is unfair when some people in the chain receive far less than a fair share, or are treated badly, while others make large profits. Unfair trade can happen in several ways.

Producers may be paid very low prices. Small farmers, such as cocoa or coffee farmers, often sell to a few powerful buyers. If the farmers have no other buyers, they may have to accept whatever price they are offered, even if it does not cover their costs.

Workers may be paid very low wages. Factories that compete to make goods as cheaply as possible may pay workers too little to live on, and make them work very long hours.

Trade rules may be unfair between countries. Some rich countries have given their own farmers money, called subsidies, so they can sell crops cheaply, and some have charged extra taxes on goods from poorer countries. This can make it hard for farmers in poorer countries to compete.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of fair and unfair trade and how unfair trade happens.

## Subtopic: The Human Cost

When trade is unfair, real people suffer. This is called the human cost of unfair trade.

Exploitation means using people unfairly for your own benefit, for example by paying them too little, making them work too hard or in danger, or taking advantage of the fact that they are poor and have no other choices. Exploited workers may work twelve hours or more a day, six or seven days a week, for wages that are not enough to buy food, rent and school fees.

Unsafe working conditions are a serious problem. In 2013, an eight-storey building called Rana Plaza, near Dhaka, in Bangladesh, collapsed. It contained clothing factories that made goods for shops in many countries. More than one thousand one hundred people, mostly garment workers, were killed, and many more were injured. Workers had been told to keep working even after cracks appeared in the walls. This disaster shocked the world and led many companies and governments to promise better safety checks.

Child labour is work that harms children or keeps them out of school. International organisations estimate that more than one hundred million children around the world are in child labour. Many work on farms, including some cocoa, cotton and sugar cane farms, and others in mines, factories, markets and homes. Child labour robs children of their education, their health and their childhood.

Workers may also be denied basic rights, such as a written contract, paid sick leave, a safe place to eat and rest, or the freedom to join a trade union. Some are forced to work to pay off debts that never seem to get smaller, which is a kind of modern slavery.

Unfair trade also keeps families and whole communities poor, because they cannot earn enough to improve their lives, even when they work very hard.

The questions for this section are with you now: exploitation, unsafe work and child labour.

## Subtopic: What Can Be Done

Many people and organisations are working to make trade fairer.

Governments make laws about minimum wages, working hours, safety at work and child labour, and send inspectors to check that factories and farms follow them. In South Africa, the law sets minimum wages for many workers, limits working hours and protects children from harmful work.

Trade unions are organisations of workers who join together to ask for fair pay and safe working conditions. Together, workers have a stronger voice than one worker alone.

Fair trade organisations work with farmers and workers to make sure they receive fair prices and decent conditions, and they put a special label on products that meet their standards. We look at a positive fair trade project in the next lesson.

Consumers can help too. A consumer can ask where a product comes from and how it was made, look for fair trade labels, support businesses that treat workers well and avoid wasting things. Buying something only because it is very cheap may sometimes mean that someone else paid the real cost.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the people who do the most work always earn the most; in unfair trade, they often earn the least. Two: thinking a cheap price means nobody paid; the human cost may be hidden. Three: thinking child labour means helping at home; it means work that harms children or keeps them out of school. Four: thinking ordinary people cannot do anything; laws, unions, fair trade and consumer choices all help.

The questions for this section are with you now: ways to make trade fairer.
