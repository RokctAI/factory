# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers fair trade through the case of a positive project: small-scale rooibos farmers in South Africa who sell their tea through fair trade. The anchor is a Grade 6 class from Clanwilliam, in the Western Cape, on an outing into the dry, rocky hills of the Cederberg and Bokkeveld, where rooibos grows. A farmer from a small farming community shows them the bushes, with thin, needle-like green leaves, growing in sandy soil. She explains how the farmers cut the stems in summer, chop them, wet them and leave them in heaps to ferment, and then spread them out in the sun, where they turn a rich red-brown colour. Later, the class is given a cup of rooibos tea, and the teacher hands around a box of rooibos with a small round label printed on it. The label shows that the tea is fair trade. The big idea is that fair trade tries to make sure that farmers and workers are paid fairly and treated well, and that it can help whole communities to improve their lives. The principles of fair trade and a positive fair trade project are examinable.

## Subtopic: How Fair Trade Works

In the last lesson, we saw how unfair trade can leave farmers and workers poor and unsafe. Fair trade is one way that people have tried to change this.

Fair trade is a system of trading in which producers in poorer communities are paid fair prices, workers have decent conditions, and buyers make a long-term commitment to trade with them. Independent organisations check that farms and businesses meet fair trade standards, and then allow them to put a fair trade label on their products.

Fair trade has several important parts.

First, a fair minimum price. For many products, buyers must pay producers at least a minimum price that covers the cost of producing in a sustainable way, even when world prices fall very low. This protects farmers from losing money in bad years.

Second, a fair trade premium. Buyers pay an extra amount of money, called the premium, on top of the price. The farmers or workers decide together, democratically, how to spend it on projects that help their community.

Third, standards for people. There must be no child labour that harms children, and no forced labour. Workers must have safe conditions and the right to join a trade union.

Fourth, standards for the environment. Farmers must protect the soil, water and plants, and limit harmful chemicals.

## Subtopic: The Rooibos Story

Rooibos means red bush in Afrikaans. It is a shrub that grows naturally only in a small part of South Africa, mainly in the Cederberg area around Clanwilliam and in the nearby Bokkeveld, in the Western Cape and Northern Cape. The area has hot, dry summers, winter rain and sandy, acidic soil, which suits rooibos. People have made tea from it for a long time, and today it is drunk in many countries around the world.

Rooibos is a big business, but in the past, most of the money went to large farms and companies. Many small-scale farmers in poor rural communities, whose families had lived and worked in the area for generations, had little land, little money and few ways to sell their tea for a good price. Under apartheid, black and coloured farmers in particular had very limited access to land, loans and markets.

From around the end of the 1990s and early 2000s, some of these small-scale farmers formed cooperatives. A cooperative is a business that is owned and run by its members, who share the work, the decisions and the profits. For example, small farmers in the Suid Bokkeveld near Nieuwoudtville formed the Heiveld Cooperative, and farmers around Wupperthal, in the Cederberg, also formed a cooperative. Working together, they could process their tea, pack it, meet quality standards and sell directly to fair trade buyers in Europe and elsewhere.

## Subtopic: What Fair Trade Changed

Selling through fair trade has helped small-scale rooibos farmers in several ways.

They receive a fairer and more stable price for their tea, so they can plan ahead and are less hurt when prices fall.

They receive the fair trade premium, which members decide how to use. Rooibos cooperatives have used such money for things like equipment for processing the tea, training, transport, and support for their communities, such as help for schools, young people or older members.

They have more control. Because they own the cooperative, members make decisions together and learn business skills, such as bookkeeping, marketing and quality control.

They care for the environment. Many small rooibos farmers grow their tea in an organic way, without artificial chemicals, and look after the wild plants around their farms, which is important in this special and fragile area.

Fair trade has also given small farmers more confidence and recognition. Some have travelled to trade fairs to meet the people who buy their tea, and customers in other countries can read about the communities that grew it.

Fair trade is not perfect. Fair trade products often cost a little more, and only some buyers choose them. Farmers still face droughts, which are a serious threat to rooibos. But fair trade has shown that trade can be organised in a way that respects the people who do the work.

Two: thinking the premium goes to the shop; it goes to the farmers or workers to spend on community projects.
