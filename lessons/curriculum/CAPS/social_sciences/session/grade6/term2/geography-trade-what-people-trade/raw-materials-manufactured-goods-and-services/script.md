# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers what people trade: raw materials, or primary products, manufactured goods, or secondary products, and skills and services. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Graaff-Reinet, a town in the Karoo, in the Eastern Cape. The class visits a sheep farm outside the town on shearing day. In a big shed, shearers work quickly and carefully, clipping the thick wool from merino sheep with electric shears. The wool comes off in one piece, called a fleece, and is thrown onto a table, where workers sort it and pack it into large bales. The farmer explains that the bales will travel by truck to a buyer in Gqeberha, and then to factories in South Africa and overseas, where the wool will be cleaned, spun into yarn and knitted or woven into jerseys, blankets and suits. Back at school, the teacher holds up a woolly jersey and asks the class to list everyone who helped to make it and bring it to the shop. The list is long. The big idea is that people trade three different kinds of things: raw materials from nature, goods made from them, and services and skills. These three kinds of products, and examples of each, are examinable.

## Subtopic: Raw Materials

Raw materials are natural resources that are taken from nature and have not yet been made into something else. They are also called primary products, because producing them is the first step in making goods. The work of taking raw materials from nature is called primary activity.

There are four main kinds of primary activity.

Farming produces crops and animal products, such as maize, wheat, sugar cane, fruit, cotton, wool, milk, meat and hides. The wool from the merino sheep in Graaff-Reinet is a raw material from farming.

Mining produces minerals from under the ground, such as gold, platinum, coal, iron ore, diamonds and copper.

Fishing produces fish and other sea foods, such as hake, pilchards and rock lobster.

Forestry produces wood from trees grown in plantations or forests. In South Africa, large plantations of pine and gum trees grow in Mpumalanga, KwaZulu-Natal and the Eastern Cape.

The amount of raw materials produced can change a lot from year to year. A drought can mean a poor harvest of maize, and a storm at sea can keep fishing boats in the harbour. Mines can be slowed down by power cuts, floods or accidents. When less is produced, the price often goes up.

Many raw materials are traded in large amounts at fairly low prices for each kilogram or tonne. Countries that have a lot of raw materials, like South Africa, often export them to countries with many factories.

The questions for this section are with you now: raw materials, primary products and the four kinds of primary activity.

## Subtopic: Manufactured Goods

Manufactured goods are products made by changing raw materials into something new and more useful, usually in a factory. They are also called secondary products, because making them is the second step, after the raw materials have been produced. The work of making goods is called secondary activity, or manufacturing.

When the wool from Graaff-Reinet is washed, spun into yarn and knitted into a jersey, it becomes a manufactured good. Other examples are bread made from wheat flour, sugar made from sugar cane, paper made from wood, steel made from iron ore, jewellery made from gold, chocolate made from cocoa beans and cars made from steel, rubber, glass and plastic.

Some manufactured goods are made in several stages. Iron ore is turned into steel, and then steel is used to make car parts, and the car parts are put together into cars. Each stage adds value, which means the product becomes worth more money.

Manufacturing needs factories, machines, energy such as electricity, and many workers with different skills. South Africa has factories in all its big cities, especially in Gauteng, eThekwini, Cape Town and Nelson Mandela Bay. Manufacturing creates many jobs, and the goods can be sold in South Africa or exported.

The questions for this section are with you now: manufactured goods, secondary products and adding value.

## Subtopic: Skills and Services

The third kind of thing people trade is skills and services. A service is useful work done for other people, where you do not get a physical product to take home. The work of providing services is called tertiary activity.

Think back to the jersey. A truck driver transported the wool and the finished jerseys. A bank lent money to the factory. An advertiser made a poster to sell the jerseys. A shop assistant helped a customer choose one. These people did not make the jersey, but they provided services that helped it to be sold.

There are many kinds of services: transport, banking, insurance, shops, tourism, education, health care, communication, entertainment, repairs, cleaning and security. Teachers, nurses, doctors, hairdressers, mechanics, police officers, tour guides and computer programmers all provide services.

Skills can be traded too. A skill is the ability to do something well, learnt through training and practice. An engineer, a builder or a chef sells their skills. Some South Africans with skills, such as doctors or engineers, work in other countries, and some people from other countries come to work in South Africa, which is a way of trading skills.

In South Africa, as in many countries, most people who work are employed in services. Many services are also provided in the informal sector, by people who work for themselves without a big company, such as street hairdressers, car guards, minibus taxi owners and people who repair shoes or phones at the side of the road.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking raw materials are only minerals; farming, fishing and forestry also produce raw materials. Two: thinking sugar and bread are raw materials; they are manufactured from sugar cane and wheat. Three: thinking services are not real work because you cannot touch them; services create millions of jobs. Four: thinking each product needs only one kind of work; most products need primary, secondary and tertiary work.

The questions for this section are with you now: skills, services and tertiary activity.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the jersey from the sheep farm in Graaff-Reinet — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: From Nature

Raw materials come from farming, mining, fishing and forestry.

Keep the bales of wool in the shearing shed in mind and try the questions for this part: raw materials.

## Subtopic: Made in a Factory

Manufactured goods are made from raw materials. Wool becomes a jersey.

Hold the knitted jersey in mind and try this part's questions: manufactured goods.

## Subtopic: Work for Others

Services are useful work done for people, like driving, teaching and selling.

Pocket summary of the lesson. People trade three kinds of things. Raw materials, or primary products, are taken from nature by farming, mining, fishing and forestry, such as wool, gold, fish and wood. Manufactured goods, or secondary products, are made from raw materials, usually in factories, such as jerseys, bread, steel and cars, and making them adds value. Skills and services, or tertiary activities, are useful work done for others, such as transport, banking, teaching and selling.

Hold the long list of people who brought the jersey to the shop in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: skills and services.
