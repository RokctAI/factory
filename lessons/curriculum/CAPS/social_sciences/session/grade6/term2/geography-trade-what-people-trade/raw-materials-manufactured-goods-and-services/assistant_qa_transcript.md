### subtopic_1 — Raw Materials
**Assistant:** Is electricity a raw material, a manufactured good or a service?

**Tutor:** That is a tricky one, and it is a good example of how the three groups connect. The coal that is burnt in many South African power stations is a raw material from mining. Sunlight and wind used by solar panels and wind turbines are natural resources too. At the power station, the energy in coal, sun or wind is changed into electricity, which is a kind of manufacturing, so electricity is a produced good, made from raw materials. Then electricity is sent along power lines to homes and businesses, and the company that delivers it and sends out the bills is providing a service. So you could say that electricity involves all three: primary, secondary and tertiary activities. Many things in modern life are like this, which is why geographers think about the whole chain from nature to the customer.

### subtopic_2 — Manufactured Goods
**Assistant:** Why are merino sheep farmed in the Karoo?

**Tutor:** The Karoo is a dry, semi-desert area with hot summers, cold winters and low rainfall. It is too dry for most crops without irrigation, but it is covered with small, tough bushes and grasses that sheep can eat. Merino sheep are hardy and can survive in dry conditions, and they grow fine, soft wool that is valuable for clothing. The farms in the Karoo are very large, because each sheep needs a big area to find enough food. Sheep farming has been important in the Karoo for about two hundred years, and South Africa is one of the world's important producers of fine wool. Some Karoo farmers also keep Angora goats, which produce a soft fibre called mohair. In dry years, farmers may have to sell some of their animals or buy extra feed.

### subtopic_3 — Skills and Services
**Assistant:** Which jobs will be most important when I am grown up?

**Tutor:** Nobody can know for certain, but there are some clear trends. Services are growing in almost every country, so jobs in health care, education, technology, tourism and transport are likely to stay important. As computers and machines do more routine work, skills like problem solving, creativity, working with people and understanding technology will become more valuable. The world will also need people who can produce food and energy in ways that protect the environment, such as in solar and wind power, careful farming and water management. Manufacturing will still matter, especially when countries make more finished goods from their own raw materials. The good news is that skills learnt at school, like reading carefully, working with numbers, explaining your ideas and asking good questions, are useful in almost every job. You might even do a job that does not exist yet.
