# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the Term 1 History topic, introduces the new topic, explorers from Europe find southern Africa, and sets out the key dates and questions for the term. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Cape Town, in the Western Cape. The teacher pins two maps side by side on the board. One is a modern map of the world. The other is a copy of a world map drawn in Europe in the 1400s, based on very old ideas. On the old map, Europe, North Africa and parts of Asia are drawn, but the shapes are strange, the Americas are missing completely, and the southern part of Africa is not shown at all. Some old maps even showed the Indian Ocean as a closed sea, with land all around it. The learners laugh at first, and then they start to ask questions. Did the people who drew this map know that Africa had a southern tip? Did they know that people lived there? The big idea is that, in the 1400s, Europeans did not know the sea route around southern Africa, and that their voyages of exploration changed the history of southern Africa forever. The introduction to this topic is examinable.

## Subtopic: Looking Back at Term 1

In Term 1, we studied Mapungubwe, an African kingdom in the Limpopo Valley. From about 1220 to 1300 AD, it was the first state in southern Africa, with a sacred king who lived on top of Mapungubwe Hill, a town of about five thousand people and distinct social classes.

Mapungubwe was rich because it was part of a huge trading network. Porters carried gold and ivory on foot to Sofala and other towns on the East African coast. Swahili, Arab, Persian and Indian traders sailed dhows across the Indian Ocean with the monsoon winds, taking African gold and ivory to Arabia, India and even China, and bringing back glass beads, cloth and porcelain.

So, long before any Europeans arrived, southern Africa was already linked to the wider world through the Indian Ocean. After Mapungubwe, power moved to Great Zimbabwe, and the trade continued.

Around 1500 AD, the Portuguese arrived on the East African coast and built a fort at Sofala, in 1505. That arrival is where the story of this term begins: how did ships from Europe reach southern Africa and the East African coast, and why did they come?

Your teacher may begin with a short baseline activity, to find out what you remember and already know about explorers. It is not a test to pass or fail.

The questions for this section are with you now: Mapungubwe, the Indian Ocean trade and the arrival of the Portuguese.

## Subtopic: Europe Looks Outwards

In the 1400s, Europe was a group of kingdoms and cities, such as Portugal, Spain, England, France and city states in Italy, such as Venice and Florence. Europeans wanted goods from the East, especially spices such as pepper, cinnamon, cloves and nutmeg, and silk and precious stones, from India, China and the islands of South-East Asia.

These goods reached Europe along long routes over land and sea, through the Middle East. Many traders handled them along the way, and each one added to the price. By the time spices reached Europe, they were very expensive.

Some European rulers and merchants began to wonder: was there a sea route to the East that avoided these middlemen? If ships could sail all the way around Africa, they could buy spices directly in India.

An explorer is a person who travels to places that are new to them, to find out about them. In this topic, we study European explorers who sailed into unknown waters. The period from the 1400s to the 1600s, when European sailors made many long voyages, is often called the Age of Exploration.

Portugal, a small country on the Atlantic coast of Europe, led the way. Portuguese ships sailed further and further south along the west coast of Africa during the 1400s. Spain sent Christopher Columbus west across the Atlantic in 1492.

The questions for this section are with you now: why Europeans looked for a sea route to the East and what an explorer is.

## Subtopic: Our Term of Explorers

This term, we will study several big questions.

What changed in Europe that made exploration possible? We will learn about the Renaissance, a time of new ideas, art and science, and about the life of Leonardo da Vinci. We will learn about new inventions and ideas, such as gunpowder, the magnetic compass and a ship called the caravel, many of which came to Europe from other parts of the world.

Why did Europeans explore? We will look at curiosity, trade and profit, spreading the Christian religion and finding the sea route to the East.

What happened when they reached southern Africa? We will follow Bartolomeu Dias, who sailed around the southern tip of Africa in 1488 and met Khoikhoi herders at Mossel Bay, Vasco da Gama, who reached India in 1498, and the Dutch East India Company, which set up a station at the Cape in 1652.

The title of the topic says explorers from Europe find southern Africa. But we must remember that southern Africa was not lost. The Khoikhoi, the San and African farming communities had lived here for a very long time. It was only new to the Europeans. Historians try to tell the story from more than one point of view.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking Europeans were the first to link southern Africa to the world; the Indian Ocean trade came much earlier. Two: thinking southern Africa was empty when Europeans arrived; many people already lived here. Three: thinking old European maps were complete; they left out large parts of the world. Four: thinking explorers sailed only for adventure; trade and profit were very important reasons.

The questions for this section are with you now: the key questions, people and dates of this term.
