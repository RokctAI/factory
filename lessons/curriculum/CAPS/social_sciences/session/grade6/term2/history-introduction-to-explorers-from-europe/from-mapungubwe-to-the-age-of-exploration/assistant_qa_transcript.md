### subtopic_1 — Looking Back at Term 1
**Assistant:** Why did old European maps leave out so much of the world?

**Tutor:** Mapmakers can only draw what they know or have been told. In the 1400s, European mapmakers relied on old books, especially the work of a Greek scholar called Ptolemy, who lived about one thousand three hundred years earlier, and on the reports of travellers and sailors. Few Europeans had travelled far south along Africa or far across the oceans, so mapmakers simply did not know what was there. Sometimes they filled empty spaces with guesses, imaginary lands or drawings of sea monsters. Mapmakers in other parts of the world, such as China and the Arab world, knew more about the Indian Ocean and the East African coast, because their traders sailed there. As European explorers returned with new information, European maps changed quickly. Within about a century, the shape of Africa was drawn much more correctly.

### subtopic_2 — Europe Looks Outwards
**Assistant:** Did Chinese ships reach Africa before the Portuguese?

**Tutor:** Yes. In the early 1400s, the Chinese emperor sent a series of huge fleets across the Indian Ocean, led by an admiral called Zheng He. His ships were much bigger than European ships of the time, and some fleets had many ships and thousands of sailors. Between about 1405 and 1433, his fleets visited South-East Asia, India, Arabia and the East African coast, including places in what are now Somalia and Kenya. They traded, collected gifts for the emperor and even took a giraffe back to China. Then the Chinese government stopped the voyages and turned its attention inland. So, decades before Dias and Da Gama, Chinese, Arab, Indian and Swahili sailors already knew the Indian Ocean well. What was new about the Portuguese was that they arrived from the Atlantic, around the south of Africa.

### subtopic_3 — Our Term of Explorers
**Assistant:** Why is the topic called explorers find southern Africa?

**Tutor:** The title describes events from the point of view of the European explorers, for whom southern Africa was unknown and new. In older history books, written mainly by Europeans, the arrival of European ships was often called discovery, as if nothing had happened before. Today, historians are more careful. The Khoikhoi, the San and African farming communities had lived in southern Africa for a very long time, with their own ways of life, leaders and beliefs. They were the ones who watched strange ships arrive. So, when you read the title, think of it as the story of when Europeans first found their way to southern Africa. A good historian always asks: whose point of view is this, and how might other people have seen the same events?
