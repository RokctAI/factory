# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a case study of how a raw material becomes a manufactured good: from cocoa to chocolate. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in East London, in the Eastern Cape. On a Friday afternoon, the teacher breaks a large slab of milk chocolate into squares, one for each learner. Before they eat, she asks them to read the wrapper. The ingredients are listed: sugar, milk powder, cocoa butter, cocoa mass and a few others. A learner asks where cocoa comes from, and whether it grows in South Africa. The teacher explains that South Africa does not grow cocoa at all, because our climate is not right for it, and that all the cocoa in our chocolate is imported. She pins a map of the world on the board and draws a long line from a small farm in West Africa, across the sea to a factory, and back to a shop in East London. The big idea is that chocolate is made from a raw material, cocoa beans, grown far away by farmers in tropical countries, and that most of the value is added after the beans leave the farm. The journey from cocoa to chocolate is examinable.

## Subtopic: Growing Cocoa

Chocolate is made from the seeds of the cocoa tree, called cocoa beans. Cocoa trees first grew in the tropical forests of Central and South America. People there, such as the Maya and the Aztecs, made a bitter chocolate drink long before Europeans arrived. In the 1500s, Spanish travellers brought cocoa to Europe, where people added sugar and it became popular.

Cocoa trees need a hot, wet climate all year round, with plenty of rain and no frost. They grow best in tropical areas within about twenty degrees north or south of the equator, often in the shade of taller trees. That is why they cannot grow in South Africa, which is mostly too dry and too cold in winter.

Today, most of the world's cocoa is grown in West Africa. Côte d'Ivoire, also called Ivory Coast, and Ghana are the two biggest producers, and together they grow more than half of the world's cocoa. Cocoa is also grown in countries such as Nigeria, Cameroon, Indonesia, Ecuador and Brazil.

Most cocoa is grown on small family farms, of only a few hectares each. Millions of farmers depend on cocoa for their living.

The cocoa pods are large, oval fruits that grow directly on the trunk and thick branches of the tree. When they are ripe, they turn yellow, orange or red. Farmers cut them down carefully, usually with a long knife called a machete.

The questions for this section are with you now: where cocoa comes from, the climate it needs and where it is grown today.

## Subtopic: From Bean to Bar

On the farm, farmers split the pods open. Inside are about thirty to forty beans, covered in a sweet white pulp. The beans and pulp are scooped out, heaped up and covered with banana leaves, or put in boxes, for about a week. During this time, they ferment, which means natural changes take place that help the chocolate flavour to develop. Then the beans are spread out in the sun to dry for about a week, and turned often. Dried beans are packed into sacks and sold to buyers, who sell them on to exporters.

The sacks are taken to ports, such as Abidjan in Côte d'Ivoire, and shipped to factories. Many of the biggest cocoa factories are in Europe, but some are in the countries where cocoa is grown, and some are in other parts of the world.

In the factory, the beans are cleaned and roasted, which brings out their chocolate smell. Their shells are cracked off, and the small pieces inside, called nibs, are ground into a thick brown paste, called cocoa mass or cocoa liquor. Some of this paste is pressed to separate the fat, called cocoa butter, from the solid part, which becomes cocoa powder.

To make chocolate, factories mix cocoa mass and cocoa butter with sugar, and for milk chocolate, milk powder. The mixture is stirred and smoothed for hours, heated and cooled carefully, poured into moulds and cooled into slabs. The slabs are wrapped, packed, shipped and trucked to shops. South Africa has its own chocolate factories, which import cocoa beans or cocoa products from other countries.

The questions for this section are with you now: the steps from cocoa beans to chocolate slabs.

## Subtopic: Who Earns the Money

The journey from cocoa to chocolate shows clearly how value is added. A sack of dried cocoa beans is worth much less than all the chocolate that can be made from it. Each step adds value: buying and transporting the beans, roasting and grinding, mixing with other ingredients, moulding, wrapping, advertising and selling.

But the money is not shared equally. Cocoa farmers do some of the hardest work, yet many receive only a small share of the price of a chocolate bar, by some estimates less than a tenth. The factories, the brand owners, the transport companies and the shops receive the larger share. Many cocoa farmers in West Africa are poor, and some have said that they have rarely or never tasted the chocolate made from their beans.

There are other problems too. Because farmers earn so little, some families cannot afford to pay workers, and children in some cocoa-growing areas have been made to do dangerous work instead of going to school. Some cocoa forests have also been cut down to plant more cocoa trees.

Some cocoa-growing countries are trying to add more value at home, by building their own factories to grind cocoa and make chocolate. Fair trade schemes, which we study later this term, try to pay farmers better prices.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking cocoa grows in South Africa; it needs a hot, wet tropical climate. Two: thinking chocolate grows on trees; trees grow cocoa beans, which factories turn into chocolate. Three: thinking the farmer earns most of the price of a chocolate bar; most of the money goes to later steps. Four: thinking chocolate has always been a sweet bar; it began as a bitter drink in the Americas.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the value of chocolate is shared.
