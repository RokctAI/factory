# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the values of selected raw materials and manufactured goods, and why manufactured goods are usually worth more. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Tzaneen, in Limpopo, an area famous for growing tomatoes, avocados, mangoes and bananas. A parent who farms tomatoes brings a crate of ripe red tomatoes to the class. The teacher puts a bottle of tomato sauce, a tin of tomato soup and a small packet of tomato paste next to the crate. She explains that the farmer sells the whole crate to a buyer for much less than you would pay for the same weight of tomatoes turned into sauce in the shop. Then she asks the class to explain why. The learners start listing everything that happens to the tomatoes after they leave the farm: washing, cooking, mixing, bottling, labelling, packing, transporting and selling. The big idea is that each step of turning raw materials into manufactured goods adds value, so manufactured goods are usually worth much more than raw materials. Comparing the values of raw materials and manufactured goods is examinable.

## Subtopic: Adding Value

Value is how much something is worth, usually measured by the price people are willing to pay for it.

When raw materials are changed into manufactured goods, their value increases. This is called adding value. Value is added because work, skills, machines, energy and other materials are needed to make the new product.

Follow the tomatoes from Tzaneen. First, the farmer grows and picks the tomatoes. That takes land, water, seeds, fertiliser and the work of farm workers. Next, a truck driver takes them to a factory. At the factory, workers and machines wash, sort, cook and blend the tomatoes, and mix in other ingredients, such as sugar, vinegar and spices. The sauce is poured into bottles, sealed, labelled and packed into boxes. Then the boxes are transported to warehouses and shops. Advertising tells people about the sauce. Finally, the shop sells it to consumers.

At every step, someone has to be paid: the farmer, the workers, the drivers, the owners of the machines, the electricity supplier, the bottle maker, the advertiser and the shop. Each business also needs to make a profit. All of these costs are added to the price. That is why a bottle of tomato sauce costs much more than the tomatoes inside it.

The questions for this section are with you now: what value is and how adding value works.

## Subtopic: Comparing Values

The same pattern is true for many products.

Wheat is a raw material grown on farms in the Western Cape and the Free State. A bag of wheat is fairly cheap. When it is milled into flour, the flour is worth more. When the flour is baked into bread, the bread is worth more again. When it is made into biscuits in fancy packets, the value rises further.

Wood from a pine plantation in Mpumalanga is sold by the tonne at a fairly low price. Turned into planks, it is worth more. Turned into a carefully made table, it is worth much more.

Iron ore from the Northern Cape is cheap per tonne. Steel made from it is worth more. A car made from steel is worth very much more.

Some raw materials are very valuable even before they are processed, because they are rare, beautiful or very useful. Gold, platinum and diamonds are examples. Even so, a gold necklace or a cut diamond in a ring is usually worth much more than the raw gold or the rough diamond, because of the skill and work added.

The prices of raw materials can go up and down quickly. If there is a good harvest everywhere, the price of maize may fall. If many countries need more coal, its price may rise. Prices of manufactured goods usually change more slowly.

Packaging and branding add value too. Two bottles of sauce may contain almost the same ingredients, but a well-known brand with an attractive label and lots of advertising often sells for more. Customers pay partly for the name, because they trust it.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing the values of raw materials and the goods made from them.

## Subtopic: Why It Matters

Why should countries care about adding value?

Countries that mainly export raw materials earn less money than countries that turn raw materials into manufactured goods. If a country sells iron ore cheaply and then buys back cars at a high price, much of the value, the profit and the jobs end up in the other country.

Manufacturing creates many jobs, in factories, in transport, in offices and in shops. So when raw materials are processed in the country where they are produced, more people there get work, learn skills and earn wages.

South Africa exports many raw materials, such as coal, iron ore and platinum. It also has many factories that add value, making steel, cars, food products, chemicals, clothing and paper. Many people believe that South Africa should process more of its own raw materials, for example by cutting and polishing more diamonds, making more jewellery from its gold and platinum, and making more products from its fruit and wool. This would create more jobs and earn more money for the country.

Countries that depend mostly on one or two raw materials are also at risk when the prices of those raw materials fall. A country with many different exports is safer.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking raw materials are always worth more because they come first; manufactured goods usually have more value. Two: thinking the shop gets all the money; the money is shared along the whole chain. Three: thinking adding value means simply raising the price; it means adding work, skill and materials. Four: thinking it does not matter where goods are manufactured; the jobs and profits stay where the value is added.

The questions for this section are with you now: why adding value matters for jobs and for countries.
