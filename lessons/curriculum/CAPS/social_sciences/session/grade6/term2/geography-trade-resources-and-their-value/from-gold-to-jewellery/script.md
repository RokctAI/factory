# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a second case study of how a raw material becomes a manufactured good: from gold to jewellery. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Welkom, a town in the Free State that grew up around gold mines in the twentieth century. On the way to their outing, the bus passes old mine headgear, the tall steel towers above the shafts, and the big pale mine dumps on the edge of town. The class visits a small jeweller's workshop. At a wooden bench, a goldsmith is making a ring. She shows them a tiny piece of gold, then melts it with a small flame, pours it into a mould, rolls it thinner, bends it into a circle, joins the ends, and files and polishes it until it shines. She tells them that the gold she uses was once part of hard rock deep under the ground, perhaps not far from where they are standing. The big idea is that gold goes through many steps, from rock underground to finished jewellery, and that a great deal of value is added at every step. The journey from gold to jewellery is examinable.

## Subtopic: Mining the Gold

Gold is a rare, heavy, yellow metal that does not rust or tarnish, and can be shaped easily. People have valued it for thousands of years, as we saw at Mapungubwe.

In 1886, gold was found on the Witwatersrand, a ridge of rocks in what is now Gauteng. Thousands of people rushed there, and the city of Johannesburg grew up around the gold mines. The gold-bearing rocks form a large underground basin, called the Witwatersrand Basin, which stretches under parts of Gauteng, the Free State and North West. Towns such as Welkom, Carletonville and Klerksdorp grew up around mines in this basin.

For most of the twentieth century, South Africa produced more gold than any other country. Today it produces far less, because much of the easier gold has already been mined, and several other countries now produce more.

The gold in the Witwatersrand is in thin layers of rock, called reefs, that slope deep underground. Some South African mines are among the deepest in the world, reaching more than three kilometres below the surface. Deep down, the rock is very hot, so huge fans and cooling systems pump cool air into the tunnels.

Miners drill holes into the rock face and blast it with explosives. The broken rock, called ore, is carried to the shaft and lifted to the surface in large containers. Most ore contains very little gold, often only a few grams in a whole tonne of rock. So a huge amount of rock must be mined to get a small amount of gold.

The questions for this section are with you now: where gold is mined in South Africa and how it is mined.

## Subtopic: From Rock to Gold Bar

At the surface, the ore goes to a processing plant near the mine. Here it is crushed into small pieces and then ground into a fine powder, like sand. Chemicals are used to dissolve the tiny pieces of gold out of the powder, and then the gold is collected again from the liquid. The rock powder that is left over is pumped onto mine dumps, which is why there are large pale dumps around mining towns.

The collected gold is melted in a very hot furnace and poured into moulds to make bars. These bars are not yet pure gold. They are sent to a refinery, where the gold is cleaned of other metals until it is almost completely pure. South Africa's main gold refinery is the Rand Refinery, in Germiston, Gauteng, which refines gold from many mines in South Africa and from other African countries.

Pure gold bars are very valuable. They are bought by banks, investors, coin makers and jewellery makers, in South Africa and around the world. South Africa exports much of its gold as bars. Some is made into coins, such as the Krugerrand, a famous South African gold coin.

At each stage, from mining, to processing, to refining, value is added, and many people are employed: miners, engineers, geologists, safety officers, truck and train drivers, plant workers and refinery workers.

The questions for this section are with you now: how gold ore is processed and refined into gold bars.

## Subtopic: From Gold Bar to Jewellery

Pure gold is very soft, so jewellery is usually made from gold mixed with other metals, such as silver and copper, to make it harder and stronger. This mixture is called an alloy. The amount of gold in jewellery is measured in carats. Twenty-four carat gold is almost pure gold. Eighteen carat gold is about three-quarters gold. Nine carat gold is a little more than one-third gold. Jewellery is often stamped with a tiny mark that shows its carat.

Jewellers, or goldsmiths, buy gold and design and make jewellery. They melt the gold, mix it into an alloy, pour it into moulds, roll it into sheets or pull it into wire, cut and bend it, join pieces together, add precious stones such as diamonds, and polish it. Some jewellery is made by hand in small workshops, like the one in Welkom, and some is made in large factories with machines.

A gold ring sells for much more than the value of the gold inside it, because of the design, skill, time, tools, advertising and the shop's costs. A lot of the world's gold jewellery is made in countries such as India, China and Italy, and some of it is imported back into South Africa. This means that, although South Africa mines gold, a lot of the value added from making jewellery is earned in other countries.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking gold is found in big lumps; most ore has only a few grams of gold in a tonne of rock. Two: thinking gold jewellery is always pure gold; it is usually an alloy. Three: thinking all the value is in the raw gold; design and skill add a lot of value. Four: thinking South Africa still produces the most gold in the world; it produces far less than it once did.

The questions for this section are with you now: how gold becomes jewellery and how value is added.
