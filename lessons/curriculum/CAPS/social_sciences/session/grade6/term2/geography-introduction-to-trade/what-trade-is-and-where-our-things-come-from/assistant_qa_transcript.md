### subtopic_1 — What Trade Is
**Assistant:** Why do labels say made in one country but assembled in another?

**Tutor:** Many modern goods are made in several steps, in different places. Take a cellphone. The design may come from one country, the tiny computer chips from another, the screen from a third and the battery from a fourth. Then all the parts are sent to a factory, often in Asia, where workers put them together. Putting parts together is called assembling. So a label might say designed in one country and assembled in another. Clothing is similar: cotton may be grown in one country, spun and woven in another, and sewn into a shirt in a third. This shows how connected the world is through trade. One product can travel thousands of kilometres, in pieces, before it reaches your school bag. It also means that many people in many countries had a job because of that one product.

### subtopic_2 — Trade Everywhere
**Assistant:** Is a spaza shop part of international trade?

**Tutor:** A spaza shop is a small local shop, usually run from a home, that sells everyday goods to people in the neighbourhood. When you buy bread or airtime there, that is local trade. But if you look at what is on its shelves, you may find international trade too. Rice might be grown in Asia, tinned fish might come from another country, and some sweets and cooldrinks might be made by international companies. The spaza owner buys stock from wholesalers, who buy from factories and importers, who buy from producers around the world. So even the smallest shop is at the end of long chains of trade that can stretch across the world. Spaza shops are also very important in South Africa, because they create jobs and make it easier for people to buy goods close to home.

### subtopic_3 — Our Term of Trade
**Assistant:** Can I trade a service, like helping someone?

**Tutor:** Yes. A service is something useful that one person does for another, and people often pay for services or exchange them. If a learner washes a neighbour's car for some money, that is selling a service. If two friends agree that one will help the other with Maths, and the other will help with English, that is exchanging services, a kind of barter. Many adults earn their living by selling services: teachers, doctors, plumbers, hairdressers, taxi drivers, shop assistants and computer programmers all do. In South Africa, services are a very large part of the economy, and tourism, banking, transport and communication are important services that bring money into the country. Sometimes people also help each other without being paid, as a kindness or as part of a community. That is generous, but it is not really trade.
