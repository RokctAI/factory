# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the Geography topic for Term 2: trade, with a focus on South Africa and the world. We look at what trade is, the key words for the topic, and the different levels at which trade happens. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Soweto, in Gauteng. On the first day of the topic, the teacher asks a learner to tip the contents of a school bag onto the front desk. Out come a lunch box with a sandwich and an apple, a bottle of cooldrink, a calculator, pens and pencils, an exercise book, a cellphone in a cover, and a jersey. The teacher writes the name of each item on the board, and the class tries to guess where each one came from, by reading labels, packets and stickers. They find words like made in South Africa, made in China, product of the Western Cape and assembled in Vietnam. Soon the board is covered in arrows pointing to places near and far. The big idea is that almost everything we use reaches us through trade, and that trade links our homes to the rest of the country and the world. The meaning of trade and its key words are examinable.

## Subtopic: What Trade Is

Trade is the buying and selling, or exchanging, of goods and services between people, businesses, regions or countries.

Goods are things you can touch, such as bread, shoes, cars, coal and oranges. Services are useful things that people do for us, such as teaching, nursing, fixing a car, cutting hair, driving a taxi or banking. We pay for both.

In trade, there is always someone who sells and someone who buys. The seller provides the goods or services, and the buyer pays for them, usually with money. Both sides should gain something: the buyer gets something they need or want, and the seller gets money that they can use to buy other things.

A producer is a person or business that makes goods or provides services, such as a farmer growing maize, a factory making shoes or a hairdresser. A consumer is a person who buys and uses goods and services. Every learner in the Soweto class is a consumer. Many people are both: a farmer produces maize, but also consumes bread, petrol and electricity.

A market is any place or system where buyers and sellers meet to trade. It can be a street market, a spaza shop, a big supermarket in a shopping mall, or an online shop on the internet.

Prices are part of trade. A price is the amount of money a seller asks for goods or a service. When many people want something and there is not much of it, the price usually goes up. When there is plenty of something and fewer people want it, the price usually goes down. This is why tomatoes can be cheap in summer and more expensive in winter.

The questions for this section are with you now: trade, goods, services, producers, consumers and markets.

## Subtopic: Trade Everywhere

Trade happens at different levels, from very small to very big.

Local trade happens within a community, a village, a town or a city. When a learner in Soweto buys vetkoek from a neighbour, or a family buys bread at the spaza shop on the corner, that is local trade.

National trade happens between different parts of the same country. Apples grown in the Western Cape are trucked to shops in Gauteng. Sugar from KwaZulu-Natal is sold in the Free State. Coal from Mpumalanga is used in power stations in other provinces. This trade happens inside South Africa's borders.

International trade happens between different countries. South Africa sells oranges and grapes to countries in Europe, and buys phones and computers from countries in Asia. Goods cross borders and oceans by ship, aeroplane, truck and train. Trade with our neighbours, such as Botswana, Namibia, Lesotho, Eswatini, Zimbabwe and Mozambique, is also international trade.

Trade is not new. In Term 1 we learnt how the people of Mapungubwe traded gold and ivory for glass beads and cloth across the Indian Ocean, about eight hundred years ago. Today trade is much faster and much bigger, because of ships that carry thousands of containers, aeroplanes, trucks, railways and the internet.

Trade is very important to South Africa. It creates jobs for people who produce, transport and sell goods. It earns money for the country. And it gives us goods that we cannot produce ourselves.

The questions for this section are with you now: local, national and international trade.

## Subtopic: Our Term of Trade

This term, we will answer several questions about trade.

Why do people trade? We will learn that no person, community or country can produce everything it needs, and that people trade their surplus, the extra they produce, for things they lack. We will learn how people traded goods directly, called barter, before money, and how money made trade easier.

What do people trade? We will learn the difference between raw materials, such as gold, wool and cocoa beans, manufactured goods, such as jewellery, jerseys and chocolate, and services and skills.

What is it worth? We will see why manufactured goods are usually worth much more than the raw materials they are made from, through two case studies: from cocoa to chocolate, and from gold to jewellery.

Is trade fair? We will look at the human cost of unfair trade, when workers and farmers are paid too little, and at fair trade projects that try to make trade fairer.

We will also learn the words exports, for goods a country sells to other countries, and imports, for goods it buys from other countries.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking trade only means big companies and ships; buying a loaf at the spaza shop is trade too. Two: thinking services are not part of trade; we buy and sell services every day. Three: thinking trade is a modern invention; people have traded for thousands of years. Four: thinking a person is either a producer or a consumer; most people are both.

The questions for this section are with you now: the questions we will study about trade this term.
