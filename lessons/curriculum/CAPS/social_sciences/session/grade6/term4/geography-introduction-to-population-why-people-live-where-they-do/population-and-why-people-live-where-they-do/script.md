# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the Term 4 Geography topic, population: why people live where they do, in South Africa and around the world. The anchor is a Grade 6 class at a school in Tzaneen, a town in the green, hilly farming country of Limpopo, known for its tea, avocados, mangoes and bananas. The teacher projects a famous satellite picture onto the board: the Earth at night, made by joining many photographs taken from space on cloudless nights. The class gasps. Some parts of the world are covered in bright clusters of city lights, while others are almost completely dark. A thin, glowing line runs through the dark Sahara, following the River Nile. The east of the United States, Europe, India and eastern China blaze with light. In South Africa, there is a big bright patch around Gauteng and smaller lights along the coasts, but large dark areas in the Northern Cape. The learners also find the faint lights of Tzaneen. A learner asks why people crowd into some places and leave others almost empty. The big idea is that people are spread very unevenly over the Earth, and that this is because of natural and human reasons. ## Subtopic: What Population Means

Population means the number of people who live in a particular place, such as a village, a town, a province, a country or the whole world. The study of population, including how many people there are, where they live and how this changes, is part of Geography.

To know how many people live in a country, governments hold a census. A census is an official count of all the people in a country, usually done every ten years or so. Census workers visit households or people fill in forms, sometimes online, answering questions about how many people live there, their ages, languages, work, schooling and services such as water and electricity. In South Africa, the census is run by Statistics South Africa, called Stats SA. Censuses were held in 1996, 2001, 2011 and 2022.

The 2022 census counted about sixty-two million people in South Africa. The world's population is much bigger. According to the United Nations, it passed eight billion people in 2022. That is eight thousand million people.

Governments need population information to plan where to build schools, clinics, roads, houses and water supplies. Businesses use it to decide where to open shops.

The questions for this section are with you now: what population means and how a census counts people.

## Subtopic: Natural Reasons for Where People Live

Throughout history, people have chosen to live in places that help them survive and live well. Some reasons are natural, linked to the environment.

The most important natural reason is water. People need water to drink, cook, wash, water crops and give to animals. That is why many settlements grew up next to rivers, lakes and springs, like the towns along the River Nile in Egypt, or along the Orange and Vaal Rivers in South Africa.

Climate matters too. Most people prefer places that are not too hot, too cold, too wet or too dry. Very few people live in the freezing polar areas or the driest deserts. In South Africa, more people live in the wetter east than in the dry west.

People also look for flat land, which is easier to build on and farm than steep mountains, and for fertile soil to grow food. Natural resources, such as wood, minerals and good grazing for animals, attract people too. Natural features such as a sheltered bay for a harbour, or a hill that is easy to defend, have also been important.

The area around Tzaneen is a good example. It has a warm climate, good summer rainfall, rivers flowing down from the mountains and fertile soil, so farms and villages are found all over the area. By contrast, the Kalahari and the dry parts of the Northern Cape have very little water, so people live far apart, mostly near boreholes, springs or rivers.

## Subtopic: Human Reasons for Where People Live

Other reasons are human, linked to what people do and decide.

Jobs are one of the strongest reasons today. People move to places where they can find work, such as cities with factories, offices and shops, or mining towns. Johannesburg grew very quickly after gold was found there in 1886, because thousands of people came to work on the mines.

Transport also matters. Settlements grow at ports, at river crossings, at railway junctions and where roads meet, because these are good places for trade. Services, such as schools, hospitals, clinics, shops, electricity and running water, attract people to towns and cities.

History and laws have shaped where people live as well. In South Africa, apartheid laws forced many people to live in certain areas because of their race, and the effects can still be seen today. Family ties, culture and religion also influence where people choose to live.

This term, we will look at the reasons for the location of settlements in South Africa, rural and urban areas, how population is spread across our provinces and the world, and a case study of a great city.

Two: thinking a census is a guess; it is an official count of people. Three: thinking only natural reasons decide where people live; jobs, transport, services and laws matter too.
