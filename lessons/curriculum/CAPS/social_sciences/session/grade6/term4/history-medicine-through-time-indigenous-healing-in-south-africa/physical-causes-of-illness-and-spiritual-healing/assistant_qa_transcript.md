### subtopic_1 — Physical and Spiritual Causes of Illness
**Assistant:** How long does it take to train as a traditional healer?

**Tutor:** Training differs between healers, regions and traditions, but it often takes a long time, sometimes several years. A person training to become a diviner usually lives with or works closely with an experienced healer, who becomes their teacher. During this time, which is known in isiZulu as ukuthwasa, the trainee learns about medicinal plants and how to prepare them, the rituals and ceremonies, the meaning of dreams and how to use divining objects, as well as rules of behaviour. Training often ends with a ceremony that marks the person's graduation as a healer. Herbalists also learn for years, often from older family members or teachers, about which plants to use and how much is safe.

### subtopic_2 — Kinds of Healers and Spiritual Healing
**Assistant:** Do doctors and traditional healers ever work together?

**Tutor:** In some places, they do. Because many South Africans visit traditional healers before or as well as going to a clinic, health officials have recognised that healers can play an important role. In some programmes, traditional healers have been given training about illnesses such as tuberculosis, HIV and diabetes, so that they can recognise the warning signs and refer patients to clinics for testing and treatment. Some healers have helped to spread health messages in their communities. Doctors, in turn, can learn to understand and respect their patients' beliefs, which helps patients trust them. Working together in this way can help people get the right care sooner.

### subtopic_3 — Indigenous Healing Today
**Assistant:** Is it all right to believe in both the ancestors and modern medicine?

**Tutor:** Many South Africans hold beliefs about the ancestors and also use modern medicine, and they do not see these as being in conflict. For example, a family may hold a ceremony to honour the ancestors when someone is ill and also make sure the person takes the medicine prescribed at the clinic. South Africa's Constitution protects freedom of religion, belief and opinion, so people may hold the beliefs that are meaningful to them. At the same time, it is important to follow medical advice for illnesses that science has shown can be treated, such as TB, which needs the full course of medicine to be cured. Respecting different beliefs and staying healthy can go together.
