# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how indigenous healers in South Africa understand physical causes of illness and spiritual healing. The anchor is a Grade 6 class at a school in eShowe, in the hills of KwaZulu-Natal, during Heritage Month in September. The school has invited a registered traditional healer to speak at assembly. She arrives wearing traditional beadwork and carries a small bag of dried roots and leaves. She explains, in isiZulu and then in English, that she is an isangoma, a diviner, and that she also knows many medicinal plants. She tells the learners that when someone comes to her, she asks not only where it hurts, but also about their family, their dreams and their relationships with others and with their ancestors. She says that some illnesses must be treated with plants, some need ceremonies, and some need a clinic, and that she sends people with serious illnesses such as tuberculosis to the clinic. The learners listen carefully and ask many questions. The big idea is that indigenous healing in South Africa understands illness as having both physical and spiritual causes, and treats the whole person, and that it continues alongside Western medicine today. ## Subtopic: Physical and Spiritual Causes of Illness

For many centuries, indigenous peoples of southern Africa have had their own systems of healing, with knowledge passed down from generation to generation, mostly by word of mouth.

In indigenous healing, illness can be understood as having physical causes. These include injuries such as cuts, burns and broken bones, eating spoiled food, drinking dirty water, insect bites and snakebites, cold weather, infections that spread from person to person, and old age. Healers treat these with plant medicines, ointments, steaming, setting bones and caring for wounds.

Many people also believe that some illnesses have spiritual causes. In many African belief systems, the ancestors, the spirits of family members who have died, continue to watch over and guide the living. If customs are neglected, or the ancestors are not honoured, some people believe this can lead to illness or misfortune. Some people also believe that illness can be caused by the ill will of other people.

Because of these beliefs, indigenous healing often looks at the whole person. It considers the body, the mind, the person's relationships with their family and community, and their relationship with the ancestors. A person is seen as healthy when all of these are in balance.

## Subtopic: Kinds of Healers and Spiritual Healing

There are different kinds of indigenous healers, with different names in different languages.

Herbalists are experts in medicines made from plants, roots, bark and sometimes animal products. In isiZulu, a herbalist is called an inyanga, and in isiXhosa an ixhwele. Herbalists learn which plants treat which illnesses, how to prepare them, and how much to give.

Diviners are healers who are believed to communicate with the ancestors in order to find out the cause of an illness or problem. In isiZulu, a diviner is called an isangoma, and in isiXhosa an igqirha. Many diviners say that they were called by their ancestors to become healers, sometimes through a period of illness or strong dreams. They then train for a long time under an experienced healer. To find the cause of a problem, a diviner may throw a set of bones, shells and other objects and read the pattern they make, or interpret dreams.

Spiritual healing may include ceremonies to honour the ancestors, such as slaughtering an animal and sharing a meal, singing, drumming and dancing, prayers, and protective charms. Some people also go to faith healers, who heal through prayer.

Other languages have their own words. In Sesotho and Setswana, a traditional healer is often called a ngaka, and the ancestors are called badimo. In isiZulu, the ancestors are called amadlozi. Different communities have different customs, so it is important not to assume that all indigenous healing is the same everywhere in South Africa.

The San had their own healing tradition. In healing dances, San healers entered a trance to draw sickness out of people. Many experts think that some San rock paintings show these trance dances.

## Subtopic: Indigenous Healing Today

Indigenous healing remains very important in South Africa today. Many South Africans consult traditional healers, and many use both traditional healers and Western medicine, such as clinics and hospitals.

During colonial times and apartheid, traditional healing was often looked down on, and some laws restricted it. Since democracy, it has been officially recognised. The Traditional Health Practitioners Act of 2007 recognises traditional healers and set up a council to register them and set standards, so that the public is protected.

In some areas, traditional healers and clinics work together. Healers have been trained to recognise the signs of serious illnesses such as tuberculosis and HIV, and to send patients to clinics for testing and treatment.

There are also concerns. Some plant remedies can be harmful if the wrong amount is taken, and if a person with a serious illness delays going to a clinic, they can become much sicker. That is why respect, honesty and cooperation between different kinds of healers matter.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking indigenous healing deals only with spirits; it also treats physical causes with plants and practical care. Two: thinking all traditional healers are the same; herbalists and diviners have different roles. Three: thinking traditional healing is illegal; it is recognised by law. Four: thinking people must choose only one kind of medicine; many use both.
