# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the use of indigenous plants to cure diseases in South Africa. The anchor is a Grade 6 class at a school in Alice, a small town in the Eastern Cape, home to the University of Fort Hare. The class visits a community garden where a local herbalist grows medicinal plants in neat beds, rather than collecting them all from the wild. He shows them a tall, spiky aloe and breaks a leaf to show the bitter yellow juice and the clear gel inside. He crushes a buchu leaf so that the learners can smell its strong, minty scent. He points out a grey, feathery bush called wild wormwood, which many families make into a tea for coughs and colds. Then he shows them a small plant with dark red flowers, which he calls umckaloabo, and tells them that its roots have been used for coughs and chest problems in this area for generations, and that companies overseas now make medicine from it. He warns the learners never to take a plant medicine without the advice of someone who knows, because some plants are poisonous. The big idea is that indigenous peoples of South Africa built up deep knowledge of healing plants over centuries, that some of this knowledge has fed into modern medicine, and that both the plants and the knowledge must be protected. ## Subtopic: South Africa's Healing Plants

South Africa has an enormous variety of plants, many of which grow nowhere else in the world. Over centuries, the San, the Khoikhoi and African farming communities learned which plants could heal. Thousands of South African plant species are used in traditional medicine.

Healers use different parts of plants: leaves, roots, bark, bulbs, flowers and seeds. These can be made into teas, boiled into mixtures, dried and ground into powders, made into ointments for the skin, or burned so that the smoke can be breathed in.

The bitter aloe, which grows in the Eastern and Western Cape, has thick leaves with a bitter yellow juice that has long been used to treat stomach problems, and a clear gel that is used on burns and the skin. Buchu, a small shrub from the mountains of the Western Cape, was used by the Khoikhoi and San for stomach and bladder problems and for wounds, and later became popular with European settlers too, who even exported it to Europe in the eighteen hundreds. Wild wormwood, called umhlonyane in isiZulu and isiXhosa, is used as a tea for coughs, colds, flu and fevers.

Knowledge of these plants was usually passed down by word of mouth, from older healers to younger ones and from grandparents to grandchildren. Healers knew where each plant grew, which season was best to collect it, which part to use and how much was safe. This is called indigenous knowledge, and it was built up by careful observation over many generations.

## Subtopic: From Traditional Remedy to Modern Medicine

Some indigenous plant knowledge has led to modern medicines and products.

Umckaloabo is a small geranium that grows in the Eastern Cape, Lesotho and nearby areas. Healers there used its roots for coughs, colds and chest problems. In the early nineteen hundreds, an Englishman who was ill with tuberculosis claimed that a healer's remedy made from it had helped him, and he took it to Europe. Today, companies in Europe make medicines from the plant for coughs and bronchitis, which are sold in many countries.

Hoodia is a spiny succulent from the dry Kalahari and Namaqualand. For a very long time, San hunters chewed pieces of it on long hunting trips to reduce hunger and thirst. Scientists in South Africa studied the plant, and companies became interested in using it in products to help people lose weight. The San said that they should benefit from their knowledge, and in 2003, an agreement was signed so that the San would receive a share of any money made from the plant.

Plants have given medicine many important drugs. For example, aspirin was developed from a substance found in willow bark, and a malaria medicine called quinine comes from the bark of a South American tree. Scientists in South Africa continue to test indigenous plants to find out which substances in them work, and whether they are safe.

## Subtopic: Protecting Plants and Knowledge

The use of medicinal plants brings challenges.

The first challenge is over-harvesting. Many medicinal plants are collected from the wild, and large numbers are sold in traditional medicine markets, such as a large market in Durban. When too many are dug up, or when bark is stripped from trees, the plants can die out in some areas. The pepper-bark tree, whose bark is used for colds and chest infections, has become rare in the wild because of this. Growing medicinal plants in gardens and nurseries, as the herbalist in Alice does, helps protect wild plants.

The second challenge is protecting knowledge. Indigenous knowledge belongs to the communities that discovered it. South African law now says that when companies use indigenous plants and knowledge to make products, the communities must agree and must share in the benefits, as in the hoodia agreement with the San.

The third challenge is safety. Some plants are poisonous, and the right amount matters. Plant medicines should be used only with expert advice, and serious illnesses should also be checked at a clinic.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking plant medicine is not real medicine; many modern medicines came from plants. Two: thinking all plants are safe because they are natural; some are poisonous. Three: thinking companies may freely use indigenous knowledge; communities must agree and share the benefits. Four: thinking wild medicinal plants can never run out; over-harvesting threatens some.
