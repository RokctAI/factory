### subtopic_1 — What Medicine Is
**Assistant:** Did ancient African societies besides Egypt have medicine?

**Tutor:** Yes. Across Africa, many societies developed their own knowledge of healing over thousands of years, although much of it was passed on by word of mouth rather than written down, so it is harder for historians to study. Healers learned which plants could treat wounds, fevers, stomach problems and other illnesses, and how to set broken bones. Written records from the city of Timbuktu, in West Africa, include old manuscripts on many subjects, which some scholars say include medicine. Some European travellers in the past described African healers performing skilled treatments. In southern Africa, indigenous healers have a long tradition of plant medicine and spiritual healing, which we will study in the next two lessons.

### subtopic_2 — Medicine in the Ancient World
**Assistant:** What is the Hippocratic Oath?

**Tutor:** The Hippocratic Oath is an ancient Greek promise linked to the name of Hippocrates, although historians are not sure he wrote it himself. In it, doctors promised to treat the sick to the best of their ability, to do no harm to their patients, and to keep private what they learned about patients. Today, doctors in many countries, including South Africa, take a modern oath or pledge when they qualify, based on similar ideas, such as respecting patients, keeping their information confidential, treating everyone equally and always putting the patient's health first. The idea of first, do no harm is still one of the most important principles in medicine.

### subtopic_3 — From the Islamic World to the Renaissance
**Assistant:** Why did people believe wrong ideas for so long?

**Tutor:** In the past, people could not see germs or look inside a living body, so they had to explain illness using what they could observe. Ideas such as the four humours seemed to make sense of symptoms like fever or vomiting. Respected teachers like Galen were trusted for centuries, and some people thought it was wrong to question them. Cutting up human bodies to study them was often not allowed, so mistakes were not discovered. Books had to be copied by hand, so new ideas spread slowly. Change came when people began to test ideas carefully, observe and experiment, share their results through printed books and journals, and later invent tools such as the microscope.
