# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the Term 4 History topic, medicine through time. The anchor is a Grade 6 class at a school in Thohoyandou, in the green hills of Vhembe in Limpopo. Their teacher asks the learners what their families do when someone is sick. One learner describes how her grandmother boils leaves from a plant in the garden to make a bitter tea for coughs. Another says his family goes straight to the clinic. A third says they do both: first a home remedy, then the clinic if it does not work. Then the school nurse visits and opens her first-aid kit, showing plasters, bandages, antiseptic cream and painkillers, and explains how each one helps. The teacher points out that the grandmother and the nurse are both trying to do the same thing: to heal. People have tried to heal illness and injury for as long as there have been people, she says, and their ideas have changed greatly over thousands of years. The big idea is that medicine has a long history, in which people in different places developed different ideas about the causes and cures of illness, some right and some wrong, and that knowledge grew step by step. The meaning of medicine and its early history are examinable.

## Subtopic: What Medicine Is

Medicine is the knowledge and practice of preventing, diagnosing and treating illness and injury. The word medicine also means a substance, such as a tablet or syrup, that is used to treat illness.

To diagnose means to find out what illness a person has, by asking questions, examining the body and doing tests. To treat means to try to cure the illness or ease its symptoms. To prevent means to stop people from getting ill in the first place, for example through clean water, good food and vaccines.

Every society in history has had healers, people with special knowledge of illness and cures. In southern Africa, there have long been indigenous healers who use plants and other remedies, and who also deal with spiritual matters. In other parts of the world, there were priests, herbalists, doctors and nurses.

When we study medicine through time, we look for change, the ways that ideas and practices have changed, and continuity, the ways they have stayed the same. For example, people still use plants as medicines today, which is continuity, but we now also understand germs, which is change.

Remember from Term 3 that South Africa's Constitution gives everyone the right to have access to health care services, which shows how important medicine is in our lives today.

The questions for this section are with you now: what medicine is and the ideas of change and continuity.

## Subtopic: Medicine in the Ancient World

In ancient Egypt, more than three thousand years ago, doctors wrote down their knowledge on papyrus scrolls. Some of these scrolls list hundreds of remedies made from plants, animals and minerals. Egyptian doctors set broken bones, bandaged wounds and put honey on them, which we now know can help to fight infection. But they also believed that gods and spirits caused some illnesses, so they used prayers and charms too.

In ancient Greece, about two and a half thousand years ago, a doctor called Hippocrates taught that illnesses have natural causes, not punishments from the gods. He told doctors to observe their patients carefully and to write down their symptoms. He is often called the father of Western medicine, and new doctors in many countries still take an oath, a promise to care for patients, based on ideas linked to his name.

The Greeks also believed that the body contains four fluids, called humours, and that illness happens when they are out of balance. This idea was wrong, but doctors in Europe believed it for nearly two thousand years.

The Romans built aqueducts to bring clean water to their cities, as well as public baths and sewers, which helped to keep people healthy. A Greek doctor in Rome called Galen studied anatomy by cutting up animals, and his books, including his mistakes, were followed for many centuries.

The questions for this section are with you now: medicine in ancient Egypt, Greece and Rome.

## Subtopic: From the Islamic World to the Renaissance

About a thousand years ago, scholars in the Islamic world, in cities such as Baghdad, Cairo and Córdoba, translated the old Greek medical books and added new knowledge of their own. They built hospitals that treated people from many backgrounds. A Persian doctor called Ibn Sina, known in Europe as Avicenna, wrote a huge medical encyclopaedia called The Canon of Medicine, which was used in European universities for hundreds of years.

In Europe, in the fourteenth century, a terrible disease called the Black Death spread, killing a very large share of the population, perhaps a third or more. Nobody knew what caused it.

During the Renaissance, which you studied in Term 2, people began to question old ideas. In 1543, a doctor called Andreas Vesalius published a book with detailed drawings of the human body, based on cutting up human bodies, which corrected many of Galen's mistakes. In 1628, William Harvey showed that the heart pumps blood around the body in a circle. The printing press helped spread these ideas.

This term, we will study indigenous healing in South Africa and then some of the great scientific discoveries that changed medicine: vaccination, germs, surgery and heart transplants.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking medicine began only recently; people have tried to heal for thousands of years. Two: thinking old ideas were all wrong; some, like using honey on wounds and clean water, were useful. Three: thinking the four humours idea was correct; it was wrong, though it lasted a long time. Four: thinking all medical knowledge came from Europe; Egypt, the Islamic world, Africa and Asia all contributed.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Islamic world, the Renaissance and what we will study.
