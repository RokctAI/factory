# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers heart surgery, Christiaan Barnard and the world's first human heart transplant. The anchor is a Grade 6 class from a school in Mitchells Plain, in Cape Town, on an outing to Groote Schuur Hospital, a large hospital on the slopes of Devil's Peak. In one part of the old hospital building is the Heart of Cape Town Museum, set up in the actual rooms where the first heart transplant took place. The learners walk into an operating theatre arranged exactly as it was in 1967, with life-size models of the doctors and nurses in their gowns and masks bending over two patients on two tables. Their guide explains that on one table lay a young woman who had died after a car accident, and on the other a man whose heart was failing. She shows them old newspaper front pages from around the world with huge headlines about a doctor from Cape Town. The learners are amazed that such a famous event happened in their own city. The big idea is that in 1967, Christiaan Barnard and his team at Groote Schuur Hospital performed the world's first human heart transplant, a milestone in the history of medicine that built on many earlier discoveries. Barnard and the first heart transplant are examinable.

## Subtopic: Christiaan Barnard and Heart Surgery

Christiaan Barnard was born in 1922 in Beaufort West, a small town in the Karoo, where his father was a church minister. He studied medicine at the University of Cape Town and became a doctor. In the nineteen-fifties, he went to the United States to train in heart surgery at the University of Minnesota, where some of the most important early heart operations were being done.

Heart surgery had become possible only recently. It depended on all the discoveries we studied in the last lesson: anaesthetics, sterile operating theatres, blood transfusions and X-rays. It also depended on a new machine called the heart-lung machine, which takes over the work of the heart and lungs during an operation, pumping blood and adding oxygen to it, so that the surgeon can stop the heart and repair it.

When Barnard returned to Cape Town, he set up a heart surgery unit at Groote Schuur Hospital and performed many open-heart operations. He and his team, including his brother Marius, also practised transplanting hearts in dogs in the hospital's research laboratory. Other surgeons, especially in the United States, had been doing similar research for years. The big remaining problem was rejection: the body's immune system attacks a new organ because it recognises it as foreign.

The questions for this section are with you now: Christiaan Barnard and the beginnings of heart surgery.

## Subtopic: The First Heart Transplant, 1967

The patient was Louis Washkansky, a fifty-three-year-old shop owner in Cape Town. His heart was badly damaged and failing, he also had diabetes, and he was not expected to live long. He agreed to take the risk of a transplant.

On the afternoon of the second of December 1967, Denise Darvall, a twenty-five-year-old bank worker, was crossing a road in Cape Town with her mother when they were hit by a car. Her mother was killed, and Denise suffered terrible brain injuries from which she could not recover. Her father, Edward Darvall, was asked whether her heart could be donated. He agreed, and is reported to have said that if they could not save his daughter, they must try to save this man.

That night and into the early morning of the third of December, Barnard and a team of about thirty doctors, nurses and technicians worked in two theatres. Denise's heart was removed and placed in Washkansky's chest, and the blood vessels were carefully stitched together. Then the surgeons gave the heart an electric shock. It started to beat. The operation was a success.

Washkansky woke up and was able to talk. The news flashed around the world, and Barnard became world famous overnight. But to stop his body from rejecting the new heart, Washkansky was given drugs that weakened his immune system. He caught pneumonia, a lung infection, and died eighteen days after the operation. The new heart had worked until the end.

## Subtopic: After 1967

In January 1968, Barnard performed a second heart transplant, on a dentist called Philip Blaiberg, who lived for about nineteen months and was able to go home. Around the world, many surgeons attempted heart transplants in the following year, but most patients died within weeks or months because of rejection and infection, and the number of operations dropped.

In the early nineteen-eighties, a new medicine called cyclosporine was introduced, which controlled rejection much better. After that, heart transplants became far more successful. Today, thousands of heart transplants are performed around the world each year, and many patients live for ten years or more.

The story also shows the injustice of apartheid. Hospitals were segregated by race. Hamilton Naki, a black laboratory assistant at Groote Schuur, had no formal medical training because of apartheid, yet he became highly skilled at surgery on animals in the research laboratory and helped train many doctors. Some later reports wrongly claimed that he took part in the first transplant, but his real skill was honoured in 2003 with an honorary degree from the University of Cape Town.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the first heart transplant happened in the United States; it happened in Cape Town. Two: thinking the new heart failed; it worked until Washkansky died of pneumonia. Three: thinking Barnard worked alone; a team of about thirty took part. Four: thinking heart transplants became safe immediately; rejection was a major problem until better medicines in the nineteen-eighties.

The questions for this section are with you now: what happened after 1967 and how transplants save lives today.
