# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the fight against infectious disease, the vaccination against smallpox and Edward Jenner. The anchor is a Grade 6 class at a school in Worcester, in the Breede River valley of the Western Cape. For a homework task, the learners brought their Road to Health booklets, the little books given to every baby at the clinic, which record their growth and their vaccinations. In class, they compare the vaccination pages. Every booklet has a list of dates and stamps for injections and drops given at birth, at six weeks, at ten and fourteen weeks and later, against diseases such as tuberculosis, polio, measles and whooping cough. A learner notices that none of them was vaccinated against smallpox, even though her grandfather has a small round scar on his arm from a smallpox vaccination. Their teacher explains that the children did not need it, because smallpox no longer exists, and that it was the first disease ever beaten by a vaccine. The big idea is that vaccination, first developed by Edward Jenner against smallpox, protects people from infectious diseases, and that it led to smallpox being wiped out completely. Jenner and the smallpox vaccine are examinable.

## Subtopic: Smallpox: A Deadly Disease

Smallpox was one of the most feared diseases in history. It was an infectious disease, which means it spread from person to person, through the air and by touching the sick person or their bedding and clothes. People with smallpox had a high fever and pains, and then their skin broke out in painful blisters filled with pus.

Smallpox killed about three in every ten people who caught it, and even more among young children. Many survivors were left with deep scars on their faces, and some became blind. For thousands of years, smallpox epidemics swept through towns and countries. By some estimates, it killed hundreds of millions of people in the twentieth century alone.

Smallpox also struck southern Africa. In 1713, a smallpox epidemic broke out at the Cape, thought to have come from a ship in Table Bay. It killed many settlers and enslaved people, and it had a devastating effect on the Khoikhoi of the south-western Cape, many of whom died. Later epidemics struck the Cape again.

Long before Jenner, people in parts of Africa, India, China and the Middle East had discovered a way to protect themselves, called inoculation. They took a small amount of material from a smallpox blister and rubbed it into a scratch on a healthy person's skin. Usually, the person caught a mild form of smallpox and was then protected for life. But it was risky: some people became very ill or died, and they could spread smallpox to others. In the seventeen-twenties, inoculation spread to Britain and to North America, where an enslaved African man named Onesimus described the practice from his homeland.

The questions for this section are with you now: smallpox and inoculation.

## Subtopic: Jenner's Experiment

Edward Jenner was a country doctor in the village of Berkeley, in the west of England. Like many country people, he had heard that milkmaids who caught cowpox, a mild disease of cows that caused a few sores on their hands, did not seem to catch smallpox.

Jenner decided to test this idea. In May 1796, a milkmaid called Sarah Nelmes came to him with cowpox sores on her hand. Jenner took some pus from one of her sores and put it into two small cuts on the arm of James Phipps, the eight-year-old son of his gardener. James became slightly unwell for a few days with a mild fever, then recovered.

About six weeks later, Jenner did something that would not be allowed today. He inoculated James with material from a real smallpox sore. James did not get smallpox. The cowpox had protected him. Jenner repeated his tests on a number of other people, and in 1798 he published his results in a short book.

Jenner called his method vaccination, from the Latin word vacca, meaning cow, because it used cowpox. A vaccine trains the body's defences, called the immune system, to recognise and fight a disease without the person having to suffer the disease itself. Vaccination was much safer than the old method of inoculation, because cowpox was a mild disease that did not spread like smallpox.

The questions for this section are with you now: Jenner's experiment and how vaccination works.

## Subtopic: From Opposition to Eradication

At first, not everyone trusted Jenner's vaccine. Some doctors who earned money from the old method of inoculation opposed it. Some people thought it was wrong to put material from an animal into a human. A famous cartoon showed vaccinated people growing cow heads. But as more and more people were vaccinated and stayed healthy, the vaccine spread across Europe, to the Americas, to Asia and to Africa within a few years.

Over the next century and a half, many countries made vaccination against smallpox compulsory. In 1967, the World Health Organization began a worldwide campaign to wipe out smallpox. Health workers tracked down every case and vaccinated everyone around the sick person, which stopped the disease from spreading. The last natural case of smallpox was in Somalia in 1977, and in 1980, the World Health Organization declared that smallpox had been eradicated, which means wiped out completely. It is the only human disease ever wiped out in this way.

Jenner's work started the science of vaccines, which today protect people against many diseases, such as measles, polio and tuberculosis, which is why the Worcester learners received their vaccines as babies.

The questions for this section are with you now: opposition to vaccination and the eradication of smallpox.
