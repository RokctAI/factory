# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the discoveries that made surgery possible: anaesthetics, avoiding infection, blood transfusions and X-rays. The anchor is a Grade 6 class at a school in Krugersdorp, in the Mogale City area on the West Rand of Gauteng. A learner's mother, who is a theatre nurse at a large hospital, visits the class in her green scrubs, with a cap and a mask. She explains what happens before an operation. The patient is given an anaesthetic, so that they fall into a deep sleep and feel no pain. Everyone in the theatre scrubs their hands and arms, and wears sterile gowns, gloves and masks. All the instruments are sterilised in a machine that uses very hot steam. Before many operations, the patient has X-rays or scans so that the surgeon can see inside the body, and bags of donated blood are ready in case the patient loses too much. A learner asks whether surgery was always like this. The nurse shakes her head and says that two hundred years ago, an operation was a terrifying and often deadly experience. The big idea is that surgery became safe only after discoveries in the eighteen hundreds and early nineteen hundreds solved the problems of pain, infection and blood loss, and allowed doctors to see inside the body. These discoveries are examinable.

## Subtopic: Conquering Pain

Before the eighteen-forties, there were no effective anaesthetics. Patients were awake during operations. They were sometimes given alcohol or opium, and they were held or strapped down by strong assistants. The pain was so terrible that many patients died of shock. Surgeons worked as fast as they could, and some could cut off a leg in less than a minute. Only operations on the outside of the body, such as removing limbs, were usually attempted.

In the eighteen-forties, doctors and dentists in the United States began experimenting with gases that made people unconscious. In 1846, a dentist called William Morton gave a patient ether to breathe at a hospital in Boston, and a surgeon removed a lump from the patient's neck while he slept, feeling no pain. News spread quickly across the world.

In 1847, a Scottish doctor called James Simpson discovered that chloroform also worked, and it was easier to use than ether. When Queen Victoria of Britain used chloroform during the birth of one of her children in 1853, many people began to accept anaesthetics. Early anaesthetics had dangers, and some patients died from too much chloroform, but over time safer drugs and methods were developed.

The questions for this section are with you now: the problem of pain and the discovery of anaesthetics.

## Subtopic: Conquering Infection and Blood Loss

Even with anaesthetics, many patients died after operations, because their wounds became infected. Surgeons wore old, bloodstained coats and did not wash their instruments or hands between patients.

Joseph Lister, a British surgeon working in Glasgow, read about Pasteur's discovery that germs cause decay. He thought that germs might cause the infection in wounds. In 1865, he began to use carbolic acid, a chemical that kills germs, to clean wounds, dressings and instruments, and later sprayed it in the air during operations. One of his first patients was a boy with a badly broken leg, where the bone had pierced the skin, which usually led to deadly infection. The leg healed without infection. The number of Lister's patients who died after surgery fell sharply. Chemicals that kill germs are called antiseptics.

Later, surgeons moved to aseptic surgery, which means keeping germs out of the operating theatre altogether, with steam-sterilised instruments, scrubbed hands, sterile gowns, masks and rubber gloves.

Blood loss was the third problem. Doctors had tried giving blood from one person to another, but many patients had dangerous reactions and died. In 1901, an Austrian scientist called Karl Landsteiner discovered that people have different blood groups, and that transfusions are safe only when the blood groups match. Later, scientists found ways to stop stored blood from clotting and to keep it cold, so that blood banks could be set up. Today, the South African National Blood Service collects blood from volunteer donors.

The questions for this section are with you now: antiseptics, aseptic surgery and blood transfusions.

## Subtopic: Seeing Inside the Body

The fourth great discovery let doctors see inside the body without cutting it open. In November 1895, a German scientist called Wilhelm Röntgen was experimenting with electricity in glass tubes when he noticed a mysterious glow. He discovered invisible rays that could pass through soft flesh but not through bone or metal. He called them X-rays, because X stands for something unknown. One of the first X-ray pictures showed the bones of his wife's hand, with her ring.

Within months, hospitals around the world were using X-rays to find broken bones and swallowed objects, and army doctors used them to find bullets, including during the South African War of 1899 to 1902. Röntgen received the first Nobel Prize in Physics in 1901. Later, people learned that too many X-rays can harm the body, so today X-ray staff wear protective lead aprons.

A South African, Allan Cormack, born in Johannesburg, helped to develop the mathematics behind CT scanners, which use X-rays to make detailed pictures of slices of the body, and he shared a Nobel Prize in 1979. Today, doctors also use other ways of seeing inside the body without X-rays, such as ultrasound, which uses sound waves and is used to check on babies before they are born, and MRI scans, which use very strong magnets.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking patients were always asleep during surgery; before anaesthetics they were awake. Two: thinking Lister discovered germs; Pasteur did, and Lister used his ideas. Three: thinking any blood can be given to any patient; blood groups must match. Four: thinking X-rays are completely harmless; too many can harm the body.

The questions for this section are with you now: X-rays and seeing inside the body.
