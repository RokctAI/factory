### subtopic_1 — Conquering Pain
**Assistant:** Who can donate blood in South Africa?

**Tutor:** In South Africa, people can usually donate blood from the age of sixteen, as long as they are healthy, weigh at least fifty kilograms and meet the other health requirements of the South African National Blood Service. Donors answer questions about their health and lifestyle, and a small drop of blood is tested to check that they have enough iron. The donation itself takes only about ten minutes, and the body replaces the blood quickly. One donation can help save several lives, because the blood can be separated into different parts, such as red cells, plasma and platelets, for different patients. Blood is needed every day for accident victims, mothers who lose blood in childbirth, and people having operations or cancer treatment.

### subtopic_2 — Conquering Infection and Blood Loss
**Assistant:** What is the difference between an X-ray and a CT scan?

**Tutor:** An ordinary X-ray sends rays through the body from one direction and makes a flat picture, like a shadow, in which bones show up white. It is quick and very useful for broken bones and chest problems, but organs can overlap on the picture. A CT scan also uses X-rays, but the machine moves around the body, taking many X-ray measurements from different angles. A computer then uses mathematics to build up detailed pictures of thin slices through the body, and even three-dimensional images. This lets doctors see organs, blood vessels and injuries much more clearly. Allan Cormack, born in Johannesburg, worked out key parts of this mathematics, and Godfrey Hounsfield in Britain built the first scanner.

### subtopic_3 — Seeing Inside the Body
**Assistant:** Why were rubber gloves first used in surgery?

**Tutor:** According to a well-known story from the eighteen-nineties, an American surgeon called William Halsted asked a rubber company to make thin rubber gloves for a nurse in his operating theatre, because the strong chemicals used to clean hands were damaging her skin. The gloves protected her hands so well that other members of the surgical team began to wear them too. Over time, doctors realised that gloves also helped to keep germs from their hands out of patients' wounds, so they became a normal part of aseptic surgery. Today, surgeons and nurses wear sterile gloves that are thrown away after every operation, together with sterile gowns and masks.
