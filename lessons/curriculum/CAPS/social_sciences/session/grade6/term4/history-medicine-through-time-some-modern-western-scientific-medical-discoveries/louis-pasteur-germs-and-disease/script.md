# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the connection between germs and disease and the work of Louis Pasteur. The anchor is a Grade 6 class from a school in Bethlehem, a farming town in the eastern Free State, on an outing to a dairy just outside the town. The learners watch through a glass window as fresh milk from the farm's cows flows through shining steel pipes. The dairy manager explains that the milk is heated to about seventy-two degrees Celsius for a short time, then quickly cooled and packed into cartons. She holds up a carton and points to the word pasteurised. Heating the milk, she says, kills harmful germs that could make people ill and makes the milk last longer. A learner asks where the word pasteurised comes from. The manager smiles and says it is named after a French scientist called Louis Pasteur, who lived more than a hundred and fifty years ago and proved that tiny, invisible living things cause milk to go sour and people to get sick. The big idea is that Louis Pasteur proved the connection between germs and disease, which changed medicine for ever and led to pasteurisation and new vaccines. Pasteur and germ theory are examinable.

## Subtopic: Old Ideas About Disease

Before Pasteur, people did not know that germs caused disease. Germs, also called microbes or micro-organisms, are tiny living things, such as bacteria, that are far too small to see without a microscope.

Microscopes had been invented in the sixteen hundreds, and some scientists had seen tiny living things in water and other substances. But most people did not believe that such tiny creatures could make people ill.

Instead, people had other explanations. Many believed in the miasma theory, the idea that diseases were caused by bad air, or poisonous smells, from rotting rubbish, sewage and swamps. Others still believed in the old idea of the four humours being out of balance. Many scientists also believed in spontaneous generation, the idea that living things, such as maggots on rotting meat or tiny creatures in spoiled broth, appeared from nothing, created by the rotting itself.

Some people came close to the truth. In 1847, a Hungarian doctor called Ignaz Semmelweis, working in a hospital in Vienna, noticed that many mothers were dying of fever after giving birth when they were treated by doctors who came straight from cutting up dead bodies. When he made the doctors wash their hands in a chlorine solution, far fewer mothers died. But many doctors refused to believe him.

## Subtopic: Pasteur Proves Germ Theory

Louis Pasteur was born in France in 1822. He was a chemist, not a doctor. In the eighteen-fifties, makers of wine, beer and vinegar asked him why their drinks sometimes went bad. Looking through his microscope, he found that tiny living organisms, called yeasts, were needed to make alcohol, but that other microbes made the drinks go sour.

Pasteur then showed that gently heating wine killed the harmful microbes and stopped it from spoiling, without ruining its taste. This process was later used for milk and other foods, and became known as pasteurisation.

To prove that germs come from the air and do not appear from nothing, Pasteur carried out a famous experiment in the early eighteen-sixties. He put broth, a kind of soup, into glass flasks and bent their long necks into an S shape, like the neck of a swan. He boiled the broth to kill any germs. Air could enter the flasks, but dust and germs from the air got trapped in the curve of the neck. The broth stayed fresh for a long time. But when he broke off the neck or tipped the flask so that the broth touched the trapped dust, the broth soon went bad.

This proved that spontaneous generation was wrong, and that microbes from the air make things go bad. Pasteur argued that germs also cause diseases in animals and people. This idea is called germ theory. Pasteur was not the only scientist working on it, and other scientists soon built on his work, as we will see in the next lesson about Robert Koch.

## Subtopic: Vaccines and Pasteur's Legacy

Pasteur went on to develop new vaccines. In about 1879, while studying chicken cholera, his team found that an old, weakened culture of the germs did not make chickens ill, but protected them from fresh, strong germs. Pasteur realised that weakened germs could be used as vaccines. He named all such treatments vaccines, in honour of Jenner's work.

In 1881, Pasteur held a public test of a vaccine against anthrax, a deadly disease of sheep and cattle. He vaccinated half of a group of sheep and left the other half unvaccinated. Then he gave all of them anthrax germs. The vaccinated sheep survived, while the unvaccinated sheep died. In 1885, he used a vaccine against rabies, a deadly disease spread by the bites of infected animals, on a nine-year-old boy called Joseph Meister, who had been badly bitten by a dog. The boy survived.

Pasteur's work changed medicine and daily life. It led to better hygiene, such as washing hands, boiling water and sterilising equipment, and it inspired surgeons to fight infection. In 1888, the Pasteur Institute opened in Paris to study diseases and vaccines.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking Pasteur was a doctor; he was a chemist. Two: thinking germs appear from nothing; his flask experiment proved they come from the air. Three: thinking pasteurisation boils food until it is ruined; it heats it gently to kill harmful germs. Four: thinking Pasteur invented the first vaccine; Jenner did, and Pasteur developed new ones using weakened germs.
