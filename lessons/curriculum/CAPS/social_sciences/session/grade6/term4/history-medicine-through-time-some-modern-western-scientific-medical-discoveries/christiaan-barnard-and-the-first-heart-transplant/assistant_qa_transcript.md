### subtopic_1 — Christiaan Barnard and Heart Surgery
**Assistant:** How can someone become an organ donor in South Africa?

**Tutor:** In South Africa, adults can register as organ donors with organisations such as the Organ Donor Foundation of South Africa, and they receive a card or sticker to show that they want to donate. Just as important, they should tell their family, because doctors usually ask the family for permission after a person has died. Organs such as the heart, lungs, liver and kidneys, and tissues such as corneas from the eyes, can save or improve the lives of many people. Some people also donate a kidney while they are alive, often to a family member, because people can live healthily with one kidney. In South Africa, many people are waiting for organs, and there are not enough donors.

### subtopic_2 — The First Heart Transplant, 1967
**Assistant:** Why was the first heart transplant controversial?

**Tutor:** The operation raised difficult questions. One was about death: doctors had to decide when a person was truly dead, especially when the brain was badly injured but the heart could still beat with help. This led to new medical rules about brain death. Some people also felt that the heart, long seen as the centre of life and feelings, should not be moved from one person to another, while others argued that it was simply a pump. Others questioned whether the risk to patients was worth it, since the first patients lived only a short time. The story also took place under apartheid, when hospitals were segregated, which raised questions about who could receive the best care.

### subtopic_3 — After 1967
**Assistant:** What is the Heart of Cape Town Museum?

**Tutor:** The Heart of Cape Town Museum is a museum at Groote Schuur Hospital in Cape Town that tells the story of the world's first human heart transplant. It is set in the original rooms of the hospital where the operation took place in 1967. Visitors can see the two operating theatres arranged as they were, with life-size models of the surgical team, the patient and the donor, as well as original equipment, photographs, letters and newspaper reports from around the world. The museum also tells the stories of Louis Washkansky, Denise Darvall and her family, and the many people who worked behind the scenes. It helps visitors understand both the science and the human side of the event.
