# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the germs that cause TB and Robert Koch. The anchor is a Grade 6 class at a school in Gqeberha, on Algoa Bay in the Eastern Cape, on the twenty-fourth of March, World TB Day. A nurse from the local clinic visits the class with posters and a small model of a pair of lungs. She explains that tuberculosis, usually called TB, is a disease caused by germs that mostly attack the lungs, and that South Africa has one of the highest numbers of TB cases in the world. She tells the learners the signs to watch for: a cough that lasts more than two weeks, losing weight, sweating at night and fever. Then she says something that surprises them: TB can be cured, completely, if a person takes the right medicine every day for six months. A learner asks why World TB Day is on the twenty-fourth of March. The nurse explains that it was on that day, in 1882, that a German doctor named Robert Koch announced that he had found the germ that causes TB. The big idea is that Robert Koch developed methods to identify the exact germs that cause particular diseases, including TB, and that his discovery made it possible to fight TB. Koch and the TB germ are examinable.

## Subtopic: Robert Koch and His Methods

Robert Koch was born in Germany in 1843 and trained as a doctor. For several years, he worked as a country doctor in a small town. According to a well-known story, his wife gave him a microscope as a birthday present, and he began to study germs in his spare time.

Pasteur had shown that germs cause decay and argued that they cause disease. Koch went further. He wanted to prove which particular germ causes which particular disease. In 1876, he showed that a specific rod-shaped bacterium causes anthrax, the disease of sheep and cattle that Pasteur also studied.

To do this, Koch and his team developed important new methods that scientists still use today. They used dyes to stain bacteria, so that the tiny, almost see-through germs could be seen clearly under the microscope. They grew bacteria on a solid jelly, so that each kind of germ formed its own separate spot, called a colony, which could be studied on its own. A member of his team, Julius Petri, designed the shallow glass dish with a lid that is still called a Petri dish. Koch also photographed bacteria through the microscope, so that other scientists could see what he had seen.

Koch set out careful rules for proving that a germ causes a disease: the germ must be found in every sick animal, grown on its own in the laboratory, cause the same disease when put into a healthy animal, and be found again in that animal.

## Subtopic: Discovering the Germ That Causes TB

Tuberculosis was one of the deadliest diseases in the world in the eighteen hundreds. In Europe, it killed a very large number of people, especially in crowded, damp cities. Many people believed it was inherited, passed down in families, because it often affected several members of one family.

TB germs are very small and very hard to stain and grow, because they grow slowly and have a waxy coat. Koch worked for months to find the right dyes and methods. Finally, he managed to stain the TB germs and see them as tiny rods in the lungs of people who had died of TB. He grew them in the laboratory, and showed that they caused TB in animals.

On the twenty-fourth of March 1882, in Berlin, Koch announced to a meeting of scientists that he had found the germ that causes tuberculosis. Many in the room were stunned. This proved that TB is an infectious disease, spread from person to person, not something that is simply inherited. World TB Day is held on that date every year.

Koch later went to Egypt and India and identified the germ that causes cholera, a disease spread through dirty water. He was awarded the Nobel Prize in 1905. Not all his ideas worked: a treatment for TB that he announced in 1890 failed as a cure. Koch and Pasteur were rivals, partly because France and Germany were rivals at the time, but their work together founded the modern science of germs.

## Subtopic: TB in South Africa Today

TB is still a serious problem in South Africa, which has one of the highest rates of TB in the world. It is a leading cause of death, partly because people with HIV are much more likely to get ill with TB.

TB spreads through the air. When a person who is sick with TB in the lungs coughs, sneezes or talks, they release tiny droplets containing germs, which others can breathe in. It spreads most easily in crowded, poorly ventilated rooms.

TB can be prevented and cured. Babies in South Africa are given the BCG vaccine at birth, which helps protect young children from severe TB. Opening windows to let in fresh air, covering your mouth when you cough and getting tested early all help. TB tests and treatment are free at government clinics. The medicine must be taken every day for about six months, even when the person starts to feel better. If people stop early, the germs can become resistant to the medicine, which is much harder to treat.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking TB is inherited; Koch proved it is caused by a germ that spreads through the air. Two: thinking TB cannot be cured; it can be cured with about six months of medicine. Three: thinking you can stop taking TB medicine when you feel better; you must finish the course. Four: thinking Koch and Pasteur worked as a team; they were rivals, though both advanced germ theory.
