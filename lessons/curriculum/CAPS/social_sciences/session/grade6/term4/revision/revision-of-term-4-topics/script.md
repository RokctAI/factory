# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the Term 4 topics. In Geography, the topic was population: why people live where they do, settlements in South Africa, rural and urban areas, population distribution and density, the provinces, population around the world and a case study of Cairo. In History, the topic was medicine through time: indigenous healing in South Africa and modern Western scientific discoveries, from Jenner's vaccine to Barnard's heart transplant. The anchor is a revision afternoon for a Grade 6 class in Richards Bay, a port town on the north coast of KwaZulu-Natal, known for its deep-water harbour, coal terminal and aluminium smelters. The class divides the classroom into three corners. Corner 1 has a map of South Africa covered in dots and a bar graph of the provinces. Corner 2 has a world map with red and blue stickers and a picture of Cairo beside the Nile. Corner 3 has a timeline running along the wall, from a healer's bag of dried plants to a model heart. Groups move from corner to corner, answering the questions on each card. The big idea is that people live where nature and human activities make life possible, and that medicine has advanced through the knowledge of many people over time. The whole of Term 4 is examinable.

## Subtopic: People in South Africa

At Corner 1, the class remembers South Africa. Population is the number of people in a place, counted in a census by Stats SA; the 2022 census counted about sixty-two million people. Settlements grew where there was water, such as rivers and springs, a good climate and rainfall, useful vegetation, flat land, sheltered harbours and mountain passes. Resources and human activities created towns too: diamonds at Kimberley, gold at Johannesburg, coal at eMalahleni, fishing on the west coast and trade at ports such as Durban and Richards Bay. Under apartheid, laws such as the Natives Land Act and the Group Areas Act forced people into certain areas, as at District Six and Sophiatown.

Rural areas are the countryside, with farms and villages, and urban areas are towns and cities. Urbanisation is the growth in the share of people living in towns and cities, driven by push factors, such as a lack of jobs and services, and pull factors, such as jobs, schools and hospitals.

Population distribution is the pattern of where people live, and population density is the number of people divided by the area. South Africa's average density is about fifty people per square kilometre. Gauteng has the most people and the highest density, and the Northern Cape, the largest province, has the fewest people and the lowest density.

When reading graphs of the provinces, always check the title, the axes, the scale and the units before comparing bars. Remember that a province with many people is not always the most crowded, because its area matters too.

The questions for this section are with you now: settlements, urbanisation, distribution and density in South Africa.

## Subtopic: People Around the World

At Corner 2, the class remembers the world. The world's population passed eight billion in 2022, but it is spread very unevenly. About six in ten people live in Asia, and India and China each have more than one point four billion people. Crowded regions include South and East Asia, Europe, the north-east of the United States, West Africa and the Nile valley. Deserts, cold lands, rainforests and high mountains are sparsely populated. Fertile river plains, reliable rain, coasts and jobs attract people, while cold, dry, steep land, dense forest and disease discourage them.

A megacity has more than ten million people. Many of the largest cities are in Asia, such as Tokyo and Delhi, and in Africa there are Cairo, Lagos and Kinshasa. Cairo, the capital of Egypt, lies on the Nile where the valley opens into the delta, in a desert country. It grew there because the Nile gave water, fertile soil and transport, its position helped trade, and it has been a capital for more than a thousand years.

The questions for this section are with you now: world population and the case study of Cairo.

## Subtopic: Medicine Through Time

At Corner 3, the class walks along the timeline. It begins long ago, with remedies in ancient Egypt, Hippocrates' search for natural causes and Ibn Sina's great medical book, and shows both change and continuity. Indigenous healers in South Africa understand illness as having physical causes and, in many people's beliefs, spiritual causes linked to the ancestors. Herbalists heal with plants, and diviners seek causes through the ancestors. Plants such as aloe, buchu, wild wormwood, umckaloabo and hoodia are part of a rich indigenous knowledge that must be protected, along with the plants themselves. Since 2007, the law has recognised traditional healers, and many South Africans use both traditional healers and clinics.

In 1796, Edward Jenner used cowpox to protect against smallpox, creating the first vaccine, and smallpox was declared eradicated in 1980. Louis Pasteur proved that germs cause decay and disease, invented pasteurisation and made vaccines from weakened germs. Robert Koch found the germ that causes TB in 1882. Surgery became safe with anaesthetics in the eighteen-forties, Lister's antiseptics from 1865, X-rays in 1895 and blood groups in 1901. In December 1967, Christiaan Barnard's team performed the world's first heart transplant at Groote Schuur Hospital in Cape Town. Each discovery built on earlier ones, and today vaccines, antibiotics and safe surgery save millions of lives.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: mixing up distribution and density; density is people divided by area. Two: thinking the largest province has the most people; the Northern Cape is largest but has the fewest. Three: thinking Jenner used smallpox for his vaccine; he used cowpox. Four: thinking the first heart transplant happened overseas; it happened in Cape Town.

The questions for this section are with you now: indigenous healing and modern medical discoveries.
