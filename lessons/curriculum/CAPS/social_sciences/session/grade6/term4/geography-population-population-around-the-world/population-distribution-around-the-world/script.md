# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how population is distributed around the world. The anchor is a Grade 6 class at a school in Newcastle, an industrial town in northern KwaZulu-Natal. The teacher has pinned a large world map onto the wall and gives each group a sheet of red and blue stickers. Red stickers are for places where many people live, and blue stickers for places where very few people live. Using their atlases and a world population map, the groups get to work. Soon, red stickers crowd onto eastern China, India and Bangladesh, the island of Java in Indonesia, Japan, Europe, the north-east of the United States, Nigeria in West Africa and a thin line along the River Nile. Blue stickers spread across the Sahara, the deserts of Arabia and Australia, Siberia, northern Canada, Greenland, the Amazon rainforest, the Himalaya mountains and Antarctica. When they step back, the learners are surprised by how much of the map is blue. The big idea is that the world's population is spread very unevenly, and that most people live in a few crowded regions, mainly because of natural conditions and human activities. World population distribution is examinable.

## Subtopic: Crowded Regions of the World

The world's population passed eight billion people in 2022, according to the United Nations. But these people are not spread evenly over the land.

Asia has by far the most people. About six out of every ten people in the world live in Asia. The two countries with the most people, India and China, each have more than one point four billion people. South Asia, including India, Pakistan and Bangladesh, and East Asia, including eastern China, Japan and Korea, are among the most crowded regions on Earth. Southeast Asia, especially the island of Java, is also very crowded.

Africa has the second largest population of any continent, about one and a half billion people, and its population is growing faster than that of any other continent. Many people live in West Africa, especially Nigeria, along the River Nile in Egypt, around the Great Lakes of East Africa, in Ethiopia and in parts of southern Africa.

Europe is densely populated, especially in the west. In North America, the north-east of the United States and south-eastern Canada are crowded, as are parts of California. In South America, most people live near the coasts, especially in south-eastern Brazil.

Overall, most of the world's people live in the Northern Hemisphere, because most of the Earth's land is there.

Today, more than half of the world's people live in towns and cities rather than in rural areas. Some of the world's largest cities, such as Tokyo, Delhi, Shanghai and Dhaka, are in Asia, and each has tens of millions of people in its wider city area. We will look more closely at major cities in the next lesson.

The questions for this section are with you now: the most densely populated regions of the world.

## Subtopic: Empty Regions of the World

Large areas of the world are sparsely populated, with very few people.

Hot deserts, such as the Sahara, the Arabian Desert, the Kalahari and the deserts of central Australia, have very few people, because there is so little water. People live mainly at oases, along rivers and in a few cities.

Cold areas, such as Siberia in Russia, northern Canada, Alaska and Greenland, have long, freezing winters, so few people live there. Antarctica has no permanent population at all, only scientists who stay at research stations for months at a time.

Tropical rainforests, such as the Amazon and the Congo Basin, are sparsely populated because the dense forest makes travel and farming difficult, the soil is poor, and there are diseases. Indigenous people live there, but in small numbers.

High mountains, such as the Himalayas, the Andes and the high plateau of Tibet, are sparsely populated, because they are cold, steep and hard to farm, and the air is thin.

The questions for this section are with you now: the sparsely populated regions of the world.

## Subtopic: Reasons for the Pattern

The world's population pattern can be explained by positive and negative factors.

Positive factors attract people. The most crowded regions often have flat, fertile land, such as the great river plains and deltas of the Ganges in India and Bangladesh, the Yangtze in China and the Nile in Egypt, where rich soil and water allow farmers to grow a lot of food. A moderate climate with reliable rain helps too, such as the monsoon rains that water the rice fields of South and East Asia. Coasts attract people for trade and fishing. Natural resources, industry, jobs and services attract people to cities.

Negative factors discourage people from living in a place. These include climates that are too cold or too dry, land that is too steep or too high, dense forest, poor soil and disease.

History also plays a part. Some regions, such as the river valleys of China, India and Egypt, have been farmed for thousands of years, and large populations grew up there over a very long time.

The same factors explain the pattern in South Africa. Just as the world's people crowd onto fertile plains and coasts and avoid deserts, South Africans crowd into the wetter east and the cities, and few live in the dry Northern Cape and the Kalahari.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the world's people are spread evenly over the land; most live in a few crowded regions. Two: thinking Africa has the most people; Asia has by far the most. Three: thinking Antarctica has towns; it has no permanent population, only research stations. Four: thinking rainforests are crowded because they are full of life; they are full of plants and animals, but few people.

The questions for this section are with you now: the natural and human reasons for the world's population pattern.
