# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the total population and average population density of South Africa's nine provinces, and how to read graphs about them. The anchor is a Grade 6 class at a school in Komani, the town formerly called Queenstown, in the Eastern Cape. Their teacher has drawn two large bar graphs on the board. The first is titled Population of the provinces, 2022 census. Along the bottom are the names of the nine provinces, and up the side is a scale in millions of people. The bar for Gauteng is so tall that it almost reaches the top of the board, while the bar for the Northern Cape is tiny. The second graph is titled Population density of the provinces, with a scale in people per square kilometre. Here, Gauteng's bar is even more dramatic, many times taller than any other. One learner points out that the Eastern Cape has a tall bar on the first graph, but a short one on the second, and asks how that can be. The big idea is that graphs help us compare the provinces, and that the number of people and the population density tell different stories, because the provinces are very different in size. Reading graphs of the population and density of the provinces is examinable.

## Subtopic: Reading Graphs

Graphs show information as a picture, so that we can compare numbers quickly. To read any graph correctly, follow these steps.

First, read the title, which tells you what the graph is about and often the date and source. Second, read the labels on the two axes. The horizontal axis along the bottom often shows the categories, such as the names of provinces, and the vertical axis up the side shows the values, such as numbers of people. Third, check the scale and the units. Are the numbers in thousands or millions? Is density in people per square kilometre? Fourth, read the value of each bar by following its top across to the scale. Finally, compare the bars and look for the highest, the lowest and any patterns.

A bar graph uses bars of different heights to compare separate categories, such as provinces. A line graph uses points joined by a line to show how something changes over time, such as South Africa's population from one census to the next. A pie chart shows how a whole is divided into parts, such as the share of the country's people living in each province.

For example, a pie chart of South Africa's population in 2022 would show Gauteng's slice as about a quarter of the circle and KwaZulu-Natal's slice as about a fifth, while the Northern Cape's slice would be a very thin sliver. When you compare two graphs, check that they use the same year and the same source, otherwise the comparison may not be fair.

The questions for this section are with you now: how to read bar graphs, line graphs and pie charts.

## Subtopic: Population of the Provinces

According to the 2022 census, the populations of the provinces, rounded, were as follows. Gauteng had the most people, about fifteen million. KwaZulu-Natal was second, with about twelve million. The Western Cape had about seven point four million, and the Eastern Cape about seven point two million. In the 2022 census, the Western Cape had more people than the Eastern Cape for the first time. Limpopo had about six and a half million, Mpumalanga about five million, North West nearly four million, and the Free State about three million. The Northern Cape had the fewest people, only about one point four million.

So about one in every four South Africans lives in Gauteng, even though it is the smallest province.

A line graph of South Africa's census results shows that the total population has grown steadily, from about forty-one million in 1996, to about forty-five million in 2001, about fifty-two million in 2011 and about sixty-two million in 2022. The provinces with the fastest growth have mostly been Gauteng and the Western Cape, because many people have moved there for jobs and services.

## Subtopic: Population Density of the Provinces

Population density is the number of people divided by the area. The provinces are very different in size. The Northern Cape is by far the largest, covering nearly a third of South Africa. Gauteng is the smallest, covering only about one and a half per cent of the country.

When we divide each province's population by its area, Gauteng has by far the highest density, more than eight hundred people per square kilometre. KwaZulu-Natal is second, with roughly one hundred and thirty people per square kilometre. Mpumalanga, the Western Cape and Limpopo have between about fifty and seventy. The Eastern Cape has roughly forty, North West roughly thirty-five, and the Free State roughly twenty. The Northern Cape has the lowest density, only about three or four people per square kilometre.

This answers the learner's question in Komani. The Eastern Cape has many people, but it is a large province, so its density is lower than smaller provinces like KwaZulu-Natal. A province with many people is not always crowded, and a province with few people is not always empty in every part. Within each province, people are spread unevenly, crowded in cities and spread out on farms.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the province with the largest area has the most people; the Northern Cape is largest but has the fewest. Two: thinking a tall population bar always means high density; area matters too. Three: reading a graph without checking the scale; the units may be thousands or millions. Four: thinking South Africa's population has stayed the same; it has grown at every census.
