# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how resources, human activities and laws have influenced the location of settlements in South Africa. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Kimberley, the capital of the Northern Cape, on an outing to the Big Hole. The learners walk onto a viewing platform that sticks out over the edge, and gasp as they look down into an enormous hole, its sides dropping steeply to a lake of green water far below. Their guide explains that in 1871, diamonds were found on a small hill on a farm here. News spread fast, and thousands of diggers rushed in from all over South Africa and the world, living in tents and shacks. They dug the hill away by hand, with picks and shovels, until it became this huge hole. Within a few years, a busy town had grown up around it, with shops, churches, a stock exchange and even electric street lights. The guide says that without diamonds, Kimberley might never have existed. The big idea is that many South African settlements grew because of resources and human activities, such as mining, fishing and trade, and that laws, especially under apartheid, also decided where people could live. Laws, resources and human activities as reasons for settlement are examinable.

## Subtopic: Mining Towns

Valuable minerals have created many of South Africa's towns and cities. When minerals are found, people come to work in the mines, and shops, schools, houses, roads and railways follow.

Kimberley grew because of diamonds, found in the area from 1867 and on the hill that became the Big Hole in 1871. Johannesburg grew even faster after gold was found on the Witwatersrand in 1886. Within a few years, a mining camp had become a large town, and today Johannesburg is South Africa's biggest city and the heart of its economy. Other gold mining towns grew up along the Witwatersrand and later in the Free State, such as Welkom.

Other minerals created other towns. eMalahleni, once called Witbank, and nearby towns in Mpumalanga grew because of coal. Rustenburg in North West grew with platinum mining. Kathu in the Northern Cape grew with iron ore mining, and Phalaborwa in Limpopo with copper and other minerals.

Mining towns depend on their mines. When a mine closes because the minerals run out or are no longer worth digging, jobs are lost and the town can shrink. Pilgrim's Rest in Mpumalanga began as a gold rush town in the eighteen-seventies. When mining ended, it became a heritage town that now depends on tourism.

The questions for this section are with you now: how mining created settlements.

## Subtopic: Fishing, Trade and Other Activities

Fishing has created settlements along South Africa's coast. The cold Benguela Current along the west coast brings water rich in nutrients, which feed huge numbers of small fish such as pilchards and anchovies, and larger fish such as hake. Fishing towns such as Lambert's Bay, St Helena Bay, Saldanha and Port Nolloth grew up there, with fishing harbours, boats and fish factories. Hout Bay and Gansbaai in the Western Cape are also fishing towns.

Trade has created many settlements too. Ports grew where ships could load and unload goods: Durban, Cape Town, Gqeberha, East London, Richards Bay and Saldanha. Durban is one of the busiest container ports in Africa. Inland, towns grew at railway junctions, such as De Aar in the Northern Cape, and where important roads meet. Musina grew near the border with Zimbabwe, close to the busy Beitbridge border post. Market towns grew in farming areas, where farmers sell their crops and animals and buy supplies.

Industry also creates settlements. Secunda in Mpumalanga was built in the nineteen-seventies to house workers at a large plant that makes fuel from coal. Tourism helps towns grow too, such as towns near the Kruger National Park and along the coast.

The questions for this section are with you now: fishing, trade, industry and tourism.

## Subtopic: Laws, Past and Present

Laws have also decided where people live. Under colonial rule and apartheid, laws forced people to live in certain places because of their race.

The Natives Land Act of 1913 allowed black South Africans to own land only in small areas called reserves, a small fraction of the country, although they were the large majority of the population. Later, these reserves became homelands. Many families were moved there, far from the cities where the jobs were, so many men became migrant workers, leaving their families for months to work on mines and in cities.

The Group Areas Act of 1950 divided towns and cities into separate areas for different race groups. Black, coloured and Indian people were forced out of many neighbourhoods declared for whites only. In Johannesburg, Sophiatown was destroyed from 1955, and its people were moved to Soweto. In Cape Town, District Six was declared a white area in 1966, and tens of thousands of people were removed and their homes bulldozed. Townships were built on the edges of cities, far from the centre.

Since 1994, these laws have been removed. Today, people may live anywhere they can afford. Land restitution laws allow some people who lost land to claim it back or receive compensation. Municipal planning laws now decide where homes, shops and factories may be built, and new housing has been built, but the shape of apartheid cities can still be seen.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking every town grew only because of water; minerals, fish and trade created many towns. Two: thinking mining towns last for ever; when mines close, towns can shrink. Three: thinking people chose freely where to live under apartheid; laws forced them into certain areas. Four: thinking apartheid's effects disappeared in 1994; its patterns can still be seen in our cities.

The questions for this section are with you now: how laws in the past and present affect where people live.
