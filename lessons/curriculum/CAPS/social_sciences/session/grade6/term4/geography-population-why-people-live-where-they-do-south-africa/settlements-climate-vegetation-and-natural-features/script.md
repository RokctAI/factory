# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how climate, vegetation and natural features influence where settlements are found in South Africa. The anchor is a Grade 6 class at a school in Vereeniging, an industrial town on the Vaal River, on the southern edge of Gauteng. On a sunny afternoon, the class walks along a path beside the river. They watch rowing boats glide past, see fishermen on the banks and spot a big pipe that takes water to a factory. Their teacher points out that the river divides Gauteng from the Free State, and that there are towns and suburbs all along its banks. She hands out a map of South Africa and asks the learners to circle every town that lies on or near a river. Soon, the map is covered with circles. Then she asks them to find towns with the word fontein in their names, which is Afrikaans for a spring, and they find Bloemfontein, Matjiesfontein and many more. The learners begin to see that towns did not grow in random places. The big idea is that settlements grow in places where nature makes life easier, especially where there is water, a good climate, useful vegetation and helpful natural features. The natural reasons for the location of settlements are examinable.

## Subtopic: Settlements and Water

A settlement is any place where people live, from a single farm or a small village to a town or a large city. Every settlement has a reason for being where it is.

The most important natural reason is water. People, animals and crops all need a reliable supply of water. That is why, all over South Africa and the world, settlements grew next to rivers, lakes and springs.

Many South African towns lie along rivers. Vereeniging, Parys and other towns lie on the Vaal River. Upington and Aliwal North lie on the Orange River, South Africa's longest river. East London grew at the mouth of the Buffalo River, and Pietermaritzburg on the Msunduzi River.

In dry areas, springs were especially important. Bloemfontein grew up around a spring, and the Eye of Kuruman, a strong spring in the dry Northern Cape, made Kuruman possible. That is why so many town and farm names include fontein. Rivers were also important because people needed to cross them. Places where a river was shallow enough to cross were called drifts, and some settlements grew at these crossing points.

Today, dams and pipelines carry water to towns far from rivers. Gauteng, for example, gets much of its water from the Vaal Dam and from the Lesotho Highlands Water Project.

The questions for this section are with you now: settlements and water.

## Subtopic: Climate and Vegetation

Climate helps to decide where people live. People prefer places that are not too hot or too cold and that get enough rain for crops and animals. In South Africa, rainfall decreases from east to west, so more people live in the wetter east, and fewer live in the dry west. KwaZulu-Natal, Gauteng, Mpumalanga and the Eastern Cape have many more settlements than the Northern Cape.

The south-western Cape has a winter rainfall climate with mild temperatures, which suits crops such as grapes, wheat and fruit, and many farming towns grew up there, such as Paarl, Stellenbosch and Worcester. The warm, wet east coast suits sugar cane and subtropical fruit.

Natural vegetation is linked to climate. The grasslands of the Highveld and of KwaZulu-Natal provided good grazing for cattle, so herders and farmers settled there long ago. Forests provided wood for building and fuel, and Knysna grew partly because of the timber of its forests. In the dry Karoo, the vegetation is sparse, so farms must be very large, and farmhouses and small towns are far apart. In very dry areas, such as the Kalahari, people live far apart, mainly where there is water.

The questions for this section are with you now: how climate and vegetation affect where people live.

## Subtopic: Natural Features

Other natural features have also influenced the location of settlements.

Flat land is easier to build on and to farm than steep slopes, so many towns are built on plains, plateaus and in valleys. River valleys often have fertile soil, washed down by the river, which is good for farming.

A sheltered bay or river mouth makes a good harbour, where ships are protected from storms. Cape Town grew at Table Bay, where ships stopped for fresh water and food. Durban grew around a large natural bay, which became one of the busiest ports in Africa. Saldanha Bay on the west coast is a deep natural harbour. Towns with port in their names, such as Port Nolloth, Port Alfred and Port Elizabeth, the old name of Gqeberha, show the importance of harbours.

Mountains can block settlement, but towns often grow where roads pass through mountains, at mountain passes, or at the foot of mountains where streams flow down. Long ago, people sometimes settled on hills because they were easier to defend, like Mapungubwe Hill. Beautiful beaches and mountains also attract tourists today, which helps towns such as Plettenberg Bay to grow.

Often, a settlement grew because several natural advantages came together in one place. Pietermaritzburg, for example, was built on gently sloping land in a river valley, with water from the Msunduzi River, good summer rainfall and grassland around it. When you study a map, look for these clues: rivers, springs, flat land, sheltered coasts and mountain passes.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking settlements are found in random places; each has natural and human reasons. Two: thinking water matters only for drinking; it is also needed for farming and industry. Three: thinking most South Africans live in the dry west; more live in the wetter east. Four: thinking fontein in a town name means a fountain in the town square; it means a natural spring.

The questions for this section are with you now: flat land, harbours, mountains and other natural features.
