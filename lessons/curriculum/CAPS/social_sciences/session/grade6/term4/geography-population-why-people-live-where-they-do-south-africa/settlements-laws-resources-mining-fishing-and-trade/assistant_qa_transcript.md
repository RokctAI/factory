### subtopic_1 — Mining Towns
**Assistant:** How deep is the Big Hole?

**Tutor:** The Big Hole was dug by miners between 1871 and 1914, at first entirely by hand with picks and shovels, and later with help from machines. It is about two hundred and fifteen metres deep, although much of it is now filled with water and rubble, so you cannot see all the way down. Its surface covers an area of about seventeen hectares, roughly the size of more than twenty soccer fields. Diamond mining continued underground below the hole until 1914, when the mine closed. It is often described as one of the largest hand-dug holes in the world. Today, the Big Hole is a museum and tourist attraction, where visitors can see displays about the diamond rush and the lives of the miners.

### subtopic_2 — Fishing, Trade and Other Activities
**Assistant:** What was District Six like before the removals?

**Tutor:** District Six was a lively, crowded neighbourhood close to the centre of Cape Town, below Table Mountain. People of many backgrounds lived there, including coloured, black, Indian and white families, as well as people of many religions, and it was famous for its music, its markets and its strong sense of community. Many people were poor, but neighbours knew and helped one another. In 1966, the apartheid government declared it a white area under the Group Areas Act. Over the next years, tens of thousands of people were forced to move to townships on the Cape Flats, far from their work and schools, and almost all the houses were bulldozed. Only some churches and mosques were left standing. Today, the District Six Museum tells their story, and some former residents have returned through land restitution.

### subtopic_3 — Laws, Past and Present
**Assistant:** What is the sardine run?

**Tutor:** The sardine run is a natural event that happens along the coast of the Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal in some winters, usually between about May and July. Huge shoals of sardines, also called pilchards, move north along the coast in cooler water. The shoals can be kilometres long, and they attract dolphins, sharks, seabirds such as gannets, and whales, which come to feed on them. When shoals come close to the shore in KwaZulu-Natal, people rush to the beaches to catch sardines in nets and buckets. The sardine run is not the same every year; in some years, it is spectacular, and in others, the fish stay far out at sea. Scientists think water temperature and currents affect it.
