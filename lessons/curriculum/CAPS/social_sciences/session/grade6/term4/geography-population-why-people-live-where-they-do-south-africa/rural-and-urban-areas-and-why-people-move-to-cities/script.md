# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers rural and urban areas and why people move from rural areas to towns and cities. The anchor is a Grade 6 class at a school in Khayelitsha, a large township on the Cape Flats in Cape Town. During a class discussion, a learner called Lindiwe tells her story. Until she was eight, she lived with her grandmother in a village in the Eastern Cape, among green hills, with cattle grazing near the houses and a river where children swam in summer. The houses were far apart, the nearest clinic was a long walk away, and her school had no library. Her mother worked in Cape Town and sent money home every month. Two years ago, her mother brought her to live in Khayelitsha, so that she could go to a bigger school and the family could be together. Lindiwe says she misses the quiet, the open space and her grandmother, but she likes the library, the shops and having electricity every night. Many classmates nod; their families have similar stories. Every December, they say, the taxis and buses are full of people going home to their villages. The big idea is that rural and urban areas are very different, and that many people move from rural to urban areas in search of a better life. Rural and urban areas and urbanisation are examinable.

## Subtopic: Rural and Urban Areas

Settlements can be divided into rural and urban areas.

Rural areas are the countryside. They include farms, villages and small settlements, with open land between them. Fewer people live there, and they are spread out. Many people in rural areas work in primary activities, which means taking resources from nature, such as farming, forestry and fishing. Rural areas often have fewer services, such as hospitals, banks and large shops, so people may have to travel far to reach them. Life is often quieter, with more open space and close community ties.

Urban areas are towns and cities. Many people live close together, in houses, flats and informal homes, with buildings, roads and services packed into a small area. Most people in urban areas work in secondary activities, such as making goods in factories, or tertiary activities, which means providing services, such as working in shops, offices, banks, schools and hospitals. Urban areas usually have more services, such as hospitals, universities, shopping centres and public transport, but they can also have problems such as traffic, pollution, crime and overcrowding.

Some places are in between, such as small towns that serve the farms around them. In South Africa, some former homeland areas are also hard to describe, because many people live close together in large villages, like a town, but with few jobs or services, like a rural area.

The questions for this section are with you now: the differences between rural and urban areas.

## Subtopic: Why People Move to Towns and Cities

When people move from one place to another to live, it is called migration. When the share of a country's people living in towns and cities grows, it is called urbanisation. In South Africa and around the world, many people have migrated from rural areas to urban areas.

Geographers explain migration with push factors and pull factors. Push factors are problems that push people to leave a place. In rural areas, these include a lack of jobs, poverty, few services such as clinics, electricity and running water, poor schools, drought and not enough land to farm.

Pull factors are attractions that pull people towards a place. In urban areas, these include jobs, higher wages, better schools and universities, hospitals, electricity, running water, shops, entertainment and the chance of a better life. Often, family members who already live in the city help newcomers to find a home and work.

In South Africa, history has also played a part. Under apartheid, pass laws and influx control laws stopped many black South Africans from moving freely to the cities. After these laws were removed in 1986, urbanisation speeded up. Today, about two out of every three South Africans live in urban areas, and many people from the Eastern Cape, Limpopo and other provinces have moved to Gauteng and the Western Cape.

The questions for this section are with you now: urbanisation and push and pull factors.

## Subtopic: Results of Urbanisation

Urbanisation brings both opportunities and problems.

In cities, many people find jobs, better schools and health care, and new businesses grow. But when cities grow very fast, there are not always enough houses, jobs, schools, clinics, water and electricity for everyone. Many newcomers cannot afford formal houses, so they build informal homes, often called shacks, in informal settlements, sometimes on land without proper services or on land that floods. There may be traffic jams, pollution and unemployment, because there are more job seekers than jobs.

In rural areas, urbanisation also brings changes. Many young adults leave, so villages may be left mostly with grandparents and children, like Lindiwe's village. Fewer people may farm the land. But money sent home by family members working in cities helps many rural households to buy food, pay school fees and build homes.

Many people keep strong links with both places. They live and work in the city, but return to their rural homes for holidays, family events and ceremonies, and some plan to go back when they retire. This is sometimes called circular migration.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking rural areas have no people; they have fewer people, spread out. Two: thinking everyone who moves to the city finds a job; there are often more job seekers than jobs. Three: thinking urbanisation affects only cities; rural areas change too. Four: mixing up push and pull factors; push factors drive people away, pull factors attract them.

The questions for this section are with you now: the results of urbanisation for cities and rural areas.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Rural areas are the countryside, with farms and villages, fewer people and fewer services, where many work in farming, forestry and fishing. Urban areas are towns and cities, with many people close together, more services and jobs in factories, shops and offices. Urbanisation is the growth in the share of people living in urban areas, caused by push factors such as lack of jobs and services, and pull factors such as jobs and better schools.
