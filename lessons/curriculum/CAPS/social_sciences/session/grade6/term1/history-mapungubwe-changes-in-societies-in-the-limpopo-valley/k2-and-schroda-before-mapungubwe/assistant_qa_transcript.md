### subtopic_1 — Schroda
**Assistant:** Why is the settlement called K2?

**Tutor:** The name K2 comes from the archaeologists who first studied the area in the 1930s. When archaeologists work at a large area with many sites, they often give each site a short code name, made of letters and numbers, so they can keep their notes and maps organised. This settlement was one of the sites they labelled, and the label K2 stuck. Some people have also used the name Bambandyanalo for the same settlement. By coincidence, K2 is also the name of the second-highest mountain in the world, in Asia, but there is no connection. It is a good reminder that many places in archaeology have names given by researchers, not by the people who lived there. We do not know what the people of K2 called their own home, because they did not leave any writing.

### subtopic_2 — K2
**Assistant:** Why would archaeologists be excited about a rubbish heap?

**Tutor:** A rubbish heap, or midden, is one of the best sources of information about everyday life. People throw away the things they use every day: broken pots, ash from fires, bones from the animals they ate, worn-out tools, lost beads and seeds. These things build up in layers over many years, with the oldest at the bottom. By digging carefully through the layers, archaeologists can learn what people ate, which animals they kept or hunted, what crafts they practised, what goods they traded and how all of this changed over time. Gold objects in royal graves tell us about the rich and powerful, but a midden tells us about ordinary people too. The huge midden at K2 also tells archaeologists that many people lived there for a long time.

### subtopic_3 — What Changed from Schroda to Mapungubwe
**Assistant:** Were the clay figurines at Schroda toys?

**Tutor:** We cannot be completely sure, because the people who made them did not leave writing to explain them. Some of the figurines show cattle, sheep, goats, wild animals and people, and some are quite unusual. Researchers have compared them with the customs of farming communities in southern Africa in more recent times. In some of these communities, clay figures were used during initiation, the ceremonies and teaching that mark the change from childhood to adulthood. Elders used the figures to teach young people lessons about life, marriage, behaviour and responsibilities. So many researchers think the Schroda figurines may have had a similar purpose. It is also possible that some were simply made for fun. This is a good example of how historians make careful interpretations from evidence, and why they often use words like may, perhaps and probably.
