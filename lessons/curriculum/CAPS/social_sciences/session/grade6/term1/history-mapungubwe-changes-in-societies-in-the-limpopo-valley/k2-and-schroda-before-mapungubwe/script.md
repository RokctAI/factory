# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers two settlements in the Limpopo Valley that came before Mapungubwe: Schroda and K2. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Makhado, in Limpopo. Their teacher has filled two large trays with layers of differently coloured sand. One tray is labelled Schroda and the other K2. Hidden in the layers are copies of the kinds of things archaeologists have found at each site: small clay animals, ivory pieces, glass beads, bits of pottery, bone tools and spindle whorls. Working like archaeologists, the learners use paintbrushes and spoons to uncover each find, and they write down where they found it and which layer it was in. At the end, they compare the two trays. The big idea is that Schroda and K2 were large settlements that grew because of farming, ivory and trade, and that they show the steps by which society in the valley changed before Mapungubwe. These settlements and what they tell us are examinable.

## Subtopic: Schroda

Schroda is the name of a farm in the Limpopo Valley, not far from Mapungubwe Hill, where the remains of a large settlement were found. Archaeologists believe it was occupied roughly between 900 and 1000 AD, or a little after. It is one of the earliest large settlements in this part of the valley, and it may have been the capital of its time.

Archaeologists often call the people who lived at Schroda the Zhizo people, after a style of pottery that they made, with particular shapes and decorations. Pottery styles help archaeologists to tell different groups apart, even when there is no writing.

At Schroda, archaeologists found many glass beads that came from far away, across the Indian Ocean. These beads are among the earliest signs that people in this valley were trading with the East African coast. They also found pieces of ivory, some of it worked into bracelets and other objects, which suggests that ivory was being collected and prepared, both for local use and for trade.

Schroda is also famous for its many clay figurines, small models of animals and people. Some researchers think they may have been used in ceremonies, perhaps to teach young people important lessons when they became adults. We cannot be sure, because the people who made them did not leave writing to explain them.

So Schroda shows us a big village of farmers and herders whose leaders were already taking part in long-distance trade.

The questions for this section are with you now: when people lived at Schroda and what was found there.

## Subtopic: K2

K2 is the name archaeologists gave to a settlement at the foot of Mapungubwe Hill, very close to it. It is sometimes also called Bambandyanalo. Archaeologists believe that K2 was occupied roughly from about 1000 AD to about 1220 AD, after Schroda.

K2 was bigger than Schroda. Many more people lived there, perhaps a few thousand at its largest. In the centre of the settlement, archaeologists found a huge heap of rubbish, called a midden, made up of ash, broken pots, animal bones, beads and other waste, which built up over many years. Earlier in K2's history, the centre had been a cattle kraal, as in an ordinary farming village. Later, the rubbish heap grew over the place where the kraal had been. Some archaeologists think this shows a change in how the leaders used the centre of the settlement.

At K2, archaeologists found thousands of imported glass beads. They also found large, rough beads that were made at K2 itself by melting down small imported glass beads and reshaping them. Ivory working was common, and many bone and ivory tools and ornaments were found. Animal bones in the midden show that the people of K2 kept cattle, sheep and goats, and also hunted wild animals, so farming and herding remained the base of their lives.

Spindle whorls were found at K2 too. A spindle whorl is a small round disc with a hole in the middle, usually made from broken pottery, used to spin cotton thread. This shows that people at K2 spun thread, probably to weave cloth, perhaps copying the cloth that came from the coast.

The questions for this section are with you now: when people lived at K2 and what its finds tell us.

## Subtopic: What Changed from Schroda to Mapungubwe

Comparing the two trays, the class in Makhado notices a clear pattern of change.

First, settlements grew bigger. Schroda was large, but K2 was larger still, and Mapungubwe, later, was bigger again.

Second, trade grew. There were some imported glass beads at Schroda, but many thousands at K2. People also made new things from the trade goods, like the beads melted down at K2, and began to make their own cotton cloth.

Third, the power and wealth of leaders grew. More trade gave leaders more goods to control and to give away, which made them richer and more powerful than ordinary people.

Fourth, the way the settlement was arranged changed. In an ordinary farming village, the cattle kraal and the chief's home were in the centre, among everyone else. At K2, the centre changed. Then, around 1220 AD, the leader moved up from K2 onto the top of Mapungubwe Hill, high above everyone else, while ordinary people lived below. The leader was now separated from the people, which was a new idea in the valley. This is where we begin the story of the kingdom of Mapungubwe.

There is also continuity, meaning things that stayed the same. Farming, cattle, pottery making and iron working carried on throughout.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking K2 is a mountain; here it is the name of a settlement near Mapungubwe Hill. Two: thinking Schroda came after K2; Schroda came first. Three: thinking the midden was just useless rubbish; for archaeologists, it is a treasure of clues. Four: thinking every glass bead at K2 was imported; some large beads were made at K2 from melted imported beads.

The questions for this section are with you now: change and continuity from Schroda to K2 to Mapungubwe.

## Subtopic: Up the Hill

Bigger villages, more trade, richer leaders. Then the leader moved up the hill.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Schroda was a large settlement in the Limpopo Valley from roughly 900 to 1000 AD, where archaeologists found clay figurines, ivory and some of the earliest imported glass beads. K2, at the foot of Mapungubwe Hill, was occupied roughly from 1000 to 1220 AD. It was bigger, with a huge central midden, many imported beads, beads made from melted glass and spindle whorls for cotton. Around 1220 AD, the leader moved onto Mapungubwe Hill.
