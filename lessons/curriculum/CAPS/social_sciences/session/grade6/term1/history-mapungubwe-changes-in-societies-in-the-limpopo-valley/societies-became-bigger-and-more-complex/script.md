# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how societies in the Limpopo Valley became bigger, more organised and more complex between about 900 and 1300 AD. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Musina, in Limpopo, not far from where the Limpopo and Shashe Rivers meet. The teacher has given each group clay, sticks and grass, and the class has built three models on one long table. On the left is a small farming homestead: a few round huts with grass roofs, a cattle kraal in the middle, a grain store and a small field of sorghum. In the middle is a large village with hundreds of huts, a huge kraal, a busy work area where people carve ivory and make beads, and traders arriving with cloth and glass beads. On the right is a town with stone walls and a royal home on top of a flat hill, while ordinary people live in the valley below. A paper arrow under the table runs from 900 AD on the left to 1300 AD on the right. The big idea is that over about four hundred years, small farming communities in the Limpopo Valley changed into a large and complex kingdom. How and why societies changed is examinable.

## Subtopic: Small Farming Communities

In Grade 5, we learnt about the first farmers in southern Africa. Many centuries before 900 AD, people who farmed crops and kept cattle, sheep and goats had settled in parts of southern Africa. They made iron tools, such as hoes and spears, and they made clay pots.

These early farmers lived in homesteads and small villages, often near rivers where the soil was good and there was water for people and animals. A homestead was usually a family group, with huts arranged around a cattle kraal. The men looked after the cattle, and the women did most of the farming and worked in the home, growing crops such as sorghum, millet, beans and pumpkins.

Each community had a chief or headman. The chief settled disputes, gave out land and led the people in important ceremonies, such as asking the ancestors for rain. Cattle were very important. They were a sign of wealth, they were used for lobola, or bridewealth, and they were given as gifts to build friendships.

In a small community, most people did similar work. Most families farmed and kept animals. There were differences in wealth, but they were fairly small. People knew one another, and the chief lived among them.

The questions for this section are with you now: how early farming communities lived and were led.

## Subtopic: Growing Bigger

From about 900 AD, the middle Limpopo Valley, around the meeting of the Limpopo and Shashe Rivers, began to change. Villages grew bigger, and some became the centres of large areas.

There were several reasons. First, the valley had good farmland on the floodplains next to the rivers. When the rains were good, farmers could grow a lot of grain, and more food meant that more people could live in one place. Researchers think that during parts of this time, the climate was a little wetter than it is now.

Second, the valley had many elephants, and elephant tusks, or ivory, were very valuable to traders. The valley was also close to areas with gold. Traders from the East African coast wanted ivory and gold, and in return they brought glass beads and cloth, which people in the valley valued.

Third, the Limpopo River was a route for people and goods travelling between the inland areas and the coast of the Indian Ocean.

As trade grew, more people came to live in the valley, to farm, to hunt elephants, to make things and to trade. Settlements such as Schroda, and later K2, became large villages, with hundreds or even thousands of people, much bigger than an ordinary farming homestead.

At K2, for example, archaeologists found a huge heap of rubbish in the middle of the settlement, made up of broken pots, ash, animal bones and other waste left by many people over many years. A rubbish heap that big tells us that a large number of people lived there for a long time.

The questions for this section are with you now: why settlements in the Limpopo Valley grew bigger.

## Subtopic: More Organised and More Complex

A bigger society needs more organisation. When thousands of people live in one area, someone must settle arguments, share out land, organise work, keep order and deal with traders.

The leaders of the valley gained a lot of power by controlling trade. They decided who could trade, collected ivory and gold, and received the glass beads and cloth in return. They could give these valuable goods to their followers as rewards, which made people loyal to them. Over time, some leaders became richer and more powerful than ordinary chiefs. They had officials, or advisers, to help them rule, and some lesser chiefs answered to them.

Society also became more complex. In a complex society, people do many different kinds of work. Some people specialised, which means they became experts in one kind of work: ivory carvers, bead makers, potters, iron and copper workers, gold smiths and cotton spinners and weavers. Some were traders, some were soldiers or messengers, and many were still farmers and herders.

Differences between rich and poor people grew larger. By about 1220 AD, at Mapungubwe, the leader and the royal family lived apart from ordinary people, on top of a hill. Society had divided into classes, or levels. This was a big change from the small homestead where the chief lived among everyone else.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking Mapungubwe appeared suddenly; it grew out of earlier settlements over centuries. Two: thinking trade was the only reason for growth; good farmland, rain and the river mattered too. Three: thinking everyone in a complex society does the same work; people specialised. Four: thinking leaders gained power only through fighting; controlling trade and sharing wealth were very important.

The questions for this section are with you now: leaders, specialists and classes in a complex society.
