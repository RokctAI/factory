### subtopic_1 — How the Contents Page Is Organised
**Assistant:** Why is South Africa near the front of our atlas, but not in other countries' atlases?

**Tutor:** Atlases are made for the people who will use them. A school atlas made for South African learners puts South Africa and Africa first, and gives them many pages, because those are the places you need to know best: your province, your country and your continent. An atlas made for learners in Brazil would probably put Brazil and South America first and give South Africa only a small space, maybe on a map of the whole of Africa. A world atlas made for adults might simply start with the world and then go through the continents in a fixed order. There is no single correct order. What matters is that the contents page tells you the order used in that atlas, so you can always find your way around, whichever atlas you open.

### subtopic_2 — Using the Index
**Assistant:** What do I do if a place is not in the index?

**Tutor:** First, check your spelling and try other spellings, because some places have more than one spelling or name. For example, the Orange River is also called the Gariep, and Mbombela used to be called Nelspruit. Second, check whether the name starts with a word such as Lake, Mount, Port or Cape, and look under the main word instead. Third, remember that an atlas cannot fit every village or farm. If a place is very small, it may only appear on large-scale maps of a province or on a digital map. In that case, find the nearest bigger town in the index and look for your place nearby on that map. Your teacher or a road map can help too. Knowing which towns are near your home is a great skill for finding small places.

### subtopic_3 — Grid References
**Assistant:** Who decided to put grid squares on maps?

**Tutor:** Mapmakers have used grids for a very long time, because they make it easy to describe a position. Some early maps in China and in ancient Greece already used lines to divide the land into squares or bands. Later, mapmakers in Europe used lines of latitude and longitude, which form a grid over the whole Earth. Street maps and atlases began using simple letter and number grids because they are quick and easy for anyone to use, with no need to understand degrees. Today, the same idea is used in many places: in stadium seating, on chessboards, in spreadsheets on a computer and in board games. Once you understand how a grid works, you can read all of these. In the coming weeks, you will learn the latitude and longitude grid, which is the most exact grid of all.
