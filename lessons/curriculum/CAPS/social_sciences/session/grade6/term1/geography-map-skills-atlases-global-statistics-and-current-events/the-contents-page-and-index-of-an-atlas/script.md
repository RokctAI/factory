# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how the contents page of an atlas is organised, and how the index and grid references help us find places quickly. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Upington, in the Northern Cape, near the Gariep, or Orange, River. The teacher holds an atlas speed challenge. Two learners stand at the front with the same atlas and a stopwatch is ready. The first challenge is: find the map of South America. Learner one starts at page one and turns the pages one by one, looking at every map. Learner two opens the contents page, runs a finger down the list, sees South America and a page number, and turns straight to the right map. The class cheers. The second challenge is harder: find the town of Kuruman. This time the winner uses the index at the back. The big idea is that an atlas is carefully organised, and that the contents page and the index are tools that save us time. Using them correctly is examinable.

## Subtopic: How the Contents Page Is Organised

The contents page is near the front of the atlas, usually on the first pages. It is a list of everything in the atlas, in the same order as the pages, with a page number next to each item.

Most school atlases are organised from big to small, or from far to near, in sections. One common order is like this. First come pages that explain how to use the atlas, with symbols, keys and map skills. Then come maps of the whole world, both physical and political, and often thematic world maps, such as climate, vegetation and population. Next come sections on each continent: Africa, Europe, Asia, North America, South America, Australia and Oceania, and Antarctica. Inside each continent section, there are maps of the whole continent and then of smaller regions or countries. Finally, there are the statistics pages, the flags and the index.

South African school atlases often place South Africa and Africa near the front, because they are the most important places for learners here. Inside the South Africa section, there are usually physical, political and thematic maps of the whole country, and then maps of each province.

Each section has a heading. The headings help you jump to the right part. If you want a rainfall map of Africa, first find the Africa section, then look for rainfall inside it.

Some atlases also have a small picture map, called a key map or map index, near the contents page. It shows the outline of the world or a continent with boxes drawn on it, and each box has a page number. If you want the map of a certain area, you find the area on the key map and read the page number in its box. This is very useful when you know where a place is, but not what the map of that area is called.

The questions for this section are with you now: where the contents page is and how it is organised.

## Subtopic: Using the Index

The contents page helps you find a map. But what if you need a single place, such as Kuruman, a small town in the Northern Cape, and you do not know which map it is on? Then you use the index.

The index is at the back of the atlas. It is a long list of place names in alphabetical order, from A to Z. Next to each name, it tells you the page number of a map that shows the place and a grid reference, and often the place's latitude and longitude, which we will study soon. Sometimes the index also tells you what kind of place it is, for example a town, a river or a mountain, and which country it is in.

Some place names begin with words such as Lake, Mount or Port. Many atlases list these places under the main part of the name, so Lake Victoria may be found under V, as Victoria, Lake. Mount Kilimanjaro may be under K. If you cannot find a place, try the other part of its name.

Because the index is in alphabetical order, you must know the alphabet well. Look at the first letter, then the second and third letters. Kuruman comes after Kroonstad and before KwaDukuza, because u comes after r in Kroonstad but before w in KwaDukuza.

The questions for this section are with you now: what an index is and how to use it.

## Subtopic: Grid References

When you turn to the page from the index, the map is full of names. A grid reference tells you exactly where to look. Many atlas maps have letters along the top and bottom edges and numbers along the sides, or the other way around. Imaginary lines divide the map into squares, called grid squares. A grid reference such as C4 means: go to column C and row 4, and look in the square where they meet. The place will be inside that square.

Grid references work like the seats in a big stadium. Your ticket says block C, row 4, and you go straight to your seat without checking every seat in the stadium.

Some atlases use latitude and longitude lines instead of letters and numbers. These lines are more exact, and they work on every map in the world, which is why we will spend the next few weeks learning them.

Back in Upington, the class tries a final challenge with the grid: find the Augrabies Falls, on the Gariep River, west of the town. The index gives the page and the grid square, and the winner points to the falls in just a few seconds.

Learners who practise using the contents page, the index and grid references can find almost any place in the atlas in under a minute.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: paging through the whole atlas to find a map; use the contents page first. Two: looking for a small town on the contents page; small places are in the index at the back. Three: giving up when Lake Victoria is not under L; try V for Victoria. Four: reading a grid reference as row first when the atlas uses column first; check how the letters and numbers are set out on that map.

The questions for this section are with you now: grid squares and grid references.
