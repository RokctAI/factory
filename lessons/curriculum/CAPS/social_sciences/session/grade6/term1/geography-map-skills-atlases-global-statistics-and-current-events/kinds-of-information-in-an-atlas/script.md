# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the kinds of information an atlas gives us: different kinds of maps, global statistics, and how an atlas helps us follow world events. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Mthatha, in the Eastern Cape. A box of new school atlases has arrived, and the class carries it to the library. Each pair of learners gets an atlas. The teacher gives them a challenge card with six missions: find a map that shows mountains, find a map that shows country borders, find a map that shows rainfall, find a map of a city, find the flag of Brazil, and find the population of China. The pairs race to complete the missions, and as they turn the pages they discover how much one book can hold. The big idea is that an atlas is a collection of maps and facts about the world, and that different maps answer different questions. Knowing what is in an atlas, and which map to use, is examinable.

## Subtopic: Physical and Political Maps

An atlas is a book of maps. Many people link the name to Atlas, a giant in old Greek stories who was said to hold up the sky, because pictures of him appeared on some early books of maps, more than four hundred years ago.

The first mission is a map that shows mountains. That is a physical map. A physical map shows the natural features of the land: mountains, plateaus, plains, rivers, lakes and deserts. It uses colours to show height above sea level. Low land near the sea is usually green, higher land is yellow and orange, and the highest mountains are brown or even purple. The sea is blue, often darker blue where it is deeper. On a physical map of South Africa, you can see the green coastal plain of KwaZulu-Natal, the brown Drakensberg and the high, flat plateau of the interior.

The second mission is a map that shows country borders. That is a political map. A political map shows the borders between countries or provinces, and the cities and capital cities, often marked with a dot or a star. Each country is usually a different colour, so the borders are easy to see. Political maps do not show height. On a political map of Africa you can count Africa's fifty-four countries and find South Africa's neighbours.

The questions for this section are with you now: what an atlas is, physical maps and political maps.

## Subtopic: Thematic and City Maps

The third mission is a map of rainfall. A map that shows one theme, or topic, is called a thematic map. Atlases have many thematic maps: climate and rainfall, temperature, natural vegetation, soils, farming, mining, population, languages and transport routes. Each thematic map has its own key, which explains what the colours and symbols mean. For example, on a rainfall map, dark blue might mean more than 1 000 millimetres of rain a year, and pale yellow might mean less than 250 millimetres. On a map of South Africa, this quickly shows that the east is much wetter than the west.

The fourth mission is a city map. Many atlases have street maps or plans of big cities, such as Johannesburg, Cape Town or Durban. These maps show a small area in a lot of detail, with main roads, railways, parks, the airport and important buildings.

Some atlases also include satellite images, which are photographs of the Earth taken from space, and diagrams that explain things like the seasons or the water cycle. Near the front or back, there is usually a page that explains the symbols used on the maps, such as a small aeroplane for an airport, a thin blue line for a river and a dotted line for a border.

Atlases usually have maps of the whole world first, then maps of each continent, and then maps of smaller areas, such as countries and provinces. A South African school atlas usually has many pages on South Africa and Africa, and fewer pages on places far away.

The questions for this section are with you now: thematic maps, keys and city maps.

## Subtopic: Statistics, Flags and the News

The fifth and sixth missions take the learners to the pages of facts. Many atlases have pages of flags, which show the flag of every country. They also have tables of statistics. Statistics are facts given as numbers. An atlas may list the area of each country in square kilometres, the number of people who live there, its capital city, its largest cities, its main languages and its currency. There are also lists of world records, such as the highest mountains, the longest rivers, the largest lakes and deserts and the biggest countries.

Global statistics help us compare places. For example, the world now has more than eight billion people. South Africa's 2022 census counted about sixty-two million people. China and India each have more than one billion people. Russia is the largest country by area, and the Nile is usually listed as the longest river. Statistics change over time, so it is important to look at the date of the atlas, and newer atlases or official websites are better for the latest numbers.

An atlas also helps us understand the news. When we hear about a flood, an earthquake, a sports event or an election in another country, we can look up the place in the atlas and see where it is, what the land is like and which countries are nearby. For example, when Cyclone Idai struck Mozambique in 2019, an atlas showed that the city of Beira lies on the coast of the Indian Ocean, where tropical cyclones can arrive from the sea, and that Zimbabwe and Malawi, which were also badly affected, are close by.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: using a political map to find mountains; mountains are shown on a physical map. Two: thinking green on a physical map means grass or forest; green usually means low land. Three: reading a thematic map without its key; the key explains the colours. Four: thinking statistics never change; populations and other numbers change, so check the date.

The questions for this section are with you now: flags, statistics and using an atlas to follow the news.
