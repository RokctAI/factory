## Subtopic: Grade 6 History

History is the study of the past: what happened, when it happened, why it happened, and how it affected people. Historians ask questions and look for evidence to answer them.

In Grade 6, there are four History topics. In Term 1, we study an African kingdom long ago in southern Africa: Mapungubwe. In Term 2, we study explorers from Europe who found the sea route around southern Africa. In Term 3, we study democracy and citizenship in South Africa. In Term 4, we study medicine through time, from indigenous healing to modern medical discoveries.

You will be assessed in different ways. Informal assessment happens all the time, through class work, homework, short quizzes and discussions, and it helps you and your teacher see what you understand. Formal assessment counts towards your marks for the year. It includes tests, in which you answer questions about sources, such as pictures, maps and pieces of writing, and write a paragraph. In Term 3, you will also do a project. Your teacher will give you the dates. The most important skills are reading sources carefully, using evidence and writing clear paragraphs.

At the start of a topic, your teacher may give you a short baseline activity, to find out what you already know. It is not a test you can fail. It simply shows where to start.

## Subtopic: Remembering the Golden Rhino

In Grade 5, on the heritage trail through the provinces, we visited Mapungubwe, in Limpopo.

Mapungubwe is near the place where the Limpopo River and the Shashe River meet, where the borders of South Africa, Botswana and Zimbabwe come together today. The name Mapungubwe is often said to mean place of the jackal, or hill of the jackal, although people explain the name in different ways.

In 1933, a small group of people, shown the way by a local man, climbed the steep sandstone hill and found graves on the top. In the graves were objects made of gold. The most famous is the golden rhinoceros. It is made of thin sheets of gold, pressed and shaped around a carved wooden body, and held on with tiny gold tacks. Over the centuries, the wood rotted away, but the gold remained. Soon after, the University of Pretoria began careful archaeological digs at Mapungubwe, and the gold objects are still kept by the university today.

Mapungubwe was declared a World Heritage Site in 2003. The area is also a national park. South Africa named one of its national orders, an honour given by the President, the Order of Mapungubwe, which shows how important this kingdom is to our heritage.

## Subtopic: How We Know About the Past

The people of Mapungubwe did not leave written records, so we cannot read their own books or letters. How, then, do we know about them?

We use sources. A source is anything that gives us information about the past. Primary sources come from the time we are studying. At Mapungubwe, these are mostly objects and remains: gold objects, pottery, glass beads, tools, the bones of animals that people ate, the remains of houses and walls, and the graves of the people themselves. Archaeologists are scientists who dig carefully to find and study these remains. They record exactly where each object is found and which layer of soil it comes from, because deeper layers are usually older.

Secondary sources are written later by people who study the past, such as history books, museum displays and articles by archaeologists. Your textbook is a secondary source.

We also learn from oral history, the stories and traditions passed down by word of mouth in communities who live in the region today, and from writings by Arab and other travellers who visited the trading towns on the East African coast.

To place events in time, historians use dates and timelines. Mapungubwe was at its greatest from about 1220 to about 1300 AD. The letters AD mean that the year is counted from the traditional date of the birth of Jesus. Some books write CE, which means Common Era, and has the same years. The years from 1201 to 1300 make up the thirteenth century.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking that the people of Mapungubwe left no history because they left no writing; objects and oral traditions are history too. Two: thinking the golden rhino is solid gold; it is gold foil over a wooden body that rotted away. Three: thinking archaeologists just dig for treasure; they record everything carefully to understand how people lived. Four: thinking the thirteenth century means the years starting with thirteen; it means 1201 to 1300.
