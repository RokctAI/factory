# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the Term 1 topics. In Geography, the topic was map skills with a focus on the world: the globe and directions, atlases, latitude and longitude, hemispheres, places in the news, and scale. In History, the topic was an African kingdom long ago in southern Africa: Mapungubwe, the societies before it, the first state, its classes and its trade. The anchor is a revision afternoon for a Grade 6 class in Kroonstad, in the Free State. The school hall has three tables. Table 1 has a globe with lines of latitude and longitude, an atlas open at the index, and a world map with pins showing places in the news. Table 2 has a map of South Africa, rulers, paper strips and cards with word scales and line scales. Table 3 has a picture of the golden rhino, a timeline from 900 to 1300 AD and a map of the Indian Ocean. Groups move from table to table, answering the questions on each card. The big idea is that maps help us find and measure places anywhere in the world, and that sources help us understand an African kingdom that left no writing. The whole of Term 1 is examinable.

## Subtopic: Atlases and Positions

At Table 1, the class remembers the basics. A globe is a round model of the Earth. The equator divides it into the Northern and Southern Hemispheres. The eight compass points are north, north-east, east, south-east, south, south-west, west and north-west, and directions are always given from a fixed point.

An atlas is a book of maps and facts. Physical maps show height with colours, political maps show borders, and thematic maps show one theme, such as rainfall, with a key. The contents page at the front lists the sections in order, usually from the world to the continents to countries. The index at the back lists places in alphabetical order, with a page number and a grid reference.

Lines of latitude run across the globe, parallel to the equator, and measure how far north or south a place is, from zero to ninety degrees. Lines of longitude run from pole to pole and measure how far east or west of the Greenwich Meridian a place is, from zero to one hundred and eighty degrees. The special lines are the equator, the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn, the Arctic and Antarctic Circles and the Greenwich Meridian. We write latitude first, then longitude, with directions. Johannesburg is at about twenty-six degrees south, twenty-eight degrees east.

The Greenwich Meridian and the one hundred and eighty degree line divide the Earth into the Eastern and Western Hemispheres. South Africa is in the Southern and Eastern Hemispheres. To locate a place in the news, we find the name, look it up in the index, read its degrees and pin it on the map.

The questions for this section are with you now: the globe, atlases, latitude and longitude, and hemispheres.

## Subtopic: Scale and Distance

At Table 2, the class remembers scale. Scale is the relationship between a distance on a map and the real distance on the ground. A large-scale map shows a small area, such as a street, in a lot of detail. A small-scale map shows a large area, such as the world, with little detail.

A word scale uses words, such as one centimetre represents fifty kilometres. It is easy to read, but it becomes wrong if the map is enlarged or reduced. A line scale is a bar divided into equal parts, each labelled with a real distance. It changes size with the map, so it stays correct. Always start at the zero and check the units.

To measure a straight-line distance with a ruler, measure from the centre of one dot to the centre of the other and multiply by the word scale. With a paper strip, mark both places, put the first mark on the zero of the line scale and read the distance at the second mark. Johannesburg to Durban is about five hundred kilometres in a straight line, but about five hundred and seventy kilometres by road, because roads bend.

The questions for this section are with you now: scale, word and line scales, and measuring distance.

## Subtopic: Mapungubwe

At Table 3, the class remembers History. Between about 900 and 1300 AD, societies in the Limpopo Valley grew bigger, more organised and more complex. Schroda, from about 900 to 1000 AD, had clay figurines, ivory and early glass beads. K2, from about 1000 to 1220 AD, was bigger, with a huge midden, many beads and spindle whorls for cotton.

Around 1220 AD, the leader moved onto Mapungubwe Hill. Mapungubwe became the first state in southern Africa, ruled by a sacred king believed to be close to the ancestors and the rain. Stone walls marked the royal area, the first stone-walled palace in southern Africa. It was the first town, with about five thousand people and distinct classes: royals on the hill, important people near it and ordinary people in the valley. Gold objects, such as the golden rhino, bowl and sceptre, made of gold foil over wood, were symbols of royal power.

Porters carried gold and ivory on foot to Sofala on the coast. Traders in dhows, using the monsoon winds, took them to Arabia, India and beyond, and brought back glass beads and cloth, an early form of globalisation. After about 1300, power moved to Great Zimbabwe. The goods and routes showed continuity, but the centres of power changed, and around 1500 the Portuguese arrived.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: mixing up latitude and longitude; latitude runs across and is given first. Two: thinking a large-scale map shows a large area; it shows a small area in detail. Three: thinking the golden rhino is solid gold; it is gold foil over a wooden body. Four: thinking Mapungubwe's goods went to the coast by wagon; they were carried on foot.

The questions for this section are with you now: Mapungubwe, its society and its trade.

Schroda, K2, then Mapungubwe: a sacred king, classes, gold and trade.

Pocket summary of the term. An atlas has physical, political and thematic maps, a contents page and an index. Latitude and longitude give every place an address in degrees, latitude first. South Africa is in the Southern and Eastern Hemispheres. Scale tells us how much smaller a map is than reality, and lets us measure real distances.
