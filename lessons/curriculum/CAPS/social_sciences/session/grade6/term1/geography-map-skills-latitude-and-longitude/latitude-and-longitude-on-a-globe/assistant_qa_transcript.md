### subtopic_1 — Lines of Latitude
**Assistant:** How can a degree measure distance on Earth?

**Tutor:** Imagine slicing the Earth in half, from the North Pole to the South Pole, and looking at the cut face. You would see a circle with the centre of the Earth in the middle. From the centre, draw one line to the equator and another line to Polokwane. The angle between the two lines is Polokwane's latitude, about twenty-four degrees. So a degree is really an angle measured from the centre of the Earth. Because the Earth is so big, each degree of latitude is roughly one hundred and eleven kilometres on the ground. So Polokwane, at about twenty-four degrees south, is roughly two thousand six hundred kilometres south of the equator. For more exact positions, each degree is split into sixty smaller parts, called minutes, but in Grade 6 we usually work in whole degrees.

### subtopic_2 — Lines of Longitude
**Assistant:** Why was Greenwich chosen as zero degrees longitude?

**Tutor:** Unlike the equator, which is in the middle between the poles, there is no natural starting line for longitude, so people had to agree on one. By the 1800s, many countries used different starting lines, which was confusing for sailors and mapmakers. Britain was a powerful sea-trading country, and the Royal Observatory at Greenwich had produced star charts and sea maps that sailors all over the world already used. At a meeting in Washington, in the United States, in 1884, representatives of many countries voted to use the meridian through Greenwich as zero degrees. Not every country agreed at first, but over time it became the world standard. Today, visitors to Greenwich can stand with one foot in the eastern hemisphere and one foot in the western hemisphere, on a line marked on the ground.

### subtopic_3 — The Special Lines
**Assistant:** Can I really stand on the Tropic of Capricorn in South Africa?

**Tutor:** Yes. The Tropic of Capricorn crosses the far north of South Africa, in Limpopo province. There is a marker beside the N1, the main road north from Polokwane towards Musina, where people stop to take photographs. Remember that the line itself is imaginary, so there is nothing to see on the ground except the marker. Also, the exact position of the tropics moves very slowly over thousands of years, because the tilt of the Earth's axis changes a tiny bit, so the marker may be slightly away from the true line today. On the line, around the twenty-first or twenty-second of December, the midday sun is directly overhead, so a person standing upright has almost no shadow at noon. South of the Tropic of Capricorn, where most South Africans live, the midday sun is never directly overhead.
