# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers lines of latitude and longitude on a globe, measured in degrees, and the special named lines. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Polokwane, in Limpopo. Each pair of learners gets an orange and a koki pen. The teacher explains that an orange is round, like the Earth, so it can become a small globe. First the learners mark the top of the orange as the North Pole and the bottom as the South Pole. Then they draw a line all the way around the middle. Next they draw rings above and below it. Finally they draw lines from the top to the bottom, like the segments of a peeled orange. When they hold the oranges up, each one is covered in a grid of lines. The teacher tells them that the Tropic of Capricorn, one of the lines on their oranges, crosses Limpopo a little north of Polokwane. The big idea is that latitude and longitude form an imaginary grid over the whole Earth, so that we can describe the exact position of any place. Latitude, longitude and degrees are examinable.

## Subtopic: Lines of Latitude

The line around the middle of the orange is the equator. It is the starting line for measuring latitude, so it is zero degrees of latitude. The rings above and below the equator are lines of latitude.

Lines of latitude run from west to east around the Earth. They are all parallel, which means they never meet or cross, like the rungs of a ladder. That is why they are also called parallels. The equator is the longest line of latitude. The rings get smaller and smaller towards the poles.

Latitude tells us how far north or south of the equator a place is. It is measured in degrees, written with a small circle after the number. The equator is zero degrees. The North Pole is ninety degrees north, and the South Pole is ninety degrees south. No place can have a latitude of more than ninety degrees. So every latitude must say whether it is north or south of the equator. For example, Polokwane is at about twenty-four degrees south, and Cairo, in Egypt, is at about thirty degrees north.

We use degrees because the Earth is round. Imagine a line from the centre of the Earth to the equator, and another line from the centre to a place. The angle between the two lines, measured in degrees, is the latitude of that place.

The questions for this section are with you now: lines of latitude, parallels and degrees north and south.

## Subtopic: Lines of Longitude

The lines the learners draw from top to bottom are lines of longitude. Lines of longitude run from the North Pole to the South Pole. They are also called meridians. They are not parallel: they are far apart at the equator and they all meet at the poles, like the segments of an orange.

Longitude needs a starting line too. In 1884, at a meeting of many countries, it was agreed that the starting line would pass through Greenwich, in London, in the United Kingdom, where there was an important observatory, a place for studying the stars. This line is called the Greenwich Meridian, or the Prime Meridian, and it is zero degrees of longitude.

Longitude tells us how far east or west of the Greenwich Meridian a place is. It is measured in degrees from zero to one hundred and eighty degrees east, and from zero to one hundred and eighty degrees west. The line at one hundred and eighty degrees is on the opposite side of the Earth from Greenwich, mostly in the middle of the Pacific Ocean. So every longitude must say east or west. South Africa is east of Greenwich. Polokwane is at about twenty-nine degrees east.

Longitude is also linked to time. The Earth turns all the way around, through three hundred and sixty degrees, in twenty-four hours, so it turns about fifteen degrees every hour. Places further east see the sunrise earlier. That is one reason why South Africa's clocks are two hours ahead of Greenwich Mean Time, the standard time measured at Greenwich.

The questions for this section are with you now: lines of longitude, meridians and the Greenwich Meridian.

## Subtopic: The Special Lines

Some lines of latitude have special names, because of the way the sun shines on the Earth.

The Tropic of Cancer is at about twenty-three and a half degrees north. The Tropic of Capricorn is at about twenty-three and a half degrees south. The area between the two tropics is called the tropics. Here, the midday sun is directly overhead on at least one day of the year, and it is warm or hot all year round. The Tropic of Capricorn crosses South Africa in Limpopo, and there is a marker beside the main road north of Polokwane where travellers can stop and stand on the line.

The Arctic Circle is at about sixty-six and a half degrees north, and the Antarctic Circle is at about sixty-six and a half degrees south. Inside these circles, there is at least one day in summer when the sun never sets, and at least one day in winter when it never rises.

The special line of longitude is the Greenwich Meridian, at zero degrees, with the one hundred and eighty degree line opposite it.

When we put latitude and longitude together, the lines cross and make a grid. Every place on Earth is where one line of latitude meets one line of longitude. We always give latitude first, then longitude. Polokwane is at about twenty-four degrees south and twenty-nine degrees east.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: mixing up latitude and longitude; latitude lines run across, like the rungs of a ladder, and longitude lines run from pole to pole. Two: forgetting north, south, east or west; twenty-four degrees on its own is not a position. Three: thinking latitude can go up to one hundred and eighty degrees; latitude stops at ninety degrees at the poles. Four: thinking lines of longitude are parallel; they meet at the poles.

The questions for this section are with you now: the tropics, the polar circles and giving a position with latitude and longitude.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the orange globes in Polokwane — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Rings Across

Latitude lines go across. They measure north or south of the equator.

Keep the rings around the orange in mind and try the questions for this part: lines of latitude.

## Subtopic: Lines from Top to Bottom

Longitude lines go from pole to pole. They measure east or west of Greenwich.

Hold the orange segments in mind and try this part's questions: lines of longitude.

## Subtopic: Named Lines

Equator, two tropics, two polar circles, and Greenwich.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Lines of latitude run across the globe, parallel to the equator, and measure how far north or south of the equator a place is, from zero to ninety degrees. Lines of longitude run from pole to pole and measure how far east or west of the Greenwich Meridian a place is, from zero to one hundred and eighty degrees. The Tropic of Capricorn crosses Limpopo. We give latitude first, then longitude.

Hold the orange covered in its grid of lines in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: the special lines.
