# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers latitude and longitude on a flat map: how to read the position of a place in degrees, and how to find a country or city from its degrees. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Bloemfontein, in the Free State. The teacher has stuck a large world map on the classroom wall, with lines of latitude and longitude printed on it every ten degrees. She hands each group an envelope of clue cards. Each card has only two pieces of information: a latitude and a longitude. The groups must find the place on the map, write its name, and bring their answers back to the teacher. The first card says thirty degrees north, thirty-one degrees east. The second card says thirty degrees south, thirty-one degrees east. The groups are surprised to find that the two answers are in very different places, although the numbers look almost the same. The big idea is that latitude and longitude give every place on Earth an exact address, which works on flat maps as well as on globes. Reading and using positions in degrees is examinable.

## Subtopic: The Grid on a Flat Map

On a globe, lines of latitude are circles and lines of longitude meet at the poles. When mapmakers flatten the round Earth onto paper, they still draw these lines, but they look different.

On many world maps in school atlases, lines of latitude are drawn as straight lines going across the map, from left to right. Lines of longitude are drawn going up and down, from top to bottom. On some maps, the lines of longitude are straight and parallel, so the grid looks like a pattern of squares or rectangles. On other maps, they curve towards the poles, more like they do on a globe. Either way, the idea is the same.

The numbers are printed along the edges of the map. On the left and right edges, you see the degrees of latitude, with zero at the equator, numbers going up to the north towards the top, and numbers going up to the south towards the bottom. Along the top and bottom edges, you see the degrees of longitude, with zero at the Greenwich Meridian, numbers increasing to the east towards the right, and numbers increasing to the west towards the left.

Atlases do not draw every line, because the map would be covered in lines. They usually draw a line every five, ten or even twenty degrees. To find a place between two lines, we estimate. If a city is halfway between the twenty and thirty degree lines, its latitude is about twenty-five degrees.

The questions for this section are with you now: how latitude and longitude lines appear on a flat map and where the degrees are printed.

## Subtopic: Reading a Position

To read the position of a place on a map, follow these steps.

Step one: find the place on the map.

Step two: find its latitude. Look at the lines of latitude near the place and read the numbers on the side of the map. Decide whether the place is north or south of the equator. Estimate if it lies between two lines.

Step three: find its longitude. Look at the lines of longitude near the place and read the numbers along the top or bottom. Decide whether the place is east or west of the Greenwich Meridian.

Step four: write latitude first, then longitude, and include the directions.

Let us try it with South African cities. Johannesburg is at about twenty-six degrees south, twenty-eight degrees east. Bloemfontein is at about twenty-nine degrees south, twenty-six degrees east. Durban is at about thirty degrees south, thirty-one degrees east. Cape Town is at about thirty-four degrees south, eighteen degrees east.

Now some cities in the rest of the world. Nairobi, in Kenya, is very close to the equator, at about one degree south, thirty-seven degrees east. London is at about fifty-one degrees north, right on the Greenwich Meridian at zero degrees. Rio de Janeiro, in Brazil, is at about twenty-three degrees south, forty-three degrees west. Sydney, in Australia, is at about thirty-four degrees south, like Cape Town, but very far east, at about one hundred and fifty-one degrees east.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four steps for reading a position and the positions of some cities.

## Subtopic: Finding a Place from Its Degrees

Now we do the opposite: we are given the degrees and must find the place. This is what the class in Bloemfontein did with their clue cards.

Step one: read the latitude. Start at the equator and move up the map if it says north, or down if it says south, until you reach the right line. Step two: read the longitude. Start at the Greenwich Meridian and move right if it says east, or left if it says west. Step three: where the two meet is the place you want.

The first clue was thirty degrees north, thirty-one degrees east. Moving up from the equator to thirty degrees north, then right from Greenwich to thirty-one degrees east, we land on Cairo, the capital of Egypt, beside the Nile River. The second clue was thirty degrees south, thirty-one degrees east. This time we move down from the equator, and we land on Durban. The longitude is the same, but one city is north of the equator and the other is south, thousands of kilometres apart. This is why the direction letters are so important.

We can also find countries. At zero degrees, twenty degrees east, on the equator, we find the Democratic Republic of the Congo. At about twenty degrees south, forty-seven degrees east, we find the island of Madagascar.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: giving longitude first; the rule is latitude first, then longitude. Two: leaving out north, south, east or west; Cairo and Durban show why this matters. Three: counting latitude from the top of the map; latitude is counted from the equator. Four: reading the numbers on the wrong edge; latitude is on the sides of the map, and longitude is along the top and bottom.

The questions for this section are with you now: finding a city or country when you are given its latitude and longitude.
