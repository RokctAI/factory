# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how to locate major current events and places in the news on a map, using the atlas, latitude and longitude. This is a skill we will use all year. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Mbombela, in Mpumalanga. On the back wall of the classroom, the teacher has put up a large world map with lines of latitude and longitude. Next to it is a box of coloured pins and small cards. Every Monday, two learners bring a news story from the radio, television, a newspaper or a trusted news website. They find the place in the atlas, work out its position in degrees, pin it on the map and write a short headline card with the date. By the end of the year, the map will be covered in pins from all over the world. The big idea is that maps help us understand the news, and that Geography helps explain why things happen where they do. Locating places in the news on a map is examinable.

## Subtopic: From the News to the Atlas

Every news story happens somewhere. A good news report answers the questions what, who, when, where, why and how. For map work, the where question is the most important.

Step one is to find the place name in the story. Sometimes it is a city, such as Durban. Sometimes it is a country, a province, a river, a mountain or an ocean. Sometimes a story names a small town, and you also need to know which country or province it is in.

Step two is to look up the place in the atlas index at the back of the atlas. The index gives the page number and the grid reference, and often the latitude and longitude. Turn to that page and find the place on the map.

Be aware that some places have changed their names. Nelspruit is now called Mbombela, and Port Elizabeth is now called Gqeberha. An older atlas may still use the old names, so a news report and an atlas may not match. If you cannot find a name, ask whether it might have changed.

Step three is to read or check its position in degrees, latitude first, then longitude, with north or south and east or west.

Step four is to mark the place on the world map with a pin, sticker or dot, and to write a short label: the date, the place, its position and a headline of a few words.

Where does the news come from? We should use trusted sources, such as well-known newspapers, radio and television news, and their official websites. Messages forwarded on social media are not always true. A good habit is to check whether more than one trusted source reports the same story.

## Subtopic: Stories on the Map

Here are some real news events the class in Mbombela pins on their map as examples.

In April 2022, very heavy rain caused terrible floods in KwaZulu-Natal, especially in and around Durban and eThekwini. Hundreds of people died, and many homes, roads and bridges were destroyed. On the map, the learners pin Durban at about thirty degrees south, thirty-one degrees east, on the coast of the Indian Ocean.

In March 2019, Cyclone Idai struck the coast of Mozambique near the city of Beira, and then moved inland, causing floods in Mozambique, Zimbabwe and Malawi. The learners pin Beira at about twenty degrees south, thirty-five degrees east. Looking at the map, they see that Beira is on the Indian Ocean coast, where tropical cyclones sometimes arrive from the warm sea.

In September 2023, a strong earthquake struck in the Atlas Mountains of Morocco, south-west of the city of Marrakesh, and many villages were damaged. The learners pin it at about thirty-one degrees north, eight degrees west. They notice that this is in both the Northern and Western Hemispheres.

Not all news is bad news. In October 2023, the Springboks won the Rugby World Cup final in Paris, in France. The learners pin Paris at about forty-nine degrees north, two degrees east, just east of the Greenwich Meridian.

## Subtopic: Asking Geography Questions

Pinning a place is only the start. A geographer then asks: why did this happen here? What is this place like? Who was affected, and how?

For the Durban floods, a geographer might ask about the very heavy rainfall, about homes built on steep slopes or close to rivers, and about how water drains from the land. For Cyclone Idai, they might ask why cyclones form over warm oceans and why low, flat coastal land floods easily. For the earthquake in Morocco, they might ask why earthquakes happen in mountain areas where huge plates of the Earth's crust push against each other. For the rugby final, they might ask how fans travel so far, and why Paris is easy to reach by air from many countries.

The news map also shows patterns. After a few months, the class may notice that some kinds of events happen in the same kinds of places, such as floods along coasts and rivers, or that some parts of the world appear in the news much more often than others.

Throughout the year, our Geography topics will link to the news. In Term 2, trade stories about prices, ports and exports. In Term 3, stories about droughts, heat waves and forests. In Term 4, stories about cities and population.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: pinning a story without checking the place in the atlas; always look it up. Two: mixing up places with similar names; check the country too. Three: trusting a forwarded message without checking; use trusted news sources. Four: only pinning the place and stopping there; ask why it happened there.
