### subtopic_1 — North and South
**Assistant:** Why are the seasons opposite in the two hemispheres?

**Tutor:** The Earth's axis is tilted, at about twenty-three and a half degrees, and it stays tilted in the same direction as the Earth travels around the sun during the year. For part of the year, the Southern Hemisphere leans towards the sun. The sun is higher in our sky, the days are longer and the sun's rays are more direct, so it is summer here, in December and January. At the same time, the Northern Hemisphere leans away from the sun, so it has shorter days, weaker sunlight and winter. Six months later, in June and July, the Earth is on the other side of the sun. Now the Northern Hemisphere leans towards the sun and has summer, while we lean away and have winter. That is why people in Europe can have snow at Christmas, while we have Christmas lunch outside in the heat.

### subtopic_2 — East and West
**Assistant:** What happens when you cross the International Date Line?

**Tutor:** The International Date Line is where one calendar day ends and the next begins. If you fly west across it, from America towards Asia, you jump forward one day. For example, Tuesday suddenly becomes Wednesday. If you fly east across it, you go back one day, so you could have two Tuesdays in a row. This happens because time zones get later as you travel east around the world, and somewhere the date has to change, otherwise you would add a whole day after travelling all the way around. The line was placed in the Pacific Ocean, roughly along one hundred and eighty degrees, because very few people live there. It zigzags around some island groups so that each country can keep the same date across all its islands.

### subtopic_3 — Where South Africa Lies
**Assistant:** Is Cape Agulhas really where two oceans meet?

**Tutor:** Cape Agulhas is the most southern point of Africa, and it is the official place where the Atlantic Ocean and the Indian Ocean are said to meet. International mapmakers agreed to use the line of longitude through Cape Agulhas, at about twenty degrees east, as the boundary between the two oceans on maps. But in the real sea there is no wall or line. The water mixes, and the meeting of the warm Agulhas Current from the Indian Ocean with the cold Benguela Current from the Atlantic happens over a wide area, which moves with the seasons and the weather. Many people think the two oceans meet at Cape Point, near Cape Town, but by the official agreement, Cape Agulhas is the place. The sea there can be very rough, and many ships have been wrecked along that coast.
