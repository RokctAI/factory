### subtopic_1 — The Grid on a Flat Map
**Assistant:** Why do some world maps make Africa look smaller than it really is?

**Tutor:** It is impossible to flatten a round Earth onto paper without stretching or squashing some parts. Mapmakers use different methods, called projections, and each one changes the Earth in some way. One famous projection, the Mercator map, was made for sailors in the 1500s. It keeps directions correct, which helped sailors steer, but it stretches places more and more the further they are from the equator. On a Mercator map, Greenland looks about as big as Africa, but Africa is really about fourteen times bigger. Europe also looks bigger than it really is. Other projections show the sizes of the continents more fairly, but they bend shapes instead. That is why it is a good idea to compare a flat map with a globe, which shows true sizes and shapes.

### subtopic_2 — Reading a Position
**Assistant:** How do phones know our latitude and longitude?

**Tutor:** Most phones have a receiver for satellite navigation, often called GPS. Many satellites travel around the Earth, high above us, and each one sends out radio signals with the exact time. A phone picks up signals from several satellites at once. Because the signals take slightly different times to arrive, the phone can work out how far it is from each satellite, and from that it calculates its own position on Earth as a latitude and longitude. A map app then shows that position as a dot on a map. Your phone may show your position with many decimal places, which is very exact, but it is still the same system of degrees that you are learning in Grade 6. Ships, aeroplanes, delivery drivers and rescue teams all depend on it every day.

### subtopic_3 — Finding a Place from Its Degrees
**Assistant:** How exact are the degrees we use in class?

**Tutor:** In Grade 6 we usually round to whole degrees, which is good for finding a city or a country on an atlas map. But one degree of latitude covers about one hundred and eleven kilometres, so whole degrees are not exact enough to find a particular street or building. For more exact positions, each degree is divided into sixty minutes, and each minute into sixty seconds. These minutes and seconds are parts of an angle, not parts of an hour. A position given in degrees, minutes and seconds can find a place to within a few metres. On phones, positions are often given as degrees with decimals instead. When an atlas index gives a position like twenty-six degrees and twelve minutes south, it is telling you that Johannesburg is a little more than twenty-six degrees south of the equator.
