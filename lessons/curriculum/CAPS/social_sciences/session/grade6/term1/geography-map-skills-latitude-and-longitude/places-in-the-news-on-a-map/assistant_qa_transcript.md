### subtopic_1 — From the News to the Atlas
**Assistant:** How do I know if a news story is true?

**Tutor:** Start by asking where the story comes from. Well-known newspapers, radio and television stations have reporters and editors who check facts before they publish, and they correct their mistakes. A message forwarded on a phone often has no name or source, and nobody may have checked it. Next, look for the same story from at least two trusted sources. If only one unknown website or one group chat is talking about it, be careful. Check the date too, because old stories are sometimes shared again as if they were new. Look at the photos: sometimes a picture from a different place or year is used. If a story makes you very angry or very scared, slow down before sharing it. Asking an adult or your teacher is always a good idea.

### subtopic_2 — Stories on the Map
**Assistant:** Why do cyclones hit Mozambique but not usually South Africa?

**Tutor:** Tropical cyclones form over very warm ocean water, usually between the tropics. In our part of the world, they form over the warm south-west Indian Ocean, often near Madagascar, mainly in the summer months from about November to April. Many of them move west or south-west towards Madagascar and Mozambique. Mozambique has a long, low coastline facing the Indian Ocean, much of it inside the tropics, so it is in their path. By the time a cyclone travels further south towards South Africa, the sea is often cooler and the storm usually weakens or turns away. But the remains of cyclones can still bring very heavy rain and floods to Limpopo, Mpumalanga and KwaZulu-Natal, which has happened several times. So South Africans also need to watch cyclone warnings.

### subtopic_3 — Asking Geography Questions
**Assistant:** Why do some countries appear in the news more than others?

**Tutor:** There are many reasons. Big countries with many people, such as China, India and the United States, are often in the news because what happens there affects many other countries, for example through trade or prices. News organisations also report more on places where they have reporters, and on places their audience cares about, such as neighbouring countries or countries with family links. Big disasters, elections, wars and sporting events make headlines everywhere. But some places, especially small or poor countries, are rarely in the news, even when important things happen there. A news map wall helps you notice this. When you see a big empty area on your map, it is worth asking why we hear so little from there, and finding out a little more about it.
