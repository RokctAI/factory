### subtopic_1 — The First State
**Assistant:** Do we know the name of the king of Mapungubwe?

**Tutor:** No, we do not know the names of any of the kings of Mapungubwe. The people did not write, so no records of names have survived from that time. There may have been several kings over the eighty years or so that Mapungubwe was a state, one after another. Archaeologists know that important people were buried on the hilltop with gold and other precious objects, and that they were probably members of the royal family, but the graves do not tell us their names. Some communities in the region today have oral traditions about early kingdoms and leaders, and historians listen to these carefully. But it is difficult to link these stories directly to particular people from eight hundred years ago. So when we talk about the king of Mapungubwe, we mean the ruler, whoever he was at that time.

### subtopic_2 — A Sacred King
**Assistant:** How did ordinary people at Mapungubwe live?

**Tutor:** Most people at Mapungubwe were ordinary farmers and herders, who lived in the valley around the base of the hill and on the flat land nearby. They lived in round huts with clay walls and grass roofs, grew crops such as sorghum, millet and beans, and kept cattle, sheep and goats. Many were also skilled workers. Some made pottery, some worked iron to make hoes, axes and spears, some carved ivory and bone, and some spun cotton. Some hunted, especially elephants, for ivory. They probably gave part of their harvest, their work or their trade goods to the king, as a kind of tribute or tax, and in return the king was expected to bring rain, keep peace and reward loyal followers. Ordinary people did not live on the hill and were probably not allowed into the royal area without permission.

### subtopic_3 — The Hill and Its Walls
**Assistant:** Why did a sacred king live apart from the people?

**Tutor:** In many societies around the world, people believed that their most important leaders had a special, holy power. To protect that power and to show how special the leader was, the leader was kept apart from everyday life. Ordinary people might not be allowed to look directly at the king, eat with him or come close to his home without permission. At Mapungubwe, archaeologists think the king lived in a private royal area on the hilltop, separated by height and by stone walls. This separation made him seem more powerful and more mysterious, and it strengthened the belief that he was close to the ancestors. Similar ideas about sacred, secluded rulers have been described in later kingdoms in southern Africa, which is one reason historians think the kings of Mapungubwe may have been seen in this way.
