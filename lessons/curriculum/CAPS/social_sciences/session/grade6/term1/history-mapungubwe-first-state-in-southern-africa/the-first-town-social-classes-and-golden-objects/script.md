# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers Mapungubwe as the first town in southern Africa, its distinct social classes, and the golden rhinoceros and other golden objects as symbols of royal power. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Mokopane, in Limpopo. On the teacher's desk is a pile of cardboard, glue, kitchen foil and toothpicks. Each group cuts out a small rhino shape, covers it with foil, and presses the foil carefully around the legs, the horn and the ears, pinning it with toothpicks. When they hold up their shiny rhinos, the teacher explains that the gold smiths of Mapungubwe worked in a similar way, but with real gold foil, tiny gold tacks and a carved wooden body. Then she draws a big triangle on the board, divided into three layers, and asks: who would have owned a golden rhino at Mapungubwe? Who would have used clay pots and iron hoes? The big idea is that Mapungubwe was a town in which people were divided into clear social classes, and that gold was used to show the power of the royal family. The first town, social classes and the meaning of the golden objects are examinable.

## Subtopic: The First Town

A town is a place where many people live close together, and where many of them do work other than farming. A town is usually a centre of trade, power and crafts for the area around it.

Historians describe Mapungubwe as the first town in southern Africa. Several things made it a town and not just a large village. First, a large number of people, perhaps about five thousand, lived there together. Second, many people did specialised work, such as making pottery, working iron, copper and gold, carving ivory and bone, making beads and spinning cotton. Third, Mapungubwe was the centre of trade for a large area. Gold and ivory from the surrounding region were brought there and sent on to the coast, and glass beads and cloth came back. Fourth, it was the centre of power, where the king lived and where important decisions were made.

The town was also arranged in a planned way. The royal area was on top of the hill. Some important families lived in areas close to the hill, and ordinary people lived further out in the valley. Where you lived showed how important you were.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a town is and why Mapungubwe is called the first town in southern Africa.

## Subtopic: Distinct Social Classes

A social class is a group of people in a society who have a similar level of wealth, power and respect. When a society has distinct classes, the differences between the groups are clear and are often passed on from parents to children.

At Mapungubwe, historians describe three main levels, like the layers of the triangle on the board in Mokopane.

At the top was the royal class: the king and the royal family. They lived on the hilltop, apart from everyone else. They controlled trade and wealth, and they were buried with gold objects.

In the middle were important people, sometimes called the elite, such as the king's advisers, officials, important relatives and lesser chiefs. They helped the king rule, and some lived close to the hill. They had more wealth than ordinary people, such as more cattle and more beads.

At the bottom, and by far the largest group, were the ordinary people, or commoners. They were farmers, herders, craft workers and hunters. They lived in the valley and on the land around the hill. They gave part of their harvest, work or trade goods to the king.

How do archaeologists know about these classes? They look at where people lived, how big and well built their homes were, what objects were found in their homes and rubbish, and especially what people were buried with. Graves on the hilltop contained gold and many thousands of beads. Graves in the valley usually had few or no precious objects.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three social classes and how archaeologists know about them.

## Subtopic: Gold and Royal Power

Gold was rare and precious at Mapungubwe. It was obtained from gold-bearing areas in the region, probably including parts of what is now Zimbabwe, and gold workers at Mapungubwe hammered it into thin foil, drew it into wire and made it into beads, bangles and sculptures.

The most famous golden objects, found in royal graves on Mapungubwe Hill, are the golden rhinoceros, a golden bowl and a golden sceptre. A sceptre is a rod carried by a ruler as a sign of power. All three were made of thin gold foil, shaped over a carved wooden core that has since rotted away, and held in place with tiny gold tacks. There were also gold bangles, made from coiled gold wire, and thousands of small gold beads.

Why were these objects symbols of royal power? Gold was rare, beautiful and did not rust or fade, so it showed great wealth. Only the most powerful people could get gold and pay skilled smiths to work it. The objects were buried only with members of the royal class, not with ordinary people, so they marked the difference between the king and everyone else.

The rhino itself may have had special meaning. The rhinoceros is a powerful, strong and sometimes dangerous animal, and some researchers think it may have stood for the strength and leadership of the king. But we cannot be sure, because no one wrote down what it meant. Today, the golden rhino is a national treasure.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking everyone at Mapungubwe was equally rich; there were distinct classes. Two: thinking gold objects were used by ordinary people every day; they belonged to the royal class. Three: thinking a town is simply a big village; a town has specialised workers, trade and power. Four: being sure about what the rhino meant; it is an interpretation, and researchers still discuss it.

The questions for this section are with you now: the golden objects and why gold was a symbol of royal power.
