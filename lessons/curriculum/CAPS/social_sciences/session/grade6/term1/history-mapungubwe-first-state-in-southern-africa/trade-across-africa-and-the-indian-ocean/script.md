# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers trade between Mapungubwe, the East African coast and the lands around the Indian Ocean, the goods that were traded, how goods were carried on foot, and why this trade is called an early form of globalisation. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Thohoyandou, in Limpopo. The teacher has drawn a big map of Africa and the Indian Ocean on old sheets, and spread it across the floor of the school hall. One learner stands on Mapungubwe, holding the end of a ball of string. Another stands on the coast of Mozambique, at a place called Sofala. A third stands in Arabia, a fourth in India and a fifth in China. The string is passed from one to the next until it stretches across the whole map. Then the teacher gives each learner cards showing goods, such as gold, ivory, glass beads, cloth and porcelain, and asks them to pass the goods along the string in the right direction. The big idea is that Mapungubwe was part of a huge trading network that linked southern Africa with Asia, and that this trade helped to make Mapungubwe rich and powerful. Trade, goods and globalisation are examinable.

## Subtopic: A Network Across the Ocean

By the time of Mapungubwe, there were trading towns along the coast of East Africa, in what are now Somalia, Kenya, Tanzania and Mozambique. Some of these towns were Mogadishu, Mombasa, Kilwa and Sofala. The people of many of these towns came to be known as the Swahili. Their language, Kiswahili, is an African language that borrowed many words from Arabic, because traders from Arabia, Persia and India came there often.

Traders sailed across the Indian Ocean in wooden ships called dhows, with large triangular sails. They used the monsoon winds, which blow in one direction for part of the year and in the opposite direction for another part. A trader could sail from Arabia or India to East Africa with one monsoon, wait at the coast, and sail home months later with the other.

Sofala, on the coast of what is now Mozambique, was one of the most southern of these trading places. Traders there wanted gold and ivory, and the best supplies came from the interior, including the Limpopo Valley. So Sofala became linked to Mapungubwe by routes running inland along rivers and paths.

From the coast, goods could travel on to Arabia, Persia, India and even China. In this way, a gold bead from Mapungubwe and a glass bead from India could pass through many hands, across thousands of kilometres.

The questions for this section are with you now: the East African coast, dhows, the monsoon winds and Sofala.

## Subtopic: Goods Out and Goods In

What did Mapungubwe send out to the coast? The most valuable exports were gold and ivory. Ivory from African elephants was prized in India and China, where craftsmen carved it into beautiful objects. Gold was wanted everywhere. Other goods probably included animal skins and furs, and perhaps copper.

What came back to Mapungubwe? The most common imports were glass beads, in huge numbers. Many were made in India, and some probably came from other places around the Indian Ocean. Cloth came too, especially cotton cloth, which was colourful and valued because it was different from local clothing made of animal skins. Archaeologists have also found a few broken pieces of Chinese porcelain, a fine kind of pottery, at Mapungubwe and nearby sites. These pieces had travelled all the way from China.

There was also trade inside Africa. Communities in the interior traded with each other for salt, iron tools, copper, cattle, grain and pottery. Mapungubwe was at the centre of much of this inland trade too, collecting gold and ivory from a wide area and sharing out beads and cloth.

The king controlled the most important trade. By deciding who could trade gold and ivory, and by giving out imported beads and cloth to loyal followers, the king grew richer and more powerful.

The questions for this section are with you now: exports and imports and the role of the king in trade.

## Subtopic: Journeys on Foot and Globalisation

How did goods get from Mapungubwe to the coast? There were no roads, no wheeled carts, and no horses or donkeys were used in this region at the time. In many low-lying areas, tsetse flies carried a disease that could kill cattle, so using oxen to carry loads would have been difficult on many routes. Rivers like the Limpopo were not easy to sail on, because of sandbanks, rapids and dry seasons.

So goods were carried on foot. Teams of porters carried loads of ivory, gold, skins and other goods on their heads and shoulders, walking along paths that often followed the rivers. The journey to the coast was hundreds of kilometres long and probably took many weeks. Porters faced heat, wild animals, rivers to cross and the risk of being robbed. Some goods may have passed from one community to the next, in stages, rather than being carried all the way by the same people.

Globalisation means the linking of different parts of the world through trade, travel and the sharing of goods and ideas. Today we live in a very globalised world, with phones made in Asia and fruit from South Africa sold in Europe. The trade between Mapungubwe, the East African coast, Arabia, India and China was an early kind of globalisation. It linked people who never met each other, across Africa and Asia, about eight hundred years ago.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking goods were carried to the coast in wagons; they were carried on foot. Two: thinking Mapungubwe traded only with nearby villages; it was linked to Arabia, India and China. Three: thinking glass beads were made at Mapungubwe from sand; most were imported from far away. Four: thinking globalisation is only a modern idea; long-distance trade linked continents long ago.

The questions for this section are with you now: how goods were carried and why this trade was an early form of globalisation.
