### subtopic_1 — The First Town
**Assistant:** How did gold smiths at Mapungubwe work with gold?

**Tutor:** Gold is a soft metal, which means it can be hammered very thin without breaking. The gold workers of Mapungubwe probably heated gold and then hammered it on a stone into thin sheets, called foil. They pressed this foil carefully over carved wooden shapes, such as the rhino, the bowl and the sceptre, and fixed it in place with tiny gold tacks, like very small nails. They also pulled gold into thin wire and coiled it around to make bangles, and they made many tiny gold beads. All of this needed great skill, patience and good tools, and it was probably done by specialists who served the royal family. Archaeologists study the objects under microscopes to see the marks of hammering and the tiny tacks, which tells them how the objects were made.

### subtopic_2 — Distinct Social Classes
**Assistant:** Was the golden rhino a one-horned rhino?

**Tutor:** This is a question many people ask. The golden rhino appears to have only one horn, while the black and white rhinos of southern Africa both have two horns. Some people have wondered whether it shows a different kind of rhino, from Asia, where some rhinos have one horn. But there is no good evidence for that. Most researchers think the gold smith simply made a simple, stylised rhino, or that a second horn was lost or damaged over the centuries. Some have suggested that the single horn may have had a special meaning. The honest answer is that we do not know for sure. This is an example of why historians use careful words, such as perhaps or may have, when they are making an interpretation and not stating a proven fact.

### subtopic_3 — Gold and Royal Power
**Assistant:** Is it fair that some people at Mapungubwe were so much richer than others?

**Tutor:** That is a good question for a historian, because it asks us to think about values. In many societies in history, including Mapungubwe, wealth and power were not shared equally, and a small royal class had much more than everyone else. At the time, many people probably accepted this, because they believed that the king was sacred, close to the ancestors and responsible for bringing rain and keeping peace. In return for their tribute, ordinary people expected protection, gifts and good harvests. Today, in a democracy like South Africa, we believe that all people should have equal rights, even if they do not have equal wealth. When we study the past, we try to understand how people thought at that time, without simply judging them by today's ideas. But it is still good to ask questions about fairness.
