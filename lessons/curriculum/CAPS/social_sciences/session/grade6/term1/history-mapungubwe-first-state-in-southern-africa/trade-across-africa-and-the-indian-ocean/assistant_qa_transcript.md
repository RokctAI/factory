### subtopic_1 — A Network Across the Ocean
**Assistant:** How do archaeologists know where the glass beads came from?

**Tutor:** Archaeologists and scientists study the beads very carefully. They look at the colour, shape and size of each bead, and at the way it was made. Many of the small glass beads found at Mapungubwe and K2 were made by drawing out a long tube of hot glass and cutting it into tiny pieces, a method used in South Asia and some other places around the Indian Ocean. Scientists can also test the chemical ingredients of the glass. Different glass makers used different kinds of sand and minerals, so the chemical recipe of the glass is a bit like a fingerprint. By comparing the beads with glass from known workshops, they can suggest where the beads were made. These studies show that most beads came from across the Indian Ocean, which is strong evidence of long-distance trade.

### subtopic_2 — Goods Out and Goods In
**Assistant:** What happened to the gold and ivory once it left Africa?

**Tutor:** Once gold and ivory reached the coast, Swahili, Arab and other merchants traded them on across the Indian Ocean. Ivory was especially valued in India and China. African elephant ivory was larger and easier to carve than the ivory of Asian elephants, so craftsmen used it to make ornaments, bangles, handles, combs, chess pieces and religious objects. Gold was used for jewellery, coins and decorations, and it added to the wealth of trading cities and rulers in the Middle East and Asia. Some coastal towns in East Africa, such as Kilwa, grew very rich from controlling this trade, and built stone mosques and palaces. So the work of hunters, miners, smiths and porters in the Limpopo Valley helped build wealth in faraway places, even though most of them never saw the ocean.

### subtopic_3 — Journeys on Foot and Globalisation
**Assistant:** Did people from Mapungubwe ever sail to India?

**Tutor:** As far as we know, the people of Mapungubwe did not travel all the way to India themselves. The trade worked more like a relay race, with goods passing from one group of people to the next. People from Mapungubwe and the surrounding region collected gold and ivory and carried them part of the way, or traded them to other communities closer to the coast. At coastal towns like Sofala, Swahili merchants bought them and sold them on to traders from Arabia, Persia or India, who carried them across the ocean. Glass beads and cloth travelled the same chain in the opposite direction. So most people in the chain only knew the people next to them. This is very similar to how many goods travel today, through farmers, factories, shipping companies, shops and buyers who never meet one another.
