# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the king of Mapungubwe and sacred leadership, the first stone-walled palace, and the significance of Mapungubwe Hill, at the time when Mapungubwe was the first state in southern Africa, from about 1220 to about 1300 AD. The anchor is a Grade 6 class from Lephalale, in Limpopo, on a school trip to Mapungubwe National Park. A park guide leads them up a long wooden staircase that climbs the side of Mapungubwe Hill, a sandstone hill with steep, rocky sides and a flat top. At the top, the learners are out of breath, but the view is wide: the dry bush stretches in every direction, and in the distance they can see the line of trees along the Limpopo River. The guide points to the low remains of stone walls on the hilltop, and then down to the valley floor, where most of the people once lived. She asks: why would a king choose to live up here? The big idea is that Mapungubwe was the first state in southern Africa, ruled by a sacred king who lived apart from ordinary people on top of the hill. The king, sacred leadership and the importance of the hill are examinable.

## Subtopic: The First State

A state is a large society, ruled by one central leader and his government, with power over many people and a large area. In a state, there are usually different levels of leaders, officials who help to rule, rules that everyone must follow, and different classes of people.

Mapungubwe is called the first state in southern Africa. At its height, from about 1220 to about 1300 AD, it was ruled by a king. Archaeologists estimate that about five thousand people may have lived at Mapungubwe itself, and that the king's power reached over a much bigger area, perhaps including many smaller settlements in the Limpopo Valley and the lands around it, in parts of what are now South Africa, Botswana and Zimbabwe. Smaller settlements had their own chiefs, who were under the authority of the king at Mapungubwe.

The king did many things. He controlled trade, especially the trade in gold and ivory with the East African coast. He gave out wealth, such as glass beads, cloth and cattle, to reward loyal followers. He settled serious disputes and made important decisions with the help of his advisers. He led the most important ceremonies.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a state is and why Mapungubwe is called the first state in southern Africa.

## Subtopic: A Sacred King

The king of Mapungubwe was not only a political leader. He was also a sacred leader. Sacred means holy, or connected with the spiritual world.

In many farming societies of southern Africa, the ancestors were very important. People believed that the spirits of the ancestors watched over the living and could help or harm them. The most important help of all was rain, because without rain crops failed and cattle died.

Historians and archaeologists think that the king of Mapungubwe was seen as the person closest to the ancestors and to the spiritual world. He was believed to be able to ask the ancestors for rain, and to protect the people and the land. Rainmaking ceremonies were very important, and some researchers think that places on and near the hill were used for rain ceremonies.

Because he was sacred, the king was kept apart from ordinary people. Ordinary people probably saw him rarely, and they had to show great respect. He lived in the royal area on top of the hill with his family, advisers and servants. This idea of a leader who is sacred and separate from the people was new in the valley. In earlier villages, the chief had lived among his people.

When members of the royal family died, they were buried on top of the hill. Some of these royal graves contained gold objects, such as the golden rhino, golden bowl and sceptre, and thousands of gold and glass beads.

The questions for this section are with you now: sacred leadership, the ancestors and rain.

## Subtopic: The Hill and Its Walls

Why did the king live on top of Mapungubwe Hill? Historians suggest several reasons.

First, height showed status. Living high above the people showed clearly that the king and his family were more important than everyone else. Everyone in the town below could look up and see the royal hill.

Second, the hill separated the sacred king from ordinary people, which fitted the idea of sacred leadership.

Third, the hill was easy to protect. Its sides are steep cliffs, and only a few narrow paths lead to the top. Holes cut into the rock in some places may have held wooden poles to help people climb.

Fourth, the hill may have had spiritual importance, perhaps linked to rain and the ancestors.

On top of the hill and around its base, the people of Mapungubwe built stone walls. These walls did not hold up roofs. The houses were still round huts, made of poles and clay with grass roofs. Instead, the walls marked out and separated important spaces, such as the king's private area, and showed the power of the royal family. Building with stone needed many workers and good organisation, which only a powerful leader could arrange. The royal area on Mapungubwe Hill is often described as the first stone-walled palace in southern Africa. Later, much bigger stone walls were built at Great Zimbabwe, which followed Mapungubwe.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the king lived among ordinary people like an earlier chief; he lived apart on the hill. Two: thinking the stone walls were the walls of houses; they marked out and separated important spaces. Three: thinking the king was only a political leader; he was also a sacred leader linked to the ancestors and rain. Four: thinking Mapungubwe was a single village; it was a state ruling a large area.

The questions for this section are with you now: why the king lived on the hill and the meaning of the stone walls.
