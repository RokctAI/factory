### subtopic_1 — Grade 6 Geography
**Assistant:** Why do we need to learn Geography?

**Tutor:** Geography helps you understand the world you live in and make good decisions in it. Map skills help you find your way, whether you are reading a map of a game reserve, following directions on a phone or planning a trip to visit family in another province. Learning about trade helps you understand why bread or petrol prices change, and where the things in your house come from. Learning about climate helps farmers decide what to plant and helps towns prepare for droughts and floods. Learning about population helps governments decide where to build schools, clinics and roads. Many jobs use Geography too, such as pilots, town planners, farmers, tour guides, weather forecasters and people who look after nature reserves. And Geography is simply interesting, because it explains why places look and feel so different from one another.

### subtopic_2 — The Globe, the Equator and the Poles
**Assistant:** Is the equator very hot?

**Tutor:** Places near the equator are usually warm or hot all year round, because the sun is high in the sky there for the whole year. Its rays hit the ground almost straight down, so they heat the land strongly. Many places near the equator also get a lot of rain, which is why there are tropical rainforests in the Congo Basin and in South America. But not every place on the equator is hot. Height above sea level matters too. Mount Kenya stands very close to the equator, yet it is so high that there is snow and ice near its top. Quito, the capital city of Ecuador, is also near the equator, but it is high in the mountains, so it is mild and pleasant. We will learn much more about this in Term 3, when we study climates around the world.

### subtopic_3 — Eight Directions from a Fixed Point
**Assistant:** How can I find north without a compass?

**Tutor:** There are a few clever ways. In South Africa, the sun rises roughly in the east and sets roughly in the west. Around midday, the sun is in the northern part of the sky, because most of South Africa lies south of the Tropic of Capricorn. So if you stand at noon with your back to the sun, you are facing roughly south, and north is behind you. Your shadow at midday points roughly south. At night, people in the Southern Hemisphere use the Southern Cross, a group of bright stars, to find south. These methods are only rough guides. For accurate directions, use a compass or a map with a north arrow. Many cellphones also have a compass app, but it works best away from metal objects. Try finding north from your own home, and then check it with a compass if you can.
