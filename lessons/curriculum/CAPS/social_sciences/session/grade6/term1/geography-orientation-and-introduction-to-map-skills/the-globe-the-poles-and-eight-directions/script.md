# Part 1 — Expert

This session opens Grade 6 Geography and revises the Grade 5 ideas we need for map work: the globe, the equator, the poles, the compass points and the eight directions from a fixed point. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Kimberley, in the Northern Cape, in the first week of the school year. Their teacher has painted a huge compass rose on the netball court, with north pointing towards the school gate. She puts a globe on a table in the middle of the circle and asks the learners to stand on different points of the compass. One learner stands on north-east, another on south-west, and the class has to say where each person is standing, compared with the globe in the middle. The big idea is that maps and globes help us describe where places are, and that every direction is given from a fixed point. These skills are the base for everything we do in Geography this year, and they are examinable.

## Subtopic: Grade 6 Geography

Geography is the study of the Earth, its places, its people and how people and places affect each other. In Grade 6 there are four Geography topics, one for each term.

In Term 1, we study map skills, with a focus on the world. We will learn how to use an atlas, how to find any place on Earth using latitude and longitude, and how to use scale to measure distances on a map.

In Term 2, we study trade. We will learn why people trade, what they trade, how raw materials become manufactured goods, and what fair and unfair trade mean, with a focus on South Africa and the world.

In Term 3, we study climate and vegetation around the world. We will learn about hot, mild and cold climates, wet and dry areas, tropical rainforests, hot deserts and coniferous forests.

In Term 4, we study population, and why people live where they do, in South Africa and around the world.

Each term also has History topics, so Geography and History together make up Social Sciences. In both subjects, there will be class work, homework, tests and a project. Your teacher will explain when each one happens.

The questions for this section are with you now: what Geography is and the four Geography topics in Grade 6.

## Subtopic: The Globe, the Equator and the Poles

A globe is a model of the Earth. Like the Earth, it is round, like a ball. Because it is round, a globe shows the true shapes and sizes of the continents and oceans. A flat map always stretches some parts of the Earth to fit them onto flat paper, but a map is easier to carry, fold and put in a book.

A globe usually spins on a rod. This rod shows the Earth's axis, an imaginary line through the centre of the Earth that the Earth turns around once every day. One end of the axis is the North Pole, at the top of the globe. The other end is the South Pole, at the bottom. The North Pole is in the middle of the frozen Arctic Ocean. The South Pole is on the ice of Antarctica.

Halfway between the two poles, an imaginary line runs all the way around the middle of the Earth. This line is the equator. The equator divides the Earth into two halves, called hemispheres. The half north of the equator is the Northern Hemisphere, and the half south of it is the Southern Hemisphere. South Africa is in the Southern Hemisphere. Europe and most of Asia are in the Northern Hemisphere. The equator passes through Africa, across countries such as Kenya, Uganda, the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Gabon, so Africa has land in both hemispheres.

## Subtopic: Eight Directions from a Fixed Point

A compass is an instrument with a magnetic needle that points north. Once we know where north is, we can find all the other directions.

The four main compass points, called cardinal points, are north, east, south and west. Going around clockwise, they are always in the same order. A rhyme can help: Never Eat Slimy Worms, for north, east, south and west.

Between the four main points are four in-between points. North-east is between north and east. South-east is between south and east. South-west is between south and west. North-west is between north and west. Together, these make the eight points of the compass.

A direction always starts from a fixed point. On the netball court, the globe is the fixed point. A learner standing on the east point is east of the globe. But if we use the learner as the fixed point, the globe is west of the learner. Directions flip when we change the fixed point. For example, Kimberley is south-west of Pretoria, so Pretoria is north-east of Kimberley.

On most maps, north is at the top. A north arrow on the map shows which way north is, and it is a good habit to look for it before you read any directions.

Directions are useful in everyday life. A taxi driver, a farmer looking for lost cattle, a pilot flying from Kimberley to Cape Town and a ship's captain in a storm all need to describe directions clearly. Saying that the dam is somewhere over there is not helpful. Saying that the dam is three kilometres north-east of the farmhouse tells everyone exactly which way to go. Later this term, we will add distances using scale, and exact positions using latitude and longitude.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying east-north instead of north-east; in an in-between direction, north or south always comes first. Two: thinking the equator is a real line painted on the ground; it is an imaginary line. Three: giving a direction without a fixed point; we must always say from where. Four: thinking north is always the direction you are facing; north is a fixed direction, shown by a compass or a north arrow.

The questions for this section are with you now: the eight compass points and directions from a fixed point.
