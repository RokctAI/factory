# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers change and continuity in the trade between the East African coast and settlements in the interior, over several hundred years, from before Mapungubwe to the arrival of the Portuguese. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Giyani, in Limpopo. The teacher has strung two washing lines across the classroom. A sign on the first says continuity, things that stayed the same. A sign on the second says change, things that were different. Each group has a pile of cards with facts about trade from different times, and a basket of pegs. The groups must read each card, decide which line it belongs on, and peg it in the right place, from the earliest on the left to the latest on the right. Some cards cause arguments, because they seem to belong on both lines. The big idea is that historians look at both change and continuity, and that the East Coast trade kept some features for centuries while other things changed a great deal. Recognising change and continuity in this trade is examinable.

## Subtopic: What Change and Continuity Mean

Change and continuity are two of the most important ideas in History.

Change means that something became different over time. For example, a settlement grows bigger, a new leader takes over, or a new kind of goods appears.

Continuity means that something stayed the same, or carried on, over a long time. For example, people keep farming the same crops, or traders keep using the same route.

Both happen at the same time. When historians study a long period, they ask two questions: what changed, and what stayed the same? They also ask why. A change may have many causes, and a continuity may continue because something works well or because people have no other choice.

Think about your own family. Over many years, your family may have moved house, bought a phone or got a new baby. Those are changes. But your family may still speak the same home language, cook the same favourite dish on Sundays and visit the same relatives in December. Those are continuities.

In this lesson, we look at the trade between the East African coast and settlements inland, such as Schroda, K2, Mapungubwe and later Great Zimbabwe, from about 900 AD to about 1500 AD, roughly six hundred years.

## Subtopic: What Stayed the Same

On the continuity line, the class in Giyani pegs many cards.

The main goods stayed much the same for centuries. Gold and ivory went out from the interior to the coast. Glass beads and cloth came in from across the Indian Ocean.

The way goods were carried stayed the same. Inland, goods were carried on foot by porters, because there were no wheeled carts and pack animals were hard to use. At sea, traders sailed dhows, using the monsoon winds.

The coastal trading towns stayed important. Swahili merchants at towns such as Sofala and Kilwa were the middlemen between the interior and the traders from Arabia, Persia and India.

The role of leaders stayed the same. Inland, the leaders who controlled the gold and ivory trade became rich and powerful, whether at Schroda, K2, Mapungubwe or Great Zimbabwe. They used imported goods to reward their followers.

Some cards cause an argument. The card that says leaders grew rich from trade is true at Schroda, at K2, at Mapungubwe and at Great Zimbabwe, so it belongs on the continuity line. But the card that says the king lived on a hill apart from his people describes something new at Mapungubwe, so it belongs on the change line. The class learns that the same topic, leadership, can show both continuity and change.

Everyday life also showed continuity. Most people in the interior remained farmers and herders, growing grain, keeping cattle and making pottery and iron tools, even as trade grew around them.

The questions for this section are with you now: the goods, transport, towns and leaders that stayed the same.

## Subtopic: What Changed

On the change line, the class pegs a different set of cards.

The centre of power moved. First Schroda, from about 900 AD, then K2, then Mapungubwe, from about 1220 to about 1300 AD. After about 1300, Mapungubwe was abandoned. Researchers suggest several possible reasons, such as a period of colder, drier weather that made farming harder, and a shift in trade. The main centre of power and trade moved north, to Great Zimbabwe, in what is now Zimbabwe, which became very large and powerful from about 1300 to about 1450, with huge stone walls. It was closer to many of the goldfields.

The amount of trade changed. Over time, more and more gold reached the coast, and the coastal town of Kilwa grew very rich by controlling much of the gold trade passing through Sofala. Kilwa built stone mosques and a large palace, and even made its own coins.

Then came a very big change. Around 1500 AD, ships from Portugal, in Europe, sailed around the southern tip of Africa and arrived on the East African coast. The Portuguese wanted to control the gold trade. They attacked some coastal towns, and in 1505 they built a fort at Sofala. Swahili traders lost much of their control, and the old trading system was disrupted. We will learn more about the Portuguese explorers next term.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking history is only about change; continuity matters too. Two: thinking trade stopped when Mapungubwe was abandoned; it moved to new centres such as Great Zimbabwe. Three: thinking the goods traded changed completely every century; gold, ivory, beads and cloth carried on for hundreds of years. Four: thinking Europeans started trade on the East African coast; it had been busy for centuries before they came.

The questions for this section are with you now: the changes in the East Coast trade, from Schroda to the arrival of the Portuguese.

## Subtopic: The Change Line

Schroda, K2, Mapungubwe, Great Zimbabwe. Kilwa grew rich. The Portuguese came.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Change means something became different over time, and continuity means it stayed the same. In the East Coast trade from about 900 to about 1500 AD, gold and ivory went out and beads and cloth came in, porters walked to the coast and dhows sailed with the monsoon, and inland leaders grew powerful from trade. But the centre of power moved from Schroda to K2, Mapungubwe and Great Zimbabwe, Kilwa grew rich, and around 1500 the Portuguese arrived and took over Sofala.

Hold the last card pegged on the change line, the fort at Sofala, in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: what changed.
