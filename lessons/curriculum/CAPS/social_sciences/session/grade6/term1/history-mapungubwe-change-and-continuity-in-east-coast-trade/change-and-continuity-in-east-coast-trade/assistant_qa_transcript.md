### subtopic_1 — What Change and Continuity Mean
**Assistant:** Why did Mapungubwe end?

**Tutor:** Historians and archaeologists are not completely sure, and they think several reasons may have worked together. One reason is climate. Some scientific studies suggest that around 1300 the weather in the region became colder and drier for a time. In a hot, dry valley where farming already depended on good rains, a few bad years could mean failed harvests and hungry people. If people believed the king was responsible for rain, failed rains could also weaken his authority. Another reason is trade. Power and trade seem to have shifted north, to Great Zimbabwe, which was closer to many of the goldfields and may have taken over the trade routes to the coast. As people moved away to new centres, Mapungubwe was gradually left empty. This is a good example of how historians look for more than one cause.

### subtopic_2 — What Stayed the Same
**Assistant:** How is Great Zimbabwe connected to Mapungubwe?

**Tutor:** Great Zimbabwe is in what is now southern Zimbabwe, a few hundred kilometres north-east of Mapungubwe. Archaeologists see many similarities between the two. Both had a king who lived apart from ordinary people, on high ground surrounded by stone walls. Both grew rich from the gold and ivory trade with the East African coast, and both used imported glass beads and cloth. Many archaeologists think that Great Zimbabwe continued the kind of state that began at Mapungubwe, and that some of the ideas about kingship and power were passed on. Great Zimbabwe became much bigger, with enormous stone walls built without mortar, and it may have had more than ten thousand people at its height. Today it is a World Heritage Site, and the country Zimbabwe is named after it. A carved stone bird from Great Zimbabwe appears on Zimbabwe's flag.

### subtopic_3 — What Changed
**Assistant:** Why did the Portuguese want to control the gold trade?

**Tutor:** In the 1400s and 1500s, gold was very valuable in Europe, and Portugal wanted to become rich and powerful through trade. Portuguese sailors had heard that gold came from the interior of Africa and was traded through the East African coast. By sailing around southern Africa, they hoped to reach these trading towns directly, and also to reach India for spices. If they could control Sofala, they believed they could take over the gold trade and keep the profits for Portugal, instead of the Swahili and Arab merchants. So in 1505 they built a fort at Sofala. In practice, they never fully controlled the trade, because the gold came from far inland, and African leaders and traders found other routes. But their arrival changed the old trading system on the coast. We study the Portuguese explorers next term.
