### subtopic_1 — The Ruler Method
**Assistant:** Why is it called as the crow flies?

**Tutor:** The saying is old, and it comes from the idea that a crow flies straight from one place to another, without following roads or paths. People believed that crows, unlike people on foot or on horseback, did not have to go around hills, forests or rivers, so they took the shortest route. In real life, birds do not always fly in a perfectly straight line, because they look for food, avoid danger and use the wind. But the saying stuck. In Geography, as the crow flies simply means the straight-line distance between two places, measured directly on a map. Some people say as the bird flies, or in a straight line, and they all mean the same thing. It is a useful way to say that you are not talking about the road distance.

### subtopic_2 — The Paper Strip Method
**Assistant:** Is a straight line on a flat map really the shortest route on a round Earth?

**Tutor:** For short distances, such as between towns in one province, a straight line on the map is a very good guide to the shortest route. But over very long distances, such as flying from Johannesburg to New York, the answer is more surprising. Because the Earth is round, the shortest route between two faraway places is a curve on most flat maps. It is called a great circle route. You can test this with a globe and a piece of string: stretch the string tightly between two faraway cities, and you will see that it follows a path that looks curved on a flat map. That is why long-distance flights sometimes seem to take a strange curved path on a map in the aeroplane. In Grade 6, though, we measure straight lines on maps of South Africa, which works well.

### subtopic_3 — Straight Line or Road
**Assistant:** How do I measure when the second mark falls between two numbers on the line scale?

**Tutor:** Then you estimate carefully. Look at where the mark falls between the two numbers. If the line scale is marked every hundred kilometres, and the mark is exactly halfway between two hundred and three hundred, the distance is about two hundred and fifty kilometres. If it is about a quarter of the way past two hundred, it is about two hundred and twenty-five kilometres. Some line scales have smaller divisions on the left of the zero, which help you to be more exact. Another trick is to measure the leftover piece with a ruler and use the word scale for that bit. Always write about in front of an estimated answer. In tests, teachers usually accept answers within a sensible range, because nobody can measure a map perfectly. Show your working, so the teacher can see your method.
