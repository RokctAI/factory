### subtopic_1 — Word Scales
**Assistant:** Why do some line scales have a small extra piece before the zero?

**Tutor:** That small piece is there to help you measure short or awkward distances accurately. Imagine a line scale where each main part stands for one kilometre, but you want to measure a distance of two and a quarter kilometres. On the main part of the scale, you can only easily read whole kilometres. So mapmakers add an extra part to the left of the zero, which is divided into smaller pieces, such as quarters or tenths. To use it, put one end of your paper strip on a whole number to the right of zero, such as two, and let the other end fall on the small divided section to the left of zero. Then you add the whole kilometres to the small amount. That is why it is so important to find the zero first, and not simply start at the left end of the bar.

### subtopic_2 — Line Scales
**Assistant:** Which scale do pilots and sailors use?

**Tutor:** Pilots and sailors use special maps called charts, which are drawn very carefully at known scales, often written as ratio scales, such as one to five hundred thousand. They also use line scales and latitude and longitude. Sailors have a handy trick: they measure distances against the latitude marks on the side of the chart, because one minute of latitude is equal to one nautical mile, which is about one point eight five kilometres. This means the edge of every sea chart works like a built-in line scale. Pilots and sailors also use instruments and satellite navigation today, but they still learn to read charts by hand, in case the electronics fail. Being able to read any kind of scale, quickly and accurately, is an important safety skill in their jobs.

### subtopic_3 — Changing Between Scales
**Assistant:** Can I make my own line scale for a map of my classroom?

**Tutor:** Yes, and it is a great way to understand scale. First, measure your classroom with a tape measure, for example ten metres long and eight metres wide. Next, choose a scale that fits your page, such as one centimetre represents one metre. Then your map will be ten centimetres long and eight centimetres wide. Draw the walls, and then measure and draw the desks, the door and the board at the same scale. Finally, draw a line scale at the bottom: a straight line ten centimetres long, with a mark every centimetre, labelled zero, one, two, and so on, up to ten metres. Write the word scale underneath as well: one centimetre represents one metre. Now ask a friend to use your line scale to measure the real length of the board from your map, and check their answer with the tape measure.
