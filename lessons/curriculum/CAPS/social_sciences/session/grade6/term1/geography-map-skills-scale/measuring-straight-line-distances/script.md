# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how to measure straight-line distances between cities on a map, using a ruler with a word scale and a paper strip with a line scale. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Johannesburg, in Gauteng. The teacher tells the class to imagine that they have won a holiday, with stops in Durban and Cape Town. Before they can plan it, they must find out how far each city is from Johannesburg. Each group gets a map of South Africa from the atlas, a ruler, a pencil and a strip of scrap paper. At the bottom of the map is a word scale that says one centimetre represents fifty kilometres, and a line scale marked from zero to four hundred kilometres. The groups measure, calculate and compare their answers, and then check them against a road atlas. The big idea is that the scale of a map lets us work out real distances, and that the straight-line distance between two places is always the shortest distance. Measuring distances on a map is examinable.

## Subtopic: The Ruler Method

The first method uses a ruler and a word scale.

Step one: find the two places on the map. Cities are usually shown by dots. Always measure from the centre of one dot to the centre of the other dot.

Step two: lay the ruler so that its edge touches both dots, with the zero of the ruler exactly on the centre of the first dot. Read the distance in centimetres at the centre of the second dot. If it falls between two marks, read the millimetres too.

Step three: multiply the map distance by the word scale. On the class map, Johannesburg to Durban measures ten centimetres. The word scale says one centimetre represents fifty kilometres. Ten times fifty is five hundred, so the straight-line distance is about five hundred kilometres.

Let us do a second example. Johannesburg to Cape Town measures about twenty-five centimetres on the same map. Twenty-five times fifty is one thousand two hundred and fifty, so the straight-line distance is about one thousand two hundred and fifty kilometres. Atlases give it as roughly one thousand two hundred and sixty kilometres, so the class is very close.

If the map distance includes millimetres, such as four point six centimetres, multiply that number by the scale. Four point six times fifty is two hundred and thirty kilometres.

The ruler method is quick and accurate, but it only works if you know the word scale, and only if the map has not been enlarged or reduced.

The questions for this section are with you now: measuring with a ruler and calculating with a word scale.

## Subtopic: The Paper Strip Method

The second method uses a strip of paper and a line scale. This method still works on maps that have been enlarged or reduced.

Step one: lay the straight edge of the paper strip on the map so that it touches the centres of both places.

Step two: make a small, sharp pencil mark on the edge of the strip at the centre of each dot, and write the first letter of each place next to its mark, for example J for Johannesburg and D for Durban.

Step three: move the strip down to the line scale. Put the first mark exactly on the zero of the line scale, with the edge of the strip along the scale.

Step four: read the distance on the line scale where the second mark falls. If the second mark falls between two numbers, estimate.

What if the distance is longer than the line scale? Then mark the end of the line scale on the strip, slide the strip along so that this new mark sits on the zero, and keep measuring. Add the pieces together at the end. For Johannesburg to Cape Town, the strip has to be moved three or four times along a line scale that is four hundred kilometres long.

The paper strip method needs no calculation at all. The line scale does the work for you.

The questions for this section are with you now: the steps of the paper strip method and how to measure distances longer than the line scale.

## Subtopic: Straight Line or Road

The distance we measure with a ruler or a straight paper strip is called the straight-line distance. People also say it is the distance as the crow flies, because a bird can fly straight from one place to another, over mountains, rivers and towns.

People who travel by road cannot go in a straight line. Roads bend around mountains, follow valleys, cross rivers at bridges and pass through towns. So the road distance between two places is always longer than the straight-line distance. When the class checks a road atlas, they find that Johannesburg to Durban is about five hundred and seventy kilometres by road, compared with about five hundred kilometres in a straight line. Johannesburg to Cape Town is about one thousand four hundred kilometres by road, compared with about one thousand two hundred and sixty in a straight line.

Straight-line distances are useful for pilots, who fly fairly directly between airports, and for comparing how near or far places are. Road distances are useful for drivers. To measure a curved road on a map, we can lay a piece of string or cotton along every bend of the road, then straighten it and measure it against the scale.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: measuring from the edge of a dot instead of its centre; always use the centre. Two: starting the paper strip at the left end of the line scale instead of the zero. Three: forgetting to multiply by the scale; ten centimetres is not ten kilometres. Four: thinking the road distance and the straight-line distance are the same; the road is usually longer.

The questions for this section are with you now: straight-line distance, road distance and why they differ.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with the holiday plan in Johannesburg — plain words, same facts.

Measure in centimetres. Multiply by the word scale.

Keep the ten centimetres from Johannesburg to Durban in mind and try the questions for this part: the ruler method.

## Subtopic: Mark and Match

Mark both places on a paper strip. Match it to the line scale from zero.

Hold the paper strip with its J and D marks in mind and try this part's questions: the paper strip method.

## Subtopic: The Crow and the Car

The straight line is the shortest. The road is longer.

Pocket summary of the lesson. To measure a straight-line distance with a ruler, measure between the centres of the two dots in centimetres and multiply by the word scale. To use the paper strip method, mark both places on the edge of a strip, then lay it on the line scale with the first mark on zero, and read the distance. The straight-line distance, as the crow flies, is shorter than the road distance, because roads bend.

Hold the road atlas showing five hundred and seventy kilometres to Durban in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: straight line or road.
