# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the concept of scale and the difference between small-scale and large-scale maps, from world maps to street maps. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Gqeberha, in the Eastern Cape. The teacher lays six maps on the classroom floor in a row. The first is a map of the whole world, on one page. The second is a map of Africa. The third is a map of South Africa. The fourth is a map of the Eastern Cape. The fifth is a map of the city of Gqeberha and Nelson Mandela Bay. The sixth is a street map of the area around the school, where the learners can find the school gate, the corner shop and the taxi rank. All six maps are about the same size on paper. The teacher asks: how can a whole world and one street fit onto the same size of paper? The big idea is that every map is drawn to a scale, which tells us how much smaller the map is than the real world, and that the scale decides how much area and how much detail a map can show. The concept of scale and the meaning of small-scale and large-scale maps are examinable.

## Subtopic: What Scale Means

The real world is enormous. A road from Gqeberha to Cape Town is hundreds of kilometres long. No piece of paper could show it at its real size. So mapmakers shrink the real world to fit onto paper. They shrink everything by the same amount, so that the map keeps the right shapes and distances compared with each other.

Scale is the relationship between a distance on the map and the real distance on the ground. It tells us how many times smaller the map is than the real world. For example, a scale of one to one hundred thousand means that one centimetre on the map stands for one hundred thousand centimetres on the ground, which is one kilometre. Everything on the map is one hundred thousand times smaller than in real life.

Scale is not only for maps. A model car may be made at a scale of one to forty-three, so it is forty-three times smaller than the real car. An architect draws the plan of a house to scale, so that the builder knows exactly how long each wall must be. A drawing of your classroom on a page of your book is also a scale drawing.

Every proper map should show its scale, usually near the key or at the bottom of the map. Without a scale, we cannot work out real distances from the map.

The questions for this section are with you now: what scale means and why maps are drawn to scale.

## Subtopic: Small Area, Big Detail

Now look again at the six maps on the floor in Gqeberha. On the street map, the school is a big rectangle, and you can see every street name, the corner shop and even the taxi rank. On the world map, all of Gqeberha is just a tiny dot, or it is not shown at all. Both maps are on the same size of paper, but they use very different scales.

A large-scale map shows a small area of land, such as a few streets, a farm, a game reserve or a town. Because the area is small, the map can show it in a lot of detail. Things look large on the map. Street maps, town plans and maps of a school are large-scale maps. A typical scale for a street map might be one to ten thousand.

A small-scale map shows a large area of land, such as a country, a continent or the whole world. Because the area is so big, the map can only show a little detail: big rivers, mountain ranges, borders and big cities. Things look small on the map. World maps and maps of continents in an atlas are small-scale maps. A world map might have a scale of one to one hundred million.

A simple way to remember: large scale, large detail, small area. Small scale, small detail, large area.

The questions for this section are with you now: large-scale maps and the detail they show.

## Subtopic: From World Maps to Street Maps

The six maps on the floor go from the smallest scale to the largest scale. The world map has the smallest scale, because it shrinks the whole Earth onto one page. The street map has the largest scale, because it only shrinks a few streets onto one page.

As we move along the row, from world to Africa, to South Africa, to the Eastern Cape, to the city and then to the street, the area shown gets smaller, and the detail gets greater. Each map is like zooming in with a camera. A map app on a phone does the same thing when you pinch the screen to zoom in: you move from a small-scale view to a large-scale view.

We choose the scale according to what we need. To plan a flight from Johannesburg to London, we need a small-scale map that shows both continents. To find the way from the taxi rank to the school gate, we need a large-scale street map. A small-scale map is useless for finding a house, and a large-scale map is useless for planning a long journey.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a large-scale map shows a large area; it shows a small area in large detail. Two: thinking a world map is a large-scale map because the world is large; it is a small-scale map. Three: thinking a map without a scale can be used to measure distance; we need the scale. Four: thinking maps are drawn at different scales in different parts of the same map; on most maps, one scale applies to the whole map.

The questions for this section are with you now: ordering maps from small scale to large scale and choosing the right map.
