# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the different ways a map shows its scale, especially word scales and line scales, how to read them, and how to change one kind of scale into another. The anchor is a Grade 6 class in Hogsback, a village in the forested Amathole mountains of the Eastern Cape. The class is planning a short hike to a waterfall. The teacher has drawn a simple trail map, with the school, the forest path, a stream and the waterfall. At the bottom of the map are two scales. One is a sentence: one centimetre represents one kilometre. The other is a bar with marks labelled zero, one, two and three kilometres. The map is a little small, so the teacher enlarges it on the photocopier, and hands out the bigger copies. Then she asks a tricky question: which of the two scales on the enlarged copy is still correct? The big idea is that a map can show its scale in different ways, and that each way has strengths and weaknesses. Reading and using word scales and line scales is examinable.

## Subtopic: Word Scales

A word scale uses words to describe the scale. It is usually written as a sentence, such as one centimetre represents one kilometre, or one centimetre represents fifty kilometres, or one centimetre on the map is two kilometres on the ground.

A word scale is easy to read and easy to understand. It tells you directly what one unit on the map stands for. If two places are four centimetres apart on a map with a word scale of one centimetre represents one kilometre, then they are four kilometres apart on the ground. If the word scale is one centimetre represents fifty kilometres, then four centimetres represents two hundred kilometres.

A word scale has one big weakness. It is only correct when the map is printed at its original size. If the map is enlarged or reduced on a photocopier, printed bigger or smaller, or shown on a screen, then one centimetre on the new map no longer matches one kilometre on the ground. The words do not change, but the map has, so the word scale becomes wrong.

There is also a third kind of scale, called a ratio or number scale. It is written with numbers, such as one to one hundred thousand, which means that one unit on the map stands for one hundred thousand of the same units on the ground. One to one hundred thousand is the same as one centimetre represents one kilometre, because one hundred thousand centimetres make one kilometre.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a word scale is, how to use it and its weakness.

## Subtopic: Line Scales

A line scale, also called a bar scale, is a straight line or bar drawn on the map, divided into equal parts. Each mark is labelled with the real distance it stands for, such as zero, one, two and three kilometres.

To read a line scale, look carefully at three things. First, find the zero. On many line scales, zero is not at the far left end. Instead, there is a short extra piece to the left of the zero, divided into smaller parts, such as quarters or tenths of a kilometre, for measuring short distances accurately. Second, look at the units, such as metres or kilometres. Third, check how much each part stands for. On the Hogsback map, each part is one centimetre long and stands for one kilometre.

The great strength of a line scale is that it is drawn on the map itself. When the map is enlarged on a photocopier, the line scale is enlarged by exactly the same amount. When the map is made smaller, so is the line scale. So the line scale always stays correct. That is why most atlas maps, road maps and map apps show a line scale.

Back in Hogsback, the class compares the old and new copies with a ruler. On the enlarged copy, each part of the line scale now measures one and a half centimetres, but it is still labelled one kilometre, and it still matches the map. The word scale, however, still says one centimetre represents one kilometre, which is now wrong.

The questions for this section are with you now: how to read a line scale and why it stays correct.

## Subtopic: Changing Between Scales

Sometimes we need to change one kind of scale into another.

To change a line scale into a word scale, measure one part of the line scale with a ruler. Suppose one part is one centimetre long and is labelled five kilometres. Then the word scale is one centimetre represents five kilometres. If one part is two centimetres long and labelled ten kilometres, then two centimetres represents ten kilometres, so one centimetre represents five kilometres.

To change a word scale into a line scale, draw a straight line, and mark it every centimetre. Label the first mark zero, and the next marks with the distance from the word scale. If the word scale is one centimetre represents five kilometres, the labels will be zero, five, ten, fifteen and twenty kilometres.

To change a word scale into a ratio scale, change both parts into the same unit. One kilometre is one hundred thousand centimetres. So one centimetre represents five kilometres becomes one centimetre represents five hundred thousand centimetres, which is written as one to five hundred thousand.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: measuring a line scale from its left end instead of from the zero; always start at zero. Two: trusting a word scale on an enlarged copy; it is no longer correct. Three: forgetting the units; kilometres and metres are very different. Four: thinking a line scale and a word scale on the same map show different distances; on the original map, they show the same scale in different ways.

The questions for this section are with you now: changing a line scale into a word scale, and a word scale into a line scale or a ratio.
