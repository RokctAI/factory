# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers alpha-numeric grid references: what a grid is, how a grid reference is made from a letter and a number, how to find a place on a map from its grid reference, and how to give the grid reference of a place. The anchor is a Grade 4 classroom in Welkom, a mining town in the Free State. The teacher has pinned a large map of the neighbourhood on the wall. The map shows the school, a clinic, a park, a church, the police station, a shopping centre, a taxi rank, a soccer field and a small dam. Over the map, the teacher has drawn straight lines across and down, making a grid of squares. Along the top, the columns have the letters A, B, C, D and E. Down the left side, the rows have the numbers 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5. The teacher calls out: treasure hunt! The treasure is hidden in block D4. The big idea is that a grid divides a map into blocks, and that a letter and a number together give the exact block where a place can be found. Reading and giving grid references is examinable.

## Subtopic: What a Grid Is

A grid is a pattern of straight lines that cross each other, making squares. The lines that go up and down are called vertical lines. The lines that go across are called horizontal lines. Together, they divide the map into small squares called blocks.

The blocks are arranged in columns and rows. A column is a line of blocks that goes up and down, from the top of the map to the bottom. A row is a line of blocks that goes across, from one side to the other.

In an alpha-numeric grid, the columns are named with letters of the alphabet, and the rows are named with numbers. Alpha comes from alphabet, and numeric comes from numbers. On the Welkom map, the letters A, B, C, D and E are written along the top, one letter above each column. The numbers 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 are written down the left side, one number next to each row.

So every block on the map belongs to one column and one row. That means every block can be named by one letter and one number.

You have seen grids like this before. A chessboard is a grid of squares. Seats in a stadium or a cinema are often found by a block letter and a row number. In a calendar, the days are arranged in a grid of rows and columns.

Grids are useful on maps because a map can have hundreds of places on it, and it is hard to describe where a small place is. A grid gives each part of the map a short name.

The questions for this section are with you now: grids, columns and rows, and alpha-numeric grids.

## Subtopic: Reading a Grid Reference

A grid reference is the letter and the number that name one block on the map. In an alpha-numeric grid reference, we always write the letter first and the number second, with no space between them, like this: D4. We never write 4D.

To find a place from its grid reference, follow these steps. Take the grid reference D4. First, find the letter D along the top of the map. Put your finger on it. Second, slide your finger straight down column D. Third, stop when you reach row 4, by checking the number on the left side. The block where column D and row 4 meet is block D4. Look inside it, and you will find the treasure.

The learners take turns. The teacher says B2, and a learner finds the letter B, slides down to row 2, and finds the school. The teacher says E5, and another learner finds the dam in the bottom right corner. The teacher says A1, and they find the park in the top left corner.

It helps to say the steps aloud: along to the letter, then down to the number. Some learners use two fingers, one on the letter at the top and one on the number at the side, and move them towards each other until they meet in the block.

The questions for this section are with you now: grid references, letter first and number second, and finding a block.

## Subtopic: Giving a Grid Reference

Now the learners do it the other way around. The teacher names a place, and the learners must give its grid reference.

To give a grid reference, follow these steps. First, find the place on the map. The clinic is shown by a red cross. Second, look straight up from the clinic to the top of the map, and read the letter of that column. It is C. Third, look straight across from the clinic to the left side of the map, and read the number of that row. It is 3. Fourth, write the letter first and the number second: C3. The clinic is in block C3.

Sometimes a place is big and covers more than one block. The soccer field covers parts of blocks D1 and E1. In that case, we give the block where most of it is, or where its symbol is, or we give both blocks: D1 and E1.

Grid references are used on real maps all the time. Many street maps and tourist maps have an index, which is a list of street names or places with a grid reference for each one. If you want to find a street, you look it up in the index, read its grid reference, and go straight to that block.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: writing the number first, such as 3C; always write the letter first. Two: counting the blocks from the wrong side; always use the letters and numbers written on the map. Three: forgetting that a big place can cover more than one block; give the main block or both. Four: guessing instead of following the column and row carefully; use your fingers to line them up.

The questions for this section are with you now: giving grid references, big places, and map indexes.
