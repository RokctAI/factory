# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers symbols and keys on maps. It explains what a symbol is, the different kinds of symbols used on maps, what a large-scale map is, and how a key, also called a legend, explains the symbols. The anchor is a Saturday outing to Stellenbosch, a university town in the Western Cape. Amahle, a Grade 4 learner, is visiting with her family, and her father hands her a tourist map of the town centre. At first, the map looks crowded and confusing: little crosses, a blue wiggly line, letters such as H and P, black lines with tiny ticks across them, and green patches. Then Amahle notices a box in the bottom corner, with each little picture and its meaning written next to it. Suddenly the whole map makes sense. The big idea is that maps use symbols to show real things in a small space, and that the key tells us what each symbol means. Using symbols and keys is examinable.

## Subtopic: What Symbols Are

A map is a drawing of a place from above. But a map is small, and a place is big. If the mapmaker drew every building, tree and car in full detail, the map would be far too crowded to read. So mapmakers use symbols.

A symbol is a simple sign that stands for something real. Symbols are small, simple and easy to recognise. They save space and make maps clear.

There are four main kinds of map symbols.

Picture symbols are simple little drawings that look like the thing they stand for. A small tree stands for trees or a forest. A small tent stands for a camp site. A cross on top of a small square stands for a church.

Letter symbols use one or two letters. H often stands for a hospital, P for parking, and PO for a post office. The letter is usually the first letter of the word.

Line symbols are lines that show long, narrow things. A blue wiggly line is a river. A thick red or black line is a main road, and a thin line is a smaller road. A black line with short lines across it, like the tracks of a train, is a railway line. A dotted or dashed line might show a footpath or a border.

Colour symbols use colour to show areas. Blue is used for water, such as rivers, dams, lakes and the sea. Green is often used for parks, forests and grass. Grey or pink might show built-up areas full of buildings.

On Amahle's map, the Eerste River, which flows through Stellenbosch, is a blue wiggly line. The old churches are little crosses. The university sports fields are green.

## Subtopic: Symbols on a Large-scale Map

Maps can show a small area or a large area.

A large-scale map shows a small area, such as a school, a farm, a village or the centre of a town, in a lot of detail. Because the area is small, there is space to show individual buildings, streets, paths, and even single trees. Amahle's map of the centre of Stellenbosch is a large-scale map. It shows each street with its name, and buildings such as the town hall, the museum, the churches, the library and the police station.

A small-scale map shows a large area, such as a whole province or the whole of South Africa, with much less detail. There is no space to show single buildings. A whole city might be shown with just one dot.

On a large-scale map, symbols are used for many kinds of buildings and places. A school might be a small building symbol with a flag, or the letter S. A shop might be a shopping trolley. A petrol station might be a petrol pump. A clinic or hospital might be a cross or the letter H. A place of worship might be a cross for a church or a crescent moon for a mosque. A bus stop or taxi rank might be a picture of a bus.

Different maps may use different symbols for the same thing. One map may show a hospital as an H, and another as a red cross. That is why every map needs its own key.

The questions for this section are with you now: large-scale and small-scale maps, and symbols for buildings and places.

## Subtopic: Keys on South African Maps

A key is a list of all the symbols used on a map, with the meaning of each one written next to it. It is also called a legend. The key is usually in a box in one corner of the map.

To read a map, first look at the title, which tells you what place the map shows. Then study the key. When you see a symbol on the map, find the same symbol in the key, and read its meaning.

South Africa has official maps made by a government office in Cape Town, called the Chief Directorate: National Geo-spatial Information. These maps use the same set of symbols across the whole country. For example, on these maps, rivers are blue, main roads are red, railways are black lines with cross-lines, and dams are blue areas. This means that a person who learns the symbols can read any of these maps, from the Limpopo River to Cape Agulhas.

Tourist maps, such as Amahle's, often use more colourful picture symbols, so that visitors can find restaurants, museums, hotels and parks easily.

A good key is clear. Each symbol in the key must look exactly like the symbol on the map, and each meaning must be short and clear.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking symbols are decorations; every symbol stands for something real. Two: guessing what a symbol means; check the key. Three: thinking all maps use the same symbols; different maps may use different symbols, so always use the map's own key. Four: thinking a large-scale map shows a large area; it shows a small area in lots of detail.
