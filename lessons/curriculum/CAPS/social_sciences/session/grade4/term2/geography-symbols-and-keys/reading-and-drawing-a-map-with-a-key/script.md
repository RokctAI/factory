# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how to read maps of a farm, a village and part of a town using symbols and a key, and how to draw your own map with symbols and a key. The anchor is a sugar cane farm near KwaDukuza, on the north coast of KwaZulu-Natal. Sanele, a Grade 4 learner, lives on the farm, where his mother works in the farm office. Tall green sugar cane grows on the rolling hills all around, and a small stream runs along the bottom of the valley. His teacher has made copies of a map of the farm for the whole class, and later a map of a nearby village and a map of part of KwaDukuza. Then each learner must draw a map of their own area. The big idea is that we read a map by using its key to understand the symbols, and that we can draw our own maps as plan views, using simple symbols and a clear key. Reading a map and drawing a map with a key are examinable.

## Subtopic: Reading a Map of a Farm

The map of the farm has a title at the top: Map of Sibonelo Sugar Farm. In the bottom corner there is a key. Sanele reads the key first. Green squares mean sugar cane fields. A small house shape means a building. A blue wiggly line means a stream. A blue shape means a dam. A dashed brown line means a farm road. A dotted line means a footpath. A small tree means trees. A line of small crosses means a fence.

Now Sanele reads the map. In the middle there is a small house shape with the label farmhouse. Next to it are more building symbols: the farm office, a big shed for tractors, and the workers' houses. Most of the map is covered in green squares, the cane fields. A blue wiggly line runs along the bottom of the map: the stream. Next to the stream is a blue shape: the farm dam, which stores water for the dry months. Dashed brown lines run between the fields: the farm roads used by tractors and trucks to carry the cut cane. A dotted line runs from the workers' houses to the road: a footpath.

Sanele can use the map to answer questions. Where is the dam? Near the stream at the bottom of the farm. What is next to the farmhouse? The farm office. How would a truck get from the shed to the main road? Along the farm road.

When you read a map, always use the key. Do not guess what a symbol means.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading the symbols on a farm map, and answering questions from a map.

## Subtopic: Reading a Map of a Village or Town

Next, the class reads a map of a nearby village. The key shows different symbols. A house shape stands for homesteads. A flag on a building stands for the school. A cross stands for the church. A red cross stands for the clinic. A blue circle stands for a communal tap. A trolley stands for a shop. A thick line stands for the tar road, and thin lines stand for gravel roads.

From the map, the learners can see that the homesteads are spread out over the hills, the school and the clinic are close to the tar road, and there are three communal taps. A learner who lives in the village points to her own homestead.

Then the class reads a map of part of the town of KwaDukuza. This map is busier. The streets are drawn as white lines with their names written along them. Blocks of buildings are grey. The key shows symbols for the hospital, the police station, the post office, the library, the taxi rank, the railway station, a mosque, churches, a Hindu temple and a park. KwaDukuza has people of many cultures, and the map shows many places of worship.

The learners practise reading the map. What is next to the taxi rank? Which street is the library on? How many places of worship can you find? They also notice that the town map shows more symbols, packed closer together, than the farm map, because a town has many more buildings and services.

The questions for this section are with you now: reading maps of a village and part of a town.

## Subtopic: Drawing Your Own Map

Now it is Sanele's turn to be the mapmaker. Here are the steps to draw a map.

First, choose the area and write a title, such as: Map of my home and the farm road.

Second, draw the area as a plan view, as if you were looking down from above. Draw big things first, such as roads and rivers, then buildings and other features. Put each thing in the right place compared with the things around it.

Third, use simple symbols. Choose a symbol for each kind of thing: a small square for a house, a flag for the school, a blue line for the stream, a line for the road. Keep the symbols small, simple and different from each other. Use the same symbol every time for the same kind of thing.

Fourth, draw a key in a box in one corner. Copy each symbol into the key exactly as it looks on the map, and write its meaning next to it.

Fifth, draw an arrow that points north, with the letter N, so that people know which way the map faces. We will learn more about north in the lesson on compass directions.

Finally, check your map. Does it have a title, a key and a north arrow? Is every symbol in the key? Could a visitor use it to find their way?

The error museum, four exhibits. One: reading a map without looking at the key; the key explains the symbols. Two: drawing buildings as side views on a map; a map is a plan view. Three: using a symbol on the map that is not in the key; every symbol must be in the key. Four: forgetting the title; the title tells the reader what place the map shows.

The questions for this section are with you now: the steps for drawing a map with symbols and a key.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with Sanele's sugar cane farm — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Read the Farm Map

Read the key first.

Green squares: cane fields. Blue line: stream.

Keep the farm dam next to the stream in mind and try the questions for this part: reading a farm map.

## Subtopic: Read a Village and a Town

The village map shows homesteads, a school and taps.

The town map shows streets, a taxi rank and many buildings.

Hold the busy map of KwaDukuza in mind and try this part's questions: village and town maps.

## Subtopic: Draw a Map

Title, plan view, simple symbols, a key, a north arrow.

Pocket summary of the lesson. To read a map, look at the title, then use the key to understand each symbol, and find features and answer questions from the map. To draw a map, write a title, draw the area from above, use simple symbols, make a key in a corner box, and add an arrow pointing north. Every symbol on the map must be in the key.

Hold Sanele's own map with its key in the corner in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: drawing a map.
