### subtopic_1 — What Symbols Are
**Assistant:** Why is water always blue on maps, even when a river is brown?

**Tutor:** Using blue for water is a rule that mapmakers all over the world agree on. It is called a convention, which means a way of doing things that everyone accepts. Real rivers in South Africa are often brown, because they carry soil, especially after rain. The Orange River and the Limpopo River often look brown. Dams can look green, and the sea can look grey on a cloudy day. But if every map used the real colour on the day the map was made, maps would be confusing. A brown river might look like a road. So all mapmakers use blue for every kind of water, and everybody knows it. That makes maps much easier to read, even in another country or another language. Other colours have conventions too. Green often means plants or parks. If you ever make your own map, using these same conventions will help other people understand it quickly.

### subtopic_2 — Symbols on a Large-scale Map
**Assistant:** Who decides what symbols to use on a map?

**Tutor:** The person or organisation that makes the map decides. In South Africa, the official maps of the whole country are made by a government office in Cape Town called the Chief Directorate: National Geo-spatial Information. They have a fixed set of symbols that they use on every official map, so that the maps all match. Many other countries have similar offices. Companies that make tourist maps, road maps and maps on cell phones choose their own symbols. They often use bright pictures, because their maps are for ordinary people, not for experts. Some symbols are used almost everywhere, such as blue for water and a P for parking, because people have got used to them. When you draw your own map, you are the mapmaker, so you can choose your own symbols. Just remember to keep them simple, make them different from each other, and explain every one of them in your key.

### subtopic_3 — Keys on South African Maps
**Assistant:** Can a map have too many symbols?

**Tutor:** Yes, it can. A map is useful only if it is easy to read. If a mapmaker tries to show every lamp post, every bin and every bush with a symbol, the map becomes crowded and messy, and the important things get lost. That is why mapmakers choose what to show, depending on what the map is for. A map for tourists shows museums, restaurants and hotels, but not every house. A road map shows roads, towns and petrol stations, but not shops or schools. A map for hikers shows footpaths, rivers, hills and camp sites. So before making a map, ask: who will use this map, and what do they need to know? Then show only the things that help them. On a large-scale map there is more space, so you can show more detail. On a small-scale map, such as a map of South Africa, you must leave out most details and show only the most important things.
