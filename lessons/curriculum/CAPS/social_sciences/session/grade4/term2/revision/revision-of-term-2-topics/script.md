# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the Term 2 topics. In Geography, the topic was map skills: side and plan views, symbols and keys, grid references, compass directions, and the map of South Africa with its oceans, provinces, cities and towns. In History, the topic was learning from leaders: what a leader is, the qualities of a good leader, and the life of Nelson Mandela. The anchor is a revision treasure hunt at a primary school in George, in the Western Cape, where the term began with a mug, a hat and a shoe. The teacher has hidden clue cards around the school. Each clue is a question about Term 2, and the answer tells the learners where to go next. The first clue says: look at me from above, and I am just a circle. The learners run to the staffroom, where a mug sits on the table, with the next clue underneath. The big idea is that maps are plan views that use symbols, keys, grids and directions to show places, including the provinces of South Africa, and that good leaders, such as Nelson Mandela, show qualities we can all learn from. The whole of Term 2 is examinable.

## Subtopic: Views, Symbols, Grids and Directions

The first clues remind the class about views. A side view is seen from the side, with your eyes level, and shows how tall things are. A plan view, or bird's-eye view, is seen from straight above. A mug from above is a circle, a hat is two circles, and a desk is a rectangle. Every map is a plan view.

The next clue is a map of the school with symbols. A symbol is a small picture, letter, line or colour that stands for something real. Blue is for water, a cross may be a church, and H may be a hospital. A large-scale map shows a small area in lots of detail. The key, or legend, lists every symbol and its meaning. To draw a map, write a title, draw it from above, use simple symbols, add a key and a north arrow.

The map has a grid. In an alpha-numeric grid, the columns have letters and the rows have numbers. A grid reference is the letter first and then the number, such as C3. To find a block, go along to the letter, then down to the number.

The last clue in this group is a compass. The four compass directions, clockwise, are north, east, south and west: Never Eat Soggy Weet-Bix. The sun rises in the east and sets in the west. A compass needle points north. On most maps, north is at the top, shown by a north arrow. The treasure is north of the hall. Remember that left and right change when you turn around, but north, east, south and west never do.

The questions for this section are with you now: views, symbols and keys, grid references and compass directions.

## Subtopic: The Map of South Africa

The next clue is a map of South Africa. The sea is blue, the land is green, yellow or brown, and the coastline is where they meet. The Atlantic Ocean is along the west coast and is cold. The Indian Ocean is along the south and east coasts and is warm. They meet at Cape Agulhas, the southernmost tip of Africa. South Africa's neighbours are Namibia, Botswana, Zimbabwe, Mozambique, Eswatini and Lesotho, which is surrounded by South Africa.

South Africa has nine provinces. The four coastal provinces are the Northern Cape, the Western Cape, the Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal. The five inland provinces are the Free State, North West, Gauteng, Limpopo and Mpumalanga. The Northern Cape is the biggest, and Gauteng is the smallest but has the most people.

The capitals are: Kimberley, Cape Town, Bhisho, Pietermaritzburg, Bloemfontein, Mahikeng, Johannesburg, Polokwane and Mbombela. George, where this class lives, is in the Western Cape, east of Cape Town, near the Indian Ocean coast.

To find your settlement, find your province, then its capital, then your town, using the index and grid references. To describe where you live, give your settlement, your province, a big place nearby and a compass direction.

The questions for this section are with you now: oceans, neighbours, provinces, capitals and your own settlement.

## Subtopic: Learning from Leaders

The last clues are about leaders. A leader guides a group towards a goal. People become leaders by being born into it, by being elected or appointed, or by stepping up to act.

A good leader listens to people, like Desmond Tutu at the Truth and Reconciliation Commission. A good leader is a servant of the people and works for the good of others, like Albertina Sisulu, a nurse. A good leader works with a team, like Siya Kolisi and Oliver Tambo, and has courage, like Helen Suzman and Lilian Ngoyi. A good leader is dedicated, committed and prepared to sacrifice, like Albert Luthuli, who gave up being a chief, and Gandhi, who led peaceful protests.

Nelson Mandela was born in Mvezo in 1918 and grew up in Qunu. He became a lawyer and an ANC leader who fought apartheid. He spent twenty-seven years in prison and was released in 1990. He became the first democratically elected President in 1994, and chose peace instead of revenge. It was not always easy, he was not always popular, and he was not perfect. We follow his good example on Mandela Day, and every day.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: writing a grid reference with the number first; it is letter first, then number. Two: putting the Atlantic Ocean on the east coast; it is on the west. Three: thinking the biggest province has the most people; the Northern Cape is biggest, but Gauteng has the most people. Four: thinking good leaders are perfect and always popular; they are human, and they keep trying.

The questions for this section are with you now: leaders, their qualities, and Nelson Mandela.
