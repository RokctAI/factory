### subtopic_1 — Hard Choices and Heavy Costs
**Assistant:** Why would the government not let Mandela go to his mother's funeral?

**Tutor:** The apartheid government saw Mandela as a dangerous enemy, and it wanted to keep him away from the public. They were afraid that if he attended a funeral, crowds of people would gather to see him, and that this could become a big protest against the government. They also wanted to break his spirit and make prisoners feel powerless. Prison rules for political prisoners were very strict and often cruel, and requests like this were usually refused. Mandela wrote to the authorities asking to attend his son's funeral in 1969, but his request was turned down. He wrote later that it was one of the hardest times of his life, and that he spent the night lying in his cell, remembering his son. Stories like this help us understand that political prisoners were not only punished with prison time, but were also cut off from their families in the most painful moments of life.

### subtopic_2 — Not Always Popular
**Assistant:** How can someone be called a terrorist by some people and a hero by others?

**Tutor:** People see the same person very differently depending on where they stand and what they believe. The apartheid government saw Mandela as a terrorist, because he helped to start an armed group that attacked government buildings and power lines, and because he wanted to end the government's power. Many people who supported apartheid, or who were frightened by the idea of change, agreed. But most South Africans, and many people around the world, saw him as a freedom fighter and a hero, because he was fighting against a system that was unfair and cruel, after peaceful protests had been met with violence and banning. Over time, as more people learnt the truth about apartheid and saw how Mandela chose peace after his release, views changed. Historians try to understand all these different views, and why people held them, without simply calling one side good and the other bad. That is why history needs many sources.

### subtopic_3 — Not Perfect
**Assistant:** If leaders are not perfect, why should we follow them?

**Tutor:** We should not follow any leader blindly, as if they can do no wrong. Instead, we can follow the good things they stand for, and the good examples they set. When we look at Mandela, we can learn from his courage, his patience, his willingness to listen and forgive, and his choice to step down after one term. We can also learn from his mistakes, such as how hard it was for his family, or that he wished he had acted sooner on HIV and AIDS. In a democracy, people have the right and the duty to question their leaders, to praise them when they do well, and to criticise them politely when they do wrong. If a leader does badly, people can vote for someone else. So a good follower is not someone who obeys everything. A good follower thinks carefully, supports what is right, and speaks up when something is wrong.
