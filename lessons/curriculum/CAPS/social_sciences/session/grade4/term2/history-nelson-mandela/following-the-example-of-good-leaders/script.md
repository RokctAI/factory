# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how ordinary people can follow the example of good leaders such as Nelson Mandela. It explains Mandela Day, how the qualities of a good leader can be practised in everyday life, and how we can become good leaders and good citizens. The anchor is Mandela Day at a primary school in eMalahleni, a coal mining town in Mpumalanga. It is a cold winter morning on the 18th of July. The Grade 4 class has planned its sixty-seven minutes. One group picks up litter around the school fence. Another plants spekboom bushes in the garden. A third group packs food parcels for an old age home down the road. A fourth group reads stories to the Grade R learners. As they work, their teacher asks: which qualities of a good leader are you showing right now? The big idea is that we do not need to be famous or powerful to follow the example of good leaders, and that we can practise good leadership every day in small ways. Explaining how ordinary people can follow good leaders is examinable.

## Subtopic: Mandela Day

Nelson Mandela's birthday, the 18th of July, is celebrated as Mandela Day, also called Nelson Mandela International Day. The United Nations declared it in 2009, and it is celebrated in many countries all over the world.

On Mandela Day, people are asked to spend sixty-seven minutes doing something to help others. The number sixty-seven stands for the sixty-seven years that Mandela spent working for the rights of others, as a young activist, a lawyer, a prisoner, a President and a peacemaker. Each minute stands for one year of his service.

People do many kinds of things on Mandela Day. They clean up parks, rivers and beaches. They paint schools and clinics. They plant trees and vegetable gardens. They collect food, clothes and blankets for people who need them. They visit old age homes and children's homes. They read to young children.

The idea behind Mandela Day is that everyone has the power to make the world better, even with small actions. Mandela often said that it is up to each one of us to make the world a better place, and that we should not wait for someone else to do it. Sixty-seven minutes is about an hour, which anyone can give.

But the hope is that people will not help only on one day a year. Mandela Day is a reminder to keep helping others every day. Many schools and companies now run projects all year round that started on Mandela Day.

The class in eMalahleni chooses its activities by listening to the community. The principal says the old age home needs food. The Grade R teacher says her learners love stories. So the class's plan is based on real needs.

The questions for this section are with you now: Mandela Day, sixty-seven minutes, and the kinds of things people do.

## Subtopic: Leading in Everyday Life

The qualities of a good leader are not only for presidents. Ordinary people, including children, can practise them every day.

Listening: you can listen carefully to your friends, your family and your teachers, without interrupting, and try to understand how they feel.

Serving others and working for the good of others: you can help at home without being asked, share with someone who has less, or help a classmate who is struggling with a sum.

Teamwork: in a group project or a sports team, you can do your share, encourage others and share the praise.

Courage: you can stand up for someone who is being bullied, tell the truth even when it is hard, and say no when friends want to do something wrong.

Dedication and commitment: you can keep practising something difficult, such as reading or a sport, and keep your promises.

Sacrifice: you can give up some of your playtime to help someone, like the learners reading to the Grade R class.

Forgiveness: Mandela forgave the people who had put him in prison. You can practise forgiving a friend who has hurt you and apologising when you are wrong.

The teacher in eMalahleni points out that the litter group is showing teamwork, the food parcel group is serving others, and the reading group is sacrificing playtime for younger children.

The questions for this section are with you now: practising the qualities of a good leader in everyday life.

## Subtopic: Growing into Good Leaders

Following good leaders also means learning to recognise good leadership, so that we can choose good leaders.

At school, when you vote for a class captain, think about the qualities you have learnt: does this person listen, care about others, work well with a team, and act fairly? Do not vote only for the most popular person or your best friend.

When you are eighteen years old, you will be able to vote in elections in South Africa. Many people, including Mandela, made great sacrifices so that every adult could vote. Voting is a way of choosing leaders, and good citizens use their vote wisely.

Young people can also be leaders now. In 1976, school students in Soweto stood up against unfair laws, and today young South Africans lead clubs, clean up their communities and speak up about issues such as the environment.

Remember too that leaders are not perfect. We follow their good examples, not every single thing they did. And we keep thinking for ourselves, asking whether each choice is fair and kind to everyone.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking you must be famous to be a leader; anyone can practise leadership. Two: thinking Mandela Day is only for one day; it reminds us to help every day. Three: thinking a good class captain is the most popular person; look for good leadership qualities. Four: thinking following a leader means copying everything they did; we follow their good examples.
