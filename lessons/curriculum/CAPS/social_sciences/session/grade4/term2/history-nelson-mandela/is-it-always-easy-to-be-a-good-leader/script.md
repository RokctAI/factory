# Part 1 — Expert

This session asks whether it is always easy to be a good leader, whether good leaders are always popular, and whether they are perfect, using the life of Nelson Mandela. The anchor is a class trip by Grade 4 learners from Cape Town to Robben Island. They cross Table Bay on a ferry, with Table Mountain behind them, and board a bus that takes them around the island. At the maximum security prison, their guide is a man who was himself a political prisoner on the island for many years. He leads them down a long corridor to a small cell, about two metres wide, with a mat, a blanket and a small window with bars. This was Nelson Mandela's cell. The guide says: people think being a leader is about fame and power. Let me tell you what it really cost. The big idea is that being a good leader is often very hard, that good leaders are not always popular, and that even great leaders are human and make mistakes. Discussing these questions using Mandela's life is examinable.

## Subtopic: Hard Choices and Heavy Costs

Being a good leader is not always easy. Leaders often have to make hard decisions, and every choice has a cost.

The guide tells the learners about the costs Mandela paid. He spent twenty-seven years in prison, from the age of forty-four to the age of seventy-one. He missed most of his children's childhoods. While he was on Robben Island, his mother died in 1968, and the next year his eldest son, Thembekile, was killed in a car accident. The prison authorities did not allow Mandela to go to either funeral. He wrote that these were among the saddest moments of his life. His wife, Winnie, was also arrested and banned many times, and their daughters grew up while both parents were often unable to care for them.

Mandela also had to make hard choices. In 1961, after the ANC was banned and peaceful protests were met with violence, he helped to start an armed group. This was a hard decision, and people have different views about it. In 1985, the government offered to release him if he gave up the armed struggle. He refused, because the ANC was still banned and his people were still not free. That meant staying in prison for five more years.

Later, he decided to start secret talks with the government that had imprisoned him. He did this without being able to ask most of his comrades first, because he was in prison. It was risky, because they might think he had betrayed them.

The questions for this section are with you now: the personal costs of leadership and the hard choices Mandela made.

## Subtopic: Not Always Popular

We sometimes think that good leaders are always loved. But good leaders are often unpopular, at least for a time, and with some people.

During apartheid, the government called Mandela a terrorist and a dangerous criminal. Newspapers were not even allowed to publish his photograph or his words for many years. Some other countries also saw him as a terrorist. The United States kept his name on a list of terrorists until 2008, long after he had been President.

When Mandela began talks with the government and later worked with F.W. de Klerk, some people in his own movement were angry. They thought he was giving away too much, and that he was too ready to forgive people who had done terrible things. Some white South Africans, on the other hand, feared and distrusted him.

When he wore the Springbok jersey in 1995, some people praised him, and some criticised him.

A good leader sometimes has to do what they believe is right, even when it makes them unpopular. A leader who only does what is popular may not be doing what is best for everyone. That takes courage.

Today, most people in South Africa and around the world respect Mandela. But people still discuss and disagree about some of his choices. That is normal, and it is part of studying history. A historian listens to all the different views, asks why people held them, and checks the sources, before making up their own mind.

The questions for this section are with you now: why good leaders are not always popular, and how views of Mandela changed.

## Subtopic: Not Perfect

No leader is perfect, because every leader is a human being. Good leaders make mistakes too.

Mandela himself said this clearly. He said: I am not a saint, unless you think of a saint as a sinner who keeps on trying. He meant that he had made mistakes, like everyone, but that he kept trying to do better.

Mandela admitted that he had not always been a good husband and father, because his work for freedom took him away from his family. After he became President, he also said that he should have done more, and sooner, about the spread of HIV and AIDS in South Africa. After he left office, he spoke out about it strongly and helped to fight it.

Knowing that leaders are not perfect helps us in two ways. First, it means we should look at leaders honestly, praising what they did well and learning from their mistakes. We should not treat any leader as if they can do no wrong. Second, it means that we, too, can be leaders, even though we are not perfect.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking good leaders have an easy life; leadership often brings hard choices and sacrifice. Two: thinking good leaders are always popular; Mandela was called a terrorist and criticised by some of his own people. Three: thinking great leaders never make mistakes; Mandela said he was not a saint. Four: thinking we must agree with everything a leader did; historians look at both strengths and mistakes.

The questions for this section are with you now: why leaders are not perfect, and how to look at leaders honestly.
