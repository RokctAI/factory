# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces map skills by looking at objects and places from different views. It explains the side view and the view from above, called the plan view, how familiar objects look in a plan view, and how to draw a plan of a table, a classroom and simple buildings. The anchor is a Grade 4 classroom in George, in the Western Cape. The teacher puts a mug, a hat and a shoe on a desk at the front. She asks one learner to crouch down so her eyes are level with the desk, and another learner to stand on a chair and look straight down. The two learners describe the objects, and they sound as if they are seeing completely different things. Then the class draws their own classroom, as a bird flying over with the roof taken off would see it. The big idea is that the same object looks different from different views, and that maps are drawn as plan views, from directly above. Recognising and drawing side views and plan views is examinable.

## Subtopic: The Side View

A view is what you see from where you are looking. The side view is what you see when your eyes are level with the object, looking at it from the side. It is how we see most things every day: people, houses, trees and cars from the front, back or side.

The learner crouching down sees the mug from the side. She sees its height, its straight sides, the handle sticking out, and perhaps a picture printed on it. She sees the hat with its round top and its brim sticking out on both sides. She sees the shoe with its sole, its heel, its laces and the opening at the top.

A side view shows how tall something is. We can see the shape of the front or the side, but we cannot see the top properly. In a side view, things in front can hide things behind them. If two mugs stand one behind the other, the front mug hides the back mug.

Photographs taken by a person standing on the ground are usually side views. When you look at a building from across the street, you see its front wall, its windows and door, and the slope of its roof.

Side views are useful for showing what something looks like, but they are not good for showing where things are in a place, because things hide each other, and you cannot see the whole layout.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a side view is, and what it shows.

## Subtopic: The View from Above

The plan view is what you see when you look straight down on an object from directly above. It is also called a bird's-eye view, because it is how a bird flying overhead would see things.

The learner on the chair looks straight down. The mug becomes a circle, the rim, with a smaller circle inside, and a little bump on the side, the handle. The hat becomes two circles, a big circle for the brim, and a smaller circle in the middle for the top. The shoe becomes a long, rounded shape, wider at the toes and narrower at the heel, with an opening near the back.

In a plan view, we cannot see how tall things are. A tall mug and a short mug with the same rim look exactly the same from above. But we can see the shape of the top and where things are, next to each other.

There is also a view in between, called an oblique view, where you look down at an angle, like from the top of a hill or a tall building. You can see the tops and some of the sides. Many photographs from aeroplanes are oblique views.

Photographs taken straight down from an aeroplane, a drone or a satellite are plan views. They are called aerial photographs, and mapmakers use them to draw maps.

The questions for this section are with you now: plan views, bird's-eye views, oblique views and aerial photographs.

## Subtopic: Drawing Plans

A plan is a drawing of a place or an object in plan view. Every map is a plan.

To draw a plan of a table, imagine you are looking straight down on it. A rectangular table becomes a rectangle. If there is a book and a pencil case on it, draw their shapes from above, in the right places. Do not draw the table legs, because from above they are hidden under the table top.

To draw a plan of a classroom, imagine the roof has been lifted off and you are looking down from above. Draw the outside walls as a large rectangle. Leave a gap for the door, and show the windows on the walls. Then draw each desk as a small rectangle, the teacher's table as a bigger rectangle, chairs as small squares, and a round dustbin as a circle. Put each one in its correct place. The desks in the George classroom are in four rows, so the plan shows four rows of little rectangles.

To draw a plan of a simple building, such as a house, draw the outline of its walls from above. A plan of a house shows the rooms, the walls between them and the doors. A plan of a school shows the classrooms, the hall, the playground and the gate.

Keep a plan simple, neat and in the right shapes. Plans help builders build houses, and maps help people find their way.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: drawing table legs on a plan view; from above, they are hidden. Two: thinking a plan view shows how tall something is; it shows only the top. Three: thinking a mug from above looks like a mug from the side; from above it is a circle. Four: drawing a side view and calling it a map; maps are plan views.

The questions for this section are with you now: drawing plans of a table, a classroom and buildings.
