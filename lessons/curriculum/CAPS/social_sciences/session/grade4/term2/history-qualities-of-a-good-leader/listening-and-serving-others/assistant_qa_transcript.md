### subtopic_1 — A Good Leader Listens
**Assistant:** What does reconciliation mean?

**Tutor:** Reconciliation means bringing people back together after they have been divided or have hurt each other, so that they can live in peace. It is a big word for a very difficult thing. During apartheid, many people were hurt, treated unfairly or even killed because of their race or because they opposed the government. After 1994, South Africans had to find a way to live together in one country. Some people wanted revenge. Others wanted to forget the past. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission tried a different way. It gave victims a chance to tell their stories and be heard. It also allowed people who had done terrible things to confess the full truth, and in some cases be pardoned, which is called amnesty. The idea was that the truth must be known before people can forgive and heal. Not everyone agreed that it was fair, and reconciliation is still not finished. But it helped South Africa avoid a war.

### subtopic_2 — A Servant of the People
**Assistant:** How can I be a good listener?

**Tutor:** Being a good listener is a skill, and you can practise it every day. First, stop what you are doing and look at the person who is speaking. Put down your pencil or phone. Second, let them finish without interrupting, even if you want to say something. Third, show that you are listening by nodding or saying small things like: I see, or: and then? Fourth, try to understand how they feel, not only what they say. If your friend says they did not get picked for the team, they might be feeling sad or embarrassed. Fifth, ask a question to check you understood, such as: do you mean that you want to try again next time? Finally, do not rush to give advice. Sometimes people just want to be heard. A good trick to remember is that we have two ears and one mouth, so we should listen twice as much as we speak.

### subtopic_3 — Working for the Good of Others
**Assistant:** Was Albertina Sisulu ever a leader in government?

**Tutor:** Yes. For most of her life, Albertina Sisulu could not be part of the government, because under apartheid Black South Africans were not allowed to vote or be members of Parliament. Instead, she led in her community and in organisations that fought for freedom, such as the ANC Women's League and the Federation of South African Women. She helped organise the women's march of 1956. In the 1980s, she was a leader of the United Democratic Front, a movement of hundreds of organisations against apartheid. After apartheid ended, in 1994, she was elected as a Member of Parliament in the first democratic Parliament. She had the honour of nominating Nelson Mandela to become President. She retired from Parliament in 1999. She died in 2011, at the age of 92. In 2018, South Africa celebrated what would have been her hundredth birthday, and places such as a main road in Johannesburg are named after her.
