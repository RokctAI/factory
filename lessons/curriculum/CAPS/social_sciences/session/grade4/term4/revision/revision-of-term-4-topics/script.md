# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the Term 4 topics, and links them to Term 3. In Geography, the topic was water in South Africa: the uses of water, water as a resource, the water cycle, storing water and the ways people get their water. In History, the topic was communication through time: the postal system, radio and television, typewriters, the telegraph and telephone, cell phones and cameras, and computers and the internet. The anchor is the end-of-year expo at a primary school in Upington, a town in the Northern Cape on the banks of the Orange River. Upington is in one of the driest parts of South Africa, but water from the Orange River irrigates long green rows of grapevines. In the school hall, the Grade 4 class has set up six tables, each with posters and models. Parents and other classes walk from table to table, and the learners at each table ask the visitors a revision question. Their teacher, Mrs Cloete, gives a sticker to everyone who answers correctly. The big idea is that water is a precious resource that people use, store and bring to their homes in many ways, and that communication has become faster and reaches further over time, while some old forms continue. The whole of Term 4 is examinable, together with parts of Term 3.

## Subtopic: Using Water and the Water Cycle

The first table shows a water diary. We use water every day for drinking, cooking, washing ourselves, washing clothes and dishes, cleaning, flushing toilets and watering gardens. About two thirds of our bodies is water, and we must drink clean water every day. South Africa is a dry country, so we save water with short showers, closed taps, grey water and fixed leaks, as Cape Town did during the drought of 2018.

Water has many other uses. Farming uses the most water in South Africa, for irrigating crops and for animals, like the grape farms along the Orange River. This links to Term 3, when we learnt that crops need water and that farmers irrigate in dry areas. Factories use water to make products and cool machines, mines use it to wash ore and cool tunnels, and coal power stations boil water into steam to make electricity. Water is also used for gardens, parks and recreation.

The second table shows the water cycle. About 97 out of every 100 litres of the earth's water is salty, and only about 3 is fresh, most of it frozen or underground. The sun drives the water cycle: evaporation, when water rises as vapour and leaves its salt behind; condensation, when the vapour cools and forms clouds; precipitation, when rain, hail or snow falls; and collection, when water flows in rivers, gathers in lakes and soaks underground, before returning to the sea.

Fresh water in nature is found in rivers and streams, wetlands, lakes and under the ground. Wetlands act like sponges.

The questions for this section are with you now: the uses of water, salt and fresh water, and the water cycle.

## Subtopic: Storing and Getting Water

The third table shows how people store water. People store water because rain falls only in some seasons, droughts happen, water may be far away and taps can stop. At home, they use clay pots, which keep water cool, buckets, drums, jerry cans and rain-water tanks. Stored water must be covered and kept clean. Towns store water in dams, which are walls built across rivers, and in reservoirs. Dams store water and control floods, but they flood land and lose water to evaporation.

The fourth table shows how people get their water. Some people collect water directly from rivers, streams and springs and carry it home, which takes time, is heavy work, and the water may carry germs, so it must be settled, filtered and boiled. Some get water from under the ground: a well is wide and dug by hand, and a borehole is narrow and drilled by a machine, with water pumped up by hand pumps, windmills or solar pumps. In places with no other source, water trucks bring water, but pipes and taps are the long-term answer.

Most towns get water from dams. Pipes carry it to a treatment works, where it is screened, settled, filtered and disinfected with chlorine. Pumps push the clean water to reservoirs, and pipes carry it to taps. Used water goes through sewers to a wastewater treatment works.

The questions for this section are with you now: storing water and the ways people get their water.

## Subtopic: Communication Through Time

The fifth table shows older ways of communicating. Communication means sharing messages from a sender to a receiver. Long ago, people used oral tradition, rock art, messengers and drums. The postal system began in South Africa with a letter left in a shoe in a tree in Mossel Bay in 1500, and the first post office opened in Cape Town in 1792. Stamps began in 1840. Typewriters, from 1868, made writing faster and neater with no electricity. The telegraph sent dots and dashes in Morse code along wires, and Bell's telephone of 1876 carried voices.

The sixth table shows newer ways. Radio sends sound through the air, and the SABC began in 1936. Television sends pictures and sound, and it came to South Africa only in 1976. Cameras changed from slow early photographs to digital cameras, and cell phones came to South Africa in 1994. The internet links computers worldwide, and South Africa's first link was made in Makhanda in 1988.

Like transport in Term 3, communication changed slowly for thousands of years, and then very quickly in the last two hundred years.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking homes use the most water; farming uses the most. Two: thinking evaporated sea water is salty; the salt stays behind. Three: thinking the telegraph sent voices; it sent dots and dashes. Four: thinking old forms of communication have disappeared; people still talk, tell stories and write letters.

The questions for this section are with you now: communication through time.
