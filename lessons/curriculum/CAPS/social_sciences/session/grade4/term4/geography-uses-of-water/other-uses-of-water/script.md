# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the other uses of water, beyond our daily personal uses. It explains how water is used for farming, in factories, in mines, to make electricity, in gardens and parks, and for recreation, which means sport and fun. The anchor is the Vaal River, one of the most important rivers in South Africa. It begins in Mpumalanga, flows past Gauteng and the Free State, and joins the Orange River in the Northern Cape. A Grade 4 class from Vereeniging, a town on the banks of the Vaal, goes on a day trip with their teacher, Mr Molefe. They start at the Vaal Dam, where they see sailboats and people fishing. On the way back, they pass a large coal power station with its tall cooling towers and steam rising into the sky, and in the distance, a huge factory near Sasolburg. Mr Molefe explains that the same river also helps gold mines and irrigates farms far downstream. The big idea is that water is needed for almost everything people do and make, and that farming uses the most water of all in South Africa. Explaining the other uses of water is examinable.

## Subtopic: Farming

Farming uses more water than anything else in South Africa: more than half of all the water that people use goes to farms.

Crops need water to grow. In areas where there is not enough rain, farmers use irrigation, bringing water from rivers, dams or boreholes through canals, pipes and sprinklers. Water from the Vaal River is used by the Vaalharts irrigation scheme in the Northern Cape, one of the largest irrigation areas in South Africa. There, farmers grow maize, wheat, groundnuts, cotton and pecan nuts in an area that would be too dry without irrigation. The Orange River also irrigates vineyards and fruit farms in the dry Northern Cape.

Farm animals need water to drink every day. A cow can drink several buckets of water in a day, and even more when it is hot.

Farmers also use water to clean dairies, milking machines and sheds, to wash fruit and vegetables in packhouses, and to spray crops.

Some ways of irrigating waste water. When water is sprayed into the air on a hot, windy day, a lot of it evaporates. Many farmers now use drip irrigation, where small pipes drip water slowly right at the roots of each plant. This saves a lot of water.

The questions for this section are with you now: irrigation, water for farm animals and drip irrigation.

## Subtopic: Factories, Mines and Electricity

Factories use water in many ways. They use it to make products, to clean machines and to cool hot machines. Paper mills need a lot of water to turn wood into paper. Food and drink factories use water to wash, cook and make products such as cold drinks, juice and beer. The large factory near Sasolburg uses water from the Vaal River to make fuel, plastics and other chemicals.

Mines use water to wash and separate the valuable ore from rock and soil, to cool the deep, hot tunnels of gold mines, and to keep down dust. But water from old mines can become polluted and must be cleaned before it flows into rivers.

Power stations need water to make electricity. Most of South Africa's electricity is made in coal power stations, many of them in Mpumalanga. They burn coal to boil water into steam. The steam turns machines called turbines, which make electricity. Then the steam is cooled in the tall cooling towers. Lethabo Power Station, near the Vaal River, works in this way.

Water can also make electricity directly, in a hydroelectric power station. Water rushing out of a dam turns the turbines. Hydroelectric power makes no smoke, but it needs a big river and a high dam, and South Africa has few rivers that are large enough. The Gariep Dam and the Vanderkloof Dam on the Orange River have hydroelectric power stations.

The questions for this section are with you now: water in factories, in mines and in power stations.

## Subtopic: Gardens and Recreation

In towns and cities, water is used for gardens, parks, sports fields and golf courses. Grass and plants need regular watering, especially in dry areas and in dry seasons. Firefighters also need water to put out fires, and hospitals and schools need it every day.

Water is also used for recreation, which means activities people do for fun, sport and relaxation. People swim in pools, rivers, dams and the sea. They fish, sail, canoe, row and water-ski on dams and rivers. The Vaal Dam is a popular place for sailing and fishing. Every year, paddlers race in the Dusi Canoe Marathon, from Pietermaritzburg to Durban along the Msunduzi and Umgeni rivers. Along the coast, people surf, swim and play on the beach.

Recreation brings tourists and jobs to towns near rivers, dams and the sea, such as boat hire, fishing shops, guest houses and restaurants.

Because South Africa is a dry country, all these users must share the water. When there is a drought, the government may limit how much water farms, factories and towns may use. People must also keep rivers clean, because polluted water cannot be used for drinking, farming or swimming.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking homes use the most water; farming uses the most. Two: thinking power stations need no water; coal power stations boil water into steam. Three: thinking mines only dig; they use water to wash ore and cool tunnels. Four: thinking water for fun is wasted; recreation brings jobs and tourists.

The questions for this section are with you now: water for gardens, parks, firefighting and recreation.

Factories use water to make and clean things. Mines use water to wash ore and cool tunnels. Power stations boil water into steam to make electricity.

Hydroelectric stations use rushing water from dams.

Hold the cooling towers in mind and try this part's questions: factories, mines and electricity.

Firefighters and hospitals need water too. Everyone must share water and keep rivers clean.
