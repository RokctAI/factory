### subtopic_1 — Why People Store Water
**Assistant:** How does a clay pot keep water cool?

**Tutor:** A clay pot that has not been glazed, which means it has not been covered with a shiny, waterproof coating, has many tiny holes in it, too small to see. A little of the water inside slowly seeps through these holes to the outside surface of the pot. There, the water evaporates into the air. When water evaporates, it takes heat away with it. You can feel this yourself: when you get out of a swimming pool on a windy day, you feel cold, because the water on your skin is evaporating. In the same way, as water evaporates from the outside of the pot, it cools the pot and the water inside it. This works best when the air is dry and the pot is kept in the shade, where a breeze can blow over it. People in many parts of Africa and the world have used this clever idea for thousands of years, long before fridges were invented, and some still do today.

### subtopic_2 — Storing Water at Home
**Assistant:** What is the Lesotho Highlands Water Project?

**Tutor:** The Lesotho Highlands Water Project is a huge project that South Africa and Lesotho agreed to build together. Lesotho is a mountainous country with a lot of rain and snow, while Gauteng, South Africa's busiest province, needs much more water than its own rivers can provide. So large dams, such as the Katse Dam and the Mohale Dam, were built high in the mountains of Lesotho. Long tunnels, cut through the mountains, carry the water to a river in South Africa that flows into the Vaal Dam, which supplies Gauteng. The water flows mostly downhill, so little pumping is needed. Lesotho also uses some of the water to make electricity. South Africa pays Lesotho for the water, which brings money to Lesotho. The first water arrived in 1998, and more dams are being built. But the project also had costs: some families in Lesotho had to move from their land and homes when the valleys were flooded by the new dams.

### subtopic_3 — Dams and Reservoirs
**Assistant:** How can we stop mosquitoes breeding in stored water?

**Tutor:** Mosquitoes lay their eggs on the surface of still water. The eggs hatch into tiny wriggling larvae that live in the water for about a week before turning into flying mosquitoes. Any open container of still water, even an old tyre, a tin or a blocked gutter, can become a place where mosquitoes breed. Some mosquitoes spread diseases, such as malaria, which is found in parts of Limpopo, Mpumalanga and northern KwaZulu-Natal. To stop mosquitoes, always keep water containers tightly covered with a lid, or with a cloth or mesh tied over the top, so mosquitoes cannot reach the water. Cover the opening of rain-water tanks with fine mesh. Use stored water regularly and clean the containers often. Turn over buckets, tins and old tyres that are not being used, so they cannot collect rain water. Keep gutters clean. If you see wriggling larvae in water, empty it onto dry ground, where they cannot survive.
