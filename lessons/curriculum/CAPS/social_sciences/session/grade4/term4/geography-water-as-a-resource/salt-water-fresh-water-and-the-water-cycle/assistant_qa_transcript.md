### subtopic_1 — Salt Water and Fresh Water
**Assistant:** Why does salty water make you more thirsty?

**Tutor:** Your body works hard to keep the right amount of salt in your blood, not too much and not too little. Sea water is about three or four times saltier than your blood. If you drink sea water, a lot of extra salt goes into your body. To get rid of that salt, your kidneys must make urine, and to carry the salt out, they need to use more water than you drank. So you end up losing more water than you took in. Your body becomes even drier, and you feel even more thirsty. If a person drinks only sea water for a long time, they can become very sick and even die. That is why sailors and people lost at sea have always needed fresh water to drink, and why lifeboats carry fresh water. Sea animals, such as fish, turtles and seabirds, have special body parts that help them get rid of extra salt, so they can live in the sea.

### subtopic_2 — The Water Cycle
**Assistant:** How does a desalination plant work?

**Tutor:** A desalination plant is a factory that takes the salt out of sea water so that people can drink it. The most common way today is called reverse osmosis. First, sea water is pumped from the ocean through large pipes. It is cleaned to remove sand, seaweed and tiny sea creatures. Then powerful pumps push the water at very high pressure through special sheets with holes so tiny that water can pass through, but salt cannot. On the other side of the sheets, fresh water comes out. The very salty water that is left behind, called brine, is piped back into the sea, where it must be spread out carefully so that it does not harm sea life. The fresh water has a few minerals added and is cleaned again, and then it goes into the town's water pipes. Desalination works well, but it costs a lot of money and uses a great deal of electricity, so it is usually used only where there is not enough fresh water.

### subtopic_3 — The Cycle in South Africa
**Assistant:** Why does a cold glass get wet on the outside?

**Tutor:** When you take a glass of cold water or a cold drink out of the fridge on a warm day, drops of water soon form on the outside of the glass. The water does not leak through the glass. It comes from the air. The air around us always has some invisible water vapour in it, from water that has evaporated. When the warm, moist air touches the cold glass, the air right next to the glass cools down. Cool air cannot hold as much water vapour as warm air, so some of the vapour turns back into tiny drops of liquid water on the glass. This is called condensation, and it is the same thing that happens high in the sky when clouds form. You can also see condensation on a bathroom mirror after a hot shower, on car windows on a cold morning, and as dew on grass early in the morning. Your breath on a cold window makes condensation too.
