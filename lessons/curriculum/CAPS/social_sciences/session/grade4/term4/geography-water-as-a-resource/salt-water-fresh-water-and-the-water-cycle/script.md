# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers water as a resource: salt water and fresh water on earth, and the natural water cycle. It explains where the earth's water is found, why only a tiny part of it is fresh water that people can use, and how the water cycle moves water from the sea to the land and back to the sea. The anchor is a Grade 4 classroom in Durban, close to the Indian Ocean. On Monday, the teacher, Mrs Govender, brings in a bottle of sea water that she collected at the beach. The learners taste a tiny drop on their fingers and pull faces, because it is very salty. Then they pour some into a large bowl, put a small empty cup in the middle, cover the bowl tightly with plastic wrap and place a small stone on top of the wrap, right above the cup. They leave the bowl on a sunny windowsill. On Wednesday, there are drops of water on the underside of the wrap, and a little water has dripped into the cup. A learner called Sibusiso tastes it and says it is not salty at all. The big idea is that most of the earth's water is salty, that fresh water is scarce, and that the sun drives a water cycle that turns salt water into fresh rain. Explaining salt water, fresh water and the water cycle is examinable.

## Subtopic: Salt Water and Fresh Water

From space, the earth looks blue, because about seven tenths of its surface is covered by water, mostly in the oceans and seas.

Almost all of the earth's water, about 97 out of every 100 litres, is salt water in the oceans and seas. Salt water has salt and other minerals dissolved in it. People and most land animals cannot drink it, because it makes the body lose water and become even more thirsty. We cannot use it to water crops either, because the salt harms plants.

Only about 3 out of every 100 litres of the earth's water is fresh water. Fresh water has very little salt in it, so people, animals and plants can use it. But most of this fresh water is frozen in the huge ice sheets of Antarctica and Greenland and in glaciers on high mountains. Much of the rest is underground.

Imagine all the water on earth in 100 buckets. About 97 buckets are salty. About 2 buckets are frozen ice. Only about 1 bucket is fresh water in rivers, lakes, wetlands and under the ground, that people can reach and use. And all the people, animals and plants on land must share it.

This is why fresh water is so precious, and why we must not waste or pollute it.

It is possible to remove the salt from sea water, in a factory called a desalination plant. Mossel Bay, in the Western Cape, has one. But it costs a lot of money and uses a great deal of electricity.

The questions for this section are with you now: salt water, fresh water and how much fresh water there is.

## Subtopic: The Water Cycle

The water on earth is always moving, in a never-ending circle called the water cycle. The sun is the engine that drives it.

Step one is evaporation. The sun heats the water in the oceans, rivers, lakes and dams. Some of the water turns into an invisible gas called water vapour, which rises into the air. When sea water evaporates, the salt is left behind, so the water vapour is fresh. Plants also give off water vapour through their leaves, which is called transpiration.

Step two is condensation. As the water vapour rises, it cools. Cool water vapour turns back into very tiny drops of water, which gather together to form clouds. You can see condensation when drops form on the outside of a cold glass, or on a bathroom mirror after a hot shower.

Step three is precipitation. The tiny drops in the clouds join together and become bigger and heavier, until they are too heavy to float. Then they fall to the ground as rain, or as hail or snow when it is very cold.

Step four is collection. Some rain runs over the ground into streams and rivers, and the rivers flow back to the sea. Some soaks into the soil and becomes underground water. Some collects in lakes, dams and wetlands. Then the sun heats the water again, and the cycle starts once more.

The questions for this section are with you now: evaporation, condensation, precipitation and collection.

## Subtopic: The Cycle in South Africa

Mrs Govender's bowl is a small water cycle. The sun warmed the salty water, and fresh water vapour rose: evaporation. It touched the cool plastic wrap and became drops: condensation. The drops ran down to the lowest point and fell into the cup: precipitation and collection. The salt stayed behind in the bowl.

In South Africa, most of our rain is made from water that evaporates from the oceans around us, especially the warm Indian Ocean. Winds carry moist air from the Indian Ocean inland over KwaZulu-Natal, Mpumalanga and Limpopo. When the air rises over mountains such as the Drakensberg, it cools, clouds form and rain falls. This is one reason why the east of South Africa is wetter than the west. By the time the air reaches the west, most of its water has already fallen as rain.

The water cycle means that water is never used up. The same water has been going around and around for millions of years. But the amount of fresh water in one place can change. In a drought, very little rain falls, and rivers and dams dry up.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking most of the earth's water is fresh; about 97 per 100 litres is salty. Two: thinking evaporated sea water is salty; the salt stays behind. Three: thinking clouds are made of smoke; they are tiny drops of water. Four: thinking water gets used up; it goes around in a cycle.

The questions for this section are with you now: the experiment, rain in South Africa and why water is never used up.

## Subtopic: Salty or Fresh

Most of the earth's water is salty sea water. We cannot drink it.

Only a tiny part is fresh water. Most fresh water is frozen ice or under the ground.

Keep the salty taste of the sea water in mind and try the questions for this part: salt water and fresh water.

## Subtopic: Round and Round

The sun heats the water, and it rises as vapour. That is evaporation.

The vapour cools and makes clouds. That is condensation.

Rain, hail or snow falls. That is precipitation.

Rivers carry the water back to the sea. That is collection.

Hold the cloud over the Drakensberg in mind and try this part's questions: the water cycle.

## Subtopic: Our Rain

Rain in South Africa comes mostly from water that evaporates from the oceans, especially the Indian Ocean. Water is never used up.

Pocket summary of the lesson. About 97 out of every 100 litres of the earth's water is salty, and only about 3 is fresh, most of it frozen or underground, so fresh water is precious. The sun drives the water cycle: water evaporates and leaves the salt behind, condenses into clouds, falls as precipitation and is collected in rivers, lakes and underground before returning to the sea. Water is never used up, but droughts happen.
