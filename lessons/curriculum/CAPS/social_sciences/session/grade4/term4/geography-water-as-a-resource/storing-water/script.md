## Subtopic: Why People Store Water

People store water for several reasons.

The first reason is the seasons. In most of South Africa, rain falls mainly in summer, and the winters are dry. In the Western Cape, it is the other way around. If people did not store water from the rainy season, they would have none in the dry season.

The second reason is drought. A drought is a long period, sometimes several years, with much less rain than usual. Rivers stop flowing, wetlands dry up and crops die. Stored water helps people, animals and farms to survive a drought. The Giyani area, like Cape Town, has suffered from serious droughts.

The third reason is distance. In many rural areas, the nearest river, spring or tap may be far from home. Carrying water every time it is needed takes a lot of time and work, so people collect a larger amount and store it at home.

The fourth reason is that people need water at all times of the day, even when the supply stops. Taps can stop because a pipe breaks or a pump has no electricity. Stored water means families can still drink, cook and wash.

Stored water is also needed in emergencies, such as fires, and by farmers, factories and towns that need a steady supply all year.

Because South Africa is a dry country with uneven rain, storing water is very important here.

## Subtopic: Storing Water at Home

People have stored water at home for thousands of years.

Clay pots are one of the oldest ways. In many African homes, water is kept in clay pots made by local potters. Clay pots keep water cool, because a little water seeps through the clay and evaporates from the outside, which cools the pot, even on a hot day. Some people also used gourds, which are hard, dried fruits like calabashes.

Buckets, drums and plastic containers are used to carry water and keep it at home. Many families keep water in buckets with lids, in large drums, or in plastic containers called jerry cans.

Rain-water tanks are large tanks, usually made of plastic, concrete or metal, which collect rain water from the roof. Rain runs off the roof into gutters, and a pipe carries it into the tank. A tap at the bottom lets people take water out. This is called rain-water harvesting. In some rural areas, the government and organisations have given families tanks to collect rain water.

Stored water must be kept clean and safe. Containers must be cleaned often and kept covered, so that dust, insects and animals cannot get in, and mosquitoes cannot lay eggs in the water. People should use a clean cup or ladle to take water out, not dip their hands in. If people are not sure the water is safe, they should boil it or treat it before drinking it.

## Subtopic: Dams and Reservoirs

Towns, cities, farms and factories need much more water than a tank can hold. So people store water in dams.

A dam is a strong wall built across a river. The wall holds back the river water, and a large lake forms behind it. Water is let out through pipes or gates when it is needed, and it is sent to towns, farms and factories.

South Africa has thousands of dams. The biggest, the Gariep Dam, is on the Orange River. The Vaal Dam stores water for Gauteng. Some of Gauteng's water even comes from dams in the mountains of Lesotho, such as the Katse Dam, through long tunnels.

Dams have many benefits. They store water from the rainy season for the dry season and for droughts. They control floods. Some make electricity. And people use them for fishing and boating.

But dams also have problems. They flood land that was used for farming, for homes and for wild animals, and people sometimes have to move. They block fish from swimming up rivers. And because a dam is wide and shallow, a lot of water evaporates from it in hot weather.

Towns also store clean water in reservoirs, which are large closed tanks, often on hills, so that the water can flow down to the houses.

Three: thinking a dam is a natural lake; people build a wall across a river. Four: thinking dams have no problems; they flood land and lose water to evaporation.
