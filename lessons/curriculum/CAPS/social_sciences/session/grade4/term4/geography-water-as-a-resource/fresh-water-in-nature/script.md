# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers fresh water in nature. It explains where fresh water is found in South Africa: as rain, in streams and rivers, in wetlands, in lakes, and under the ground. The anchor is a school hike in the Drakensberg, the highest mountains in South Africa, along the border with Lesotho. A Grade 4 class from Bergville, a small town at the foot of the mountains, sets off early in the morning with their teacher, Mr Hlongwane, and a mountain guide. Last night there was a thunderstorm, and the grass is still wet. As they climb, they cross a clear, cold stream, jump over small trickles of water running down the slope, and stop beside a soggy, green patch of grass where their shoes sink into the ground. The guide tells them that this is a wetland. Later, from a high viewpoint, they see a long, thin waterfall pouring over the cliffs, and far below, a river winding through the valley towards the sea. On the way down, they fill their bottles from a spring, where clean water bubbles out from between the rocks. The big idea is that all fresh water in nature comes from rain or snow, and that it is found in rivers, wetlands, lakes and under the ground. Explaining where fresh water is found in nature is examinable.

## Subtopic: Rain, Streams and Rivers

All fresh water in nature starts as precipitation, which is rain, hail or snow. South Africa is a dry country. On average, it gets only about half as much rain as the average for the whole world. The east gets more rain than the west, and some parts of the Northern Cape get very little rain at all. Most of South Africa gets its rain in summer, but the south-western part of the Western Cape gets winter rain.

When rain falls on mountains and hills, some of it runs down the slopes in small channels. These join together to make streams. A stream is a small, narrow flow of water. Streams join together to form rivers, which are larger flows of water.

The place where a river begins is its source, often high in the mountains. The place where it ends, usually at the sea, is its mouth. A smaller river or stream that flows into a bigger river is called a tributary.

The Orange River is the longest river in South Africa. Its source is in the mountains of Lesotho, and it flows west across the country to the Atlantic Ocean. The Vaal River is its biggest tributary. The Limpopo River forms part of the northern border of South Africa, and the Tugela River flows from the Drakensberg to the Indian Ocean.

Some rivers flow all year. Others, especially in dry areas, flow only after rain and are dry for the rest of the year.

The questions for this section are with you now: rain, streams, rivers, sources, mouths and tributaries.

## Subtopic: Wetlands and Lakes

A wetland is an area of land that is wet or covered with shallow water for all or part of the year. Marshes, swamps and vleis are kinds of wetlands. Wetlands are full of special plants, such as reeds, sedges and grasses, that like wet ground.

Wetlands are very important. They act like sponges. When it rains, they soak up the water and then release it slowly into streams and rivers, so rivers keep flowing in the dry season. They also slow down floods. Their plants clean the water by trapping mud and dirt. And they are homes for many birds, frogs, fish and insects.

The iSimangaliso Wetland Park, on the coast of KwaZulu-Natal, is one of the most important wetland areas in South Africa. It is a World Heritage Site, which means it is important to the whole world.

Sadly, many wetlands have been drained to build houses or grow crops, or have been polluted. Today, people work to protect and restore them.

A lake is a large body of water surrounded by land. South Africa has very few natural lakes. The largest natural freshwater lake in South Africa is Lake Sibaya, near the coast in northern KwaZulu-Natal. Most of the large bodies of water you see in South Africa are dams that people have built, not natural lakes. In dry areas, there are also pans, which are shallow hollows that fill with water after rain and dry up later.

The questions for this section are with you now: wetlands, why they matter, and lakes.

## Subtopic: Water Under the Ground

Not all rain runs into streams. Some of it soaks into the soil and trickles down through cracks and tiny spaces between grains of sand and rock. This water under the ground is called groundwater, or underground water.

Some layers of rock hold a lot of water in their cracks and spaces, like a sponge. Such a layer of rock is called an aquifer.

Sometimes, groundwater comes out of the ground by itself, on a hillside or at the bottom of a slope. This is called a spring, or a fountain. Some springs flow all year, even when it has not rained for weeks. Many towns and farms in South Africa grew up around springs, and some places are named after them, with names ending in -fontein, which means fountain or spring.

Groundwater is very important in dry areas, such as the Karoo and the Northern Cape, where there are few rivers. Many towns, villages and farms there depend on water from under the ground.

Groundwater moves very slowly, and it can take many years to fill up again. If people pump out too much, or pollute it, it can run out or become unsafe.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking South Africa gets lots of rain; it gets about half the world average. Two: thinking wetlands are useless mud; they store and clean water. Three: thinking most big water bodies in South Africa are natural lakes; most are dams. Four: thinking underground water fills up quickly; it can take many years.

The questions for this section are with you now: groundwater, aquifers and springs.
