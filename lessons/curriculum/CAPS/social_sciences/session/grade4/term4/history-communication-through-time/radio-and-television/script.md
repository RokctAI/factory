# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers radio and television, two forms of mass communication that reach millions of people at the same time. It explains how radio and television were invented, how they came to South Africa, and how they have changed people's lives. The anchor is Musa, a Grade 4 learner who lives in Newcastle, in northern KwaZulu-Natal. Every morning, his grandfather, Mkhulu Dlamini, sits at the kitchen table with a cup of tea and listens to the news on Ukhozi FM, an isiZulu radio station, on an old radio with a long silver aerial. In the afternoon, he listens to a soccer match, and the commentator shouts so excitedly when a goal is scored that Musa can almost see it. When Musa asks him about television, Mkhulu Dlamini laughs and tells him that when he was a young man, there was no television in South Africa at all. He remembers the evening of 5 January 1976, when the first television programmes were broadcast, and how the whole street squeezed into the lounge of the only family who owned a television set, to watch on a small black-and-white screen. The big idea is that radio and television carried voices and pictures through the air to millions of people at once, and changed how people get news, learn and enjoy themselves. ## Subtopic: The Radio

A radio broadcast sends sounds, such as voices and music, through the air from a radio station to radios far away. It does this using invisible waves called radio waves. A tall mast at the station sends out the waves, and the aerial of a radio receives them. The radio turns them back into sound.

In the 1890s, an Italian inventor, Guglielmo Marconi, was one of the first people to send messages using radio waves. In 1901, he sent a radio signal across the Atlantic Ocean. At first, radio was used to send messages to and from ships, but in the 1920s, radio stations began broadcasting voices and music for the public.

Radio broadcasting began in South Africa in the 1920s, in Johannesburg, Cape Town and Durban. In 1936, the South African Broadcasting Corporation, the SABC, was formed.

Radio became very popular, because a radio set was much cheaper than a television, it could run on batteries where there was no electricity, and people could listen while they worked. Families gathered around the radio for news, music, stories, sport and radio dramas.

During apartheid, the government controlled the SABC, so the news was often one-sided. Some South Africans listened secretly to Radio Freedom, which the African National Congress broadcast from other African countries.

Today, South Africa has many radio stations in all its official languages. Ukhozi FM has millions of listeners and is one of the biggest radio stations in Africa. Small community radio stations serve towns and villages.

## Subtopic: Television

Television sends both moving pictures and sound through the air to television sets. The word television means seeing from far away.

In 1926, a Scottish inventor, John Logie Baird, showed one of the first working television systems in London. In 1936, the British Broadcasting Corporation began one of the world's first regular television services. By the 1950s, millions of families in Europe and the United States had television sets.

South Africa got television much later than most countries. The government at the time worried that television would bring in ideas it did not want people to see, so it delayed it for many years. Test broadcasts began in 1975, and full television broadcasts began on 5 January 1976. At first, there was only one channel, run by the SABC, which broadcast for a few hours each evening, half in English and half in Afrikaans. In 1982, new channels began broadcasting in African languages.

Early television sets around the world showed pictures in black and white. South African broadcasts were in colour from the start, but many families first bought cheaper black-and-white sets. Over the years came more channels, pay channels, satellite dishes and, today, streaming on the internet.

Television showed people events as they happened. On 11 February 1990, people around the world watched on live television as Nelson Mandela walked out of prison. In 1995, millions watched South Africa win the Rugby World Cup.

## Subtopic: How They Changed Lives

Radio and television changed people's lives in many ways.

News travelled much faster. People could hear and see what was happening in their country and around the world on the same day, or even as it happened.

They helped people to learn. Schools broadcasts, children's programmes and documentaries taught people about history, science and nature. During the COVID-19 lockdown in 2020, when schools were closed, some lessons were broadcast on radio and television.

They brought entertainment into the home: music, sport, stories, comedy and soaps. People could support their sports teams without going to the stadium.

They helped languages and cultures. Programmes in all South African languages help to keep those languages strong, and local music and drama reach many people.

But there are also problems. Too much television can mean less time for reading, playing outside and talking with family. Some programmes are not suitable for children. And in the past, governments used radio and television to spread only their own views.

Three: thinking radio is no longer used; millions still listen every day. Four: thinking television only entertains; it also brings news and lessons.
