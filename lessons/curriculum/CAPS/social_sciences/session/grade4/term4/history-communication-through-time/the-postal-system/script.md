# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the postal system, one of the oldest ways of communicating over long distances. It explains how the postal system began in South Africa, how letters were carried over time, how stamps, addresses and sorting work, and how the post is used today. The anchor is the Post Office Tree in Mossel Bay, on the southern coast of the Western Cape. It is a large, old milkwood tree with thick, twisting branches, standing in the garden of a museum. A Grade 4 class from Mossel Bay has spent a week writing letters to pen pals at a school in Polokwane, in Limpopo. Their teacher, Mr Botha, brings them to the tree to post their letters. Under the tree stands a red post box shaped like a big old boot. Mr Botha explains that more than five hundred years ago, a Portuguese sailor left a letter in an old shoe hanging from a tree here, and that letters posted in the boot today still get a special stamp mark to remember that story. The learners drop their letters into the boot one by one. The big idea is that the postal system lets people send written messages and parcels over long distances, and that it grew from letters left under trees and stones to a network that covers the world. ## Subtopic: How the Post Began

In 1500, a Portuguese captain, Pedro de Ataíde, stopped at Mossel Bay on his way home from India. He wrote a letter about his voyage, wrapped it in an old shoe, and hung it from a tree. In 1501, another Portuguese sailor, João da Nova, found the letter. A milkwood tree said to be the same tree still stands in Mossel Bay today, and it is a national monument.

In the 1600s, sailors of many countries stopped at Table Bay, at the Cape, to get fresh water and food. They left letters under large, flat stones, with messages carved into them, such as the name of the ship and where the letters were. When the next ship arrived, sailors lifted the stones and took letters for their home country. These are called post office stones, and some can still be seen in museums in Cape Town.

The first proper post office in South Africa opened in Cape Town in 1792. Later, post offices were opened in other towns.

Inside the country, letters were at first carried by post runners, men who ran or walked long distances with bags of mail, often through rough country. Later, letters travelled by horse, by post cart and by mail coach, and then by train. The Union-Castle mail ships carried letters between Britain and South Africa every week. In 1911, the first airmail flight in South Africa carried letters by aeroplane from Kenilworth to Muizenberg, near Cape Town.

The questions for this section are with you now: the letter in the shoe, post office stones and the first post offices.

## Subtopic: Stamps, Addresses and Sorting

At first, the person who received a letter often had to pay for it. If they refused to pay, the post office earned nothing. To solve this, in 1840 Britain introduced the first postage stamp that could be stuck onto a letter, called the Penny Black. The sender paid for the stamp, and the stamp showed that the postage had been paid.

The Cape of Good Hope issued its first stamps in 1853. They were triangle-shaped, which made them unusual and easy to recognise, and today they are rare and valuable. Since then, South African stamps have shown animals, plants, famous people and important events.

To post a letter, you write the receiver's name and address on the front of the envelope: their name, the street or post office box, the town and the postal code. A postal code is a number that shows the area where the letter must go. In South Africa, postal codes have four numbers. You can write your own address on the back, so the letter can be returned if it cannot be delivered. Then you stick on a stamp and drop the letter into a post box or hand it in at a post office.

The letters are collected and taken to a sorting office, where they are sorted, first by hand and today mostly by machines that read postal codes. They travel by truck, train or aeroplane to the right area. Finally, a postman or postwoman delivers them to homes, or they are placed in post office boxes.

The questions for this section are with you now: stamps, addresses, postal codes and sorting.

## Subtopic: The Post Today

For a long time, letters were the main way to keep in touch over long distances. Families wrote to relatives working far away, people sent birthday cards and holiday postcards, and businesses sent bills and documents by post.

Today, people send fewer letters, because email, text messages and social media are much faster and cheaper. But the post is still used to send parcels, official documents, cards and items bought online.

In recent years, the South African Post Office has had serious money problems, and many branches have closed. Many people and businesses now use private courier companies, which deliver parcels quickly, often to the door.

The post still matters. A handwritten letter or card feels personal and special, and people often keep letters for many years. Old letters are also very useful sources for historians, because they show how people lived and what they thought.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the post always used stamps; stamps began in 1840. Two: thinking letters always travelled by aeroplane; runners, coaches, trains and ships came first. Three: thinking a postal code is a telephone number; it shows the area a letter must go to. Four: thinking nobody uses the post today; parcels and documents still travel by post.
