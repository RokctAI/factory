### subtopic_1 — Cameras and Photographs
**Assistant:** How was film turned into photographs?

**Tutor:** In a film camera, the film is a long strip of plastic coated with special chemicals that change when light falls on them. When you press the button, a little door in the camera, called the shutter, opens for a fraction of a second, and light from the scene makes an invisible picture on the film. After all the photos on a roll were taken, the film was wound back into its small container and taken to a photo shop. There, in a darkroom with only a dim red light, workers put the film into chemicals that made the pictures appear. The pictures on the film were negatives: light parts looked dark, and dark parts looked light. Then light was shone through each negative onto special paper, which was put into more chemicals, and the real photograph appeared. This took days, so people only saw their photos long after they took them, and they could not delete a bad photo.

### subtopic_2 — Cell Phones
**Assistant:** Why did cell phones spread so fast in South Africa?

**Tutor:** When cell phones arrived in South Africa in 1994, millions of people, especially in rural areas and townships, had never had a telephone at home, because landlines had not been built to their areas. Building telephone lines to every house is slow and expensive, but a single cell phone tower can serve thousands of people over a large area. So cell phones quickly reached places that landlines never had. Another reason was prepaid airtime. People did not need a bank account or a contract. They could buy a cheap phone and then buy airtime vouchers, even for a few rand, when they had money. Small businesses selling airtime and phone calls from containers and street stalls also helped. Cheaper phones, more towers and cheaper data later helped more people use the internet on their phones. Today, many South Africans go online mainly through their phones, rather than computers.

### subtopic_3 — How They Changed Lives
**Assistant:** What should I do if someone bullies me online?

**Tutor:** Cyberbullying is when someone uses a phone or the internet to hurt, threaten, embarrass or leave out another person, for example by sending mean messages, spreading rumours or sharing embarrassing photos. If it happens to you, remember that it is not your fault. Do not reply to the bully or send anything back, because that can make it worse. Do not delete the messages: keep them, or ask an adult to help you take screenshots, because they are evidence. Tell a trusted adult as soon as possible, such as a parent, a teacher or a school counsellor. You can also block the person and report them on the app or website. If you feel very upset or unsafe, you can call a children's helpline, such as Childline South Africa on 116, for free. And if you see someone else being bullied online, do not join in or share the messages. Instead, support the person and tell an adult.
