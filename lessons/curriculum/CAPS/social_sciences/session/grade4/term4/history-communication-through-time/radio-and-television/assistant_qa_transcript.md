### subtopic_1 — The Radio
**Assistant:** Why did South Africa get television so late?

**Tutor:** Most countries in Europe and North America had television by the 1950s, and many African countries had it by the 1960s. But South Africa only got television in 1976. The main reason was the apartheid government of the time. Some of its leaders were afraid that television, especially programmes from Britain and the United States, would show South Africans new ideas about how people of different races could live together as equals, and would weaken their control. Others worried that it would harm the Afrikaans language and culture. So, for many years, the government refused to allow television. By the 1970s, however, almost every other country had television, and many South Africans wanted it. The government finally agreed, and the SABC started a single channel in 1976. Even then, the government controlled what was shown, so the news gave mainly its own side of the story.

### subtopic_2 — Television
**Assistant:** What is a wind-up radio?

**Tutor:** A wind-up radio is a radio that does not need batteries or electricity from a plug. Instead, it has a handle that you turn, or wind. Winding the handle tightens a strong spring or turns a small generator, which makes enough electricity to play the radio for a while. Some also have small solar panels. The idea was developed in the 1990s by a British inventor, Trevor Baylis, who heard that many people in rural Africa could not get health information on the radio because batteries were too expensive and there was no electricity. The first wind-up radios were made in a factory in Cape Town, by a company that employed many workers, including people with disabilities. Wind-up radios became useful in rural areas, in emergencies such as floods and storms, and for campers. They show how an invention can help people to communicate even where there is no electricity, and a radio is still one of the cheapest ways to get news.

### subtopic_3 — How They Changed Lives
**Assistant:** How can a radio commentator describe a soccer match so well?

**Tutor:** A radio commentator has a difficult job: the listeners cannot see the game, so the commentator must paint a picture with words. Good commentators prepare carefully. Before the match, they learn the names, numbers and faces of all the players, the history of the teams and the stadium. During the match, they describe everything quickly and clearly: which team has the ball, where it is on the field, who passes to whom, and what the weather and the crowd are like. They change their voice to show excitement, tension or disappointment, and many become famous for the way they shout when a goal is scored. Often there are two commentators: one describes the action, and the other, often a former player, explains why things happen. Radio commentary in South Africa is broadcast in many languages, and some commentators on stations like Ukhozi FM are loved by millions of fans, who even turn down the television sound to listen to the radio instead.
