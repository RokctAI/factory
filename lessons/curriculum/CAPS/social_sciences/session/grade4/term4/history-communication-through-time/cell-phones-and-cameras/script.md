# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers cell phones and cameras. It explains how cameras and photographs developed, how cell phones were invented and came to South Africa, how a cell phone works, and how both have changed communication. The anchor is Ayanda, a Grade 4 learner who lives in Umlazi, a large township in Durban. On a rainy Saturday, her grandmother, Gogo Khumalo, brings out three old photo albums. The oldest photograph is of Gogo's own grandparents, standing stiffly in their best clothes, not smiling, in brown and white. Gogo explains that it was taken by a photographer in a studio, and that people had to keep very still. There are black-and-white photos of Gogo as a girl, and colour photos of her wedding, all printed on paper from film. Then Ayanda's mother takes out her smartphone and scrolls through thousands of photos and videos, some taken that very morning, and sends a photo of the albums to Ayanda's uncle in Johannesburg, who replies in seconds. The big idea is that cameras and phones began as separate inventions, that each changed communication in its own way, and that today they are joined together in the cell phone. Explaining the history of cameras and cell phones is examinable.

## Subtopic: Cameras and Photographs

A camera is a box that lets light in through a small opening with a lens, to make a picture, called a photograph. The word photograph means drawing with light.

The oldest photograph that still exists was made in France in the 1820s by Joseph Nicéphore Niépce. It shows the view from his window, and it took many hours of light to make. In 1839, Louis Daguerre made photographs that took only minutes. People had to sit very still, sometimes with their heads held in place by a stand, which is why people in very old photos look so serious.

For many years, cameras were large and expensive, and only professional photographers used them. In 1888, George Eastman, in the United States, sold a small Kodak camera loaded with a roll of film, and from 1900, a cheap camera called the Brownie, so that ordinary people, even children, could take photos. The film had to be developed in a darkroom with chemicals, and the pictures were printed on paper. Colour film became common later.

Photographs became very important sources for historians and for news. In South Africa, photographers such as Peter Magubane and Alf Kumalo took photographs that showed the world what life was like under apartheid, and the struggles of people for freedom.

In 1975, an engineer at the Kodak company built the first digital camera, which stored pictures electronically instead of on film. Digital cameras became common in the late 1990s, and soon cameras were built into cell phones.

## Subtopic: Cell Phones

A cell phone, or mobile phone, is a telephone without a wire that you can carry with you.

The first call on a handheld cell phone was made in 1973 in New York by an engineer named Martin Cooper. His phone was about the size of a brick and weighed about one kilogram. Its battery lasted only about half an hour of talking, and it took hours to charge.

Cell phones send and receive messages using radio waves. The country is divided into many small areas, called cells, which is how the cell phone got its name. Each cell has a tall tower or mast with aerials. When you make a call, your phone sends radio waves to the nearest tower, which passes the call on through the network to the person you are calling. As you travel, your call is passed from tower to tower.

Cell phones came to South Africa in 1994, the same year as the first democratic elections, when the first two cell phone networks began. Early phones were large and expensive, and calls cost a lot. Then phones became smaller and cheaper, and prepaid airtime meant people could buy airtime when they had money, without a contract. Today, most South Africans have access to a cell phone.

The first text message, or SMS, was sent in 1992. Later, after about 2007, smartphones with touch screens and the internet became common. A smartphone is like a small computer, with a camera, maps, games and apps.

The questions for this section are with you now: the first cell phones, how they work and how they came to South Africa.

## Subtopic: How They Changed Lives

Cell phones and cameras have changed the way people communicate.

People can contact family and friends almost anywhere, at any time. Families living far apart, like Ayanda's uncle, can share photos, voice notes and video calls every day.

In rural areas, where there were few landline telephones, cell phones allowed many people to make calls for the first time. People use phones to send and receive money, pay bills, check prices, find jobs, learn and get news and weather warnings.

Phones help in emergencies. People can call for help, an ambulance or the police quickly.

Cameras on phones mean that almost anyone can record events as they happen, and share them with the world. Photos and videos are now important sources for history.

But there are also problems. Phones and data cost money, so not everyone can afford them. People can spend too much time on screens. Some people use phones to bully others online, which is called cyberbullying, or to spread false information. Photos shared online can stay there for ever, so you should never share photos of others without asking, or share personal information. And phones can be stolen.
