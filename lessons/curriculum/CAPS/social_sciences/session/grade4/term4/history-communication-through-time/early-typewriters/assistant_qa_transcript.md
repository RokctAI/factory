### subtopic_1 — Writing by Hand
**Assistant:** Why is the keyboard called QWERTY?

**Tutor:** The keyboard is called QWERTY because those are the first six letters on the top row of letter keys, from left to right. Look at any computer keyboard or the keyboard on a phone, and you will see Q, W, E, R, T and Y. This layout comes from the typewriter that Christopher Latham Sholes developed in the 1860s and 1870s. On early typewriters, each key moved a metal typebar, and if two typebars next to each other swung up quickly one after the other, they could hit each other and jam. It is often said that Sholes arranged the letters to separate pairs of letters that are often typed together in English, so that the bars would jam less. Historians still discuss exactly why he chose this order. When typewriters became popular, millions of people learnt to type on QWERTY, so later machines kept the same layout, even computers and phones that cannot jam at all.

### subtopic_2 — How the Typewriter Works
**Assistant:** What is carbon paper?

**Tutor:** Carbon paper is a thin sheet of paper coated on one side with a dark, waxy ink. Before photocopiers and printers, it was the easiest way to make a copy of a typed or handwritten page at the same time as the original. A typist put a sheet of carbon paper, with the inky side facing down, between two sheets of normal paper, and rolled all three into the typewriter. When a typebar struck the top sheet, the pressure pushed the carbon ink onto the second sheet below, making a copy of every letter. With several sheets of carbon paper, typists could make a few copies at once, although the bottom copies were fainter. Offices kept the carbon copies as records of letters they had sent. Even today, when you send an email, you may see the letters cc, which stand for carbon copy. It means sending a copy of the message to another person, which shows how old words live on.

### subtopic_3 — How Typewriters Changed Lives
**Assistant:** Can blind people use a typewriter?

**Tutor:** Yes. Many blind people learnt to type on normal typewriters using touch-typing, which means knowing where each key is by feel, without looking. This let them write letters that sighted people could read. There is also a special machine called a Braille typewriter, or braillewriter. Braille is a system of raised dots that blind people read by touching them with their fingertips, invented by Louis Braille, a young blind Frenchman, in the 1820s. A braillewriter has only six main keys, one for each dot in a braille cell, plus a space key. Pressing different keys together makes the pattern of raised dots for each letter, which is pressed up from underneath the thick paper. One well-known braillewriter, made in the United States from 1951, is very strong and has been used by blind learners around the world, including in South African schools for the blind. Today, computers can also speak text aloud and print braille.
