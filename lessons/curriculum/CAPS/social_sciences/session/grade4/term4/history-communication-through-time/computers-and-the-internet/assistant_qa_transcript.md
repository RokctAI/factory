### subtopic_1 — Computers
**Assistant:** Why was the @ sign chosen for email?

**Tutor:** In 1971, an American engineer named Ray Tomlinson was working on a way to send messages between people using different computers that were connected on the early network. He needed a way to show both the person's name and the computer where their mailbox was. He looked at the keyboard and chose the @ sign, which is read as at, because it was not used in people's names and because it made sense: the message goes to a person at a certain place. So an address like learner@school means the learner at the school. Before email, the @ sign was used mostly in shops and accounts to mean at the price of, for example, 10 apples @ R2 each. Thanks to email, it became one of the most famous symbols in the world. In different languages, people have fun names for it, and in some countries it is called a little monkey or a snail, because of its curly tail.

### subtopic_2 — The Internet
**Assistant:** How does information travel through an undersea cable?

**Tutor:** An undersea internet cable is about as thick as a garden hose in deep water, and it lies on the floor of the ocean for thousands of kilometres, linking one continent to another. Inside it, protected by layers of plastic, steel wire and copper, are a few very thin strands of glass called optical fibres, each about as thin as a hair. Information, such as an email, a video or a web page, is turned into very fast flashes of light, which travel along the glass fibres at enormous speed, bouncing along inside them. Along the cable, there are machines called repeaters that make the light signals stronger. When the cable reaches land, at a landing station, the light signals are passed into networks of cables and towers, and finally to your computer or phone. Special ships lay the cables, and they also repair them when they are damaged by ship anchors, fishing nets or undersea earthquakes.

### subtopic_3 — How They Changed Lives
**Assistant:** How can I tell if information online is true?

**Tutor:** Not everything on the internet is true, because anyone can put information online. Some of it is mistakes, some is old, and some is made up on purpose to trick people. Here are some questions to ask. Who wrote it? Information from a trusted source, such as a government department, a university, a well-known news organisation or an encyclopaedia, is more likely to be true than a message from a stranger. When was it written? Old information may no longer be correct. Can you find the same information on two or three other trusted websites? If not, be careful. Does it seem too amazing, too scary or too angry to be true? Messages that make people feel very upset are often false. Is the photo real, or could it have been changed? And remember, you can always ask a teacher, a librarian or a parent to help you check. Historians do the same thing when they check whether a source is reliable.
