# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the telegraph and the telephone, the first inventions that used electricity to send messages over long distances. It explains how the telegraph and Morse code worked, how the telephone was invented and how calls were connected, and how both changed people's lives in South Africa and around the world. The anchor is Zara, a Grade 4 learner in Cape Town. On her great-grandfather's ninetieth birthday, Oupa Isaacs tells the family about his first job. When he was a boy of fourteen, he worked as a telegram messenger at a post office in Cape Town. He wore a uniform and a cap, and he rode a red bicycle through the streets, delivering telegrams to homes and offices. People were often nervous when they saw him coming, because a telegram usually meant urgent news: a birth, a death, a wedding or an exam result. He also remembers when his family got their first telephone, a heavy black one with a round dial, and how they had to ask the operator to connect calls to other towns. Zara, who video-calls her friends every day, can hardly believe it. The big idea is that the telegraph and the telephone used electricity to send messages along wires in seconds, and that this changed communication for ever. ## Subtopic: The Telegraph

Before the telegraph, the fastest way to send a message over a long distance was by horse, by train or by ship. A letter from Cape Town to London took weeks.

The electric telegraph sends messages as pulses of electricity along a wire. In the 1830s, inventors in Britain and the United States built telegraph systems. In the United States, Samuel Morse and his partner Alfred Vail developed a system and a code. In 1844, Morse sent a famous message along a wire from Washington to Baltimore, about 60 kilometres away, in an instant.

Morse code uses short signals, called dots, and long signals, called dashes, to stand for letters and numbers. For example, the letter E is one dot, the letter T is one dash, and the call for help, SOS, is three dots, three dashes and three dots. A telegraph operator tapped a key to send the dots and dashes, and an operator at the other end listened to the clicks and wrote down the message.

Telegraph wires were hung on tall poles alongside roads and railway lines. In 1866, a cable laid on the floor of the Atlantic Ocean began to carry telegraph messages between Europe and North America.

In South Africa, the first telegraph line opened in 1860, between Cape Town and Simon's Town. In 1879, an undersea cable along the east coast of Africa linked Durban to the rest of the world, so news could reach Europe in hours instead of weeks.

The questions for this section are with you now: the telegraph, Morse code and the first telegraph lines.

## Subtopic: Telegrams

A message sent by telegraph is called a telegram. To send one, you went to a post office and wrote your message on a form. You paid for every word, so people kept their messages very short, leaving out small words. For example, a telegram might say: ARRIVING TUESDAY TRAIN STOP MEET STATION. The word STOP was used instead of a full stop.

The operator sent the message by telegraph to the post office in the town where the receiver lived. There, it was written or printed on a form, put in an envelope and delivered by a messenger, often a young person on a bicycle, like Oupa Isaacs.

Telegrams were used for urgent news, such as births, deaths, illness, weddings, exam results and business orders. Newspapers used the telegraph to get news from other countries quickly. Governments and armies used it to send orders. Railways used it to tell stations when trains were coming, which made railways safer.

Later, machines called teleprinters printed telegraph messages automatically, without Morse code. As telephones, fax machines and email became common, people sent fewer telegrams, and in many countries, the telegram service was eventually stopped.

The questions for this section are with you now: telegrams, how they were sent and what they were used for.

## Subtopic: The Telephone

The telephone sends the sound of a person's voice along a wire. In 1876, Alexander Graham Bell, a teacher of deaf people who lived in the United States, received a patent for the telephone. His first famous words on the telephone were to his assistant in the next room: Mr Watson, come here, I want to see you. Other inventors were working on the same idea at the same time.

At first, calls were connected by telephone operators at a telephone exchange. The caller lifted the handset and told the operator which number they wanted, and the operator connected the two lines by plugging cables into a large board called a switchboard. Later, automatic exchanges connected calls when people turned a dial or pressed buttons.

Telephones came to South African towns in the 1880s. At first, only businesses and rich families had them. Many people used public telephones, sometimes called tickey boxes, because they once used a small coin called a tickey.

The telephone let people talk to each other directly, hear each other's voices and get answers straight away. It saved lives, because people could phone a doctor, an ambulance, the police or the fire brigade.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the telegraph sent voices; it sent dots and dashes. Two: thinking telegrams were long; people paid per word, so they were short. Three: thinking early phones connected calls by themselves; operators connected them. Four: thinking only the telephone used wires; the telegraph used wires first.

The questions for this section are with you now: the telephone, exchanges and how phones changed lives.
