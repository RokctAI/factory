### subtopic_1 — The Telegraph
**Assistant:** How do I spell my name in Morse code?

**Tutor:** Each letter in Morse code has its own pattern of dots and dashes. Here are a few to start with. A is dot dash. B is dash dot dot dot. E is just one dot, and T is just one dash, because E and T are among the most common letters in English, so Morse gave them the shortest codes. I is dot dot. M is dash dash. N is dash dot. O is dash dash dash. S is dot dot dot. So the name SAM would be dot dot dot, then dot dash, then dash dash. To show where one letter ends and the next begins, you leave a short pause between letters, and a longer pause between words. You can find a full Morse code chart in a library book or ask your teacher to help you. Then try tapping your name on a desk with a pencil, using quick taps for dots and longer taps for dashes, or flash it with a torch. Ask a friend to write down what you send.

### subtopic_2 — Telegrams
**Assistant:** Why did people pay for each word in a telegram?

**Tutor:** Sending a telegram took the work of trained operators at both ends, and the use of expensive wires, poles and cables that had to be built and looked after across long distances, even under the sea. The more words a message had, the longer it took to send, and the more of the operators' time and the line's time it used. So telegraph companies charged by the word, and messages to other countries cost even more. To save money, people learnt to write very short telegrams, leaving out words like the, a and I, and using short forms. For example, instead of writing, I will arrive on Tuesday by train, please meet me at the station, a person might write, ARRIVING TUESDAY TRAIN MEET STATION. This style of writing became known as telegraphese. In a way, it is a bit like the short text messages that people send on their phones today, when they want to say a lot in a few words.

### subtopic_3 — The Telephone
**Assistant:** Why do people steal telephone cables?

**Tutor:** Old telephone lines are made of copper wire, a metal that is valuable because it is used in many things, from electrical wires to plumbing pipes, and it can be melted down and sold as scrap metal. In South Africa, copper cable theft has been a serious problem for many years. Thieves dig up underground cables or cut down overhead wires and sell the copper. This leaves whole neighbourhoods, schools, businesses and clinics without telephone or internet connections, sometimes for weeks, and costs a great deal of money to repair. Cable theft also affects railway signals and electricity, which can be dangerous. To fight this problem, companies have replaced many copper lines with fibre optic cables, which carry information as flashes of light through thin strands of glass. Fibre cables are much faster and carry much more information, and because they contain no copper, they are worth very little to thieves. Many homes now also use wireless and cell phone networks instead.
