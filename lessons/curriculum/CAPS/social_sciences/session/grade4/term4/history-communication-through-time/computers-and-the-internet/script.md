# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers computers and the internet, the newest forms of communication in this topic. It explains what a computer is, how computers changed from huge machines to small devices, what the internet is, how it came to South Africa, and how computers and the internet have changed people's lives. The anchor is a primary school in Makhanda, a university town in the Eastern Cape. Today is a big day: the school is opening its new computer lab, with thirty computers donated by a local business. The Grade 4 learners sit in pairs in front of the screens, and their teacher, Ms Ntshona, shows them how to switch on, use the mouse and type their names. Then she tells them a surprising fact about their own town. In 1988, scientists at Rhodes University in Makhanda made the first email link between South Africa and the international internet, and in 1991, the university made South Africa's first full connection to it. A learner called Siphokazi puts up her hand and asks what people did before computers. Ms Ntshona smiles and says that is exactly what they will find out. The big idea is that computers became smaller, cheaper and more powerful over time, and that the internet links them, changing the way people communicate, learn and work. ## Subtopic: Computers

A computer is a machine that stores and works with information, following instructions called programs. It can do calculations, store words, pictures and sounds, and send them to other computers.

The idea of a computer is old. In the 1830s, an English inventor, Charles Babbage, designed a machine that could calculate, and a mathematician, Ada Lovelace, wrote instructions for it. Many people call her the first computer programmer. But the machine was never completed.

The first electronic computers were built in the 1940s. One of them, ENIAC, built in the United States in 1946, filled a large room and weighed about 27 tonnes, as much as about five elephants. It used thousands of glass tubes that got very hot, and it needed a team of people to run it. Early computers were used by governments, armies, universities and big companies, to do huge calculations.

Inventors then found ways to make the parts much smaller, especially tiny electronic parts called microchips. Computers became smaller, faster and cheaper. From the late 1970s and the 1980s, personal computers that fitted on a desk were sold for homes, schools and offices. Then came laptops, which can be carried, then tablets, and then smartphones, which are tiny computers.

Computers replaced typewriters in offices, because words could be typed, corrected and saved on the screen before printing. Today, computers are found in cars, cash registers, hospitals and many machines.

## Subtopic: The Internet

The internet is a huge network that connects billions of computers, phones and other devices all over the world, so that they can share information.

The internet began in 1969, as a small network called ARPANET that linked a few computers at universities in the United States. In 1971, the first email was sent between computers on the network, and the @ sign was chosen for email addresses. Over the next twenty years, more and more networks joined together.

In 1989, a British scientist, Tim Berners-Lee, working in Switzerland, invented the World Wide Web, a way of linking pages of information on the internet so that anyone could find and read them with a program called a browser. From the early 1990s, the web made the internet easy to use, and millions of people went online.

In South Africa, the first email link to the international internet was made from Rhodes University in Makhanda in 1988, and full internet connections followed in 1991. At first, only universities and some businesses were connected.

Information travels on the internet through cables, including fibre optic cables that carry information as light, and through radio waves from cell towers and satellites. Huge cables under the sea link South Africa to other continents. One of them, SEACOM, came ashore in 2009 at Mtunzini, in KwaZulu-Natal, and made the internet in South Africa much faster.

The questions for this section are with you now: the internet, email, the World Wide Web and how the internet came to South Africa.

## Subtopic: How They Changed Lives

Computers and the internet have changed communication more quickly than any invention before them.

People can send emails and messages, and make video calls, to anyone in the world, almost instantly and cheaply. They can find information on almost any topic using search engines. Learners can do research, take lessons online and use learning apps, which was very important when schools closed during the COVID-19 lockdown. People bank, shop, apply for jobs and use government services online. South Africans invented popular apps too, such as an early chat app called Mxit, which millions of young people used on their phones in the 2000s.

But there are also problems. Not everyone has a computer or can afford data, which is called the digital divide. Information on the internet is not always true, so we must check where it comes from. People may try to trick others into giving away passwords or money, which are called scams. Some people bully others online. And too much screen time can harm sleep, eyes and health.

So learners must stay safe online. Never share your password, address or personal photos with strangers. Ask an adult before downloading anything. Be kind, and tell a trusted adult if something online upsets you.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking the first computers were small; they filled whole rooms. Two: thinking the internet and the web are the same; the web is pages on the internet. Three: thinking everything on the internet is true; we must check sources. Four: thinking everyone has internet access; the digital divide leaves many out.
