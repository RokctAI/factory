# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers early typewriters, the machines people used to write before there was electricity in offices and long before computers. It explains how people wrote before typewriters, how the typewriter was invented, how a manual typewriter works, and how it changed people's lives. The anchor is a primary school in Kimberley, in the Northern Cape. While helping their teacher tidy the storeroom, two Grade 4 learners, Keabetswe and Liam, lift a dusty cloth off a heavy black machine with rows of round keys, each with a letter on it. They carry it carefully to the school office, where the school secretary, Mrs van Wyk, smiles. She tells them that when she was young, she learnt to type on a machine just like this one at a typing school, before she got her first job in an office in town. She rolls a sheet of paper into the machine and starts to type, and the whole class gathers around to watch. Clack, clack, clack go the keys, and with a ding, she pushes the lever to start a new line. The big idea is that the typewriter made writing faster, neater and easier to read, and that it changed offices and work, without using any electricity at all. ## Subtopic: Writing by Hand

For thousands of years, everything that was written had to be written by hand.

Long ago, people wrote with quill pens, made from the long feathers of birds such as geese. The writer cut the tip of the feather into a sharp point, dipped it into a pot of ink, called an inkwell, and wrote a few words before dipping it again. Later, pens were made with metal tips, called nibs, but they still had to be dipped in ink. Fountain pens, which hold ink inside them, became popular in the late 1800s. Pencils were also used.

Writing by hand was slow. Ink could smudge, and blots of ink could drop onto the page. Everyone's handwriting was different, and some handwriting was very hard to read.

Governments, banks, lawyers, shops and businesses needed many documents, such as letters, contracts, records and receipts. They employed clerks, whose job was to write and copy documents by hand, neatly, all day long. To make a copy, a clerk had to write the whole document again.

Before the printing press, books were also copied by hand, which is why they were rare and expensive. The printing press made many copies of books, but it was a big machine, not something for writing a single letter.

People wanted a small machine that could write neat, clear letters quickly, in an office or at home.

## Subtopic: How the Typewriter Works

Many inventors tried to make a writing machine. The first typewriter that worked well and was sold widely was invented in the United States by Christopher Latham Sholes and his partners, who patented it in 1868. From 1874, a company called Remington made and sold it.

Sholes's typewriter used the keyboard layout we still use today, called QWERTY, after the first six letters in the top row of letters. It is often said that he arranged the letters this way so that the metal arms inside the machine would not jam when people typed quickly.

A manual typewriter works without electricity. When you press a key, a lever inside swings up a thin metal arm, called a typebar. At the end of the typebar is a raised metal letter. The letter hits an ink ribbon against the paper, and it prints the letter on the page.

The paper is rolled around a rubber roller in a part called the carriage. Each time you type a letter, the carriage moves one space to the left, so the next letter goes next to it. When you are near the end of a line, a little bell rings. Then you push the carriage return lever, which moves the carriage back to the start and rolls the paper up for a new line. The shift key lifts the letters so that you can type capital letters.

Mistakes were hard to fix. Typists rubbed them out with a special eraser or covered them with white correction fluid, and sometimes had to type the whole page again. To make copies, they put carbon paper between two sheets.

## Subtopic: How Typewriters Changed Lives

Typewriters changed offices and work. A good typist could type much faster than a person could write by hand, and the letters were neat and easy for everyone to read. Businesses, governments, newspapers and schools used typewriters for letters, reports, forms, newspapers and exam papers.

Typewriters also brought new jobs. From the late 1800s, many women were trained as typists and secretaries. For many women in many countries, including South Africa, this was one of their first chances to earn their own money in an office. Typing schools taught people to type quickly without looking at the keys, which is called touch-typing.

Writers used typewriters to write books, stories and newspaper articles, and they could send neat pages to printers.

Later, electric typewriters were made, which were faster and needed less effort. From the 1980s, computers with word-processing programs replaced typewriters, because mistakes could be fixed on the screen before printing. Today, typewriters are rare, but the QWERTY keyboard lives on, on computers, laptops and cell phones. This is an example of continuity.
