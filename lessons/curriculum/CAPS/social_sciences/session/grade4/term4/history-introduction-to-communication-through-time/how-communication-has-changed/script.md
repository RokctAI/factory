# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the History topic Communication through time. It explains what communication is, how people communicated long ago, how communication has changed over time, and how many forms of communication have stayed the same. The anchor is a family gathering at the home of Palesa, a Grade 4 learner in Bloemfontein, in the Free State. It is a Sunday afternoon, and the whole family is together. Palesa's grandfather, Ntate Mokoena, sits in the shade and tells the children a story his own grandfather told him, using his hands and changing his voice for each character. Her grandmother brings out an old biscuit tin full of letters, tied with ribbon, that her mother wrote to her from Johannesburg more than fifty years ago, when there was no telephone in the village. Then Palesa's mother takes out her cell phone and makes a video call to Palesa's aunt, who works as a nurse in London. Everyone crowds around the small screen to wave and greet her, and it feels as if she is in the room. The big idea is that the ways people communicate have changed a great deal over time, becoming faster and reaching further, but that many old forms of communication are still used today. Explaining how communication has changed and how some forms have stayed the same is examinable.

## Subtopic: What Is Communication?

Communication means sharing information, ideas, feelings or messages with other people. When you talk to a friend, write a note, wave to a friend across the street or send a text message, you are communicating.

Every act of communication has a sender, who sends the message, a message, which is what is being shared, and a receiver, who gets the message. It also needs a way to carry the message, such as speech, writing, pictures or a phone.

People communicate in many ways. They use spoken words, in their home languages, such as Sesotho, isiZulu, Afrikaans or English. They use written words, in letters, books and messages. They use body language, such as nodding, smiling, frowning, pointing and shaking hands. They use pictures and signs, such as road signs and drawings. And some people use sign language, which uses hand shapes and movements. South African Sign Language became the twelfth official language of South Africa in 2023.

Communication can be personal, between two people or a small group, like a conversation or a letter. It can also be mass communication, which reaches many people at the same time, like a radio broadcast, a television programme, a newspaper or a website.

To find out how people communicated in the past, historians use sources, such as old letters, photographs, objects in museums, and the memories and stories of older people, like Palesa's grandparents.

The questions for this section are with you now: what communication is and the different ways people communicate.

## Subtopic: Communication Long Ago

Before there was writing, people communicated mainly by speaking. They passed on their history, laws, beliefs and knowledge by telling stories, singing songs and reciting praise poems, from older people to younger people. This is called oral tradition. In many South African communities, praise singers, called iimbongi in isiXhosa and izimbongi in isiZulu, recited praise poems about chiefs, kings and important events.

People also communicated with pictures. Thousands of years ago, San artists painted and engraved animals, people and ceremonies on rock walls and in rock shelters across southern Africa. Many of these rock paintings can still be seen today, for example in the Drakensberg and the Cederberg.

To send messages over long distances, people used messengers or runners, who carried spoken messages from place to place. Some communities used drums, horns or fires to send signals across the hills, for example to warn of danger.

Writing was invented about five thousand years ago, in Mesopotamia and in Egypt. With writing, people could keep records and send messages that did not change as they were passed on. For a very long time, everything was written by hand, so books were rare and few people could read.

About 1450, Johannes Gutenberg, in Germany, made a printing press that could print many copies of a page quickly. Books and later newspapers became cheaper, and more people learnt to read.

The questions for this section are with you now: oral tradition, rock art, messengers, writing and printing.

## Subtopic: Change and Continuity

In the last two hundred years, communication has changed faster than ever before. The postal system carried letters across the country and the world. The telegraph and the telephone sent messages along wires. Radio and television reached millions of people at once. Then came computers, cell phones and the internet, which let people send messages, photos and videos around the world in seconds.

These changes brought many good things. Families living far apart, like Palesa's aunt in London, can stay in touch every day. News travels quickly, and people can find information easily. But there are also problems, such as false news spreading quickly, people spending too much time on screens, and people who cannot afford phones or data being left out.

Many forms of communication have also stayed the same, which historians call continuity. People still talk face to face, tell stories, sing songs, use body language and write letters and cards. Praise poets still perform at important events, such as the inauguration of a president. Ntate Mokoena's story was passed down in the same way as stories long ago.
