### subtopic_1 — Why We Need Water
**Assistant:** What is grey water, and is it safe to use?

**Tutor:** Grey water is water that has already been used once, for washing, and is not too dirty. It includes water from baths, showers, hand basins and washing machines. It may have some soap, dirt and skin oils in it. Grey water is not the same as water from toilets, which is called black water, and which must never be reused at home because it carries dangerous germs. Grey water is not safe to drink or to cook with. But it can be used again for some jobs, which saves clean tap water. It can be used to flush the toilet, by pouring it into the bowl or the cistern. It can also be used to water gardens and lawns, but it is better not to pour it on vegetables you will eat raw, and it should be used soon, because if it is stored for days, it starts to smell and germs grow in it. Always wash your hands after handling grey water.

### subtopic_2 — How We Use Water Every Day
**Assistant:** Why did Day Zero never come?

**Tutor:** Day Zero was the day when Cape Town's dams would become so low that most taps would be switched off, and people would have to collect a daily ration of water at collection points. In early 2018, it looked as if this might happen within a few months. But Day Zero never came, for several reasons. Most importantly, the people of Cape Town saved a huge amount of water. Families, schools, businesses and farmers cut their water use by about half. The city also lowered the water pressure in the pipes, so less water leaked out, and it fixed leaks quickly. Farmers in the area agreed to take less water from the dams. The city began to look for new sources of water, such as underground water and cleaning used water. Finally, in the winter of 2018, the rains returned and the dams began to fill again. The drought taught many South Africans to value every drop.

### subtopic_3 — Saving Water at Home
**Assistant:** How much water should a person drink in a day?

**Tutor:** There is no single amount that is right for everyone, because it depends on your age, your size, how active you are and how hot the weather is. A common guide is that children of about nine or ten should drink around six to eight glasses of water a day, and adults a little more. You need more water on hot days, when you play sport, or when you are sick with a fever or a runny tummy, because then your body loses more water. Some of the water we need also comes from food, such as fruit, vegetables, soup and porridge. A good sign is your urine: if it is pale yellow, you are probably drinking enough, and if it is dark yellow, you should drink more. Plain clean water is the best drink, because it has no sugar. Fizzy drinks and many juices have a lot of sugar, which is bad for your teeth and your health.
