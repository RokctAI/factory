# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how people get water by collecting and carrying it directly from rivers, streams and springs. It explains who collects water and how, the problems and dangers of collecting water, and how people can make water from rivers and streams safer to drink. The anchor is Asanda, a Grade 4 learner who lives in a village in the green, hilly countryside of the Eastern Cape. Her village does not yet have taps in the houses. Every morning before school, Asanda and her mother walk down a steep footpath to a stream in the valley, carrying empty plastic containers. They fill them where the water runs clear over the stones, away from the place where the cattle drink. Then they carry the full containers home on their heads, uphill, which takes about twenty minutes. On weekends, Asanda's older brother uses a wheelbarrow to fetch water from a spring on the other side of the hill, which the community has protected with a concrete box and a pipe. The big idea is that many people in South Africa still collect water from nature, that this takes time and hard work, and that this water must be made safe before it is used for drinking. ## Subtopic: Collecting and Carrying Water

Today, most homes in South Africa get water from taps, either inside the house or in the yard, or from a tap in the street. But in some places, especially in rural areas, people still collect water directly from rivers, streams and springs, as people have done for thousands of years.

In most families, women and girls do most of the work of fetching water, although men and boys help too. People carry water in buckets, plastic containers, pots and drums. Many women and girls carry heavy containers balanced on their heads, often with a rolled cloth to cushion the load. Others use wheelbarrows, or donkey carts that carry several drums at once.

A 20-litre container of water weighs about 20 kilograms, which is as heavy as a small child. Some families need several containers every day for drinking, cooking, washing and cleaning.

People in South Africa have found clever ways to make carrying easier. The Hippo Water Roller, invented in South Africa in 1991, is a large, round plastic drum with a handle. It holds about 90 litres of water, and it is rolled along the ground instead of being carried, so one person can move much more water with less effort.

A spring is often the best place to collect water, because the water has been filtered as it trickled through the ground. Communities protect springs by building a concrete box around them, with a pipe, so the water stays clean.

## Subtopic: Problems and Dangers

Collecting water from rivers, streams and springs causes many problems.

The first problem is time. Walking to fetch water, waiting and carrying it home can take hours each day. Children who fetch water may arrive at school late or tired, and they have less time for homework and play. Adults have less time to work and earn money.

The second problem is that carrying heavy loads every day can hurt people's necks, backs and knees, especially children's growing bodies.

The third problem is safety. Paths may be steep, slippery or lonely. Rivers can flood quickly after storms. In some areas, there are crocodiles or snakes near rivers.

The fourth problem is that the water may not be clean. Animals drink from rivers and leave their dung near the water. People may wash clothes or bathe in the river upstream. Rubbish, toilets and farm chemicals can pollute the water. Dirty water can carry germs that cause illnesses such as diarrhoea and cholera, which can be very dangerous, especially for babies and young children. In 2000 and 2001, a serious cholera outbreak in KwaZulu-Natal spread among people who drank untreated river water, and many thousands became ill.

The fifth problem is drought. In dry seasons, streams and even springs can dry up, and people must walk even further to find water.

## Subtopic: Making Water Safer

People who collect water from rivers and streams can make it safer to drink.

First, they should collect water from the cleanest place, upstream from where animals drink and people wash, and from a protected spring if there is one. They should use clean containers with lids.

Second, they can let the water stand for a while so that mud and dirt sink to the bottom. This is called settling. Then they pour off the cleaner water at the top.

Third, they can filter the water by pouring it through a clean cloth, which removes small bits of dirt and some tiny creatures. Filters made of sand and gravel also help.

Fourth, and most important, they must kill the germs. The safest way is to boil the water for at least one minute, and then let it cool in a covered container. Another way is to add a small, measured amount of household bleach, following the instructions given by clinics, and wait about half an hour before drinking. Water should then be stored in clean, covered containers.

The government aims to bring safe water to all communities, through taps, boreholes and pipes, so that nobody has to carry water from rivers. In Asanda's village, pipes for a tap in every street are already being laid.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking clear river water is always safe; germs are too small to see. Two: thinking fetching water is quick; it can take hours. Three: thinking only adults fetch water; many children do too. Four: thinking filtering kills all germs; water must be boiled or treated.
