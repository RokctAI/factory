# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how water reaches taps in towns and cities. It explains how water travels by pipe from dams to purification plants, also called water treatment works, where it is cleaned, and then to reservoirs and taps, and what happens to the water after we use it. The anchor is a visit to a water treatment works next to Midmar Dam, near Howick, in KwaZulu-Natal. A Grade 4 class from Pietermaritzburg arrives by bus and puts on safety helmets. Their guide, Mr Zondi, a water engineer, first takes them to the edge of the dam and fills a glass jar with dam water. It is slightly brown and cloudy, with tiny bits floating in it. Then he leads them along big tanks and filters, and at the end of the tour, he fills another jar from a tap. This water is perfectly clear. The learners hold the two jars side by side. Mr Zondi explains that the clean water will travel through big pipes to reservoirs and then to homes, schools and hospitals in Pietermaritzburg and the towns nearby. The big idea is that water from dams must be cleaned in a treatment works before it is safe to drink, and that a long system of pipes, pumps and reservoirs brings it to our taps. ## Subtopic: From the Dam to the Works

Most towns and cities in South Africa get their water from dams. A dam stores water from rivers, as you learnt earlier in this topic.

Water from a dam is not safe to drink straight away. It may look clean, but it contains mud, tiny bits of plants, and germs from animals and people living upstream. So it must be cleaned first.

Large pipes carry the water from the dam to a water treatment works, also called a purification plant. Purify means to make clean and pure. Some treatment works are right next to the dam, like the one at Midmar. Others are many kilometres away, and the water must be pumped or must flow downhill through long pipes or canals to reach them.

Some cities need more water than their nearby dams can give. Then water is moved from one river system to another through long pipes, tunnels and canals. For example, water from the Tugela River in KwaZulu-Natal and from the mountains of Lesotho is sent to the Vaal Dam, which supplies Gauteng. Rand Water, an organisation started in 1903, cleans water from the Vaal Dam and supplies millions of people in Gauteng and nearby provinces.

At the treatment works, engineers and scientists test the water all day and night to check how dirty it is, so they know how much cleaning it needs.

The questions for this section are with you now: why dam water must be cleaned and how it reaches the treatment works.

## Subtopic: Cleaning the Water

At the treatment works, the water is cleaned in several steps.

Step one is screening. The water flows through screens, which are like big metal sieves. They catch sticks, leaves, fish and rubbish.

Step two is mixing in chemicals. Special chemicals are added that make the tiny bits of dirt stick together into bigger, fluffy clumps, called flocs.

Step three is settling. The water flows slowly through large, still tanks. The heavy clumps of dirt sink to the bottom, where they are removed. The cleaner water at the top flows on.

Step four is filtering. The water trickles down through thick layers of sand and gravel, which catch the tiny bits of dirt that are left. This is just like the simple filter you can make with a bottle, but much bigger.

Step five is disinfection, which means killing the germs. A small amount of a chemical called chlorine is added to the water. Chlorine kills the germs that could cause diseases such as cholera and diarrhoea. A tiny amount stays in the water, so it stays safe as it travels through the pipes. That is why tap water sometimes smells a little like a swimming pool.

Throughout the process, scientists in a laboratory test samples of the water, to make sure it is clean and safe to drink. In South Africa, the Blue Drop programme checks how well towns keep their drinking water safe.

The questions for this section are with you now: screening, settling, filtering and disinfection.

## Subtopic: To the Tap and Beyond

After cleaning, powerful pumps push the clean water through large pipes to reservoirs. A reservoir is a big, closed tank that stores clean water, often built on a hill or raised on a tall tower. From there, gravity makes the water flow downhill through smaller and smaller pipes under the streets to homes, schools, hospitals and businesses. Storing water high up gives it enough pressure to come out of the taps strongly.

At most homes, a water meter measures how much water is used, so that the municipality can charge for it. Many poor households get a basic amount of water free each month.

But a lot of clean water is lost on the way through leaking and broken pipes. Fixing leaks quickly saves a great deal of water and money.

After we use water, it flows down drains and toilets into sewer pipes, as wastewater. The sewer pipes carry it to a wastewater treatment works, where it is cleaned so that it can be returned safely to a river. Then it may flow into another dam, and the journey starts again.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking dam water is safe to drink; it must be cleaned first. Two: thinking filters kill germs; chlorine kills the germs. Three: thinking reservoirs are open dams; they are closed tanks of clean water. Four: thinking used water is thrown away; it is cleaned and returned to rivers.
