# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers boreholes and wells, the ways people get water from under the ground. It explains where groundwater is found, how wells and boreholes are made and how water is brought up, and the benefits and problems of using groundwater. The anchor is a village near Mahikeng, the capital of North West. There is no river nearby, and for many years, the people collected water from an old hand-dug well at the edge of the village, lowering a bucket on a rope, or waited for a water truck. Today, the whole village has come out to watch, because a big truck with a tall drilling tower has arrived to drill a new borehole. A Grade 4 learner, Kabelo, watches with his class. A geohydrologist, a scientist who studies water under the ground, Ms Motsepe, explains how she chose the place to drill. The drill roars for three days. At about ninety metres, muddy water gushes out of the hole, and everyone cheers. A week later, a solar-powered pump is lowered into the borehole, and clean water flows into a big tank on a stand and out of taps along the street. The big idea is that groundwater is an important source of water in South Africa, especially in dry areas, and that people reach it with wells and boreholes. ## Subtopic: Water Under the Ground

You learnt that some rain soaks into the soil and trickles down through cracks and spaces in the rocks. This water under the ground is called groundwater.

The groundwater collects in layers of rock that have many cracks and spaces, called aquifers. An aquifer is not an underground lake. It is more like a sponge, or a jar full of marbles with water filling the spaces between them.

The top of the groundwater is called the water table. Above it, the soil and rock are mostly dry. Below it, the cracks and spaces are full of water. In some places, the water table is close to the surface, only a few metres down. In others, it is very deep, a hundred metres or more. In wet seasons, the water table rises, and in droughts, it can drop.

To find a good place to drill, a geohydrologist like Ms Motsepe studies maps of the rocks, looks at the land and sometimes uses special instruments that send signals into the ground. She looks for places where the rock has many cracks that can hold water.

Groundwater is very important in South Africa. Many towns, villages and farms, especially in dry areas such as North West, Limpopo, the Northern Cape and the Karoo, depend mainly on groundwater, because there are few rivers. During the Cape Town drought of 2018, many homes, schools and the city itself also drilled boreholes.

## Subtopic: Wells and Boreholes

A well is a wide hole, usually dug by hand with spades and picks, down to below the water table. Water seeps into the bottom of the well. Wells are often lined with bricks or stones so that the sides do not fall in, and they have a wall and a lid around the top to keep people, animals and dirt out. People lower a bucket on a rope to bring up the water, sometimes using a handle that winds the rope, called a windlass. Wells are usually only a few metres deep, because it is hard and dangerous to dig deeper.

A borehole is a narrow hole, often only about as wide as a dinner plate or narrower, drilled by a machine. A borehole can go very deep, sometimes more than a hundred metres, through hard rock. It is lined with a pipe, called a casing, so that the sides do not collapse.

Because a borehole is narrow and deep, the water must be pumped up. A hand pump has a handle that people push up and down. A windmill uses the wind to pump water, as on Karoo farms. Electric or diesel pumps are very strong. Solar pumps use electricity from solar panels, which is free once they are set up and works without power cuts.

The water is often pumped into a tank on a stand, so that it can flow down through pipes to taps.

## Subtopic: Benefits and Problems

Groundwater has many benefits. It is often cleaner than river water, because it has been filtered as it trickled through the ground. It is close to where people live, so they do not have to walk to a river. Boreholes can supply water even in dry places with no rivers, and groundwater is not lost to evaporation as water in dams is.

But there are also problems. Drilling a borehole and buying a pump costs a lot of money. Pumps break and need to be fixed, and electric pumps stop during power cuts. If people pump out more water than the rain puts back, the water table drops and boreholes can run dry, especially during a drought.

Groundwater can also be polluted. Pit toilets, rubbish dumps, farm chemicals and mines can let harmful germs and chemicals seep into the ground. So wells and boreholes must be built far from toilets and dumps, and the water must be tested to make sure it is safe. Some groundwater is naturally salty or has too many minerals.

Communities must look after their boreholes, use the water carefully and report broken pumps quickly.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking groundwater is an underground lake; it fills cracks and spaces in rock. Two: thinking a borehole is the same as a well; a well is wide and dug, a borehole narrow and drilled. Three: thinking groundwater never runs out; too much pumping lowers the water table. Four: thinking groundwater is always clean; toilets and dumps can pollute it.
