# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers water trucks, which carry water in large containers to places that have no other source of water. It explains which places need water trucks and why, how water is delivered, and the problems with getting water this way. The anchor is a neighbourhood in Makhanda, a town in the Eastern Cape that is also known for its university and its arts festival. For several years, Makhanda suffered from a long drought, and its dams were almost empty. In some parts of the town, the taps were dry for days or even weeks at a time. A Grade 4 learner, Lutho, lives with his grandmother in one of these streets. Twice a week, on Tuesdays and Fridays, a big water truck arrives. Lutho hears the hooter from inside the house and runs out with two containers, while his grandmother brings a bucket. The driver, Mr Jacobs, connects a thick hose to the truck and fills a big plastic tank on a stand at the corner. People line up to fill their containers from its tap. The big idea is that water trucks bring water to places without other sources of water, and that they help in emergencies, but that they cannot replace pipes and taps in the long term. ## Subtopic: Who Needs Water Trucks?

A water truck, also called a water tanker, is a truck with a large, round tank on the back that can carry thousands of litres of water. It has pumps and hoses to fill and empty the tank.

Water trucks bring water to places that do not have any other source of water nearby. These include some rural villages with no rivers, springs or boreholes close by, and with no pipes yet. They include some informal settlements on the edges of towns and cities, where people have built homes before pipes and taps were laid.

Water trucks are also used in emergencies. During a drought, when dams, rivers and boreholes dry up, trucks bring water from places that still have water. When a main pipe breaks, or a pump station stops because of a power cut, trucks supply water until it is fixed. After floods, when water supplies are damaged or polluted, trucks bring clean water. Trucks also bring water to schools, clinics and hospitals when their supply stops, because they cannot work without water.

Farmers use smaller tankers to take water to animals in far-away camps, and fire engines carry water to put out fires.

In South Africa, local municipalities are responsible for providing water to the people who live in their area. The government has set a rule that every person should have at least 25 litres of safe water a day, from a source no more than 200 metres from their home. Water trucks are one way municipalities try to meet this rule where there are no taps yet.

## Subtopic: How Water Is Delivered

The water in the trucks comes from a safe source, such as a water treatment works, a reservoir or a borehole with clean water. The truck fills up there, drives to the community and delivers the water.

There are two main ways of delivering. In the first way, the truck fills large plastic tanks, often on stands, that are placed in a street or near a group of homes. People then fill their own containers from the tank's tap whenever they need water, until the tank is empty. In the second way, the truck stops in the street, and people bring their containers to fill them straight from the truck's hose.

Many communities have a schedule, which tells them on which days the truck will come, like Lutho's Tuesdays and Fridays. People must be at home or ready when the truck arrives, and they must have enough containers to store water until the next delivery.

The water must be kept clean. The tanks on the trucks must be cleaned often and must be used only for drinking water. Community tanks must have lids, and people should use clean containers and keep them covered at home, as you learnt when you studied storing water.

Lutho's grandmother uses the water carefully. Drinking and cooking come first, then washing, and the water used for washing is used again to flush the toilet.

## Subtopic: Problems With Water Trucks

Water trucks help many people, but they also have problems.

Sometimes trucks arrive late, or do not arrive at all, because of breakdowns, bad roads, flooding or not enough trucks and drivers. Then people may have no water for days.

Water trucks are expensive. The municipality must pay for the trucks, the fuel, the drivers and the water, every week. This money could be used to lay pipes instead.

Each family gets only a limited amount, so people must use water very carefully. Elderly and sick people may find it hard to carry heavy containers home from the tank.

If the tanks on the trucks or at home are not kept clean, the water can carry germs.

Trucks also burn diesel, which adds to air pollution, and their heavy weight can damage dirt roads.

Because of these problems, water trucks are a short-term answer. The long-term answer is to build dams, treatment works, pipes and taps, and to fix leaks, so that every home has a reliable supply. In Makhanda, the town has been working to fix and upgrade its water treatment works and pipes.
