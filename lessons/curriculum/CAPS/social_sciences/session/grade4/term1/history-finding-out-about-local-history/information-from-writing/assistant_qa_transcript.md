### subtopic_1 — Kinds of Written Sources
**Assistant:** Why did people write so many letters long ago?

**Tutor:** Long ago, there were no cell phones, emails or messaging apps, and very few people had a telephone at home. A letter was the main way to send news to someone far away. People wrote to family members who had moved to cities to work, to friends, to schools and to businesses. They wrote about births, weddings and deaths, about the weather and the harvest, about prices and jobs. A letter could take days or weeks to arrive, carried by the post office on trains, ships and later aeroplanes. Many families kept their letters for years, tied in bundles. Today those letters are treasures for historians, because they tell us how ordinary people lived and what they cared about. In Term 4 we will learn more about the postal system. Next time you visit an older relative, ask if they have kept any old letters. You might be surprised at what they tell you.

### subtopic_2 — Reading a Written Source
**Assistant:** What is the difference between a primary and a secondary source?

**Tutor:** A primary source comes from the time you are studying. It was made by someone who was there, or who lived at that time. The school logbook, the letter from 1950, a photograph taken on the opening day, and an interview with someone who was a learner at the school long ago are all primary sources. A secondary source was made later, by someone who was not there, using primary sources. A history book about the school, written today, is a secondary source. So is your own project. Both kinds are useful. A primary source gives us first-hand information, close to the event, but it may only tell one person's view. A secondary source can bring many sources together and explain them, but the writer was not there, and might make mistakes. Historians like to go back to primary sources whenever they can, to check what really happened.

### subtopic_3 — Writing in Our Town
**Assistant:** Why do places in South Africa sometimes change their names?

**Tutor:** Place names carry history. Many South African towns, cities and streets were named during colonial times and apartheid, often after people who led wars against Black South Africans, or after leaders of governments that treated people unfairly. Many of the people of the country, who had their own names for these places, were never asked. Since 1994, communities have been able to suggest new names that honour people and events that are important to all South Africans, or bring back older African names. Examples include Polokwane, which was called Pietersburg, Gqeberha, which was called Port Elizabeth, and Makhanda, which was called Grahamstown. There is a careful process, where people can share their views before a name is changed. Not everyone always agrees, and that is part of living in a democracy. Learning the story behind both the old and the new names helps us understand the history of a place from different sides.
