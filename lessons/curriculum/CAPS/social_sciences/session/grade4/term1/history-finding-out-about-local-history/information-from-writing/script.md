# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how we get information about the history of a local area from writing. It explains the kinds of written sources, such as letters, diaries, newspapers, registers, books, certificates, signs and plaques, and how to read a written source carefully and ask questions about it. The anchor is a primary school in Makhanda, in the Eastern Cape, which is about to celebrate its hundredth birthday. To prepare an exhibition, the principal opens an old wooden cupboard in the office. Inside, the Grade 4 learners find a logbook written by hand by every principal since the school opened, a register of the very first learners, a box of yellowed newspaper cuttings and a bundle of letters. Outside, above the main door, they find a stone with words and a date carved into it. The big idea is that writing from the past is a source that can tell us exactly what happened, when and to whom, but that we must always ask who wrote it, when and why. Getting information from written sources is examinable.

## Subtopic: Kinds of Written Sources

A written source is any writing that gives us information about the past. Here are the main kinds.

Letters are written by one person to another. In the old days, letters were the main way people far apart kept in touch. They tell us about family news, prices, feelings and events. The bundle in the cupboard includes a letter from a parent in 1950, asking the school to build a second classroom because there were too many children.

Diaries are written by people about their own daily lives. A diary tells us what one person did, saw and felt each day.

Newspapers report events that happened in a town or a country. Makhanda has one of the oldest newspapers in South Africa, called Grocott's Mail, which started in 1870. Old newspaper cuttings in the cupboard report on school sports days and on the opening of the new school hall.

Records are written by organisations to keep track of things. The school logbook records important events: the day the school opened, a flood that closed the school, the visit of an inspector. The register lists the names and ages of learners. Birth certificates, marriage certificates, maps, and lists of people in a town are records too.

Books about the history of a town are also written sources, but they were usually written much later by people who studied other sources.

Signs and plaques are writing on buildings, statues and street corners. The foundation stone above the door says when the building was opened and by whom. Street names are also writing that tells us about the past, because many streets are named after people or events.

The questions for this section are with you now: letters, diaries, newspapers, records, books, signs and plaques.

## Subtopic: Reading a Written Source

To get information from a written source, read it slowly, at least twice. The first time, read to understand what it is about. The second time, look for facts: names, dates, places and events. Underline or write down the important facts.

Old writing can be hard to read. It may be handwritten in an old style, and it may use old words or spelling. Take your time, and ask an adult for help with difficult words.

Then ask three important questions. Who wrote it? A principal, a parent, a newspaper reporter? When was it written? At the time of the event, or many years later? Why was it written? To record facts, to complain, to persuade people, or to sell newspapers?

These questions matter, because a writer can only write what they know, and they may only tell one side. The logbook entry for a sports day was written by the principal, who was proud of the school, so it may only mention the good things. A source written at the time, by someone who was there, is called a primary source. A book written much later, using other sources, is called a secondary source.

Written sources are very useful because they often give exact dates and names, which pictures and objects cannot do. The register tells the learners that the school opened with forty-two learners in two classrooms.

The questions for this section are with you now: how to read a written source, and the questions to ask about it.

## Subtopic: Writing in Our Town

Writing about the past is all around us, not only in cupboards and libraries.

The learners walk around Makhanda looking for writing that tells history. On old buildings they find dates carved above doors. On statues and memorials they find plaques that tell who is remembered and why. On a church they find a stone with the year it was built.

Even the name of the town tells a story. The town was named Grahamstown in the early 1800s, after a British army officer called John Graham, who led soldiers in wars against the amaXhosa people who lived in the area. In 2018 the name was changed to Makhanda, to honour Makhanda, also called Nxele, a Xhosa prophet and warrior who led an attack on the town in 1819 to win back land. The two names remind us that the history of the town is told differently by different groups of people, and that names of places can change.

Street names, school names and the names of buildings in your area can all be investigated. Who were they named after, and why? Has the name changed? Old maps also show what an area looked like and what places were called long ago.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: believing everything that is written is true; writers can make mistakes or tell only one side. Two: reading a source only once; read it at least twice. Three: thinking a book written much later is the same as a source from the time; one is secondary, one is primary. Four: thinking written sources are only found in libraries; they are on buildings, signs and street names too.

The questions for this section are with you now: written history on buildings, plaques and names.
