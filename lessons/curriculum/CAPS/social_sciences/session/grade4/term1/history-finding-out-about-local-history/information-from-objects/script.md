# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how we get information about the history of a local area from objects. It explains what kinds of objects are sources, how to question an object to find out about the past, and how museums and archaeologists find and look after objects. The anchor is a classroom museum in Mbombela, the capital city of Mpumalanga. Each Grade 4 learner has brought one old object from home, with a card explaining what it is. On the tables there is a heavy grinding stone with a smooth dip in the middle, a flat iron with no plug, a paraffin lamp with a glass chimney, a three-legged cast iron pot, a clay pot, a beaded necklace, a wire car, and an old coin with a picture of a springbok. The class also learns about the golden rhinoceros of Mapungubwe, one of the most famous objects ever found in South Africa. The big idea is that objects made and used by people in the past are sources that tell us how people lived, worked and played. Getting information from objects is examinable.

## Subtopic: Objects from the Past

An object is a thing that people made or used. Objects from the past are sources, because they give us information about how people lived. Historians sometimes call them artefacts.

There are many kinds of objects. Tools and things used in the home, such as grinding stones, pots, irons, lamps and spoons. Clothes, shoes and jewellery, such as beadwork. Toys and games. Coins and banknotes. Weapons and shields. Machines, such as old sewing machines, radios and typewriters. Even buildings, bridges and old houses are objects from the past.

Look at the objects in the classroom museum. The grinding stone shows that, long ago, women ground maize or sorghum by hand, rubbing a smaller stone back and forth on a big flat stone. The dip in the middle was made by years and years of grinding. The flat iron shows that people heated irons on a stove before there was electricity. The paraffin lamp shows that many homes had no electric lights. The three-legged pot was used for cooking over an open fire.

Objects can also tell us about money and change. The old coin is from before 1961. Before that, South Africa used pounds, shillings and pennies. In 1961, South Africa changed to the rand and cents, which we still use today.

Objects can tell us about people's skills and culture. Beadwork is made with great skill, and the colours and patterns can carry meanings, such as which group a person belongs to, or messages of love. A wire car shows how children used their imagination and skill to make their own toys.

The questions for this section are with you now: what objects are, and what objects from the past tell us.

## Subtopic: Questioning an Object

To get information from an object, we question it, like a detective. Here are some useful questions.

What is it made of? Stone, clay, wood, iron, glass, plastic, cloth or beads? This tells us what materials people had. Plastic objects are usually more recent.

How was it made? By hand, or by a machine in a factory? Handmade objects were made by skilled people in the community.

What was it used for? Look at its shape. A handle is for holding. A dip is for grinding or holding something. A spout is for pouring.

Who used it? Men, women or children? Rich or poor families? For work, cooking or play?

How old is it? Is it worn, rusty or faded? Signs of wear show it was used for a long time. Dates stamped on coins help a lot.

What do we use instead today? The flat iron has been replaced by an electric iron. The paraffin lamp has been replaced, in many homes, by electric lights and solar lamps. Comparing helps us see change.

What can it not tell us? An object cannot tell us the name of the person who used it or how they felt. For that, we need other sources, such as the stories that come with the object. That is why each learner writes a card telling who owned the object and where it came from.

The questions for this section are with you now: the questions to ask about an object.

## Subtopic: Museums and Archaeology

A museum is a building where objects from the past are collected, looked after and shown to the public. Museums label each object with information. Almost every big town in South Africa has a museum. Near Mbombela, the old gold mining village of Pilgrim's Rest is a museum village, where old buildings, a printing press and gold mining tools show how people lived during the gold rush that began there in 1873.

Some objects are buried under the ground for hundreds or thousands of years. Archaeologists are scientists who carefully dig up and study these objects to learn about the past. They dig slowly, with small tools and brushes, and they record exactly where each object was found.

The most famous archaeological find in South Africa is at Mapungubwe, in Limpopo, near where South Africa meets Botswana and Zimbabwe. About eight hundred years ago, Mapungubwe was the capital of a rich African kingdom. Its people farmed, kept cattle, worked gold, and traded ivory and gold with merchants from far away, receiving glass beads and cloth in return. In 1933, archaeologists found a small rhinoceros made of thin sheets of gold on a wooden shape, buried in a royal grave on top of Mapungubwe Hill. The golden rhino shows that the people of Mapungubwe were skilled metalworkers and that their kingdom was rich and powerful. Today, Mapungubwe is a World Heritage Site.

Objects must be handled with care. Never dig up an old site yourself or take objects from it. Report finds to a museum.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking only gold and treasure are important objects; everyday things like grinding stones tell us a lot. Two: thinking objects can tell us everything; they cannot tell us names or feelings without other sources. Three: thinking South Africa always used the rand; before 1961 it used pounds, shillings and pence. Four: thinking archaeologists dig quickly with big machines; they dig slowly and carefully.

The questions for this section are with you now: museums, archaeology and Mapungubwe.
