# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how we get information about the history of a local area from stories and interviews with people. It explains oral history, the difference between a story and an interview, and how to plan, do and use an interview. The anchor is a Saturday afternoon in a village near Thohoyandou, in Limpopo. Mulalo, a Grade 4 learner, is doing a project on how her village has changed. Her grandfather, Mr Ndou, was born in the village more than seventy years ago. Mulalo has written five questions in her notebook, and her mother has lent her a cell phone to record the answers. They sit together under the big mango tree in the yard. The big idea is that people's memories are an important source of history, especially for local history that was never written down, and that a good interview needs careful planning, polite asking, careful listening and accurate recording. Getting information from stories and interviews is examinable.

## Subtopic: Stories That Carry the Past

Oral history is history that is spoken and passed on by people, rather than written down. Oral means spoken.

For thousands of years, long before most people could read and write, people in Africa and around the world kept their history alive by telling it. Elders told children the history of their family, their clan and their community around the fire in the evening. They told who their ancestors were, where the family came from, the great events and the hard times. Children listened, remembered, and later told their own children.

Some oral history is passed on in special ways. Praise poems tell the history and deeds of chiefs and kings. Clan names, called izithakazelo in isiZulu, remind people of their ancestors. Songs remember important events, such as songs sung during the struggle against apartheid. Even the names of places, rivers and mountains often carry stories.

We must be careful to tell the difference between a folktale and a history story. A folktale, such as a story about a clever hare, teaches a lesson, but it did not really happen. A history story is about real people and real events.

Oral sources are especially important for local history, because many events in small villages and townships were never written about in newspapers or books. The only record is in people's memories. When an elder dies, their memories are lost, unless someone has listened and recorded them. That is why it is so important to ask now.

The questions for this section are with you now: oral history, the ways history is passed on by speaking, and folktales and history stories.

## Subtopic: Planning an Interview

An interview is a meeting where one person asks questions and another person answers them. The person who asks is the interviewer, and the person who answers is the interviewee.

A good interview needs a good plan. First, choose a person who knows about the topic. Mulalo chose her grandfather because he has lived in the village all his life. Older relatives, neighbours, a retired teacher, a religious leader or a shopkeeper who has been in the area for many years are all good choices.

Second, ask permission. Ask the person politely if they are willing to be interviewed, and agree on a time and place. If you want to record their voice or take a photograph, you must ask for permission for that too.

Third, write your questions before the interview. Good questions are open questions, which need a longer answer, not just yes or no. For example: what was the village like when you were a child? How did you get water? How did you travel to town? What has changed the most? A closed question, such as did you have a car, can be answered with just one word, so it gives less information.

Fourth, keep your questions short and clear, and put them in a sensible order. Start with easy questions, such as where and when the person was born.

Fifth, get ready. Take your notebook, a pen, and a phone or recorder if you have permission to use one.

The questions for this section are with you now: interviewer and interviewee, permission, and open and closed questions.

## Subtopic: Doing and Using an Interview

During the interview, be polite and patient. Greet the person respectfully, and explain what your project is about. Ask one question at a time. Then listen carefully, and do not interrupt. Elders sometimes take a long road to answer a question, and the story along the way is often the most interesting part. If something is not clear, ask a follow-up question, such as: can you tell me more about that?

Mulalo's grandfather tells her that when he was a boy, he herded cattle and goats in the hills every day after school. There was no tap. The women and girls fetched water from the river in clay pots carried on their heads. There was no tarred road. To get to Thohoyandou, people walked for most of the day. When the first bus came to the village, everyone ran to the road to see it, and the children chased it until it disappeared in the dust.

Write down the important facts, such as names, dates and places, during the interview or straight after it. At the end, thank the person for their time.

After the interview, read your notes and listen to the recording. Write the information neatly. Then check it against other sources. Memories can fade, and people sometimes mix up dates. Mulalo's grandfather thinks the first bus came in about 1968. She asks two neighbours, who both remember it too. When several people remember the same thing, we can be more confident.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a folktale is history; a folktale teaches a lesson but did not really happen. Two: asking only yes or no questions; open questions give more information. Three: recording someone without asking; always ask permission. Four: thinking every memory is exactly right; check it with other people and sources.

The questions for this section are with you now: doing an interview, recording it and checking the information.
