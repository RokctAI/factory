### subtopic_1 — Pictures from the Past
**Assistant:** How did people dig the Big Hole without big machines?

**Tutor:** They dug it mostly by hand. When diamonds were found in 1871, the hill was divided into thousands of small blocks called claims, each one only a few metres wide. Each claim holder and their workers dug their own square with picks and shovels. As the squares got deeper, it became hard to climb in and out, and the walls of soil between claims kept falling in. So people built wooden platforms around the edge, and stretched ropes and wires from the platforms to the bottom of each claim. Buckets were pulled up the ropes with a winch, a wheel turned by hand or by a horse, and later by steam engines. That is why the old photographs look like a giant spider's web. The work was hard and dangerous, and many workers were hurt. Over time, the small claims were bought up by a few big companies, and the digging moved underground. Mining in the Big Hole stopped in 1914.

### subtopic_2 — Reading a Picture
**Assistant:** How do we know how old rock art is?

**Tutor:** It is not easy, and scientists use several clues. Sometimes the paint itself contains things that can be tested in a laboratory, such as tiny bits of charcoal, which can be dated using a special method. Sometimes paintings are found in caves where people lived, and the layers of soil and tools on the floor can be dated. For rock engravings, scientists study how much the rock has weathered, or changed colour, since the picture was carved. The pictures themselves are clues too. If an engraving shows an animal that disappeared from the area long ago, the engraving is probably older than that. Some rock art in South Africa is only a few hundred years old, while some is many thousands of years old. San descendants and their stories also help us understand the meaning of the pictures, such as the importance of the eland. Rock art sites are protected by law, so never touch, scratch or wet the pictures.

### subtopic_3 — Comparing Then and Now
**Assistant:** Can a photograph lie?

**Tutor:** A photograph usually shows what was really in front of the camera, so it is a very useful source. But it can still mislead us. A photographer chooses where to point the camera and what to leave out. A photograph of a beautiful street may leave out the poor area just around the corner. A photographer might ask people to stand in a certain way, to make the scene look neat or exciting. A photograph shows only one moment, which may not be how things usually were. Photographs can also be cut or changed. Long ago, people changed them by painting on them, and today it is very easy to change pictures on a computer or phone. So historians ask: who took this photograph, and why? What might be outside the frame? Do other sources agree with it? A photograph is a strong clue, but like every source, it should be checked against others.
