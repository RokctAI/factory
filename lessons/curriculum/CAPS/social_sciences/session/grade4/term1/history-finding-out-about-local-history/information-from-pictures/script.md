# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how we get information about the history of a local area from pictures. It explains the kinds of pictures that are sources, such as photographs, paintings, drawings and rock art, how to study a picture carefully, and how to compare pictures of the same place then and now. The anchor is a class trip by Grade 4 learners from a school in Kimberley, in the Northern Cape, to the Big Hole museum. On the walls of the museum hang old black-and-white photographs from the 1870s. They show a huge pit divided into hundreds of small squares, with ropes and wires stretched across it like a spider's web, and thousands of people digging. The learners then walk out onto a platform and look down into the Big Hole as it is today: a deep, quiet hole with green water at the bottom. The big idea is that pictures are sources that show us what places and people looked like in the past, and that we must study them carefully and ask questions about them. Getting information from pictures is examinable.

## Subtopic: Pictures from the Past

A picture is a source when it shows us something about the past. There are several kinds of pictures.

Photographs are pictures taken with a camera. The first cameras were invented about two hundred years ago. Early photographs were black and white, because colour photographs only became common much later. Old photographs show us exactly how places, buildings and people looked at the moment the photograph was taken.

The museum in Kimberley has photographs from the early days of the diamond diggings. Diamonds were found on a small hill there in 1871. Thousands of people rushed to the area, from all over South Africa, from other parts of southern Africa, and from Europe and other countries, hoping to find diamonds. The hill was dug away, and the digging went deeper and deeper, until it became the Big Hole. The old photographs show the miners, the buckets and the ropes they used to haul up the ground.

Paintings and drawings are pictures made by hand. Before cameras, they were the only way to record what a place looked like. Artists painted landscapes, towns and people. A painting may not be as exact as a photograph, because the artist chooses what to show.

Rock art is the oldest kind of picture in South Africa. The San people painted pictures on rock walls and carved pictures, called engravings, into rocks, thousands of years ago. Near Kimberley, at a place called Wildebeest Kuil, there are rock engravings of animals such as eland and rhinoceros. Rock art tells us about the animals that lived in the area and about the beliefs of the San.

The questions for this section are with you now: photographs, paintings and drawings, and rock art as sources.

## Subtopic: Reading a Picture

To get information from a picture, we read it like a detective. Here are some questions to ask.

What do I see? Look slowly, from one side to the other and from top to bottom. Name the people, the buildings, the transport, the clothes and the tools. In the old photograph of the diggings, the learners see men with spades and buckets, carts pulled by animals, wooden platforms, tents and huts at the edge of the pit, and ropes running from the bottom of the hole to the top.

Where was it taken? The caption, which is the writing under a picture, often tells us the place. Landmarks in the picture can also help.

When was it taken? The caption may give a date. If not, clues in the picture help. Black-and-white photographs are usually old. The clothes, the transport and the buildings also give clues. Animal carts and no cars tell us the photograph is very old.

Who made it, and why? A photographer may have wanted to show how busy the diggings were, or to sell the picture to newspapers.

What does the picture not show? The photograph shows the busy pit, but it does not tell us how the workers felt, how much they were paid, or where they slept at night. For that we need other sources. In fact, many of the workers were Black miners who came from far away, did the hardest and most dangerous work, and were paid much less than others. A single picture does not tell us that.

The questions for this section are with you now: the questions to ask about a picture, captions and clues.

## Subtopic: Comparing Then and Now

One of the most useful things we can do with old pictures is to compare them with the same place today. This shows us change and continuity. Change means what has become different. Continuity means what has stayed the same.

The learners compare an old photograph of the diggings with the view from the platform today. Changes: the busy pit full of people is now quiet; the ropes and wooden platforms are gone; the hole is much deeper and full of water; the tents and huts at the edge have been replaced by a museum, shops and streets. Continuity: the hole is in the same place, and Kimberley is still a town known for diamonds.

They also compare an old photograph of the main street of Kimberley with a photograph they take themselves. In the old picture, there are carts pulled by oxen and a tram. Today there are cars and taxis. Some old buildings are still standing.

You can do the same in your own area. Ask an older person for an old photograph of your street, school or town, then take a picture or draw the same place today. Then make a list of what has changed and what has stayed the same.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking a black-and-white photograph shows a black-and-white world; colour photographs were not common yet. Two: thinking a picture tells us everything; it does not show how people felt or what happened outside the picture. Three: ignoring the caption; it often gives the place and the date. Four: thinking only photographs are pictures from the past; paintings, drawings and rock art are too.

The questions for this section are with you now: change, continuity, and comparing pictures of then and now.
