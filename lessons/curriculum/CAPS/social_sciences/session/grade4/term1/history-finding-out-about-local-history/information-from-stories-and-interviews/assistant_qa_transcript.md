### subtopic_1 — Stories That Carry the Past
**Assistant:** Why do people forget or mix up details from long ago?

**Tutor:** Our memories are not like a video recording that keeps every detail perfectly. Over time, some details fade, especially exact dates and numbers. A person might remember that something happened when they were about ten years old, but not the exact year. Memories can also get mixed together, so two events that happened years apart might seem to have happened at the same time. Sometimes people have heard a story told so many times that they remember the story more than the event itself. And people often remember the things that made them feel strong feelings, like joy or fear, much better than ordinary days. This does not mean that oral history is not useful. It is very useful, especially for how people lived and felt. It just means that we should check details like dates against other sources, such as written records, photographs, or the memories of other people who were there.

### subtopic_2 — Planning an Interview
**Assistant:** What is a praise poet?

**Tutor:** A praise poet is a person who composes and performs poems that praise a leader, a family or a community, and tell their history. In isiXhosa and isiZulu the praise poet is called an imbongi, and the praise poems are called izibongo. Many other African languages have their own words and traditions for this. In Tshivenda, for example, there are praise poems for chiefs and families too. A praise poet often performs at important gatherings, such as the crowning of a king, weddings or national events, and may wear traditional clothes made from animal skins. The poems tell of the leader's ancestors, brave deeds, and sometimes even their mistakes, in rich and powerful language. Long before writing was common, praise poems were one way of keeping history alive. In 1994, a well-known imbongi, Zolani Mkiva, performed praise poetry at the inauguration of President Nelson Mandela, showing that this old tradition is still alive today.

### subtopic_3 — Doing and Using an Interview
**Assistant:** What if the person I interview does not want to answer a question?

**Tutor:** That is completely fine, and you must respect it. Some memories are painful. An elder may have lived through hard times, such as being forced to move from their home, losing a family member, or being treated unfairly because of their race during apartheid. They may not want to talk about those things, or they may get upset. If a person does not want to answer, simply say: that is fine, thank you, and move on to your next question. Never push or argue. Watch the person's face and voice. If they seem sad or tired, you can offer to stop and come back another day. A good interviewer is kind and patient. Remember that the person is doing you a favour by sharing their life with you. Treating them with respect is more important than getting every answer. Often, if you are kind, people share more over time.
