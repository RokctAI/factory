### subtopic_1 — Objects from the Past
**Assistant:** Why does a grinding stone have a dip in the middle?

**Tutor:** The dip was not made on purpose at the start. A grinding stone begins as a fairly flat, hard stone. A woman would kneel at one end, put a handful of maize, sorghum or other grain on it, and rub a smaller, rounded stone back and forth over the grain, pressing down hard. This crushed the grain into meal or flour. Every time she ground, tiny bits of the big stone were worn away too. After months and years of grinding, day after day, a smooth hollow formed in the middle. Some grinding stones were used by several generations of women, by a grandmother, then a mother, then a daughter, so the dip became very deep. So the dip is like a clock. A deep dip tells us the stone was used for a very long time. It also tells us how much hard work went into making food every day before there were mills and shops selling maize meal in bags.

### subtopic_2 — Questioning an Object
**Assistant:** How do archaeologists know how old an object is?

**Tutor:** They use several clues together. First, layers in the ground. Over time, soil, ash and rubbish build up in layers, so objects deeper down are usually older than those near the surface. Second, they compare the object with other objects whose age is already known. If a pot is decorated in the same style as pots from a known time, it probably comes from that time. Third, scientists can test some materials in a laboratory. Things that were once alive, such as wood, bone, charcoal and seeds, can be dated with a special method called radiocarbon dating, which measures a kind of carbon that slowly changes over time. At Mapungubwe, charcoal and other material from the layers helped show that the kingdom was at its height about eight hundred years ago. Fourth, objects with writing or dates, such as coins, are easier to date. Putting all these clues together gives archaeologists a good idea of the age.

### subtopic_3 — Museums and Archaeology
**Assistant:** Why was South Africa's money changed from pounds to rand?

**Tutor:** For a long time, South Africa used the same kind of money as Britain: pounds, shillings and pence. This system was complicated. There were twelve pennies in a shilling and twenty shillings in a pound, so working out prices and change was hard. Many countries were changing to decimal money, where everything is counted in tens and hundreds, which is much easier. In 1961, South Africa changed to the rand, with one hundred cents in a rand. The name rand comes from the Witwatersrand, the ridge of hills around Johannesburg where so much gold was found. That same year, South Africa also became a republic and left the British Commonwealth. So an old coin with pennies or shillings on it tells us it is from before 1961. Coins are very useful objects for historians, because they usually have a date stamped on them and show the pictures and symbols that were important to the country at that time.
