# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces Grade 4 Social Sciences and the first Geography topic of the year. It explains what Geography and History study, what is new about the Intermediate Phase, how learners will be assessed, and what we mean by places where people live. The anchor is the first Social Sciences lesson of the year in a Grade 4 classroom in Mthatha, in the Eastern Cape. On each desk lies a new textbook and an empty exercise book. On the wall hangs a big map of South Africa, and through the window the learners can see the busy main road, rows of houses and the green hills beyond the town. The teacher writes two words on the board: Geography and History. The big idea is that Social Sciences helps us understand the places we live in and the people who lived before us, and that every person lives in some kind of settlement. Knowing what Geography studies and what a settlement is, is examinable.

## Subtopic: Geography and History in Social Sciences

Social Sciences is one subject, but it is made of two parts: Geography and History. In Grade 4 you will learn both of them every term.

Geography is the study of places on Earth, the people who live in them, and the land, water, weather, plants and animals around them. A geographer asks questions such as: Where is this place? What is it like? Why do people live here? How do people use the land and the water? How do people change the place, and how does the place change people?

History is the study of the past. A historian asks: What happened long ago? How did people live? How do we know? History helps us understand why our world is the way it is today.

In Grade 4 the Geography topics are places where people live, map skills, food and farming in South Africa, and water in South Africa. The History topics are local history, learning from leaders, transport through time, and communication through time.

A geographer uses special tools. The most important one is a map. A map is a drawing of a place as if you were looking down on it from above. Geographers also use photographs, pictures, graphs and the information they collect by looking carefully and asking questions.

Think about the classroom in Mthatha. Through the window there is a road, houses, shops and hills. A geographer would notice all of these things and ask how they fit together. Why is the road there? Where do the people who live in the houses go to work? Where does their water come from?

A common mistake is to think that Geography is only about learning the names of places. Names matter, but Geography is really about understanding places and the people in them.

The questions for this section are with you now: what Geography and History study, and the tools a geographer uses.

## Subtopic: From Foundation Phase to Intermediate Phase

Grades R, 1, 2 and 3 are called the Foundation Phase. Grades 4, 5 and 6 are called the Intermediate Phase. Moving into Grade 4 is a big step, and it is normal to feel a little nervous.

In the Foundation Phase, you learnt about the world in a subject called Life Skills, in a part called Beginning Knowledge. You learnt about your home, your school, the seasons and how people lived long ago. Now those ideas grow into their own subject, Social Sciences.

Here are some things that change in the Intermediate Phase. You have more subjects, and each one has its own books. You read more, and the reading is longer. You write more in your own words, in full sentences and short paragraphs. You keep your own exercise book neat, with headings and dates. And you learn new words, such as settlement, landmark and symbol. A good habit is to keep a list of new words at the back of your book, with a short meaning for each one.

You will also be assessed in two ways. Informal assessment happens almost every day: classwork, homework, answering questions in class and short activities. It helps you and your teacher see what you understand, and it does not count towards your report. Formal assessment counts towards your report. In Social Sciences, each term has one formal task for Geography and one for History. In Term 1, the Geography task is a test, and the History task is a project.

Some Geography test questions will be source-based. A source is something you look at to find information, such as a picture, a map, a short piece of writing or a table. The question asks you to study the source and answer from it. There will also be a short paragraph to write.

The questions for this section are with you now: the phases of school, informal and formal assessment, and what a source is.

## Subtopic: Places Where People Live

Our first Geography topic is places where people live. People do not live alone. They live together in groups, near other people, so that they can help each other, trade, go to school and find work.

A place where people live together is called a settlement. Some settlements are tiny, with only a few houses. Others are huge, with millions of people.

There are four kinds of settlement we will study. A farm is a piece of land where people grow crops or keep animals; only a few families live there. A village is a small settlement, often in the countryside, with homes, a school, a small shop and perhaps a clinic. A town is bigger than a village, with more people, shops, offices, banks and roads. A city is the biggest kind of settlement, with many thousands or even millions of people, tall buildings, busy roads, factories and many jobs. Johannesburg, Cape Town and Durban are cities. Mthatha is a town.

Settlements grow in places that give people what they need. Most settlements are near water, such as a river or a spring. They often grow where the soil is good for farming, or where there is work, such as a mine or a harbour. Mthatha grew up beside the Mthatha River.

In this topic we will learn about the jobs people do and the buildings in different settlements, roads and footpaths, landmarks, how to explain the way from one place to another, and the things all people need.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking Geography is only about the names of places; it is about understanding places and people. Two: thinking informal classwork counts towards your report; only formal tasks do. Three: thinking a village and a city are the same size; a village is small and a city is the biggest settlement. Four: thinking a farm is not a settlement; people live there too, so it is the smallest kind of settlement.

The questions for this section are with you now: settlements, and the four kinds of settlement.
