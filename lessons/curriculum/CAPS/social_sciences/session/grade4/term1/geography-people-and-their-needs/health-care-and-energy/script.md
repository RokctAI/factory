# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers three more needs that all people share: health, care and energy. It explains why each one is a need, how people in different settlements meet these needs, and how to stay safe when using energy at home. The anchor is one day in the life of Thabo, a Grade 4 learner who lives in Motherwell, a large township in Gqeberha, in the Eastern Cape. In the morning, his mother takes his baby sister to the clinic down the road for her injections. His grandmother, whom he calls Gogo, walks him to school and is waiting at the gate when school ends. In the evening, the family cooks supper on a gas stove, and the light comes on when Thabo flicks the switch, using electricity bought with a prepaid meter. The big idea is that all people need to be healthy, to be cared for and to have energy, and that people in different places meet these needs in different ways. Explaining the needs for health, care and energy is examinable.

## Subtopic: Health

Being healthy means that our bodies and minds are well and working properly. Health is a need, because when we are ill, we cannot learn, work or play well, and some illnesses can be dangerous.

Many things help to keep people healthy. Clean water and safe toilets stop germs from spreading. Healthy food gives the body what it needs to grow and fight illness. Exercise and play make our muscles, heart and lungs strong. Enough sleep lets the body rest and grow. Washing our hands with soap, especially before eating and after using the toilet, removes germs.

When people are ill or hurt, they need health care. In Motherwell, Thabo's family goes to the public clinic. Nurses check that babies are growing well and give them injections, called vaccinations, which protect children from serious illnesses such as measles and polio. The clinic also gives medicine and advice. For more serious illnesses, people go to a hospital, where there are doctors and beds. Ambulances carry people to hospital in emergencies.

In cities and towns, clinics and hospitals are usually close by. In many rural areas, the nearest clinic can be far away, and people may have to walk a long way or take a taxi. Some areas are visited by mobile clinics, which are vehicles with nurses and medicine that travel to villages on certain days.

The questions for this section are with you now: what keeps people healthy, and how people get health care.

## Subtopic: Care

Care means being looked after by people who love us and keep us safe. Every child needs care. Babies and young children cannot feed, wash or protect themselves, so they need adults to do it. Older people and people who are ill also need care.

Care comes in many forms. Families care for each other by giving love, food, clothing and a safe home. Thabo's grandmother looks after the children while their mother is at work. She walks Thabo to school, helps him with his homework and listens when he is sad. Families look different. Some children live with both parents, some with one parent, some with grandparents, aunts or older brothers and sisters. What matters is that someone cares for them.

Communities care too. Teachers care for learners at school. Nurses care for sick people. Neighbours look out for each other's children. Some organisations care for children who have no family to look after them, or for older people who live alone. In South Africa, the Constitution says that every child has the right to family care, to basic food, shelter and health care, and to be protected from harm.

Caring is something we can all do. You can care for a younger brother or sister, help an older person carry shopping, or include a lonely child in your game.

The questions for this section are with you now: why people need care, and who cares for us.

## Subtopic: Energy

People need energy for cooking food, heating their homes and water, and having light when it is dark. Energy also runs machines, such as fridges, radios and cell phone chargers.

People use different sources of energy. Many homes in towns and cities have electricity, which comes along wires from power stations far away. Most of South Africa's electricity is made in power stations that burn coal, especially in Mpumalanga. Some electricity comes from the wind, the sun and other sources. Thabo's family buys electricity with a prepaid meter, and they use gas for cooking because it is cheaper.

In many rural areas and informal settlements, people do not have electricity, or cannot afford enough of it. They use wood, which they collect and carry home, for cooking and heating. They also use paraffin for stoves and lamps, gas, and candles for light. More and more people use solar panels, which turn sunlight into electricity.

Energy must be used safely. Paraffin stoves and candles can fall over and start fires, and in crowded informal settlements, a fire can spread very quickly from one home to the next. Keep candles and stoves away from curtains, never leave them burning when you sleep, and keep paraffin away from small children, because it is poisonous. Never touch electrical wires or plugs with wet hands.

We can also save energy by switching off lights and appliances we are not using. Saving electricity saves money for the family, and it means less coal has to be burnt at the power stations, which keeps the air cleaner.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking health only means going to the doctor; clean water, good food, exercise and washing hands keep us healthy too. Two: thinking only parents can give care; grandparents, relatives, teachers and neighbours care for children too. Three: thinking everyone in South Africa has electricity; many people use wood, paraffin, gas or candles. Four: thinking candles and paraffin are always safe; they can start fires, so they must be used carefully.

The questions for this section are with you now: sources of energy, and using energy safely.
