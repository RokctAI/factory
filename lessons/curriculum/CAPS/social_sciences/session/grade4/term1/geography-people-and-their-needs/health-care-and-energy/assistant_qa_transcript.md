### subtopic_1 — Health
**Assistant:** Why do babies need so many injections?

**Tutor:** A baby's body is still learning how to fight germs. Some germs cause very serious illnesses, such as measles, polio, whooping cough and tetanus, which can make a baby very sick or even cause death. A vaccination teaches the body how to fight a particular germ before the real germ ever arrives. The injection contains a weak or harmless part of the germ, so the body practises making defenders, called antibodies. Later, if the real germ comes along, the body remembers it and fights it off quickly. Babies need several injections because there are several dangerous illnesses to protect against, and some vaccines need more than one dose to work well. Each child has a Road to Health booklet, where the nurse writes down every injection and checks the baby's weight and growth. Because so many children are vaccinated, some of these illnesses have become rare. That protects everyone, even people who are too young or too ill to be vaccinated.

### subtopic_2 — Care
**Assistant:** What should children do if they do not feel safe or cared for?

**Tutor:** Every child has the right to be safe and cared for. If a child feels unsafe, is being hurt, or is not getting enough food or care, it is very important to tell a trusted adult. That could be a teacher, a school principal, a grandparent, an aunt or uncle, a nurse at the clinic, a religious leader or a police officer. If the first adult does not help, the child should tell another one, and keep telling until someone helps. In South Africa, there is also a free phone line called Childline, on 116, where children can talk to someone who will listen and help. It is never a child's fault if they are not being cared for properly. Friends can help too. If a friend tells you they are being hurt, listen kindly, and help them to tell a trusted adult. Looking out for each other is part of caring.

### subtopic_3 — Energy
**Assistant:** How does electricity get from a power station to a light in a house?

**Tutor:** In a power station, a big machine called a generator is turned very fast. In most South African power stations, the turning is done by steam made by burning coal to boil water. The spinning generator produces electricity. The electricity then travels along thick metal cables held up high by tall steel towers called pylons. These cables carry the electricity across the country, for hundreds of kilometres. Near towns and cities, the electricity goes to substations, which change it so that it is safe for homes. Then smaller wires on poles or under the ground carry it to each house, through a meter that measures how much is used. Inside the house, wires in the walls carry it to the plugs and light switches. When Thabo flicks the switch, he completes a path, and the electricity flows through the bulb and makes it light up. All of that happens in a fraction of a second.
