### subtopic_1 — What a Project Is
**Assistant:** Why should I not copy sentences from a book or website?

**Tutor:** There are two big reasons. The first is honesty. When you copy someone else's words and hand them in as your own, you are taking credit for work you did not do. In schools and universities, this is called plagiarism, and it is treated as cheating. The second reason is learning. When you copy, your eyes move over the words, but your brain does not have to understand them. When you read, think, close the book and then write it in your own words, you have to understand it first, so you really learn. A good trick is to read a paragraph, close the book, and tell a friend or yourself what it said. Then write that down. If you really want to use someone's exact words, because they are special, put them in inverted commas, like this, and write who said them. And always list your sources at the end.

### subtopic_2 — Planning Your Project
**Assistant:** How do I know if a website is trustworthy?

**Tutor:** Not everything on the internet is true, because anyone can put information online. Here are some checks, which you should do with an adult's help. Who made the website? Websites made by museums, universities, the government, libraries and well-known newspapers are usually more trustworthy than a website made by an unknown person. Is there a date, so you know if the information is old or new? Does the information agree with what you found in books and other sources? If three good sources say the same thing, it is probably correct. Be careful of websites that are trying to sell something, or that use lots of exclamation marks and shocking headlines. And never share your name, address, school or photographs on a website. For a Grade 4 project, the library, the local museum and interviews with people in your community are often better sources than the internet anyway, because they know your local area.

### subtopic_3 — Putting It Together
**Assistant:** What if I cannot find much information about my topic?

**Tutor:** That happens to real historians too, so do not worry. First, check that your topic is not too small or too new. You might need to make it a little wider. For example, if there is nothing about your street, try your whole suburb or village. Second, think about all four kinds of sources. Even if there are no books, there may be people to interview, old photographs in family albums, objects in people's homes, or writing on buildings and signs. Local libraries, museums, churches and municipal offices often keep old records and photographs. Third, ask your teacher for help early, not the night before it is due. Your teacher may know of sources you have not thought of. Finally, it is fine to say in your project that some information could not be found. That is honest, and it shows you understand that history has gaps.
