### subtopic_1 — Bringing Information to Class
**Assistant:** What does the exclamation mark in the name !Kora mean?

**Tutor:** It is not really an exclamation mark. It is a letter that stands for a click sound. The !Kora people, also called the Korana, and the San and Nama peoples, speak languages that use click sounds, made by pulling the tongue away sharply from different parts of the mouth. When these languages were written down, people used special symbols for the clicks, such as !, the upright line, and the double line. The ! stands for a sharp, popping click made with the tongue against the roof of the mouth. isiXhosa and isiZulu also have clicks, which came into these languages from contact with Khoe and San peoples long ago. In isiXhosa, clicks are written with the letters c, q and x, as in the word Xhosa itself. So when you see the ! in !Kora or !Kung, you are seeing part of the history of southern Africa's oldest languages, written on the page.

### subtopic_2 — Sorting and Classifying
**Assistant:** How do I draw a timeline?

**Tutor:** Draw a long straight line across your page, with an arrow on the right end to show that time moves forward. Find the oldest date in your information and the newest date. Write the oldest near the left end and the newest near the right end. Then mark the other dates in between, in order. Try to space them so that equal amounts of time take up equal spaces, if you can. For example, if your line goes from 1850 to 2030, each 10 years could be one small step. Next to each date, write a short label of what happened, such as: 1884, town named Upington. You can add a tiny drawing beside each event. If you do not know an exact date, you can write about, or show a period, such as: before the 1870s, the !Kora and Nama lived along the river. A timeline helps your reader see quickly what happened first, what came next, and how long ago it was.

### subtopic_3 — Making Notes and Building the Project
**Assistant:** Why is a first draft never perfect?

**Tutor:** Because a first draft is meant to get your ideas out of your head and onto the page. It is a first try. While you are writing it, you are thinking about what to say, so it is normal to forget a fact, put things in a strange order, or make spelling mistakes. Even famous writers and historians write many drafts before their work is finished. After the first draft, you read it again with fresh eyes, perhaps the next day. You might notice that a paragraph would make more sense earlier, that you forgot to answer one of your questions, or that a sentence is too long. A friend or family member can also read it and tell you what is confusing. Then you fix those things. Each draft gets better. That is why it is so important to start early: if you only write one draft the night before, you have no time to improve it.
