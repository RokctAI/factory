# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers what happens in the middle of a project: bringing information to class, organising and classifying it, making notes, and continuing to build the project. The anchor is the Grade 4 class in Upington, in the Northern Cape, two weeks into their local history projects. Neo has an envelope stuffed with things: a photocopy of an old photograph showing men digging a canal beside the Orange River, notes from an interview with his grandfather, a printed page about grape and raisin farming, a newspaper cutting about a flood, a drawing of an old plough, and a list of questions he still wants to answer. When he tips it all onto his desk, it is a messy pile. His teacher smiles and says: now the real work of a historian begins. The big idea is that information only becomes useful when it is organised, and that sorting, putting things in time order and making clear notes turn a pile of facts into a project. Organising, classifying and making notes are examinable.

## Subtopic: Bringing Information to Class

When you bring information to class, bring copies, not precious originals. A family photograph that is the only copy could get lost or damaged. Ask an adult to help you make a photocopy, or take a photograph of it with a phone. The same goes for objects. A valuable old object can stay safely at home. Instead, bring a drawing or a photograph of it, with notes about what it is.

Every piece of information should be labelled with where it came from. Neo writes on the back of each page: the name of the book or website, the museum, or the person he interviewed and the date. If you do not label your sources now, you will forget later, and you will not be able to write your list of sources.

Bring your interview notes neatly written out. Neo's grandfather told him that when he was a boy, the farms beside the river grew grapes, wheat and lucerne, and that the farmers relied on canals to carry water to the fields. He also remembered a great flood when the river burst its banks.

In class, learners share what they found, and may find information that helps a friend. Aletta, who is researching the camel statue, learns from a classmate that the police once used camels to patrol the Kalahari Desert, because camels can travel far without water.

Remember to check information that seems strange or surprising, by looking for a second source.

The questions for this section are with you now: bringing copies, labelling sources, and sharing information.

## Subtopic: Sorting and Classifying

To classify means to sort things into groups that belong together. Historians classify information so that they can find it and use it.

There are different ways to classify. One way is by your smaller questions. Neo makes a heading for each of his questions on a large sheet of paper: who lived here first, how the town started, how farmers use the river, and how the town has changed. Then he puts each piece of information under the right heading.

Another way is by time. Neo sorts his information into three piles: before the town, the early town, and the town today. Before the town: the !Kora and Nama peoples lived and herded animals along the river, and San people lived in the Kalahari. The early town: in the 1870s a mission station was started beside the river, canals were dug to bring river water to farms, and in 1884 the town was named Upington, after Thomas Upington, who was the prime minister of the Cape Colony at that time. The town today: vineyards grow table grapes and grapes for raisins, and Upington is a busy town with an airport.

Once information is sorted by time, you can put it in order on a timeline. A timeline is a line with dates marked on it in order, from the oldest on the left to the newest on the right. It helps you see what happened first and what came next.

You may also find information that does not help to answer your question. It might be interesting, but if it does not fit, put it aside.

The questions for this section are with you now: classifying information, sorting by questions and by time, and timelines.

## Subtopic: Making Notes and Building the Project

Notes are short points that help you remember the important facts. Good notes are short, in your own words, and organised under headings.

To make notes, read a piece of information, find the key words and facts, and write them as short points, not long sentences. For example, from a long paragraph about the canals, Neo writes: canals dug from the 1880s; carry water from the Orange River to farms; let farmers grow grapes in a dry area. Next to his notes, he writes the source in brackets.

When your notes are organised, you can start to build the project. Write a first draft for each section, using your notes. A draft is a first try, which you will improve. Read it again, or ask a friend to read it. Does it answer the question? Is it in the right order? Is anything missing? Then fix it and write the final copy neatly.

Choose pictures and drawings that help to tell the story, and write a caption for each. Add your timeline. Make sure you have answered your key question in a short conclusion at the end. Neo's conclusion says: the Orange River gave water to the people who lived here first, and later the canals brought water to farms, so the town could grow in the middle of a dry area.

Keep working a little every week, following your timeline, and ask your teacher for help if you are stuck.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: bringing a precious original photograph to school; bring a copy. Two: forgetting to write where each piece of information came from; label every source. Three: putting information in a random order; classify it and use time order. Four: writing notes as long copied sentences; notes are short points in your own words.

The questions for this section are with you now: making notes, drafts, and building the project.
