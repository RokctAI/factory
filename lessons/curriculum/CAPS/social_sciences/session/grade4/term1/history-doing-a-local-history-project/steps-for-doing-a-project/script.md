# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how to do a history project and the steps to follow in putting it together. It explains what a project is, how to choose a topic and ask good questions, how to plan, and how to present the finished project. The anchor is a Grade 4 class in Upington, a town in the Northern Cape on the banks of the Orange River. Around the town are green vineyards, watered by canals that carry water from the river, in the middle of the dry, red Kalahari. The teacher hands out a project sheet: write a local history project on a place, a building, a person or an event in Upington. Neo chooses the question: how did the Orange River help Upington to grow? His friend Aletta chooses the history of the camel statue outside the police station. The big idea is that a project is a big task made of small steps, and that following the steps in order makes it possible to do a good project on time. Knowing and following the steps of a project is examinable, and the project itself is the formal History task for Term 1.

## Subtopic: What a Project Is

A project is a piece of work where you find out about a topic yourself, over several weeks, and then present what you found out. It is different from a test, because you have more time, you collect your own information, and you choose how to present it.

In a local history project, you investigate the history of your own area. Good topics include the history of your school, your street or the name of your town, a local building such as a church, mosque, temple or old station, a local person who made a difference, a local event, how a place has changed, or the history of your own family in the area.

In a project, you work like a historian. You ask questions, find sources, check them, and write about what you found out in your own words. You will use the four kinds of sources you have learnt about: pictures, writing, stories and interviews, and objects.

A project usually ends with a product. It might be a poster, a booklet, a short written report with pictures, a model, or a talk to the class. Your teacher will tell you which kind of product to make and give you a rubric. A rubric is a list that shows how marks will be given, such as marks for information, for using sources, for neatness, and for handing in on time.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a project is, good local history topics, and rubrics.

## Subtopic: Planning Your Project

Here are the first three steps.

Step one: choose a topic. Choose something that interests you, that is about your local area, and that you can find information about. A topic that is too big, like the history of South Africa, is too much. A topic that is too small may not have enough information. Neo's topic, the Orange River and the growth of Upington, is just right.

Step two: ask questions. A good project has a key question, and some smaller questions that help to answer it. Neo's key question is: how did the Orange River help Upington to grow? His smaller questions are: who lived here before the town was built? When and why did the town start? How do farmers use the river water? What crops do they grow? How has the town changed?

Step three: plan where you will find information. Make a list of sources. Neo will look at old photographs in the library and the local museum, read about Upington in books and on a trusted website with an adult's help, interview his grandfather, who has farmed grapes for forty years, and look at old objects such as farm tools. He will also visit the river and the canals.

Make a timeline for your work, so you do not leave everything for the last night. Write down the date the project is due, and plan what you will do each week. For example: week one, choose a topic and questions; week two and three, collect information; week four, sort it and write; week five, finish and hand in.

The questions for this section are with you now: choosing a topic, asking questions and planning.

## Subtopic: Putting It Together

Here are the next four steps.

Step four: collect information. Find your sources and take notes. Keep everything in one folder or envelope, so nothing gets lost.

Step five: sort the information. Put the information under your smaller questions, like putting clothes in the right drawers. Throw away information that does not help to answer your question.

Step six: write in your own words. Do not copy whole sentences from books or websites. Read the information, close the book, and write what you understood. If you use the exact words of a person, put them in inverted commas and say who said them. Use headings for each part. Add pictures, maps or drawings with captions.

Step seven: present your project and list your sources. Make your poster or booklet neat and easy to read. Write a title, your name and your class. At the end, write a list of all the sources you used: the names of books, websites, museums, and the people you interviewed, with the date of the interview. This is called a list of sources, or a bibliography. It shows where your information came from, and it is honest.

Finally, check your work against the rubric, and hand it in on time.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: choosing a topic that is far too big; choose a local topic you can manage. Two: leaving the whole project for the last night; make a timeline. Three: copying sentences from a book or website; write in your own words. Four: forgetting the list of sources; always say where your information came from.

The questions for this section are with you now: collecting, sorting, writing and presenting a project.
