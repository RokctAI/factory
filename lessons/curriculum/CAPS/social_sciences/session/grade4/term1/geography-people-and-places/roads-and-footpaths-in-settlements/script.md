# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers roads and footpaths in settlements: what they are, who uses them, what they are made of, and how people use them safely. The anchor is the walk to school of Sipho, a Grade 4 learner in Khayelitsha, a large township on the edge of Cape Town. Every morning he leaves home at seven. He cuts across an open field on a narrow footpath, joins a busy street lined with houses and spaza shops, walks beside the main road where minibus taxis and buses stop to pick up people, and crosses at the traffic lights to reach his school. Behind the school, he can hear the trucks and cars rushing along the N2 highway. The big idea is that roads and footpaths connect the places in a settlement and connect settlements to each other, and that different kinds of roads are used in different ways. Describing roads and footpaths and their uses is examinable.

## Subtopic: Footpaths

A footpath is a narrow path for people walking. Many footpaths were never built by anyone. They were made by many feet walking the same way again and again, until the grass wore away and a bare track appeared.

People make footpaths because they are short cuts. Sipho's footpath goes straight across the field instead of all the way around it, which saves him ten minutes. Footpaths join homes to schools, shops, taps, taxi ranks and clinics. In villages and on farms, footpaths join homesteads to each other, to fields, to rivers and to the village school. Many people in rural areas walk long distances every day on footpaths.

Some footpaths are made on purpose. In towns and cities, there are pavements, also called sidewalks, along the sides of streets. They are raised above the road so that people can walk safely, away from the cars. In parks there may be paved paths, and in the mountains there are hiking trails.

Footpaths are also used by people pushing wheelbarrows or prams, by cyclists, and by animals. In rural areas, you may see cattle and goats following footpaths to water.

A problem with footpaths is that some are not safe. A footpath across an open field may be lonely, dark in winter mornings and muddy after rain. It is safer to walk with friends, and in daylight.

The questions for this section are with you now: what footpaths are, how they are made, and how people use them.

## Subtopic: Roads and Streets

A road is a wide way that vehicles can drive on. Roads are built by people using machines, and they connect places.

Some roads are gravel roads, made of stones and sand pressed down. They are common on farms and in villages. Gravel roads are cheaper to build, but they become dusty when dry, muddy and slippery when wet, and full of bumps and holes. Other roads are tarred. Tar is a black, sticky material that is spread over stones to make a smooth, hard surface. Tar roads are smoother and faster, but they cost much more to build.

Inside a settlement, the roads between houses are called streets. Streets have names, such as Mew Way or Spine Road, so that people can find addresses. Main roads are wider and busier, and they carry people from one part of the settlement to another. Taxis and buses use main roads, and there are taxi ranks and bus stops along them.

Highways, also called freeways, are very big roads that connect towns and cities. The N2 starts in Cape Town and runs along the coast through towns such as George, Gqeberha and Durban, and ends in Mpumalanga. The N stands for national road.

Roads are used by many people for many reasons. Cars, minibus taxis and buses carry people to work, school and the shops. Trucks carry goods from farms, factories and harbours to shops. Ambulances, fire engines and police cars use roads to reach people who need help. People also ride bicycles and walk along roads.

The questions for this section are with you now: gravel and tar roads, streets, main roads and highways.

## Subtopic: Using Roads and Paths Safely

Roads are useful, but they can be dangerous, because cars and trucks move fast and are heavy. Roads have special features to keep people safe.

Traffic lights tell vehicles when to stop and when to go, at busy crossings. A pedestrian crossing, or zebra crossing, is a place with white stripes painted on the road where people may cross and vehicles must stop. A stop sign tells drivers to stop completely. Speed humps are bumps that make drivers slow down near schools and homes. A bridge carries a road or a footpath over a river or another road, and a footbridge lets people cross a highway safely high above the traffic.

Sipho follows the road safety rules. He walks on the pavement, never on the road. If there is no pavement, he walks on the side of the road facing the oncoming traffic, so that he can see the cars coming. He crosses only at the traffic lights or a pedestrian crossing. Before he crosses, he stops, looks right, looks left, and looks right again, because in South Africa vehicles drive on the left side of the road, so the closest traffic comes from the right. He never crosses a highway on foot.

Roads and footpaths together form a network, like a spider's web, that connects every home to every other place in the settlement.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking footpaths are always built by machines; many are made by feet walking the same way. Two: thinking all roads are tarred; many are gravel. Three: thinking a highway is a street between houses; a highway joins towns and cities. Four: looking only one way before crossing; look right, left and right again.

The questions for this section are with you now: road safety features and the road safety rules.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with Sipho's walk to school — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Paths for Walking

A footpath is a narrow path for people walking.

Many feet make a path. It is often a short cut.

Keep Sipho's short cut across the field in mind and try the questions for this part: footpaths.

## Subtopic: Roads for Vehicles

A gravel road is made of stones and sand. A tar road is smooth and hard.

Streets are inside a settlement. Highways join towns and cities.

Hold the busy main road with its taxis in mind and try this part's questions: roads and streets.

## Subtopic: Crossing Safely

Use the pavement, the traffic lights and the zebra crossing.

Look right, left and right again.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Footpaths are narrow paths for people walking, and many are made by feet as short cuts. Roads are wider ways for vehicles; they can be gravel or tar. Streets are inside settlements, main roads carry taxis and buses, and highways join towns and cities. Traffic lights, zebra crossings and bridges help people use roads safely.
