### subtopic_1 — Footpaths
**Assistant:** Why do some footpaths cut across fields when there is a road nearby?

**Tutor:** People are clever about saving time and energy. If a road goes around the edge of a field, and your school is on the other side, walking straight across is much shorter. One person tries it, then another, then many people follow, and soon the grass is worn away into a path. Geographers call these desire lines, because they show the way people really want to walk, not the way the planners drew the roads. Desire lines are found all over the world, in parks, on campuses and across open land in towns. Some town planners study them, and later build proper paved paths where the desire lines are, because that is where people need them. So when you see a footpath, it is like a message from the community, telling us where people need to go. The only problem is safety. A short cut is not always the safest way, so it is wise to walk with others and in daylight.

### subtopic_2 — Roads and Streets
**Assistant:** Why are some roads not tarred?

**Tutor:** Building a tar road costs a lot of money. First, the ground must be dug and levelled. Then layers of stones are laid and pressed down with heavy machines. Then hot tar is sprayed and spread to make the smooth surface. Drains must be built so that rain water runs off. All of this needs machines, materials and many workers. The government has to decide where to spend its money, so it usually tars the busiest roads first, the ones that carry the most cars, taxis, buses and trucks. Quiet roads on farms and in villages are often left as gravel roads, which are much cheaper. But gravel roads are hard to use in bad weather. In the rainy season, they can turn to mud, and taxis and ambulances may struggle to get through. That is why many communities ask for their roads to be tarred, so that they can reach schools, clinics and jobs more easily.

### subtopic_3 — Using Roads and Paths Safely
**Assistant:** Why do we look right first before crossing the road?

**Tutor:** It depends on which side of the road cars drive on. In South Africa, cars drive on the left side of the road. So when you stand on the pavement and face the road, the cars in the lane closest to you are coming from your right. They are the cars that would reach you first. That is why you look right first. Then you look left, to check the cars in the far lane. Then you look right again, because a car may have come around the corner from the right while you were looking left. Only when the road is clear both ways do you cross, walking quickly but not running, and keep looking as you go. In some countries, such as the United States, cars drive on the right, so people there look left first. If you ever travel, remember to check which side the cars drive on.
