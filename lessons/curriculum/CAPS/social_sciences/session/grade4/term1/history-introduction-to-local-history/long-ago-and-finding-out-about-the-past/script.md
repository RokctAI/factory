# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the History topic for Term 1, local history. It begins by remembering what we learnt in the Foundation Phase about how people lived long ago, then explains what local history is, and introduces the four main ways we find out about the past. The anchor is a Sunday afternoon in Soweto, in Johannesburg. Lindiwe, a Grade 4 learner, sits with her grandmother on the stoep of their house. Lindiwe's teacher has asked the class to find out how life was different when their grandparents were children. Gogo smiles, goes inside, and comes back with an old photograph, a heavy black iron and a letter that her own mother wrote to her many years ago. The big idea is that history is the story of people in the past, that it happened in our own neighbourhoods as well as in faraway places, and that we can find out about it from pictures, writing, stories and objects. Knowing the meaning of local history and the four kinds of sources is examinable.

## Subtopic: How People Lived Long Ago

History is the study of the past. The past is everything that has already happened, from a minute ago to thousands of years ago. The present is now. The future is what has not happened yet.

In the Foundation Phase you learnt that people long ago lived differently from us. Gogo was nine years old more than fifty years ago. She tells Lindiwe how different life was then.

At home, her family cooked on a coal stove, which also kept the kitchen warm in winter. There was no microwave and no electric kettle. Clothes were washed by hand in a big tub and hung on a line. Ironing was done with a heavy iron that was heated on top of the stove. When it cooled down, it had to be heated again. Many homes in Soweto had a tap in the yard, not inside the house.

There were no cell phones and no computers. To speak to family far away, people wrote letters and posted them, and waited weeks for an answer. Very few homes had a telephone. Television only came to South Africa in 1976, so for most of Gogo's childhood, families listened to the radio for news, music and stories.

Children played games outside: skipping, hopscotch, diketo, which is a game played with small stones, and soccer with a ball made of plastic bags and string. They made cars from wire. Many children walked long distances to school.

Some things have stayed the same. Children still play together, families still share meals, and people still tell stories.

The questions for this section are with you now: the past, present and future, and how life long ago was different.

## Subtopic: What Local History Is

Local history is the history of the area where you live. It is the story of your street, your school, your suburb, township, village or town, and the people who lived there before you.

Every place has a history. Some local history is famous. Soweto, where Lindiwe lives, is known all over the world. Vilakazi Street in Soweto is the only street in the world where two winners of the Nobel Peace Prize once lived: Nelson Mandela and Archbishop Desmond Tutu. And in 1976, school students in Soweto marched to protest against being forced to learn in Afrikaans, and the day they marched, the 16th of June, is now remembered every year as Youth Day.

But local history is also about ordinary people and everyday places. When was your school built, and why? Who was it named after? How did your street get its name? What did your area look like fifty years ago? Was there a river, a farm or a forest where houses stand today? Who were the first families to live there, and where did they come from?

Local history matters because it helps us understand why our area is the way it is, and it helps us feel proud of where we come from. It also reminds us that history is not only about kings and presidents, but about people like our own grandparents.

The questions for this section are with you now: what local history is, and the kinds of questions it asks.

## Subtopic: How We Find Out About the Past

A person who studies the past is called a historian. Historians are like detectives. They look for clues about the past, and these clues are called sources. A source is anything that gives us information about the past.

There are four main kinds of sources that we will use this term. Gogo has brought Lindiwe one of almost every kind.

Pictures, such as photographs, paintings and drawings. Gogo's old photograph shows her as a girl in a school uniform, standing in front of a small brick house.

Writing, such as letters, diaries, newspapers, books and school records. Gogo's letter from her mother tells about family news and the price of bread.

Stories and interviews, where people tell us what they remember. When Gogo tells Lindiwe about her childhood, she is giving an oral source. Oral means spoken.

Objects, which are things people made and used, such as tools, clothes, coins, toys and buildings. Gogo's heavy iron is an object from the past.

Historians use many sources together, because one source alone may not tell the whole story. A photograph shows what a place looked like, but not what people felt. A story tells how people felt, but a person might forget some details. When different sources agree, we can be more sure that the information is correct.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking history is only about famous people; it is about ordinary people too. Two: thinking local history happened somewhere else; it happened where you live. Three: thinking a source must be a book; pictures, stories and objects are sources too. Four: trusting one source alone; historians check several sources.

The questions for this section are with you now: historians, sources, and the four kinds of sources.
