### subtopic_1 — How People Lived Long Ago
**Assistant:** Why did television come so late to South Africa?

**Tutor:** Many countries had television from the 1950s or even earlier, but South Africa only started television broadcasts in 1976. The government of that time, the apartheid government, did not allow television for many years. Some leaders worried that television programmes from other countries would bring in ideas they did not like, including ideas about equal rights for all people. So for a long time, people in South Africa got their news and entertainment from the radio, newspapers and cinemas. When television finally arrived, there was only one channel, and it was on for just a few hours each evening. At first, programmes were mostly in English and Afrikaans, and later in African languages too. Most families could not afford a television set at first, so neighbours often gathered in one house to watch. This is a good example of how local and national history can be found in the memories of our own grandparents.

### subtopic_2 — What Local History Is
**Assistant:** Why do historians call stories oral sources?

**Tutor:** The word oral means to do with the mouth, or spoken. So an oral source is information that is spoken, not written down. When your grandmother tells you about her childhood, she is giving you an oral source. For thousands of years, long before writing was common, people in Africa and all over the world passed on their history by speaking. Elders told the history of the family, the clan and the people to the children around the fire. In many African communities, praise poets, such as the imbongi in isiXhosa and isiZulu culture, recited the history and great deeds of leaders. Songs, proverbs and the names of places also carry history. Oral sources are very precious, because they often tell us about ordinary people whose lives were never written down. They also tell us how people felt. That is why historians today record interviews with older people, so that their memories are not lost.

### subtopic_3 — How We Find Out About the Past
**Assistant:** Can a source ever be wrong?

**Tutor:** Yes, sources can sometimes be wrong or only tell part of the story. A person telling a story may forget details, mix up dates, or remember things in a way that makes them look good. A newspaper might report only one side of an event. A photograph shows only what was in front of the camera at that moment, not what was happening just outside the picture, and photographs can be set up or changed. That is why historians are careful. They ask questions about every source: who made it, when, and why? Was the person there? Could they have a reason to tell only one side? Then they compare different sources. If Gogo says her house had a tap in the yard, and an old photograph also shows a tap in the yard, we can be more confident it is true. Checking sources against each other is one of the most important skills a historian has.
