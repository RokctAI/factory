# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers landmarks and journeys. It explains what a landmark is, the difference between natural and human-made landmarks, and how to describe and draw a short journey, such as the way to school. The anchor is the morning walk of Ayanda, a Grade 4 learner who lives near the Umgeni River in Durban, KwaZulu-Natal. When she steps out of her gate, the first thing she sees is a huge old fig tree on the corner. She walks down the hill, crosses a bridge over the wide brown Umgeni River, passes a tall water tower, and turns towards her school. All the way, she can see the giant white arch of the Moses Mabhida Stadium standing above the city. The big idea is that landmarks help people find their way, and that a journey can be described in words and drawn as a simple map using landmarks in the right order. Identifying landmarks and drawing a route are examinable.

## Subtopic: What a Landmark Is

A landmark is a feature that is easy to see, easy to recognise and easy to remember. People use landmarks to find their way and to explain to others where something is.

A good landmark has three qualities. It stands out, because it is big, tall, colourful or unusual. It stays in the same place, so you can find it again tomorrow. And many people know it. A parked car is not a good landmark, because it can drive away. A huge fig tree that has stood on the same corner for a hundred years is a very good landmark.

People have used landmarks for thousands of years. Long before there were maps and street signs, travellers found their way by remembering a mountain shaped like a table, a river crossing or a large rock. Even today, people often give directions using landmarks, such as: turn left at the big church, or the shop is opposite the water tower.

Landmarks can be very large and famous, or small and known only to the people of one street. Table Mountain in Cape Town is a famous landmark that can be seen from far out at sea. The Moses Mabhida Stadium arch in Durban is a famous landmark that can be seen from many parts of the city. But a bright blue house at the end of your street, or the spaza shop on the corner, can be a landmark too, for the people who live there.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a landmark is, and what makes a good landmark.

## Subtopic: Natural and Human-made Landmarks

Landmarks can be sorted into two groups.

Natural landmarks are made by nature, not by people. They include mountains, hills, rivers, waterfalls, lakes, beaches, cliffs, large rocks, big trees and forests. On Ayanda's walk, the fig tree and the Umgeni River are natural landmarks. Other natural landmarks in South Africa include Table Mountain, the Drakensberg mountains, the Orange River and the Augrabies Falls.

Human-made landmarks are built by people. They include buildings, towers, bridges, statues, monuments, stadiums, churches, mosques, temples, schools, water towers, cell phone masts, railway stations and even big signs. On Ayanda's walk, the bridge, the water tower and the stadium are human-made landmarks. Other human-made landmarks in South Africa include the Union Buildings in Pretoria and the Nelson Mandela Bridge in Johannesburg.

A tree is a little tricky. If a tree grew there by itself, it is a natural landmark. If people planted it, some would call it human-made, but we usually still call a tree a natural feature, because it is a living part of nature.

When you look at a landscape, it helps to ask one question: was this made by nature or by people? Then sort each landmark into the right group.

## Subtopic: Describing and Drawing a Short Journey

A journey is a trip from one place to another. To describe a short journey, we say where it starts, the landmarks we pass in the right order, and where it ends.

Ayanda describes her journey like this. I start at my home. I pass the big fig tree on the corner. I walk down the hill and cross the bridge over the Umgeni River. I pass the water tower. Then I reach my school. Notice that she uses the landmarks in the order she meets them. If she mixed up the order, someone following her description would get lost. Order words help a lot: first, then, next, after that and finally. They tell the listener exactly which landmark comes before which, so the journey is easy to follow.

We can also draw a journey. A drawing of a route seen from above is a kind of simple map. Here are the steps. First, draw the start, such as a small house, at one side of the page, and label it home. Second, draw the end, such as the school, at the other side, and label it. Third, draw a line or a path between them that shows the way you walk. Fourth, draw the landmarks along the path in the right order, as small simple pictures, and label each one. Fifth, add arrows to show the direction you travel.

Keep the drawing simple and clear. You do not need to draw every house. Only draw what helps someone find the way.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: choosing a parked car or a dog as a landmark; a landmark must stay in one place. Two: calling a bridge a natural landmark; a bridge is built by people. Three: putting landmarks in the wrong order; they must be in the order you pass them. Four: forgetting to show the start, the end and the direction; label home and school, and add arrows.

The questions for this section are with you now: describing and drawing a short journey.
