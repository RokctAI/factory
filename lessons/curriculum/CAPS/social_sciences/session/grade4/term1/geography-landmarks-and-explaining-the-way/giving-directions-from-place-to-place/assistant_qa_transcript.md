### subtopic_1 — Left, Right and Straight On
**Assistant:** How do people give directions in places with no street names?

**Tutor:** In many villages and rural areas in South Africa, roads have no names and there are no street signs. People there are very good at using landmarks instead. They might say: walk past the big marula tree, then turn towards the hill shaped like a hat, and our homestead is the one with the green door after the school. They also use distances in time, such as: it is about twenty minutes' walk. Sometimes people use the direction of the sun, or say up the hill or down towards the river. Many people also explain the way by naming the family who lives in a house, because everyone knows each other. Today, some people share a location pin on a cell phone, which shows a point on a digital map. But a phone needs battery power, signal and data. So the old skill of describing a route with landmarks is still very useful, and it never runs out of battery.

### subtopic_2 — Using Landmarks and Road Names
**Assistant:** How do I remember which is left and which is right?

**Tutor:** Many people, even adults, mix up left and right, so you are not alone. Here are some tricks. Hold both hands out in front of you, palms facing away, and stick out your thumbs. The thumb and first finger of your left hand make the shape of a capital L, for left. The right hand makes a backwards L. Another trick is to think about the hand you write with. Most people write with their right hand, so if that is true for you, your writing hand is your right. If you write with your left hand, remember that instead. Some people wear a watch or a bracelet on one wrist to help them remember. With practice, you will not need the tricks any more. Try playing a game at home where someone calls out left or right and you turn quickly. The more you practise, the faster your brain learns it.

### subtopic_3 — Giving Clear Directions
**Assistant:** What should I do if I get lost in a town I do not know?

**Tutor:** First, stay calm and stop walking, so you do not get even more lost. Look around for a landmark you remember, or a street sign, and think back to the last place where you knew where you were. If you can safely walk back to that place, do so. If not, ask for help from a trusted adult. Good people to ask are a police officer, a security guard at a shop or bank, a shopkeeper inside a shop, or a teacher. Ask them to point out the way, but do not go off with a stranger. If you have a phone, call your family. It is a good idea to learn your home address and a parent's phone number by heart, or to carry them on a piece of paper. Before going somewhere new, look at the route with an adult, and choose some landmarks to look out for. Then you will be less likely to get lost.
