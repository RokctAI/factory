# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers how to explain the way from one place to another. It shows how to use the words left, right and straight on, how to use landmarks and road names, and how to put directions together so that someone else can follow them. The anchor is a Saturday morning in Mahikeng, the capital city of North West. Kagiso, a Grade 4 learner, has a visitor: his cousin Palesa from Rustenburg. Palesa wants to walk on her own to the public library to borrow books for a school project. She does not know Mahikeng, and her phone has no data. Kagiso takes a sheet of paper and writes down the way, step by step, from his gate to the library door. The big idea is that good directions use left and right, straight on, landmarks and road names, in the correct order, so that a person who has never been there can find the way. Giving and following directions is examinable.

## Subtopic: Left, Right and Straight On

The most important words in directions are left, right and straight on.

Left and right depend on the way you are facing. If you hold up your hands, the left hand makes the shape of a capital letter L with the thumb and first finger. That is one trick for remembering which side is left. When you turn left, you turn towards your left hand. When you turn right, you turn towards your right hand.

Straight on, or straight ahead, means keep walking in the same direction, without turning. Go back means turn around and walk the way you came.

Here is something important. Left and right change when you turn around. If Kagiso stands at his gate facing the street, the clinic is on his left. If he turns around to face his house, the clinic is now on his right. That is why directions always begin from a clear starting point, facing a clear way. Kagiso writes: stand at the gate, facing the street.

Directions also use the words on your left and on your right, to describe where something is as you walk past it. The library is on your left means you will see it on your left-hand side. Opposite means on the other side of the road. Next to means right beside. At the end of the street means where the street stops or meets another one. At the corner means where two streets meet. Behind means at the back of something, and in front of means facing it. These small words make a big difference. The shop next to the church and the shop opposite the church are two different shops.

The questions for this section are with you now: left, right, straight on, and words that describe position.

## Subtopic: Using Landmarks and Road Names

Left and right alone are not enough. If Kagiso writes, go straight, then turn left, then turn right, Palesa will ask: where do I turn? She needs to know exactly where. That is what landmarks and road names are for.

A landmark at a turning tells the person when to turn. Kagiso writes: turn right at the water tower. The tall water tower is easy to see, so Palesa will know exactly where to turn. Good landmarks for directions include big trees, churches, schools, petrol stations, water towers, shops with big signs, bridges and traffic lights.

Road names make directions even clearer. Most streets in towns and cities have names on signs at the corners. Kagiso writes: turn left into Church Street, and turn right into Station Road. Palesa can read the name on the street sign to check that she is on the right road. In villages and rural areas, roads often have no names or signs, so landmarks are even more important there.

Distances also help. Kagiso writes: go straight on for two blocks. A block is the stretch of a street between two corners. He could also write: walk for about five minutes. Counting helps too: the library is the third building on your left.

The questions for this section are with you now: landmarks at turnings, road names and distances.

## Subtopic: Giving Clear Directions

Here are Kagiso's full directions. Stand at the gate, facing the street. Turn left into Church Street. Go straight on for two blocks, past the clinic on your right. At the water tower, turn right into Station Road. Walk past the petrol station. The library is the third building on your left, opposite the post office.

Notice the five ingredients. A clear starting point. Turning words: left and right. Straight on, with a distance. Landmarks at the turns and along the way. Road names. And the directions end with how to recognise the place you are going to.

To give clear directions, follow four steps. First, picture the route in your mind, or draw it. Second, start from a clear starting point and a clear direction. Third, give one step at a time, in order. Fourth, check by reading the directions back while you trace the route with your finger on a drawing. If any step makes you stop and wonder which way to go, add a landmark or a road name to that step. Kagiso reads his directions to his mother, who knows the town well, and she agrees that Palesa will find the library easily.

When you follow directions, read one step at a time, and look out for each landmark before you turn. If you are lost, stop, look for a landmark or a street sign, and ask a trusted adult, such as a police officer, a shopkeeper or a security guard.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: forgetting that left and right change when you turn around; always give a starting direction. Two: saying turn left without saying where; name a landmark or a road at every turn. Three: using a landmark that moves, such as a parked taxi; choose something that stays in one place. Four: giving steps in the wrong order; give them in the order the person will walk.

The questions for this section are with you now: putting directions together, and following them.
