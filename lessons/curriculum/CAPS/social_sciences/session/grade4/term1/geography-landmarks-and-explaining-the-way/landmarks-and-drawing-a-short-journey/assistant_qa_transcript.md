### subtopic_1 — What a Landmark Is
**Assistant:** Can a sound or a smell be a landmark?

**Tutor:** That is a lovely question, because it shows you are thinking about how we really find our way. Strictly speaking, a landmark is something you can see. But people do use other senses to find their way. A person who is blind may know they are near the bakery from the smell of fresh bread, or near the taxi rank from the noise of hooters and people calling out where the taxis are going. They may feel the bumps on the pavement that warn them of a crossing. Many cities put special bumpy tiles on pavements to help people who cannot see. So while a sound or a smell is not a landmark in the usual sense, it can do the same job of helping someone know where they are. When you give directions to someone, it is kind to think about what they can see, hear and feel along the way, not only what you can see.

### subtopic_2 — Natural and Human-made Landmarks
**Assistant:** Is Table Mountain the oldest landmark in South Africa?

**Tutor:** Table Mountain is very old. The rocks it is made of formed hundreds of millions of years ago, which makes it much older than the Drakensberg, for example. But it is hard to say which landmark is the oldest, because some rocks in South Africa are even older. In Mpumalanga, the Barberton Makhonjwa Mountains contain some of the oldest rocks on Earth, more than three thousand million years old. And the Vredefort Dome in the Free State is the remains of a huge crater made when a rock from space hit the Earth about two thousand million years ago. Both are World Heritage Sites, which means they are protected because they are important to the whole world. What makes Table Mountain such a famous landmark is not only its age, but its flat top, which makes it easy to recognise from far away, even from ships at sea.

### subtopic_3 — Describing and Drawing a Short Journey
**Assistant:** What if two people describe the same journey differently?

**Tutor:** That happens all the time, and both descriptions can still be correct. People notice different things. One person might remember the fig tree and the bridge. Another might remember the red shop and the church. As long as the landmarks really are along the route, and they are in the right order, both descriptions will get you there. Some descriptions are better than others, though. A good description uses landmarks that are easy to see and that stay in one place. It gives them in the right order, and it does not leave out important turns. A weak description might use something that moves, like a dog, or skip a turning, so the listener gets lost. When you check a description, try to follow it in your head, step by step, as if you were a visitor who has never been there before. If you can follow it, it is a good description.
