# Part 1 — Expert

This session revises the Term 1 topics. In Geography, the topic was places where people live: settlements, jobs and buildings, roads and footpaths, landmarks and directions, and people's needs. In History, the topic was local history: how we find out about the past from pictures, writing, stories and interviews, and objects, and how to do a project. The anchor is a revision morning in the same Grade 4 classroom in Mthatha where the year began. The class has set up three stations around the room. Station 1 has drawings of a farm, a village, a town and a city, and pictures of buildings and roads. Station 2 has a map of the walk from the school to the taxi rank, and pictures of a tap, a plate of food, a house, a clinic, a family and a candle. Station 3 has an old photograph, a letter, a recorded interview and a grinding stone. Groups move from station to station, answering the questions on each card. The big idea is that people live in different kinds of settlements, find their way using landmarks and directions, and share the same basic needs, and that we can find out about the history of our own area from many kinds of sources. The whole of Term 1 is examinable.

## Subtopic: Places Where People Live

At Station 1, the drawings remind the class that a settlement is a place where people live together. There are four kinds, from smallest to biggest: farm, village, town and city. A farm has lots of land and few people. A village is small, with a few services such as a school and a small shop. A town is bigger, with banks, a hospital and many shops. A city is the biggest, with the most people, buildings and jobs. Farms and villages are rural areas, and towns and cities are urban areas.

People do different jobs in different places. On farms, people grow crops and keep animals. In villages there are teachers, nurses and shopkeepers. Towns and cities have the most kinds of jobs, in factories, offices, shops, banks, hospitals and transport. The land, the number of people and the buildings decide the jobs in a place.

Every building has a use. Houses are homes. Kraals and coops shelter animals. Shops, schools, clinics and banks give services. Churches, mosques, synagogues and temples are places of worship. Factories make goods, and stations are where journeys start and end.

Footpaths are narrow paths for walking, often made by many feet as short cuts. Roads are for vehicles. They can be gravel or tar. Streets are inside settlements, and highways join towns and cities. To cross safely, use a pedestrian crossing or traffic lights, and look right, left and right again.

The questions for this section are with you now: settlements, jobs, buildings, and roads and footpaths.

## Subtopic: Finding the Way and People's Needs

At Station 2, the map of the walk reminds the class about landmarks. A landmark is easy to see and remember, and it stays in one place. Natural landmarks are made by nature, such as rivers, hills and big trees. Human-made landmarks are built by people, such as bridges, towers and stadiums.

To describe or draw a short journey, show the start, the landmarks in the order you pass them, and the end, with arrows for the direction. To give directions, start from a clear point and direction, and use left, right and straight on, with landmarks and road names at every turn. Remember that left and right swap when you turn around.

The pictures remind the class of what all people need. A need is something we must have to live, and a want is something we would like but can live without. All people need clean water, food, shelter, health, care and energy. People get water from taps, rivers, springs and boreholes. They grow food or buy it. Shelters keep people safe from the weather. Clinics, clean water and good food keep people healthy. Families and communities care for children and older people. People use electricity, gas, paraffin, wood and the sun for energy, and must use fire safely.

The questions for this section are with you now: landmarks, journeys, directions, and people's needs.

## Subtopic: Local History and Sources

At Station 3, the objects on the table remind the class about History. History is the study of the past. Local history is the history of the area where you live: your street, school, township, village or town.

Historians find out about the past from sources. Pictures, such as photographs, paintings and rock art, show what places and people looked like; we read them by asking what, where, when, who and why, and by reading the caption. Writing, such as letters, diaries, newspapers, registers and plaques, gives names and dates; we ask who wrote it, when and why. Stories and interviews are oral sources; we plan open questions, ask permission, listen carefully and say thank you. Objects, such as grinding stones, irons and coins, show how people lived; museums keep them, and archaeologists dig them up carefully. Historians compare several sources, because one source may not tell the whole story.

A project follows seven steps: choose a topic, ask questions, plan where to look, collect information, sort and classify it, write in your own words, and present it with a list of sources. Bring copies of sources, not precious originals, and label each one. Make short notes, put events in order on a timeline, and write a draft before the neat final copy.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying a farm is not a settlement; it is the smallest kind. Two: using a moving thing, such as a parked car, as a landmark; landmarks stay in one place. Three: calling a cell phone a need; it is a want. Four: trusting one source alone; historians check several sources.

The questions for this section are with you now: local history, sources and the project.
