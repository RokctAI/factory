# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the different ways people get their food: buying, growing, collecting, fishing and hunting. It explains each way, and why people in different places get their food in different ways. The anchor is a look at three Grade 4 learners and their families in three different parts of South Africa. Thandeka lives in a flat in the city of Durban, in KwaZulu-Natal, and on Saturdays she goes with her mother to the busy market near the city centre. Rendani lives in a village near Thohoyandou, in Limpopo, where his grandmother grows maize and vegetables and collects wild fruit from the veld. Jacob lives in Paternoster, a small fishing village on the West Coast of the Western Cape, where his father goes out to sea in a small boat to catch fish. The big idea is that people get their food in five main ways, and that the way a family gets its food depends on where they live, what the land and sea offer, and how much money they have. Explaining the ways people get food is examinable.

## Subtopic: Buying and Growing Food

Buying is the most common way people get food today, especially in towns and cities. People buy food from supermarkets, small shops, spaza shops, markets, street vendors and butcheries. To buy food, people need money, which they earn by working.

Thandeka and her mother buy most of their food. At the market in Durban, they buy tomatoes, onions, bananas and spices from traders who bring fresh food from farms every morning. At the supermarket, they buy maize meal, bread, milk and chicken. Living in a flat, they have no space to grow food, so buying is the main way they get it.

Growing means planting seeds or young plants and caring for them until the food is ready to eat. It also means raising animals, such as chickens, goats and cattle, for eggs, milk and meat. Growing food is called farming.

Rendani's grandmother grows food. She has a field of maize behind the homestead, and a garden with spinach, pumpkins, beans and tomatoes. She keeps chickens for eggs, and a few goats. In summer, when it rains, she plants. In autumn, she harvests the maize and dries it to store for the winter. Growing food saves money, but it is hard work, and it depends on rain and good soil.

Most families use more than one way. Rendani's family grows much of its food, but also buys sugar, oil, salt and soap from the spaza shop. Jacob's family eats fish that his father catches, but buys bread, vegetables and maize meal at the shop in the village.

The questions for this section are with you now: buying food, growing food, and why families use more than one way.

## Subtopic: Collecting Food from Nature

Collecting, also called gathering, means picking up or picking food that grows naturally in the wild, without planting it. People have collected food from nature for hundreds of thousands of years.

In the veld near Rendani's village, his grandmother collects many foods. In late summer, she collects marula fruit from the big marula trees. The fruit is round, yellow and juicy, and can be eaten fresh or made into jam and juice. She picks wild green leaves for morogo. In some years, she collects mopane worms, the caterpillars of a moth, from mopane trees. Some people collect wild honey, mushrooms, berries and insects such as flying ants.

People along the coast also collect food. At low tide, people collect mussels and oysters from the rocks.

Long ago, before farming, the San people of southern Africa were hunter-gatherers. They lived by gathering wild plants, roots, berries, nuts and honey, and by hunting animals. They knew hundreds of plants in the veld and which ones were safe to eat.

Collecting must be done carefully. People must know which plants and mushrooms are safe, because some are poisonous. And if people collect too much, the plants and animals may not grow back. Many areas have rules about how much people may collect.

The questions for this section are with you now: collecting food from nature, and hunter-gatherers.

## Subtopic: Fishing and Hunting

Fishing means catching fish and other sea creatures from the sea, rivers, lakes and dams. In Paternoster, Jacob's father is a small-scale fisher. He leaves early in the morning in his small boat with other fishers and uses lines and hooks to catch snoek. Some of the fish feeds the family, and the rest is sold. Big fishing companies use large boats with huge nets to catch fish such as hake and pilchards far out at sea. Large boats that drag their nets behind them through the water are called trawlers. The fish is frozen or canned in factories and sold all over the world.

Fishing is very important in coastal towns. But if too many fish are caught, there will be none left for the future. So the government has rules: fishers need permits, and there are limits on how many fish and what size of fish may be caught.

Hunting means catching or killing wild animals for food. Long ago, hunting was a main way of getting meat. San hunters used bows and poisoned arrows to hunt eland, springbok and other animals. Today, very few South Africans hunt for food. Hunting is only allowed with a permit, in certain places and at certain times of the year, to protect wild animals. Hunting animals without permission is called poaching, and it is against the law.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking all food is bought; people also grow, collect, catch and hunt it. Two: thinking collecting is the same as growing; collected food grows wild, without being planted. Three: thinking anyone may hunt any animal; hunting needs a permit, and poaching is illegal. Four: thinking the sea has endless fish; fishers must follow limits, or the fish will run out.

The questions for this section are with you now: fishing, hunting, and rules that protect fish and animals.
