# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers subsistence farming, which means farming mainly to feed yourself and your family. It explains what subsistence farming is, what happens on a subsistence farm through the year, and the problems and benefits of this way of farming. The anchor is the homestead of Siphokazi, a Grade 4 learner who lives near Flagstaff, in the hills of the Eastern Cape. Her family's homestead has three round houses and one square house, a kraal for the cattle, a chicken coop, and a vegetable garden fenced with branches. Behind the homestead is a field of maize, beans and pumpkins. Her grandfather looks after the cattle, her mother and grandmother do much of the planting and weeding, and Siphokazi and her brothers help after school and on weekends. The big idea is that subsistence farmers grow crops and keep animals mainly to feed their own families, on small pieces of land, using simple tools and family labour, and that their success depends a great deal on the rain. ## Subtopic: What Subsistence Farming Is

Subsistence farming is farming to produce food mainly for the farmer's own family, not to sell. The word subsist means to stay alive, or to have just enough to live on.

Subsistence farms are small. A family might have a field of one or two hectares and a garden near the house. A hectare is a little bigger than a soccer field. The fields are often close to the homestead, so the family can look after them easily.

Subsistence farmers usually grow a mix of crops. In South Africa, the most important crop is maize, because maize meal is used to make pap, the main food in many homes. Families also grow beans, pumpkins, potatoes, sweet potatoes, cabbage, spinach and sometimes sorghum. Growing a mix of crops means that if one crop fails, the family still has something to eat.

Many subsistence farmers also keep animals. Chickens give eggs and meat. Goats give meat and sometimes milk. Cattle give milk, meat, leather and manure, and oxen can pull a plough. In many African cultures, cattle are also a sign of wealth and are important in ceremonies, such as weddings, where cattle are given as lobola.

The work is done mostly by the family, using simple tools, such as hoes, spades and rakes, and sometimes a plough pulled by oxen or donkeys. Some families hire a tractor to plough.

Subsistence farming is common in rural areas of the Eastern Cape, KwaZulu-Natal, Limpopo and Mpumalanga, and in many other parts of Africa.

## Subtopic: A Year on a Subsistence Farm

Siphokazi's family follows the seasons. The Eastern Cape hills get most of their rain in summer.

In spring, around October and November, when the first good rains fall and the soil is soft, the family ploughs the field. Her grandfather walks behind two oxen pulling a plough, and the others follow, planting maize seeds in the furrows. They plant beans and pumpkins between the maize plants. The maize stalks give the beans something to climb, and the big pumpkin leaves cover the soil and keep it moist.

In summer, the plants grow fast. Weeds grow fast too, and they take water and food from the soil. So the whole family weeds the field with hoes. The children chase birds away from the young maize.

In autumn, around April and May, the maize cobs are dry and ready. The family harvests them by hand, removes the leaves and lets the cobs dry further in the sun. Then they store the maize in a dry place, safe from rats and insects. Some of it is ground into maize meal. Some seeds are kept to plant next year.

In winter, the field rests. The cattle are allowed to graze the dry maize stalks, and their manure helps the soil. The family eats from their stored food and from the winter vegetables in the garden, such as cabbage and spinach, watered with water carried from the tap or the river.

## Subtopic: Challenges and Benefits

Subsistence farming has many benefits. The family has fresh food and does not have to spend as much money at the shop. They know where their food comes from. Children learn farming skills from their parents and grandparents. Keeping animals is a way of saving wealth for hard times.

If the family grows more than it needs, the extra food is called a surplus. Siphokazi's family shares some of its surplus pumpkins with neighbours and sells a few bags of maize at the market in Flagstaff to buy school shoes.

But subsistence farming is also hard and risky. If the rains are late, or there is a drought, the crops can die, and the family may not have enough to eat. Hail can destroy a crop in minutes. Insects, such as stalk borers, can eat the maize. The soil can lose its goodness if the same crop is grown in it year after year, and heavy rain can wash soil away from steep fields. The fields are small, and families usually have little money for seed, fertiliser or machines.

To help, some farmers save seed from strong plants, add manure and compost to the soil, plant across the slope of a hill so that rain does not wash the soil away, and rotate crops, which means growing a different crop in a field each year. Government agricultural advisers also visit villages to give advice.
