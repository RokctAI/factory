### subtopic_1 — What Subsistence Farming Is
**Assistant:** Why do farmers plant beans and pumpkins with the maize?

**Tutor:** This way of planting is very old and very clever. In parts of the Americas, where maize, beans and pumpkins first came from, it is called the three sisters. Each plant helps the others. The tall maize stalks give the bean plants something to climb up, so the beans can reach the sunlight without needing poles. Bean plants have a special power: tiny living things in their roots take a gas called nitrogen from the air and put it into the soil. Nitrogen is like food for plants, so the beans help to feed the maize. The pumpkins spread their huge leaves across the ground. The leaves shade the soil, which keeps it moist and stops weeds from growing, and the prickly stems keep some animals away. Together, the three crops give the family a mix of foods. Maize gives energy, beans give protein, and pumpkins give vitamins. Many subsistence farmers in South Africa still plant this way today.

### subtopic_2 — A Year on a Subsistence Farm
**Assistant:** What is lobola?

**Tutor:** Lobola is a custom in many African cultures in South Africa, including among isiXhosa, isiZulu, Sesotho, Setswana and other communities. When a couple plans to marry, the family of the man gives a gift to the family of the woman. Traditionally, the gift was cattle. The number of cattle was agreed through careful discussions between the two families, led by respected family members. Lobola is not seen as buying a wife. It is a way of showing respect and thanks to the bride's family, of joining the two families together, and of showing that the man can take care of a family. Today, many families give money instead of cattle, or a mix of both, but the amount is often still counted in cattle. This custom is one reason why cattle are so important in many rural communities, beyond giving milk and meat. Customs like lobola are part of South Africa's rich cultural heritage.

### subtopic_3 — Challenges and Benefits
**Assistant:** What do subsistence farmers do when there is a drought?

**Tutor:** A drought is a long period with much less rain than usual, and it is one of the biggest dangers for subsistence farmers, because most of them depend completely on rain for their crops. When a drought comes, families use several ways to cope. They may plant crops that need less water, such as sorghum and millet, instead of maize. They may plant smaller areas and water a small garden by hand, with water carried from the tap or the river. They eat their stored food carefully, to make it last. Some families sell an animal to buy maize meal at the shop. Relatives working in towns may send money home. Neighbours often share with each other. In very bad droughts, the government and organisations may hand out food parcels or give animal feed. Some farmers dig small dams or set up tanks to catch rain water from their roofs in good years, so they have water saved for dry times.
