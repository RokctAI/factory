# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers growing food in towns and cities, which is called urban farming. It explains why people grow food in urban areas, the different places and ways they do it, and its benefits and challenges. The anchor is a community food garden in Khayelitsha, a large township on the Cape Flats near Cape Town. The soil here is sandy and the wind blows hard, but on a piece of open land between the houses, a group of women, most of them grandmothers, have built neat beds of spinach, cabbage, onions, carrots, beetroot and herbs, sheltered by a fence and rows of tall plants. One of them, Mama Zodwa, shows a Grade 4 class how she makes compost from vegetable peels and leaves. Later, the class sees photographs of a rooftop garden on top of a building in central Johannesburg, and visits their partner school in Soweto, where the learners grow vegetables in round keyhole gardens. The big idea is that people can grow food even in crowded towns and cities, in small spaces, and that this brings fresh food, saves money and brings communities together. ## Subtopic: Why Grow Food in Towns and Cities

Most people in towns and cities buy their food. So why would anyone grow food in the city?

The first reason is money. Food in shops can be expensive, and many families struggle to buy enough fresh vegetables. A small garden can give a family spinach, tomatoes, onions and herbs almost every day, saving money for other things.

The second reason is fresh, healthy food. Vegetables picked straight from the garden are fresh and full of goodness. Gardens help families eat more vegetables, which keeps them healthy.

The third reason is income. Some urban gardeners grow more than they need and sell the surplus to neighbours, at street markets or to local shops. The women in Khayelitsha sell some of their vegetables to people in the street, and the money helps to pay for school uniforms and electricity.

The fourth reason is community. A community garden brings people together. Neighbours work side by side, share seeds and tools, and teach each other. Older people pass on farming skills to younger people.

Many people living in cities today, or their parents and grandparents, grew up in rural areas where they farmed. Growing food in the city is a way of keeping those skills alive.

Urban farming also makes the city greener. Empty plots that were full of rubbish become gardens, and plants help to clean the air.

## Subtopic: Where and How

People in towns and cities grow food in many different places.

Backyard gardens are small gardens at home, in the space around a house.

Community gardens are shared gardens on open land, such as the one in Khayelitsha. Many families or a group of people work together, often with help from organisations that give seeds, tools and training.

School gardens are gardens at schools, where learners learn how to grow food. The vegetables may be used in the school feeding programme.

Rooftop gardens are gardens on the flat roofs of buildings, where there is no space on the ground. Plants grow in boxes, tubs and special beds. In central Johannesburg, some buildings have rooftop farms that grow lettuce, herbs and other vegetables above the busy streets.

Container gardens use pots, buckets, old tyres, paint tins, plastic bottles or bags filled with soil. They can stand on a stoep, a balcony or a windowsill. Even people in flats can grow herbs or tomatoes this way.

A keyhole garden is a round, raised bed with a path into the middle, shaped like a keyhole when seen from above. In the middle is a basket where kitchen scraps and grey water from washing are added, which feed the soil and keep it moist. Keyhole gardens use little water, so they are good for dry places.

Some people in towns also keep chickens in their yards for eggs, where the town's rules allow it.

## Subtopic: Benefits and Challenges

Urban farming has many benefits: fresh, healthy food; saving money; earning income; bringing people together; teaching skills; and turning empty, dirty spaces green.

But it also has challenges. Space is the biggest problem, because land in cities is crowded and expensive. Water can be a problem too, because water from taps costs money, and in a drought, cities limit how much water people may use. Gardeners save water by using mulch, which is a layer of dry leaves or grass on the soil, by watering early in the morning or late in the afternoon, and by catching rain water from roofs in tanks.

Soil in cities can be poor, sandy or even polluted. Gardeners improve it with compost. Compost is made by letting kitchen scraps, such as vegetable peels and eggshells, and garden waste, such as leaves and grass cuttings, rot together in a heap. After a few weeks, it becomes dark, crumbly and full of food for plants.

Theft is another problem, so community gardens need fences and people to watch over them. And gardening takes time and work every day.
