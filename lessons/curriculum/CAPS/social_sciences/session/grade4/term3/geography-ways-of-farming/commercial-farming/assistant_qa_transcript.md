### subtopic_1 — What Commercial Farming Is
**Assistant:** What is the difference between income and profit?

**Tutor:** Income is all the money that comes in. Profit is the money that is left over after you have paid all your costs. Imagine you sell vetkoek at school. You sell lots of them and get some money: that is your income. But you had to buy flour, oil, yeast and sugar, and pay for the gas to fry them: those are your costs. The money left after paying for all of that is your profit. If your costs are bigger than your income, you have made a loss, not a profit. A commercial farmer works in the same way, but with much bigger numbers. The income comes from selling maize, fruit or animals. The costs include seed, fertiliser, fuel, machines, electricity, water and the wages of workers. In a good year, with good rain and good prices, the farmer makes a profit. In a year with drought or very low prices, the farmer may make a loss.

### subtopic_2 — Machines, Workers and Water
**Assistant:** What is a silo?

**Tutor:** A silo is a tall building used to store large amounts of grain, such as maize, wheat or sunflower seeds, safely and dry. In South Africa, you can see tall round concrete silos, often in rows, standing next to railway lines in farming towns in the Free State, North West and Mpumalanga. They are often the tallest buildings in a small farming town, so they make excellent landmarks. Grain is carried up to the top of the silo by machines and poured in from above. When it is needed, it is let out at the bottom into trucks or railway wagons. Inside a silo, the grain is protected from rain, rats and insects, and the temperature is checked, because damp grain can go rotten. Storing grain in silos means that maize harvested in autumn can be used all year round, so there is maize meal in the shops even in spring, long before the next harvest.

### subtopic_3 — From Farm to Market
**Assistant:** Why does South Africa export oranges to other countries?

**Tutor:** South Africa is one of the biggest exporters of fresh citrus fruit, such as oranges, lemons and naartjies, in the world. Our citrus grows well in warm, sunny areas such as Limpopo, the Eastern Cape, the Western Cape and Mpumalanga. Because South Africa is in the southern half of the world, our seasons are the opposite of countries in the northern half, such as those in Europe. When it is winter there and their trees have no fruit, our citrus is ready to pick. So people in Europe and other places buy our oranges at that time. Exporting earns money for the farmers and for the country, and it creates jobs for many thousands of people who pick, pack and transport the fruit. The fruit is packed into boxes, cooled to keep it fresh, and shipped from ports such as Durban, Gqeberha and Cape Town in refrigerated containers on huge ships.
