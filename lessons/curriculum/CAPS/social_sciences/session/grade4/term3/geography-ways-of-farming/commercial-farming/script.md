# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers commercial farming, which means farming crops and animals to sell for a profit. It explains what commercial farming is, how commercial farmers use machines, workers and water, and how farm products reach shops in South Africa and other countries. The anchor is a large maize farm near Bothaville, in the Free State, owned by Mrs Mofokeng. It is May, and harvest time has come. From the farmhouse, the dry, golden maize fields stretch as far as the eye can see. A huge green combine harvester moves slowly along the rows, cutting the plants, pulling off the cobs and separating the hard yellow kernels, which pour into a trailer pulled alongside. A line of trucks waits at the edge of the field to carry the maize to tall concrete silos next to the railway line. The big idea is that commercial farmers grow crops and raise animals on large farms, mainly to sell, using machines, paid workers, and sometimes irrigation, and that their products feed people in towns and cities and are sold to other countries. Explaining commercial farming is examinable.

## Subtopic: What Commercial Farming Is

Commercial farming is farming mainly to sell crops and animals for money. The word commercial means to do with buying and selling. A commercial farmer runs the farm as a business.

The money a farmer gets from selling the crops and animals is called income. The farmer must pay for seed, fertiliser, animal feed, fuel, machines, electricity, water and workers' wages. The money left over after paying all the costs is called profit. A farmer needs a profit to keep the farm going and to support the family.

Commercial farms are usually much bigger than subsistence farms. Mrs Mofokeng's farm is more than one thousand hectares. Some commercial farms in the Free State, the Karoo and the Northern Cape are even bigger.

Many commercial farmers specialise, which means they grow mainly one or two crops, or keep mainly one kind of animal. Mrs Mofokeng grows maize and sunflowers. Other commercial farms grow wheat, sugar cane, citrus fruit, apples, grapes or vegetables. Some keep beef cattle, dairy cows, sheep, pigs or chickens. Some farms do both crops and animals.

Commercial farming produces most of the food sold in South Africa's shops. Farmers also sell some products to other countries. This is called exporting. South Africa exports a lot of citrus fruit, such as oranges and lemons, as well as grapes, apples, wine and wool.

The questions for this section are with you now: what commercial farming is, income and profit, and specialising.

## Subtopic: Machines, Workers and Water

Commercial farmers use machines to farm large areas quickly. Tractors pull ploughs to turn the soil, and planters drop seeds in neat rows. Sprayers spread fertiliser to feed the plants, and chemicals to kill weeds and insects. At harvest time, combine harvesters cut and thresh the crop in one go. Some modern farmers even use satellites and drones to check which parts of a field need more water or fertiliser.

Commercial farms also need many workers. On Mrs Mofokeng's farm, there are tractor drivers, mechanics who fix the machines, workers who check the fields and the silos, and an office worker who keeps the accounts. Fruit farms need even more workers, because fruit must be picked carefully by hand. Many farm workers live on the farm with their families. Farm workers must be paid at least the minimum wage set by law.

Many commercial farmers use irrigation, which means bringing water to crops by pipes, canals, sprinklers or drip pipes, instead of depending only on rain. Water may come from a river, a dam or a borehole. Irrigation means farmers can grow crops even in dry areas and dry seasons, such as the vineyards along the Orange River near Upington. Mrs Mofokeng's maize, though, mostly depends on the summer rain, so she watches the weather forecast very carefully.

Commercial farmers often borrow money from banks to buy machines and seed, and they buy insurance in case of hail or drought. A new combine harvester can cost as much as several houses, so farmers must plan carefully. Some farmers share or hire machines to save money. Farmers also learn new methods at farming shows, such as the big harvest show held near Bothaville every year.

The questions for this section are with you now: farm machines, farm workers and irrigation.

## Subtopic: From Farm to Market

After the harvest, commercial farm products begin a long journey.

Mrs Mofokeng's maize is carried by truck to the silos, where it is dried, checked and stored. Then it is sold. Some goes by train or truck to mills, where it is ground into maize meal and packed into bags for shops. Some goes to factories that make animal feed, breakfast cereals or cooking oil. Some is loaded onto ships at Durban harbour and exported to other countries.

Other farm products travel in different ways. Milk from dairy farms is collected every day by tankers and taken to dairies, where it is packed into cartons. Cattle are taken to auctions, where buyers bid for them. Fruit is packed into boxes in packhouses on the farm, cooled, and sent by refrigerated trucks to markets, or by refrigerated ships overseas.

Big markets in cities, such as the Johannesburg Fresh Produce Market, buy fruit and vegetables from many farms and sell them to shops, restaurants and street traders.

So commercial farming connects many people: farmers, workers, truck drivers, factory workers, shopkeepers and the families who buy the food.

Two: thinking profit is all the money a farmer gets; profit is what is left after paying the costs. Three: thinking commercial farms only grow crops; many keep animals too. Four: thinking all farm products stay in South Africa; some are exported to other countries.
