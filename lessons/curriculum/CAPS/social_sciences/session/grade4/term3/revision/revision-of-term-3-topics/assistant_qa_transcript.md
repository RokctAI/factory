### subtopic_1 — Food and Ways of Farming
**Assistant:** How can I remember all the ways of farming?

**Tutor:** Try sorting them by one simple question: who is the food for? If the food is mainly for the farmer's own family, it is subsistence farming. Think of a small plot, a hoe, a few chickens and the family working together. If the food is mainly to sell for a profit, it is commercial farming. Think of a big farm, tractors, many paid workers, silos and exports. If the food is grown in a town or city, it is urban farming. Think of backyard gardens, rooftops, school gardens and containers. Then, for any farm, ask a second question: is it growing plants or keeping animals? Plants mean crop farming, like maize or oranges. Animals mean stock farming, like cattle or sheep. Many farms do both, and that is called mixed farming. So a farm can be both commercial and crop farming, like the orange farm near Tzaneen, or subsistence and stock farming, like a family with a few goats.

### subtopic_2 — Crop and Stock Farming
**Assistant:** How do I learn all the transport dates?

**Tutor:** Do not try to learn every date as a long list. Instead, make a timeline with three lanes, one for land, one for water and one for air, and put only the most important dates on it. For land: 1652, when wheeled wagons came to the Cape; 1817, the first bicycle; 1860, the first railway in Durban; and 1886, the first petrol car. For water: 1488, when Dias sailed around the Cape; and 1807, Fulton's steamboat. For air: 1783, the first balloon flights; 1903, the Wright brothers; and 1909, the first flight in South Africa, in East London. Draw a small picture next to each date, such as a wheel, a train or a balloon, because pictures help your memory. Then notice the pattern: transport stayed slow for thousands of years, and then changed very quickly in the last two hundred years, after the steam engine was invented.

### subtopic_3 — Transport Through Time
**Assistant:** How do food and transport fit together in one term?

**Tutor:** They fit together very well, because food has to travel. Very few people eat only food that they have grown themselves. Most food is grown on farms, often far from where people live, so it must be carried to them. In the past, farmers carried their produce to market on their heads, on pack oxen or in ox-wagons, which was slow, so most food was eaten close to where it was grown. When railways came, maize, wool and fruit could be sent across the country. Refrigerated trucks, ships and aeroplanes now carry fresh fruit, milk and meat over long distances without spoiling. That is how oranges from Limpopo can be sold in other countries, and how apples from Ceres reach shops in Gauteng. So, when you answer a test question about farming, think about how the products get to people, and when you answer a question about transport, think about what it carries.
