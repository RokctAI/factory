# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers early transport on water: rafts, canoes and reed boats, and some of the first sailing ships. It explains how people first travelled on water, how they made simple boats from the materials around them, and how sails let people cross seas and oceans. The anchor is a museum in Mossel Bay, a town on the southern coast of the Western Cape. A Grade 4 class from George walks into a tall hall, and there, in front of them, stands a full-size copy of a small Portuguese sailing ship called a caravel. It was built to remember Bartolomeu Dias, whose ships landed at Mossel Bay in 1488. The learners climb on board and see how small and crowded it is. In another room, their guide, Ms Fortuin, shows them a photograph of a mokoro, a canoe carved from a single tree trunk, gliding through the Okavango Delta in Botswana. The big idea is that people first travelled on water using logs, reeds and wood, and that the sail let them use the wind to travel much further, to trade and to explore. Explaining rafts, canoes, reed boats and the first sailing ships is examinable.

## Subtopic: Rafts and Canoes

The very first water transport was probably a floating log. People found that wood floats, and they held onto logs to cross rivers.

Later, they tied several logs or thick poles together with rope or strong plants to make a raft. A raft is a flat platform that floats. People could sit or stand on it and carry goods. They moved it with poles pushed against the bottom of the river, or with paddles.

A canoe is a narrow, light boat. The first canoes were dugouts, made by hollowing out a single tree trunk. People used stone tools and fire to burn and scrape out the inside of the log. One of the oldest canoes ever found, in the Netherlands, is about ten thousand years old.

In the Okavango Delta in Botswana, people have used dugout canoes called mekoro for hundreds of years. One is called a mokoro. A poler stands at the back and pushes it through the shallow water and reeds with a long pole. Today, many mekoro are made from fibreglass instead of wood, so that fewer large trees are cut down, and tourists ride in them to see birds, hippos and elephants.

In other parts of the world, people made canoes from tree bark, or from animal skins stretched over a wooden frame.

The questions for this section are with you now: rafts, dugout canoes and the mokoro.

## Subtopic: Reed Boats

Where there were few large trees, people made boats from reeds. Reeds are tall, strong plants with hollow or spongy stems that grow in wetlands and along rivers and lakes. Because they are full of air, they float well.

People cut the reeds, let them dry, and tied them tightly into long bundles. Then they tied the bundles together into the shape of a boat, with pointed ends that curved upwards.

The ancient Egyptians, who lived along the Nile River thousands of years ago, made boats from papyrus, a kind of reed. They used them for fishing, hunting birds and carrying people and goods along the river. Paintings on the walls of their tombs show these boats.

Reed boats are still made and used today in some places. On Lake Titicaca, high in the mountains of South America, people still build boats, and even floating islands, from reeds. On Lake Chad, in central Africa, people have also used reed boats.

Reed boats were light and easy to make, but they slowly soaked up water, so they did not last very long and had to be rebuilt.

The questions for this section are with you now: what reed boats are, how they were made and where they were used.

## Subtopic: The First Sailing Ships

A sail is a large piece of cloth or woven mat that catches the wind and pushes a boat along. The ancient Egyptians were among the first people to use sails, more than five thousand years ago, on the Nile.

For more than a thousand years, traders from Arabia, Persia and India sailed to the east coast of Africa in wooden ships called dhows, with large triangular sails. They used seasonal winds, called monsoon winds, which blow one way for part of the year and the other way for the rest. Dhows carried glass beads, cloth and pottery, and took back gold and ivory. Gold from Mapungubwe, in what is now Limpopo, was traded through these coastal ports.

In the 1400s, Portuguese sailors explored the west coast of Africa in small, fast sailing ships called caravels. In 1488, Bartolomeu Dias sailed around the southern tip of Africa and landed at Mossel Bay. In 1497, Vasco da Gama sailed around the Cape and reached India the next year. In 1652, Jan van Riebeeck arrived at the Cape with three Dutch ships to set up a station for ships sailing to the East.

Sailing voyages took many months. Sailors faced storms, shipwrecks and illness from a lack of fresh food. Ships stopped at the Cape for fresh water, meat and vegetables, which is why the Dutch set up their station there. The Khoikhoi, who already lived at the Cape, traded cattle and sheep with the sailors.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking rafts and canoes are the same; a raft is flat, a canoe is hollow. Two: thinking reed boats sink at once; reeds are full of air and float. Three: thinking sailing ships have engines; they use the wind. Four: thinking Europeans were the first to sail to Africa; dhows traded there much earlier.

The questions for this section are with you now: sails, dhows, caravels and the first voyages around Africa.
