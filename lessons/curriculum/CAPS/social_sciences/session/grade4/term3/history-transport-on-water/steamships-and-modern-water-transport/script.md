## Subtopic: The First Steamships

For thousands of years, boats were moved by people with paddles or oars, or by the wind in their sails. When there was no wind, sailing ships could sit still for days. When the wind blew the wrong way, they could not sail where they wanted to.

After the steam engine was improved in the 1700s, inventors tried to use it in boats. The first steamboats were built in France, the United States and Britain in the late 1700s. In 1807, the American inventor Robert Fulton began the first successful steamboat service, on the Hudson River in New York.

Early steamboats had big paddle wheels on their sides or at the back. The steam engine turned the paddle wheels, which pushed the water and moved the boat forward. Later, ships used a propeller, a kind of spinning screw under the water at the back of the ship, which worked better on the open sea.

Ships also changed from wood to iron, and later to steel. In 1843, the SS Great Britain was launched. It was one of the first large ships made of iron and driven by a propeller, and it crossed the Atlantic Ocean.

Steamships burned coal. They needed to stop at harbours, called coaling stations, to load more coal. Many early steamships also kept their sails, in case the coal ran out. Workers called stokers shovelled coal into the ship's furnaces day and night, in hot, dark engine rooms deep inside the ship.

## Subtopic: Steamships and South Africa

Steamships changed South Africa's links with the world. Because they did not depend on the wind, they could keep to a timetable, and journeys became shorter and more reliable.

From the late 1800s, mail ships carried letters, passengers and goods between Britain and South Africa. Two companies, the Union Line and the Castle Line, joined together in 1900 to form the Union-Castle Line. For many years, its ships left Britain every week and reached Cape Town in about two weeks, then sailed on to Gqeberha, East London and Durban. In the days of sailing ships, the same journey could take two or three months.

Harbours in Cape Town, Durban and other towns were made bigger, with long walls called breakwaters to protect ships from the waves, and quays where ships could tie up to load and unload. Railways were built from the harbours to the mines and cities.

In 1869, the Suez Canal opened in Egypt. It is a long waterway joining the Mediterranean Sea and the Red Sea. Many ships sailing between Europe and Asia began to use it instead of sailing around the Cape.

Later, ships changed again. Diesel engines replaced steam engines, because they need less fuel and fewer workers. Today, few people travel between South Africa and Europe by ship, because aeroplanes are much faster, but ships still carry most of the goods.

## Subtopic: Water Transport Today

Today, most goods that South Africa sells to and buys from other countries travel by ship.

Container ships carry goods packed in large metal boxes called containers, all the same size, so they can be lifted by cranes straight onto ships, trucks or trains. The biggest container ships carry thousands of containers. Bulk carriers carry loose goods, such as coal, iron ore and grain. Tankers carry liquids, such as oil and fuel.

In South Africa, the biggest ports are Durban, Cape Town, Ngqura near Gqeberha, Richards Bay and Saldanha Bay. Ferries carry people and vehicles across short stretches of water, such as the ferry from Cape Town to Robben Island. Cruise ships carry tourists on holidays. Fishing boats and trawlers catch fish. Tugboats help big ships in harbours. The research ship SA Agulhas II carries scientists and supplies to Antarctica and to Marion Island.

Ships also cause problems. Oil spills from damaged ships can kill sea birds, fish and other sea life. In 2000, a ship called the Treasure sank near Cape Town and spilled oil, and thousands of volunteers helped to clean and save oiled African penguins.
