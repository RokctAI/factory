### subtopic_1 — Rafts and Canoes
**Assistant:** How did people make a dugout canoe with stone tools?

**Tutor:** Making a dugout canoe took a lot of time, skill and patience. First, people chose a tall, straight tree with a thick trunk and soft enough wood. They cut it down, which with stone axes could take days. Then they cut off the branches and shaped the outside of the log, making the ends narrower so it would move easily through the water. To hollow out the inside, they often used fire. They lit small, controlled fires on top of the log, let the wood burn and turn to charcoal, then scraped the burnt wood away with stone, shell or bone tools. They did this again and again, while keeping the sides wet with mud or water so they did not burn too. Little by little, the log became a boat. Later, when people had iron tools, the work became faster. In the Okavango, polers still carve mekoro with metal tools today.

### subtopic_2 — Reed Boats
**Assistant:** What is a monsoon wind?

**Tutor:** A monsoon wind is a strong wind that changes direction with the seasons. Over the Indian Ocean, for part of the year, the monsoon winds blow from the north-east, from Asia towards East Africa. For the other part of the year, they blow from the south-west, from Africa towards Asia. Sailors on dhows learnt this pattern very well, a long time ago. They would sail from Arabia or India to the east coast of Africa when the wind blew that way, trade in towns such as Kilwa, Mombasa and Sofala, and then wait for the wind to change direction before sailing home. This meant they had to stay in Africa for some months. During that time, many traders married local people, and the mixing of African, Arab and Persian ways of life helped to form the Swahili culture and language along the coast of East Africa, which is still alive today.

### subtopic_3 — The First Sailing Ships
**Assistant:** Why was the Cape called the Cape of Storms?

**Tutor:** When Bartolomeu Dias sailed around the southern tip of Africa in 1488, his ships were caught in strong storms. In fact, his ships were blown far to the south, out of sight of land, and when they turned back north, they found they had already passed the tip of Africa. On the way home, Dias saw the rocky point of land near Cape Town, and according to the old stories, he named it the Cape of Storms. The seas around the Cape can be very rough, with powerful winds and huge waves, and many ships have been wrecked along the South African coast. However, the king of Portugal is said to have renamed it the Cape of Good Hope, because it gave hope that a sea route to India could be found. Ten years later, Vasco da Gama proved this when he sailed around the Cape and reached India.
