# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the History topic Transport through time. It explains what transport is, how people moved themselves and their goods long ago, and how changes in transport have changed people's lives on land, on water and in the air. The anchor is Gogo Nomsa, a grandmother who lives in a village near Mthatha, in the Eastern Cape. Her grandson, Lwazi, is in Grade 4, and for a History project, his class asks older people how they travelled when they were young. Gogo Nomsa sits outside her house in the afternoon sun and remembers. When she was a girl, she walked about five kilometres to school every day, along a footpath over the hills. Her family used oxen to pull a sledge loaded with water drums and firewood. When her father went to work in Johannesburg, he travelled by train, and he came home only once a year. Today, Lwazi's uncle drives a minibus taxi, and his aunt once flew from East London to Cape Town in less than two hours. The big idea is that transport has changed a great deal over time, and that each change has changed the way people live, work and stay in touch. Explaining what transport is and how it has changed people's lives is examinable.

## Subtopic: What Is Transport?

Transport means moving people, animals or goods from one place to another. Goods are things that people buy, sell or use, such as food, water, wood, clothes and machines. A vehicle is anything used to carry people or goods, such as a cart, a bicycle, a car, a train, a ship or an aeroplane.

People use transport for many reasons: to get to school and work, to visit family, to go to the shops, the clinic or the hospital, to carry goods to sell, and to go on holiday.

There are three ways to travel. Transport on land includes walking, riding animals, carts, wagons, bicycles, cars, buses, taxis, trucks and trains. Transport on water includes rafts, canoes, boats, ferries and ships. Transport in the air includes balloons, aeroplanes and helicopters.

When historians study transport through time, they look for change, which means how things became different, and for continuity, which means what has stayed the same. For example, people still walk, just as they did thousands of years ago, but today many also travel by car and taxi.

To find out about transport long ago, historians use sources, just as you learnt in Term 1: old photographs, paintings, written records, objects in museums, and the stories of older people like Gogo Nomsa.

The questions for this section are with you now: what transport is, goods and vehicles, and the three ways to travel.

## Subtopic: Transport Long Ago

For most of history, people walked. They carried goods on their heads, on their backs or in their arms. In many parts of Africa, women and girls carried heavy water pots and bundles of wood on their heads, and many still do today.

Then people learnt to use animals. In different parts of the world, people tamed horses, donkeys, camels, oxen and other animals to carry loads and to pull sledges. A sledge is a flat frame, often made of wood, that is pulled along the ground without wheels.

One of the most important inventions in history was the wheel. The first wheels for carts were made about five and a half thousand years ago, in the area of the world that is now Iraq. With wheels, animals could pull carts and wagons with much heavier loads.

On water, people made rafts and canoes from logs and reeds. Later, they built boats with sails that used the wind.

For thousands of years, people could not travel in the air at all. The first flights by people in balloons happened only about 240 years ago, and the first aeroplane flew in 1903.

Because travel was slow, most people lived their whole lives close to where they were born. A long journey could take weeks or months.

The questions for this section are with you now: walking, animals, sledges, the wheel and early boats.

## Subtopic: How Transport Changed Lives

About two hundred years ago, the steam engine changed transport. Steam trains and steamships could travel faster than horses or sailing ships, and they could carry huge loads. Later came cars, buses and trucks with engines that burn petrol or diesel, and then aeroplanes.

These changes brought many good things. People could travel much further and faster. Goods could be moved cheaply, so trade grew. Food from farms could reach towns before it spoiled. New towns grew up along railway lines and at harbours. Sick people could reach hospitals quickly by ambulance, or even by air ambulance. Families living far apart could visit each other more easily.

But there were also hard changes. In South Africa, railways took many men far from their homes to work on the mines and in the cities, and they were separated from their families for many months, as Gogo Nomsa's father was. Faster transport also brought road accidents, traffic jams and air pollution from exhaust fumes.

Today, transport keeps changing. People can fly across the world in a day, order goods that arrive by truck, and travel by fast trains such as the Gautrain in Gauteng. In the coming weeks, you will look more closely at transport on land, on water and in the air.
