### subtopic_1 — The Bicycle
**Assistant:** What did a fireman do on a steam train?

**Tutor:** On a steam locomotive, the fireman had nothing to do with putting out fires. Instead, the fireman's job was to keep the fire burning. The fireman worked in the cab, the small room at the back of the locomotive, together with the driver. With a big shovel, the fireman threw coal into the firebox, the place where the fire burned. The fire had to be just right, not too low and not too high, so that the water in the boiler kept boiling and made enough steam. The fireman also checked the water level in the boiler and added water when it got low, because a boiler without enough water could be very dangerous. It was hot, hard, dirty work, especially on long journeys up steep hills, when a lot of coal was needed. Many firemen, like Mkhulu Ngcobo, learnt all about the engine this way and later became drivers.

### subtopic_2 — Steam Engines and Trains
**Assistant:** Why did people call it a penny-farthing?

**Tutor:** The name comes from two old British coins. The penny was a large coin, and the farthing was a much smaller coin, worth a quarter of a penny. When people looked at this bicycle from the side, the huge front wheel and the tiny back wheel looked like a penny next to a farthing, so they gave it that funny name. On a penny-farthing, the pedals were fixed straight onto the big front wheel, with no chain. Each turn of the pedals turned the wheel once, so a bigger wheel meant the bicycle went further and faster. Some front wheels were nearly as tall as an adult. But riding one was hard. You had to climb up high to get onto the seat, and if the front wheel hit a stone, the rider could fly over the handlebars. That is why the new bicycle with two equal wheels and a chain was called the safety bicycle.

### subtopic_3 — The Motor Car
**Assistant:** How did the first car in South Africa get here?

**Tutor:** The first car in South Africa was a Benz, made in Germany. It came by ship from Europe and was taken to Pretoria, which was then the capital of the South African Republic, also called the Transvaal. In January 1897, it was shown to the public in Pretoria, and President Paul Kruger came to see it. Many people had never seen a vehicle that moved without being pulled by animals, so crowds came to watch. The car was slow by today's standards, about as fast as a horse trotting, and it was noisy and smelly. At first, there were very few cars, and they often broke down on the rough dirt roads. But within a few years, more cars arrived, garages opened to sell petrol and fix engines, and people began to build better roads. Today, there are millions of cars in South Africa, and some are even made in factories here.
