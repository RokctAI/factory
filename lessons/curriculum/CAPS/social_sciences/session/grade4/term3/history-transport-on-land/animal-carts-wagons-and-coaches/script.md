# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers transport on land using animals: carts, wagons and coaches. It explains how people in southern Africa used animals for transport, how the ox-wagon was used, and how horse-drawn coaches carried passengers and post. The anchor is a visit to a transport museum in Johannesburg. A Grade 4 class from Soweto walks into a large hall and stops in front of an ox-wagon. It is almost as long as a classroom is wide, with huge wooden wheels taller than the learners, a long wooden pole at the front called a disselboom, and a curved canvas cover on top. Next to it stands a small, two-wheeled Cape cart and a tall, heavy coach painted red, with leather straps and seats for passengers on the roof. A museum guide, Mr Mahlangu, shows them a picture of sixteen oxen pulling a wagon across a river. The big idea is that for hundreds of years before engines, animals pulled people and goods across southern Africa, slowly but surely, and that this changed when trains and motor vehicles arrived. Explaining how people used carts, wagons and coaches is examinable.

## Subtopic: Animals at Work

Long before there were wheels in southern Africa, people used animals to help them carry things. The Khoikhoi, who herded cattle and sheep in the western parts of southern Africa, rode on oxen and used them as pack animals. Pack animals carry loads tied onto their backs. When the Khoikhoi moved to find fresh grazing, they packed their mat houses, pots and belongings onto the backs of their oxen.

Many African farming communities kept large herds of cattle. People in some communities used oxen to pull sledges loaded with firewood, crops or water. A sledge is a flat frame of wood that is dragged along the ground. Sledges are still used in some rural areas today.

Wheeled vehicles, such as carts and wagons, were brought to southern Africa by European settlers after 1652, when the Dutch East India Company set up a station at the Cape.

The animals used to pull vehicles were oxen, horses, donkeys and mules. A mule is the young of a horse and a donkey. Oxen are very strong and can pull heavy loads, but they are slow. Horses are faster, but they need more care and good food. Donkeys are small, tough and patient, and need little water.

A team of animals pulling a wagon is called a span. The animals are joined to the wagon by a wooden bar across their necks, called a yoke, and by chains or leather straps.

The questions for this section are with you now: pack animals, sledges and the animals that pulled vehicles.

## Subtopic: The Ox-Wagon

The ox-wagon was the most important vehicle in South Africa for about two hundred years. It had four big wooden wheels with iron rims, and it was pulled by a span of about twelve to sixteen oxen. A driver walked beside the oxen with a very long whip, and a young person, called a leader, walked in front of the first pair to guide them.

Ox-wagons were slow. They travelled at about the speed a person walks, and they usually covered only about twenty kilometres in a day. The oxen needed to rest, graze and drink often.

Many different people used ox-wagons. Farmers took their produce to market. Traders and hunters travelled into the interior. Missionaries used them to reach far-away places. In the 1830s, Dutch-speaking farmers from the Cape, known as Voortrekkers, travelled north-east by ox-wagon on what became known as the Great Trek. They moved into lands where African communities, such as the amaZulu, the Basotho and the amaNdebele, already lived, and this led to conflicts over land.

After diamonds were found near Kimberley in the late 1860s and gold on the Witwatersrand in 1886, transport riders used ox-wagons to carry food, tools and machines to the mines. Many of them were African and Coloured drivers and leaders who knew the roads and the animals well.

A covered wagon was also a home on wheels. Families slept, cooked and kept their belongings in and around it.

The questions for this section are with you now: the ox-wagon, who used it and why.

## Subtopic: Carts and Coaches

A cart is a smaller vehicle, usually with two wheels, pulled by one or two horses, donkeys or oxen. The Cape cart, pulled by horses, was used by farmers and townspeople to travel to town or to church. Donkey carts were used to carry goods and people, and they are still used in some rural areas today, for example in the Northern Cape and the Eastern Cape.

A coach is a large, closed vehicle for passengers, pulled by a team of horses or mules. In the late 1800s, coach companies carried passengers and post between towns such as Kimberley, Pretoria and Johannesburg. Along the way, there were stations where tired horses or mules were swapped for fresh ones. Coach journeys were faster than wagons, but they were bumpy, dusty and uncomfortable.

Animal transport had problems. Animals needed food, water and rest, and they could die of diseases. In 1896 and 1897, a terrible cattle disease called rinderpest killed most of the cattle in southern Africa. Many families lost their herds, and transport riders lost their oxen.

When railways reached the towns and mines, wagons and coaches were slowly replaced. But animal transport also had good sides: animals need no petrol, they cause no air pollution, and they can go where there are no roads at all.

Three: thinking coaches carried mainly goods; they carried passengers and post. Four: thinking animal transport has disappeared; donkey carts are still used.
