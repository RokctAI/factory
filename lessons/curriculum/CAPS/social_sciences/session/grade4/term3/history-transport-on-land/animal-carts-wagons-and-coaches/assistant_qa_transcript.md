### subtopic_1 — Animals at Work
**Assistant:** What does inspan and outspan mean?

**Tutor:** These are old South African words that come from the days of ox-wagons. To inspan means to join the oxen to the wagon, by putting the yokes on their necks and fastening the chains. To outspan means to unhitch the oxen, so they can rest, graze and drink. Ox-wagon journeys had a regular rhythm. The drivers usually inspanned very early in the morning, when it was cool, travelled for a few hours, then outspanned in the heat of the middle of the day, and travelled again in the late afternoon or evening. The places where wagons regularly stopped to rest were called outspans. They were usually near water and good grazing. Some outspans grew into small villages and later into towns. You may still find streets or places in South Africa named Outspan, and the word span is still used today for a team of oxen, or even for a team of people working together.

### subtopic_2 — The Ox-Wagon
**Assistant:** Why did transport riders lose their oxen in 1896?

**Tutor:** In 1896, a disease called rinderpest swept down from the north into southern Africa. Rinderpest was a very dangerous disease that attacked cattle, and also wild animals such as buffalo and some antelope. It spread quickly from herd to herd, and most animals that caught it died within days. In 1896 and 1897, it killed most of the cattle in southern Africa. This was a disaster for many people. African farming families, whose wealth, food and culture depended on their cattle, lost almost everything. Transport riders, who needed spans of oxen to pull their wagons, could no longer work, and the cost of moving goods rose sharply. Governments tried to stop the disease with fences, by shooting sick animals and later by vaccinating cattle. Because so many oxen died, more goods had to be carried by rail. Rinderpest has since been wiped out completely around the world.

### subtopic_3 — Carts and Coaches
**Assistant:** What was it like to travel by coach?

**Tutor:** Travelling by coach in the 1800s was fast for those days, but it was not comfortable. The coach was pulled by a team of about eight to twelve horses or mules, and the passengers sat squashed together inside, with more sitting on the roof. The roads were mostly dirt tracks, full of stones, holes and dry riverbeds, so the coach bumped, swayed and rattled all day long. In the dry season, dust covered everyone. In the rainy season, rivers flooded and the coach sometimes had to wait for the water to go down, or it got stuck in the mud. At each station along the way, the tired animals were swapped for fresh ones, and passengers could stretch their legs and eat. A journey from Kimberley to Johannesburg took a few days. Coaches also carried mailbags, so they were an important part of the postal service between towns.
