# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers three inventions that changed transport on land: the bicycle, the steam engine and the train, and the motor car. It explains how each one developed and how it changed people's lives, in the world and in South Africa. The anchor is Thabo, a Grade 4 learner in Durban, who rides his bicycle to school every morning. One Sunday, he visits his grandfather, Mkhulu Ngcobo, who worked on the railways for forty years. When he was young, Mkhulu Ngcobo was a fireman on a steam locomotive, shovelling coal into the fire, and later he became a train driver. He shows Thabo an old photograph of a huge black steam engine, with smoke pouring from its chimney, and a brass whistle he kept from his last steam train. Then Thabo's uncle drives them to the beach in his car. In one day, Thabo uses three kinds of transport that did not exist a little over two hundred years ago. The big idea is that the bicycle, the train and the car each made travel on land faster and easier, and each changed how people live and work. ## Subtopic: The Bicycle

The first bicycle was made in 1817 by a German inventor, Karl Drais. It was made of wood, with two wheels, and it had no pedals. The rider sat on it and pushed along the ground with their feet, like a balance bike that small children use today. It was called a running machine.

In the 1860s, inventors in France added pedals to the front wheel. These bicycles had wooden or iron wheels and gave such a rough ride on bumpy roads that people called them boneshakers.

In the 1870s came the penny-farthing, with a huge front wheel and a tiny back wheel. The bigger the front wheel, the further the bicycle went with each turn of the pedals. But the rider sat very high, and falls were dangerous.

In 1885, an English inventor, John Kemp Starley, made the safety bicycle. Its two wheels were about the same size, and the pedals turned a chain that drove the back wheel. This is the shape of most bicycles today. A few years later, in 1888, John Boyd Dunlop made air-filled rubber tyres, which made riding much more comfortable.

Bicycles were cheap, needed no food or fuel, and let ordinary people travel further. In South Africa, they were used by messengers, workers and police. Today, millions of people cycle for transport, sport and fun, and the Cape Town Cycle Tour is one of the largest timed cycle races in the world.

## Subtopic: Steam Engines and Trains

A steam engine burns coal or wood to heat water in a big tank called a boiler. The water boils and turns into steam. The steam pushes against parts called pistons, which move back and forth and turn the wheels. In the 1760s and 1770s, a Scottish engineer, James Watt, made the steam engine much better, and it was soon used in mines and factories.

In 1804, Richard Trevithick built the first steam locomotive to run on rails. A locomotive is the engine that pulls a train. In 1825, the first public railway to use steam locomotives opened in England, between Stockton and Darlington, and George Stephenson built its locomotive. In 1829, his famous locomotive, the Rocket, won a competition for the fastest and most reliable engine.

The first railway in South Africa opened in Durban on 26 June 1860. It was only about three kilometres long, from the town to the harbour at the Point. Soon after, railways were built from Cape Town. After diamonds and gold were found, railways were built to the mines: the line from the Cape reached Kimberley in 1885 and Johannesburg in 1892.

Trains could carry huge loads of goods and thousands of people quickly and cheaply. Towns grew up along the railway lines. But trains also carried many migrant workers far from their homes to the mines.

The questions for this section are with you now: the steam engine, the first railways and trains in South Africa.

## Subtopic: The Motor Car

In 1886, a German engineer, Karl Benz, made a motor car with an engine that burned petrol. It had three wheels and could go only about as fast as a person can run. In 1888, his wife, Bertha Benz, drove it more than one hundred kilometres to visit her mother, to show people that the car really worked.

At first, cars were made by hand and were very expensive, so only rich people could buy them. In 1908, the American Henry Ford began making the Model T, and from 1913, his factory used an assembly line, where each worker added one part as the car moved past. Cars could be made faster and more cheaply, and many more people could afford one.

The first car in South Africa was shown in Pretoria in January 1897, and President Paul Kruger came to see it. Soon, there were cars, buses and trucks on South African roads, and roads were tarred.

Later, steam trains were replaced by diesel and electric trains, which are cleaner and easier to run. South Africa used steam trains until the 1990s, and a few still run for tourists today.
