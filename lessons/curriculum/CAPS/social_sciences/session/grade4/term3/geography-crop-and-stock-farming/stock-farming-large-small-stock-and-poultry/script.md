# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers stock farming in South Africa. It explains the three groups of farm animals, which are large stock, small stock and poultry, what each group gives people, and how farmers care for their animals. The anchor is Mrs Khumalo's farm in the KwaZulu-Natal Midlands, an area of rolling green hills between Pietermaritzburg and the Drakensberg mountains, where good summer rain makes the grass grow thick. Early every morning, her black-and-white dairy cows walk into the milking shed. On the hills, a herd of Nguni cattle grazes, their hides patterned in brown, black and white. Near the house there are goats, a few sheep, a pig pen and a large shed full of chickens. A Grade 4 class from Pietermaritzburg visits for the day, and Mrs Khumalo's son, Sipho, who studied to become a vet, shows them around. The big idea is that stock farmers keep animals for meat, milk, eggs, wool and skins, and that animals need food, water, shelter and care every day. Explaining stock farming and the three groups of farm animals is examinable.

## Subtopic: Large Stock

Stock means farm animals. Stock farming means keeping animals to use or to sell. Farm animals are also called livestock.

Large stock are big farm animals, and in South Africa, this mainly means cattle.

Some cattle are kept for meat, called beef. They are called beef cattle. Many beef cattle are fattened in feedlots, where they eat specially mixed food before they are sold. The Nguni is a hardy South African breed that can live on poor grass in hot, dry places and resist many diseases. The Bonsmara is another breed that was developed in South Africa.

Some cattle are kept for milk. They are called dairy cattle. The black-and-white Holstein cows on Mrs Khumalo's farm give a lot of milk. Dairy cows are milked twice a day, usually by milking machines. The milk is cooled in a big tank and collected by a tanker every day or two. It is made into cheese, yoghurt, butter and the milk you drink.

Cattle also give us leather, from their hides, to make shoes, belts and bags. Their dung is used as manure on fields, and in some rural areas, it is dried and used as fuel or to plaster floors.

For many families in rural South Africa, cattle are also very important for culture. They are used in ceremonies and for lobola, and a large herd shows wealth and respect.

The questions for this section are with you now: large stock, beef cattle and dairy cattle.

## Subtopic: Small Stock and Poultry

Small stock are smaller farm animals: sheep, goats and pigs.

Sheep are kept for meat, called mutton or lamb, and for wool, which is used to make jerseys, blankets and carpets. Sheep are shorn once or twice a year, which means their wool is cut off with clippers. It does not hurt them, just like a haircut does not hurt you.

Goats are kept for meat and milk. They can eat leaves from bushes as well as grass, so they live well in dry areas. In many rural homes, goats are also used in ceremonies. Angora goats give a soft, shiny hair called mohair. South Africa produces about half of all the mohair in the world, mainly in the Eastern Cape.

Pigs are kept for pork, bacon, ham and sausages. They eat grain and other food and grow quickly. Pigs cannot sweat, so in hot weather they need shade and often roll in mud to keep cool.

The Dorper is a sheep breed that was developed in South Africa for meat. It does well in dry areas such as the Karoo and the Northern Cape. Merino sheep are kept mainly for their fine wool.

Poultry are farm birds. Chickens are the most common. Some are kept for their meat, and some, called layers, are kept to lay eggs. On large poultry farms, thousands of chickens live in big sheds. Free-range chickens can walk around outside during the day. Ducks, geese and turkeys are poultry too. Ostriches are farmed near Oudtshoorn in the Western Cape for meat, leather and feathers.

The questions for this section are with you now: small stock, poultry and their products.

## Subtopic: Caring for Farm Animals

Farm animals need care every day. They need food, which means grazing on grass or eating feed such as maize and hay. They need clean water to drink every day. They need shelter from cold, rain and very hot sun.

Animals also get sick. Ticks are small creatures that suck blood and spread disease. Farmers dip or spray their cattle to kill ticks. Animals are vaccinated against diseases, and a vet is called when an animal is sick. Sipho explains that healthy animals give more milk, meat and wool.

Farmers must also protect their animals. They build fences and kraals. A kraal is an enclosure where animals are kept safe at night. Farmers guard against stock theft, and some use dogs to protect sheep and goats from jackals and other predators.

Farmers must not keep too many animals on a piece of land. If they do, the animals eat all the grass and the soil is left bare. This is called overgrazing. Rain then washes the soil away, and the land is damaged. Good farmers move their animals from one camp to another so the grass can grow back.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: thinking pigs are large stock; pigs are small stock. Two: thinking poultry means only chickens; ducks, geese, turkeys and ostriches count too. Three: thinking shearing hurts sheep; it is like a haircut. Four: thinking more animals always means more money; too many cause overgrazing.

The questions for this section are with you now: how farmers care for their animals and overgrazing.
