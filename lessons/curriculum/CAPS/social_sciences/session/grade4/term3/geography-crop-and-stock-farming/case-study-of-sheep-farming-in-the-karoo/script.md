# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a case study of stock farming in South Africa: sheep farming in the Karoo. It explains what the Karoo is like, how farmers keep sheep in such a dry place, and what happens to the wool and meat. The anchor is Mr and Mrs Jacobs's farm near Graaff-Reinet, in the Eastern Cape part of the Great Karoo. The land is wide and flat, with low bushes, a few thorn trees along dry riverbeds and flat-topped hills on the horizon. A metal windmill turns in the wind next to a round dam, and white-faced sheep stand in its shade. A Grade 4 class from Gqeberha arrives by bus at shearing time and meets Mr Jacobs, his wife, who keeps the farm's records, and Mr Plaatjies, the farm manager, who has worked on the farm for more than thirty years. The big idea is that farmers have adapted to the dry Karoo by keeping sheep on very large farms, by pumping water from underground and by caring for the veld. ## Subtopic: The Karoo and Its Sheep

The Karoo is a huge, dry area in the middle and west of South Africa. It stretches across parts of the Western Cape, the Northern Cape, the Eastern Cape and a little of the Free State. It is a semi-desert, which means it is very dry, but not as dry as a true desert. The name Karoo comes from a Khoekhoe word that is usually said to mean dry or thirsty land.

Very little rain falls in the Karoo, and some years there is almost none. The summers are very hot, and the winters are cold, with frost at night and sometimes snow on the mountains.

Because it is so dry, there is not much grass. Instead, the veld is covered with small, tough bushes called Karoo bushes, many of them with small leaves that help them save water. Sheep and goats can eat these bushes, while cattle need more grass. So the Karoo is good for sheep, but not for growing crops, except where there is water for irrigation.

Two main kinds of sheep are farmed here. Merino sheep are kept mainly for their fine, soft wool. Dorper sheep, a South African breed, are kept for meat. Karoo lamb is famous across the country, and people say it tastes of the herbs and bushes the sheep eat.

## Subtopic: Farming in a Dry Place

Each bush gives a sheep only a little food, so a sheep needs a large area of veld to find enough to eat. That is why Karoo farms are very large, often thousands of hectares. Mr Jacobs's farm is so big that it takes more than an hour to drive across it on the farm roads.

Water is the biggest challenge. There are very few rivers that flow all year. Farmers drill boreholes, which are deep, narrow holes that reach water under the ground. Windmills use the wind to pump the water up. The water is stored in round dams called reservoirs, and it flows through pipes to troughs where the sheep drink. Today, some farmers also use solar pumps.

The farm is divided by fences into large areas called camps. Mr Plaatjies moves the sheep from one camp to another, so that the bushes in the empty camps have time to grow back. This helps to prevent overgrazing, which would leave the soil bare.

Sheep farmers also face predators. Black-backed jackals and caracals, which are wild cats, attack sheep, especially young lambs. Farmers use strong fences, guard dogs and collars to protect their flocks. Drought is another danger: in years without rain, the bushes dry up and farmers must buy expensive feed or sell some of their sheep.

## Subtopic: From Farm to Market

Merino sheep are usually shorn once a year. A team of shearers travels from farm to farm. In the shearing shed, each shearer holds a sheep and cuts off the wool, called the fleece, in one big piece. A good shearer can shear a sheep in a few minutes. Shearing does not hurt the sheep, and the wool grows back in time for the next year. Newly shorn sheep are kept sheltered from cold rain and wind for a few days.

The fleeces are thrown onto a table, where workers remove dirty bits and sort the wool by quality. Then the wool is pressed into large, heavy bags called bales. Trucks take the bales to Gqeberha, which is the centre of the wool trade in South Africa. There, the wool is sold to buyers, and most of it is exported to other countries, especially China, to be made into cloth for clothes and blankets.

Dorper lambs are sold at auctions in nearby towns, or taken by truck to an abattoir, where they are slaughtered for meat. The meat is sent to butcheries and supermarkets.

Sheep farming creates jobs for farm workers, shearers, truck drivers, wool sorters and factory workers. Small towns in the Karoo, such as Graaff-Reinet, Middelburg and Beaufort West, grew up as places where farmers came to sell their animals and wool, buy supplies, go to church and send their children to school.

Some Karoo farmers also keep Angora goats for mohair, especially around Graaff-Reinet and Jansenville.

Three: thinking sheep drink from rivers; most water comes from boreholes. Four: thinking all wool stays in South Africa; most is exported.
