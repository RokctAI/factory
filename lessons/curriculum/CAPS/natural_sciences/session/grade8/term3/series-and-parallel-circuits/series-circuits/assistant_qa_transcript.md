### subtopic_1 — One Pathway for the Current
**Assistant:** If the current is the same everywhere in a series circuit, what is different before and after a bulb?

**Tutor:** The energy carried by each charge is different. The current tells you how many charges pass a point each second, and that number is the same on both sides of the bulb. But each charge arrives at the bulb carrying energy it was given by the cell, and leaves the bulb having transferred some of that energy as light and heat. So the charges leaving the bulb carry less energy per charge than the charges entering it. Scientists measure the energy transferred per unit of charge as a potential difference, or voltage, in volts. A voltmeter connected across a bulb, with one lead on each side of it, measures how much energy each coulomb of charge transfers in that bulb. If you connect a voltmeter across a single bulb attached to a 3 volt battery, it reads about 3 volts, showing that almost all of the energy given to the charges by the battery is transferred in the bulb. With two identical bulbs in series, a voltmeter across each one reads about 1.5 volts: each bulb transfers half of the energy. The voltages across the components in a series circuit add up to the voltage of the battery, which is another way of saying that energy is conserved. Think of a ski lift and a series of slopes. The lift carries skiers up to the top, giving them energy; then they ski down one slope after another. The number of skiers passing any point on the route each minute is the same everywhere, like the current, but their height, and so their energy, drops on each slope, like the voltage across each bulb. You will measure voltages in series circuits in detail in Grade 9.

### subtopic_2 — Adding Resistors in Series
**Assistant:** Why do two identical bulbs in series glow equally, instead of the first one being brighter?

**Tutor:** Because the same current flows through both of them, and they have the same resistance, so they transfer energy at exactly the same rate. Many people expect the first bulb, the one nearest the positive terminal, to be brighter, imagining that the charges arrive full of energy, give most of it to the first bulb they meet, and have little left for the second. But that is not how a circuit works. As soon as the circuit is closed, the push from the cell is set up throughout the whole loop almost instantly, and the charges everywhere start drifting at the same rate. The energy transferred in each bulb depends on the current through it and its resistance. In a series circuit, the current through every component is the same, and if the bulbs are identical, their resistances are the same, so each one transfers the same amount of energy per second and glows with the same brightness. The order of the bulbs makes no difference: swapping them, or putting the cell between them, changes nothing. If the bulbs are different, for example one with a higher resistance, the bulb with the higher resistance glows more brightly, because although the current is the same, more energy is transferred where the resistance is greater. That is why in a series string of a cheap bulb and a bright bulb, the result can be surprising: a bulb designed for a high voltage, which has a higher resistance, may glow while a bulb designed for a low voltage, with a lower resistance, barely glows. The rule is always the same: same current through each, and the energy transferred depends on each one's resistance.

### subtopic_3 — Cells in Series
**Assistant:** Why does a bulb burn out if you connect it to too many cells in series?

**Tutor:** Because more cells give a bigger push, which drives a larger current through the filament, and the heating in the filament rises steeply with the current until the tungsten melts or evaporates. A small torch bulb is designed for a particular voltage, say 2.5 volts, marked on its base. At that voltage, the current through the filament heats it to about 2 500 degrees Celsius, hot enough to glow brightly but safely below the melting point of tungsten, about 3 400 degrees. Connect the same bulb to six cells in series, 9 volts, and the push is more than three times as large. The current through the filament increases, and because heating depends on the square of the current, the filament gets much hotter, very quickly. It flashes brilliantly white for a moment and then the thinnest part of the filament melts and breaks, leaving a gap. The circuit is open and the bulb is dead. Even at its correct voltage, a filament slowly wears out: tungsten atoms gradually evaporate from the hot filament and settle on the inside of the glass, which is why old bulbs develop a dark film. The filament becomes thinner at its weakest point, which then gets hotter, so evaporation speeds up there until it breaks. Running a bulb above its rated voltage speeds this up dramatically, while running it slightly below its rating makes it last much longer, though dimmer. LEDs are even more sensitive. An LED connected directly to too many cells can be destroyed in an instant, which is why LEDs are almost always connected in series with a resistor that limits the current to a safe value, usually around twenty milliamperes.

### subtopic_4 — Advantages and Disadvantages of Series Circuits
**Assistant:** How can you find the broken bulb in a series string of lights quickly, without testing each one?

**Tutor:** By halving the search each time, a method called a binary search, and by using a simple tester to check sections of the string rather than single bulbs. Suppose a series string of sixty-four bulbs does not light. Testing bulbs one at a time from one end might mean testing up to sixty-four bulbs. Instead, unplug the string and use a continuity tester, a cell and a small bulb or buzzer with two probe wires, or a multimeter on its continuity setting. Touch one probe to the start of the string and the other to the wire between bulb thirty-two and bulb thirty-three. If the tester lights, the first half of the string is complete, and the break must be in the second half. If it does not light, the break is in the first half. Then test half of the faulty half, sixteen bulbs, and so on. Each test halves the number of bulbs in which the fault could be, so sixty-four bulbs need at most six tests: sixty-four to thirty-two, sixteen, eight, four, two and one. For a string of a thousand bulbs, only about ten tests would be needed. This halving strategy is used in many fields: electricians use it to find faults in long cables, programmers use it to find the line of code that causes an error, and it is how you might guess a number between one and a hundred in at most seven guesses. With the faulty bulb found, replacing it with a bulb of the same rating restores the whole circuit. Always unplug a mains string before testing it, because the mains voltage is dangerous. Modern LED strings with built-in shunts usually stay lit when one LED fails, which makes the hunt unnecessary.
