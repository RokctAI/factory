### subtopic_1 — Two or More Pathways
**Assistant:** How is a two-way staircase light switch wired so either switch can turn the light on or off?

**Tutor:** It uses two special switches, each of which can connect one common wire to either of two other wires, and two parallel wires running between them, so that the circuit is complete whenever both switches point to the same wire. An ordinary switch has two terminals and simply connects or disconnects them. A two-way switch has three terminals: a common terminal and two others, often labelled L1 and L2. Its lever always connects the common terminal to one of the other two, never to neither. At the bottom of the stairs, the live supply goes to the common terminal of the first switch. From that switch's L1 and L2 terminals, two wires, called strappers, run up the wall to the L1 and L2 terminals of the second switch at the top of the stairs. The common terminal of the second switch goes to the light, and from the light the circuit returns to the neutral. Now follow the possibilities. If both switches connect their common to L1, current can flow from the first switch along the L1 strapper to the second switch and on to the light, so the light is on. If both point to L2, current flows along the other strapper, and the light is also on. But if one switch points to L1 and the other to L2, the current reaches a strapper at one end that is not connected at the other end, so there is a gap and the light is off. Flicking either switch changes which strapper it uses, so it always toggles the light between on and off, whatever the other switch is doing. The two strappers are two parallel paths, only one of which is ever used at a time.

### subtopic_2 — Adding Resistors in Parallel
**Assistant:** How can adding a resistor make the total resistance smaller? Doesn't more resistance mean more resistance?

**Tutor:** It depends on how the resistor is added. In series, the charges must pass through every resistor in turn, so each one adds to the difficulty of the journey and the resistances add up. In parallel, the new resistor is not an extra obstacle on the existing path; it is a new, separate path alongside it. The charges now have a choice of routes, so more of them can get from one side of the circuit to the other each second. Think of the resistance of a path as how hard it is for charge to get across, and of its opposite, how easy it is, which scientists call conductance. Every branch you add in parallel adds its own ease of flow to the total ease of flow, so the total ease goes up and the total resistance comes down. Two identical branches let through twice as much current as one, which is the same as having half the resistance. Even adding a branch with a large resistance helps a little, because it still provides some extra path, so the total resistance of a parallel combination is always less than the smallest single resistance in it. A useful image is a hall emptying after assembly. With one narrow door, it takes a long time. Open a second door, even a narrower one, and the hall empties faster, because people can now leave by both. The narrow door has not made the first door any harder to use; it has added to the total ways out. The same reasoning explains why a thick wire has less resistance than a thin one: a thick wire is like many thin wires side by side in parallel.

### subtopic_3 — Current in the Branches and the Main Line
**Assistant:** Why does more current flow through the lower-resistance branch, if both branches have the same voltage?

**Tutor:** Because the voltage across both branches is the same push, and a branch with less resistance lets that same push drive more charge through each second. Each branch in a parallel circuit is connected between the same two junctions, so every charge going through any branch has the same amount of energy available to transfer between those two points. That shared voltage is what drives the current through each branch. In a branch with low resistance, the charges meet less opposition, so for the same push they flow faster, and more charge passes each second: a larger current. In a branch with high resistance, the same push drives a smaller current. The current through each branch is the voltage divided by that branch's resistance. For example, with 12 volts across two branches, one of 4 ohms and one of 12 ohms, the first carries 3 amperes and the second 1 ampere. The main current is 4 amperes. So the branch with one third of the resistance carries three times the current. A common mistake is to think that the current splits equally at every junction, or that it prefers the shortest path entirely. Neither is true: the current divides in inverse proportion to the resistances, with every branch getting some current unless its resistance is infinite, like an open switch. At the extreme, if one branch has almost no resistance, a short circuit, almost all the current rushes through it, the total current becomes enormous, and the other branches get almost nothing. That is why a short circuit in one appliance can trip the breaker for a whole household circuit, putting out all the lights on it, even though they are in parallel.

### subtopic_4 — Parallel Circuits at Home
**Assistant:** How do I work out whether a multi-plug is overloaded?

**Tutor:** Add up the currents that all the appliances plugged into it will draw, and compare the total with the current rating of the multi-plug and of the circuit it is plugged into. Every appliance has a power rating in watts, printed on a label on its base or back, or on its charger. In South Africa, the mains supply is about 230 volts, and the current an appliance draws is its power divided by the voltage. A 2 000 watt kettle draws about 2 000 divided by 230, roughly 8.7 amperes. A 2 000 watt heater draws the same. A 1 000 watt microwave draws about 4.3 amperes, a 1 200 watt iron about 5.2 amperes, a television about half an ampere and a phone charger only a small fraction of an ampere. Because they are connected in parallel, the currents add. A kettle and a heater on the same multi-plug already draw about 17 amperes, more than the sixteen amperes that a standard South African socket and most multi-plugs are rated for. Add a microwave and the total exceeds 21 amperes. The plug and the cable of the multi-plug would heat up, possibly enough to melt and catch fire, especially if the cable is coiled or under a carpet where the heat cannot escape. A good rule is to plug only one high-power heating appliance into a multi-plug at a time, to use multi-plugs with a built-in overload protector, never to plug one multi-plug into another, and to check the rating on the multi-plug itself. Low-power appliances such as phone chargers, lamps and laptops add very little current, so several of them together are usually safe. When in doubt, spread high-power appliances over different wall sockets on different circuits.
