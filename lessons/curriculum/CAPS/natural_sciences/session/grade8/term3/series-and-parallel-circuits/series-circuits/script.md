# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines series circuits, in which all the components are connected one after another in a single loop, so that the current has only one pathway. The anchor is an old string of festive fairy lights taken out of a cupboard in December. Plugged in, not a single bulb lights. Somewhere among the fifty little bulbs, one has a broken filament, and because they are all connected one after another, that single gap has stopped the current through every bulb. Finding it means testing the bulbs one by one. The governing idea is that in a series circuit there is only one path for the current, so the same current flows through every component; that adding more resistors or bulbs in series increases the total resistance and therefore decreases the current, so bulbs become dimmer; and that a break anywhere in a series circuit stops the current everywhere. The features of series circuits, the effect of adding resistors in series on the current, the effect of adding cells in series, the measurement of current at different points, and the advantages and disadvantages of series connections are examinable, and they lead to the calculation of series resistance and voltage division in Grade 9 and Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: One Pathway for the Current

A series circuit is a circuit in which the components are connected end to end, one after another, so that they form a single loop with only one path for the current. Starting at the positive terminal of the cell and following the wire, you pass through every component in turn before arriving back at the negative terminal, without ever having a choice of route.

The simplest series circuit is a cell, a switch and a bulb. Adding a second bulb into the same loop, so that the current must pass through the first bulb and then the second, gives two bulbs in series. In a circuit diagram, the components of a series circuit all lie on the same rectangle of wire with no branches and no junction dots.

Because there is only one path, every charge that leaves the cell passes through every component in turn. Charge cannot pile up anywhere or leak away, so the current is the same at every point in a series circuit. An ammeter placed before the first bulb, between the bulbs or after the second bulb shows the same reading. Each component transfers some of the energy carried by the charges, but the charges themselves, and therefore the current, continue unchanged around the loop.

Because there is only one path, a break anywhere in a series circuit stops the current everywhere. If one bulb in a series circuit is removed or its filament breaks, there is a gap in the only path, and all the other bulbs go out. A single switch anywhere in a series circuit controls all the components together.

Drawing the circuit diagram before building helps. Draw the cell, then follow the rectangle round, placing each symbol in turn on the same line. If you can trace from the positive terminal back to the negative terminal through every component without lifting your pencil and without ever reaching a fork, the circuit is a series circuit. If at some point the path splits into two or more routes that join again later, the circuit contains a parallel section, the subject of the next lesson.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of a series circuit, how to recognise one in a circuit diagram, why the current is the same at every point, and why one broken bulb makes all the bulbs go out.

## Subtopic: Adding Resistors in Series

Every bulb and every resistor has resistance. When components are connected in series, the charges must pass through all of them, one after another, so their resistances add up. The total resistance of resistors in series is the sum of their individual resistances. Two identical bulbs in series have twice the resistance of one bulb; three have three times the resistance.

For the same cell or battery, a greater total resistance means a smaller current. So adding resistors in series decreases the current in the circuit. This is a key result in the CAPS curriculum: adding resistors in series decreases the current.

The effect is easy to see with bulbs. Connect one bulb to two cells and note its brightness. Add a second identical bulb in series, and both bulbs glow equally but more dimly than the single bulb did, because the current through them is smaller. Add a third in series, and all three are dimmer still. An ammeter in the circuit confirms that the current decreases each time a bulb is added.

The bulbs share the energy supplied by the cell. With one bulb, all of the energy given to each charge by the cell is transferred in that bulb. With two identical bulbs in series, each transfers half, so each glows less brightly. The push of the cell, its voltage, is shared between the components in series; the details of this sharing are studied in Grade 9.

Some numbers make the pattern clear. Suppose a 3 volt battery is connected to bulbs that each have a resistance of 5 ohms, and suppose, as later grades show, that the current equals the voltage divided by the total resistance. With one bulb, the total resistance is 5 ohms and the current is 3 divided by 5, which is 0.6 amperes. With two bulbs in series, the total resistance is 10 ohms and the current falls to 0.3 amperes. With three bulbs in series, 15 ohms, the current is only 0.2 amperes. Each added bulb increases the total resistance by the same amount, and the current falls each time. Real bulbs change their resistance a little as they heat up, so measured values differ slightly, but the trend is always the same.

The same idea explains a dimmer made from a variable resistor in series with a bulb. Increasing the resistance of the variable resistor increases the total resistance of the series circuit, decreasing the current through the bulb, which dims.

A fair test of the effect of adding resistors in series can use one, two, three and four identical resistors or bulbs connected in series with the same battery and an ammeter, recording the current for each number while keeping the battery, wires and temperature the same. The current decreases as the number of resistors increases.

The questions for this section are with you now: why resistances in series add, the effect of adding resistors in series on the current, why bulbs in series are dimmer than a single bulb, how the energy is shared, and a fair test of the effect of adding resistors.

## Subtopic: Cells in Series

Cells can also be connected in series, positive terminal of one to negative terminal of the next, in a single row. Their voltages add. Two 1.5 volt cells in series give 3 volts; three give 4.5 volts; four give 6 volts. This is how torches, remote controls and toys get the voltage they need from small cells.

With more cells in series, each charge receives a bigger push and more energy as it passes through the battery, so for the same resistance a larger current flows. A bulb connected to two cells in series is brighter than the same bulb connected to one cell. With three cells, it may be brighter still, until the current is so large that the filament overheats and breaks. Bulbs and devices are designed for a particular voltage, which is why a 2.5 volt torch bulb connected to a 9 volt battery will burn out almost at once.

Adding cells and adding bulbs therefore have opposite effects on the current in a series circuit. More cells, more push, more current. More bulbs or resistors, more resistance, less current. Two cells with two bulbs give roughly the same brightness per bulb as one cell with one bulb, because both the push and the resistance have doubled.

Using more cells in series to drive a larger current also uses up the stored energy faster. A device that draws a large current from a set of cells drains them quickly, which is why a powerful torch with a bright bulb needs its cells replaced more often than a small torch with a dim bulb. Cells in a series row also all carry the same current, so they tend to go flat at about the same time; mixing old and new cells in the same device wastes the new ones and can cause the old ones to leak.

If one cell in a series row is turned the wrong way round, its push opposes the others. Two cells correct and one reversed give a total of only 1.5 volts instead of 4.5 volts, and the device may not work.

The questions for this section are with you now: how to connect cells in series, the total voltage of cells in series, the effect of adding cells on the current and brightness, why adding cells and adding bulbs have opposite effects, and what happens if a cell is reversed.

## Subtopic: Advantages and Disadvantages of Series Circuits

Series circuits have some useful features and some serious drawbacks.

Advantages: a series circuit is simple, using the least wire. A single switch controls everything in the loop. The current is the same through every component, which is exactly what is needed for safety devices. That is why fuses and switches are always connected in series with the devices they protect or control: a fuse in series with an appliance carries the full current of that appliance, and a switch in series with a lamp controls it. Ammeters must also be connected in series, to measure the current in a loop. Some devices deliberately place cells in series to reach a higher voltage.

Disadvantages: if one component fails, or is removed, the whole circuit stops, as the old fairy lights show. Finding the faulty component can be slow. Each extra bulb added in series makes all the bulbs dimmer, because the current decreases. Components in series cannot be switched on and off independently. These are the reasons why the lights and sockets in a house are not connected in series.

Older strings of festive lights were connected in series, with each bulb designed for a small share of the mains voltage. Many modern strings use LEDs and include special shunts, tiny bypass paths inside each bulb holder that close if that bulb fails, so that the rest stay lit.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the current gets smaller after each bulb in a series circuit; it is the same everywhere. Two: saying adding bulbs in series makes them brighter; it makes them dimmer. Three: saying a switch must be next to the bulb it controls; anywhere in the loop works. Four: confusing adding cells in series, which increases the current, with adding resistors in series, which decreases it. Five: saying the first bulb in a series circuit uses up the current and the last gets none; all carry the same current.

Layout for marks: state that there is one pathway, that the current is the same at all points, and then the effect of the change on the total resistance, the current and the brightness.

The questions for this section are with you now: the advantages and disadvantages of series circuits, why fuses and switches are connected in series, why houses are not wired in series, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a single-lane road, more bulbs and dimmer light, and one broken bulb on a string — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Single-Lane Road

Picture a single-lane road that loops round a hill and back to where it started, with no turn-offs at all. Every car that drives on it must pass every point on the road, one after another.

A series circuit is like that. All the parts are joined one after another in a single loop, so the current has only one path. The charges leave the cell, go through the first bulb, then the second, then back to the cell.

Because there is only one lane, the same number of cars pass every point each minute. In the same way, the current is the same everywhere in a series circuit: before the first bulb, between the bulbs, after the last.

And if there is a roadblock anywhere, the whole road stops. In a series circuit, if one bulb breaks or a wire comes loose, everything goes off.

Keep the single-lane road in mind and try the questions for this part: what a series circuit is, why the current is the same everywhere, and why one break stops everything.

## Subtopic: More Bulbs, Dimmer Light

Connect one bulb to two cells. It glows brightly. Add a second bulb in series. Now both glow, but dimmer. Add a third, and all three are dimmer still.

Why? Each bulb is like a speed bump on that single-lane road. More speed bumps in a row make the whole journey harder, so fewer cars get through each minute. More bulbs in series means more resistance, so less current, so dimmer bulbs. The rule is: adding resistors in series decreases the current.

Now add cells instead. Two cells in a row give twice the push of one. More push means more current, so a brighter bulb. One and a half volts per cell: two cells give three volts, four cells give six.

So more cells, brighter. More bulbs in series, dimmer.

Hold the dimmer bulbs in mind and try this part's questions: what adding bulbs in series does to the current, and what adding cells does.

## Subtopic: One Broken Bulb on the String

You take out an old string of festive lights and plug it in. Nothing. Not one bulb lights. One tiny bulb somewhere has a broken filament, and because all the bulbs are in series, that one gap stops the current through all of them. Now you have to test fifty bulbs to find the bad one.

That is the big problem with series circuits: one fault, and everything goes off. And you cannot switch one bulb off on its own.

But series has its uses. A switch in series with a lamp controls it. A fuse in series with an appliance carries all its current, so it can melt and protect it. And an ammeter goes in series so all the current passes through it.

Pocket summary of the lesson. In a series circuit, components are joined in one loop with one path for the current. The current is the same everywhere. Adding resistors or bulbs in series increases the total resistance and decreases the current, so bulbs get dimmer. Adding cells in series increases the voltage and the current. A break anywhere stops the whole circuit. Switches, fuses and ammeters are connected in series.

Hold the road, the dimming bulbs and the festive lights, and take the final questions of the lesson: what happens when one bulb breaks, what happens when you add a bulb, and why fuses go in series.
