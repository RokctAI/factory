# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines parallel circuits, in which the current divides into two or more separate pathways, each containing its own components. The anchor is the kitchen of an ordinary home in the evening. The light is on, the fridge is humming, the kettle is boiling and a cellphone is charging. Switch the kettle off and the light stays on; unplug the phone and the fridge carries on. Every appliance works at full strength, and each can be switched on and off independently. That is only possible because the circuits in a house are connected in parallel. The governing idea is that in a parallel circuit the current has more than one path; each branch is connected directly across the energy source, so each component works independently and receives the full voltage; the current from the source divides between the branches and rejoins afterwards; and adding resistors in parallel provides extra pathways, decreasing the total resistance and increasing the total current drawn from the source. The features of parallel circuits, the effect of adding resistors in parallel, current in the branches and the main line, and the advantages and disadvantages of parallel connections, including house wiring, are examinable, and they lead to calculations of parallel resistance in Grade 9 and Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: Two or More Pathways

A parallel circuit is a circuit in which the components are connected in separate branches, so that the current has two or more paths to follow. The point where the current splits into the branches, and the point where the branches join again, are called junctions. In a circuit diagram, a parallel circuit looks like a ladder: the cell sits on one side, and two or more rungs connect across between the top wire and the bottom wire, each rung holding a component. Junctions are marked with solid dots.

Starting at the positive terminal of the cell and following the wire, you reach a junction where you have a choice of routes. Some charges go through the first branch and some through the second, and they meet again at the junction on the other side before returning to the negative terminal of the cell.

Each branch of a parallel circuit is connected directly across the terminals of the energy source. That has two important consequences. First, each branch gets the full push of the source; a bulb in a parallel branch has the same voltage across it as if it were the only component connected to the cell. Second, each branch works independently of the others. If one bulb in a parallel circuit breaks or is removed, there is still a complete path through the other branches, so the other bulbs stay lit.

Switches can be placed in individual branches, so each component can be turned on and off without affecting the others. A switch in the main line, between the cell and the first junction, controls all the branches together.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of a parallel circuit, how to recognise one in a circuit diagram, what junctions are, why each branch receives the full voltage, why one broken bulb does not affect the others, and where to place a switch to control one branch or all of them.

## Subtopic: Adding Resistors in Parallel

Adding a resistor in parallel means adding a new branch, a new pathway for the current. This has the opposite effect to adding a resistor in series.

When a second resistor is added in parallel, the charges now have two paths instead of one, so it is easier for charge to flow from one side of the circuit to the other. The total resistance of the circuit decreases. For two identical resistors in parallel, the total resistance is half the resistance of one; for three identical resistors, it is a third. The total resistance of resistors in parallel is always less than the smallest individual resistance.

Because the total resistance decreases, the total current drawn from the source increases. This is the key CAPS result: adding resistors in parallel decreases the total resistance and increases the overall current in the circuit.

The idea is like opening extra checkout tills at a busy supermarket. With one till open, a long queue moves slowly. Open a second till and twice as many shoppers get through per minute, even though each till works at the same speed. The extra till does not slow the first one down; it adds a new path.

This can be seen with bulbs. Connect one bulb to two cells, and note its brightness and the current on an ammeter in the main line. Add a second identical bulb in parallel. Both bulbs glow just as brightly as the single bulb did, because each receives the full voltage of the cells. But the ammeter in the main line now reads about twice as much, because each branch draws its own current. Add a third bulb in parallel and all three glow equally brightly, and the main current is about three times the original.

A worked example with numbers: a 3 volt battery connected to one bulb of 6 ohms gives a current of 0.5 amperes. Add a second 6 ohm bulb in parallel, and each bulb still carries 0.5 amperes, so the total current from the battery is 1.0 ampere, and the total resistance of the circuit is 3 ohms, half of 6.

The questions for this section are with you now: what adding a resistor in parallel means, why it decreases the total resistance, why it increases the total current, the brightness of bulbs added in parallel, and a numerical example.

## Subtopic: Current in the Branches and the Main Line

The current from the source divides at the first junction and the branch currents rejoin at the second junction. Because charge cannot pile up or disappear, the total current entering a junction equals the total current leaving it. So the current in the main line, between the cell and the first junction, equals the sum of the currents in all the branches.

If the branches are identical, the current divides equally. Two identical bulbs in parallel each carry half of the main current; three identical bulbs each carry a third.

If the branches have different resistances, more current flows through the branch with the lower resistance, because it is the easier path, and less through the branch with the higher resistance. For example, a branch containing a kettle element with a low resistance carries a much larger current than a branch containing a lamp with a higher resistance, even though both have the same voltage across them. That is why a kettle draws about nine amperes from the mains and a lamp only a fraction of an ampere.

To measure the currents, an ammeter can be placed in the main line, then in each branch in turn. The branch readings added together equal the main line reading. For example, with a main current of 0.9 amperes and two branches, if one branch carries 0.6 amperes, the other must carry 0.3 amperes.

Because the total current from the source increases each time a branch is added, the source works harder and supplies energy faster. A battery powering several bulbs in parallel goes flat sooner than it would powering one bulb, even though each bulb is as bright as a single bulb would be.

Real cells are not perfect. Every cell has a small resistance of its own, called internal resistance, inside it. When a large total current is drawn, some of the cell's push is used up inside the cell itself, so the voltage across the branches falls slightly. That is why, with ordinary torch cells, bulbs added in parallel may glow very slightly dimmer than a single bulb, and why a car's headlights dim for a moment when the starter motor draws its huge current. With the mains supply, which behaves almost like an ideal source, the effect is negligible.

The questions for this section are with you now: why the current divides at a junction, the relationship between the main current and the branch currents, how current divides between identical and different branches, a calculation of a missing branch current, and why a battery in a parallel circuit goes flat faster.

## Subtopic: Parallel Circuits at Home

All the lights and plug sockets in a house are connected in parallel with one another across the 230 volt mains supply. This gives three essential benefits. Every appliance receives the full 230 volts it is designed for, so each works at its proper power. Each appliance can be switched on and off independently with its own switch. And if one appliance fails or a bulb blows, the others keep working.

In the distribution board, the household wiring is divided into several circuits, for example a lighting circuit, one or more plug circuits, and a separate circuit for the stove and for the geyser. Each circuit has its own circuit breaker, and the circuits are themselves connected in parallel. Within each circuit, the lights or sockets are in parallel with one another.

The disadvantage of parallel connection follows directly from the rule that adding branches increases the total current. Every appliance switched on adds another branch and draws more current through the main wires. If too many high-power appliances, such as a kettle, a heater, an iron and a microwave, are plugged into the same circuit, perhaps through a multi-plug, the total current in the wires leading to that circuit becomes too large. The wires can overheat and cause a fire. The circuit breaker is there to trip and cut the supply before that happens; it is in series with the whole circuit, in the main line, so the full total current passes through it.

Parallel connections are also used in cars, where the headlights, radio and indicators are in parallel across the car battery, and in many festive light strings and LED strips, where the lights are wired in parallel so that one failed light does not darken the rest.

Comparing the two types: in series, one path, the same current everywhere, adding resistors decreases the current, one fault stops everything. In parallel, several paths, the full voltage across each branch, adding resistors increases the total current, each branch works independently.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying bulbs in parallel are dimmer than a single bulb; with an ideal source each is as bright as a single bulb. Two: saying adding resistors in parallel increases the total resistance; it decreases it. Three: saying the current is the same in every branch; it depends on each branch's resistance, and branch currents add up to the main current. Four: saying a broken bulb in one branch stops the whole parallel circuit; the other branches keep working. Five: plugging many high-power appliances into one multi-plug because each works fine alone; together they overload the circuit.

Layout for marks: state that there is more than one pathway, that each branch has the full voltage, then the effect of the change on total resistance, total current and brightness.

The questions for this section are with you now: why house wiring is in parallel, how circuits are arranged in a distribution board, why overloading a multi-plug is dangerous, a comparison of series and parallel circuits, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a highway with many lanes, every bulb at full brightness, and why your house is wired in parallel — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Highway with Many Lanes

Picture a highway that splits into several lanes at a toll plaza and then joins back into one road after it. Cars can go through any lane. If one lane is closed, the others keep moving.

A parallel circuit is like that. The current leaves the cell, splits at a junction into two or more branches, goes through the components in each branch, and joins up again before going back to the cell. Each branch is its own path.

Because each branch is connected straight across the cell, each one gets the full push of the cell. And because each branch is its own path, if a bulb in one branch breaks, the other branches still have a complete path, so their bulbs stay on.

You can put a switch in each branch to control just that branch, or a switch in the main line to control them all.

Keep the highway in mind and try the questions for this part: what a parallel circuit is, why one broken bulb does not stop the others, and where to put a switch.

## Subtopic: Every Bulb at Full Brightness

Connect one bulb to two cells. Now add a second bulb in parallel, on its own branch. Surprise: both are just as bright as the first bulb was alone. Add a third: all three are still at full brightness.

Each bulb gets the full push of the cells, because each sits on its own branch straight across the cells.

But here is the catch. Each branch takes its own share of current. So with two bulbs, the cell has to supply twice as much current in total. With three, three times as much. Adding resistors in parallel decreases the total resistance and increases the total current, like opening more tills at a busy supermarket so more shoppers get through each minute.

And all the branch currents add up to the current from the cell. That is also why the cell goes flat faster.

Hold the bright bulbs in mind and try this part's questions: how bright bulbs in parallel are, and what adding a branch does to the total current.

## Subtopic: Why Your House Is Wired in Parallel

In your house, every light and plug is on its own parallel branch across the mains. That is why the kettle gets the full 230 volts, why you can switch the light off without turning off the fridge, and why one blown bulb does not black out the whole house.

But every appliance you switch on adds another branch and pulls more current through the main wires. Plug a kettle, a heater, an iron and a microwave into one multi-plug, and the total current can get dangerously big. The wires heat up, and that is how many house fires start. The circuit breaker in the main line is there to switch off before that happens.

Pocket summary of the lesson. In a parallel circuit, the current splits into two or more branches. Each branch gets the full voltage and works independently, so one fault does not stop the others. Bulbs in parallel stay at full brightness. Adding resistors in parallel decreases the total resistance and increases the total current, and the branch currents add up to the main current. Houses are wired in parallel, but overloading a circuit is dangerous.
