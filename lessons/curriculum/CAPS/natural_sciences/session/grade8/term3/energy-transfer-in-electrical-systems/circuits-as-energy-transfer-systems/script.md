# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the electric circuit as a system for transferring energy from a source to a place where it is useful. The anchor is a torch during load shedding. The lights in the house go out at six in the evening. Someone finds a torch in a kitchen drawer and slides the switch forward; the bulb lights at once. Slide the switch back and it goes dark. Inside the plastic case there is nothing more than two cells, a switch, a bulb and a few strips of metal, yet together they move energy from the chemicals in the cells to light in the room. The governing idea is that an electric circuit is a closed conducting path that allows electric charges to flow continuously from one terminal of an energy source, through one or more components, and back to the other terminal; that the energy source gives energy to the charges and the components transfer that energy into other useful forms; and that if the path is broken anywhere, the flow stops everywhere. The idea of a circuit as an energy transfer system, the difference between closed and open circuits, the input and output energies of components, and the conservation of energy in circuits are examinable, and they lead to potential difference, current and power in Grade 9 and in Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: A Circuit as an Energy System

A system is a group of parts that work together to do a job. An electrical system has the job of transferring energy from where it is stored or produced to where it is needed, and converting it into a useful form.

Every electric circuit has three essential parts. First, an energy source, such as a cell, a battery, a solar panel or a generator in a power station, which gives energy to the electric charges. Second, conductors, usually copper wires, which provide a path for the charges to flow along. Third, one or more components, often called the load, which transfer the energy carried by the charges into other forms: a bulb into light, a heater into heat, a motor into movement, a speaker into sound. Most circuits also have a switch, which allows the circuit to be turned on and off.

In a torch, the energy source is two cells, which store energy in chemical form. When the circuit is complete, chemical reactions inside the cells give energy to electric charges and push them around the circuit. The charges carry the energy through the metal strips and the switch to the bulb, where the energy is transferred to the surroundings as light and heat. The charges then return to the cells, where they are given more energy, and go round again.

It is important to understand what is used up and what is not. The charges are not used up. The same charges go around the circuit again and again, like the water in a closed central heating system or the chain on a bicycle. What the circuit transfers is energy. The cell gradually runs flat because its chemicals are used up as their energy is transferred, not because it runs out of charges.

Energy is also never created or destroyed in a circuit, a principle called the conservation of energy. All the chemical energy that leaves a cell ends up somewhere: as light, as heat in the bulb, as a small amount of heat in the wires, and as a little heat in the cell itself. None of it disappears, although some of it ends up in forms that are not useful to us.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three essential parts of a circuit, the energy source and load in a torch, what is used up in a circuit and what is not, and the conservation of energy.

## Subtopic: Closed and Open Circuits

For charges to flow, they need a complete, unbroken path that goes from one terminal of the source, through the components, and back to the other terminal. Such a path is called a closed circuit, or a complete circuit. When the circuit is closed, charges flow and the components work.

If there is a gap anywhere in the path, the circuit is called an open circuit, or an incomplete circuit. Charges cannot cross the gap, because air and other insulators do not allow them through, so the flow stops throughout the whole circuit, not just at the gap. The bulb goes out even if the gap is on the far side of the circuit from the bulb.

A switch is a device for opening and closing a circuit deliberately. Inside a switch are two metal contacts. When the switch is on, the contacts touch and the circuit is closed. When the switch is off, the contacts are separated by an air gap and the circuit is open. A torch switch, a light switch on the wall, a push-button doorbell and the on-off button of a kettle all work this way.

A circuit can also be opened accidentally. A broken wire inside its plastic insulation, a loose connection, a bulb with a broken filament, a cell put in the wrong way round in some devices, or corrosion on the contacts of a battery holder can all stop a circuit from working. When a torch fails, checking each part of the path in turn is a systematic way to find the break: the cells, the contacts, the switch, the bulb and the connections.

Every part of the path must be a conductor. Metals such as copper, aluminium and steel are good conductors and are used for wires and contacts. Insulators such as plastic, rubber and glass are used to cover wires and handles, so that the charges stay in the intended path and people can handle them safely. You can test whether a material is a conductor by placing it in a gap in a simple circuit with a cell and a bulb: if the bulb lights, the material conducts.

The questions for this section are with you now: the difference between a closed and an open circuit, how a switch works, why a gap anywhere stops the whole circuit, how to find a fault in a torch, and how to test whether a material is a conductor.

## Subtopic: Input, Transfer and Output

Every component in a circuit can be described by its input energy and its output energy. The input to every component is electrical energy carried by the charges. The outputs depend on the component.

A light bulb transfers electrical energy to light and heat. An old-style filament bulb is very wasteful: only about five percent of its input energy becomes light, and the rest becomes heat, which is why it is too hot to touch. A compact fluorescent lamp transfers roughly a fifth of its input to light, and a modern LED lamp transfers even more, which is why South African households were encouraged to switch to LEDs to reduce electricity demand.

An electric heater or kettle element transfers electrical energy to heat. A motor, as in a fan, a drill or a washing machine, transfers electrical energy to kinetic energy, the energy of movement, with some heat and sound. A speaker or a buzzer transfers electrical energy to sound. A cell being recharged transfers electrical energy into chemical potential energy, to be released later.

The useful output is the output we want: light from a bulb, sound from a speaker. The rest is often called wasted energy, usually heat that spreads into the surroundings. The efficiency of a device compares its useful output with its total input. A device that turns more of its input into useful output is more efficient, and it costs less to run for the same useful result.

Energy flow diagrams, sometimes called Sankey diagrams, show this with arrows. A wide arrow for the input splits into a wider arrow for the main output and narrower arrows for the other outputs, the width of each arrow showing the amount of energy. For a filament bulb with one hundred units of input, the arrow splits into five units of light and ninety-five units of heat. The total width going out equals the width coming in, showing conservation of energy.

The questions for this section are with you now: the input and output energies of a bulb, a heater, a motor and a speaker, the meaning of useful and wasted energy, why LED lamps are more efficient than filament bulbs, and drawing an energy flow diagram.

## Subtopic: Electricity from Power Station to Plug

The circuits in a house are part of an enormous electrical system that stretches across the country.

Most of South Africa's electricity is generated by Eskom, and most of it comes from coal-fired power stations, many of them in Mpumalanga, near the coalfields. In these power stations, coal is burned to boil water into high-pressure steam. The steam spins turbines connected to generators, which transfer the kinetic energy of the turbines into electrical energy. The energy chain is: chemical energy in coal, to heat, to kinetic energy of the steam and turbine, to electrical energy. South Africa also generates electricity at the Koeberg nuclear power station near Cape Town, at hydroelectric and pumped-storage schemes, and increasingly from wind farms, mostly in the Eastern and Western Cape and the Northern Cape, and from solar farms, especially in the Northern Cape.

From the power station, the electrical energy is carried along overhead transmission lines strung on tall steel pylons, at very high voltages of up to 765 000 volts, to reduce energy losses as heat in the long wires. Substations near towns and cities step the voltage down in stages, and finally a transformer on a pole or in a small box near your street steps it down to the 230 volts supplied to homes. The wires enter the house through the meter and the distribution board, which contains circuit breakers and an earth leakage unit for safety, and then run inside the walls to the plugs and lights.

The whole national grid is one giant circuit system, and like any circuit it needs a balance: the energy fed in by the power stations must match the energy taken out by all users at every moment. When demand is greater than the supply available, the system operator switches off supply to different areas in turn, which is what load shedding is.

At home, using energy wisely reduces cost and demand: switching off lights and appliances not in use, using LED lamps, using a solar geyser or a geyser blanket and timer, and boiling only as much water as you need.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying a cell runs out of charges; it runs out of stored chemical energy. Two: saying the current is used up in a bulb; the same charges continue around the circuit. Three: saying a gap only affects the part of the circuit after it; a gap stops the whole circuit. Four: saying a bulb turns all its energy into light; most becomes heat in a filament bulb. Five: saying energy is lost or destroyed; it is transferred, sometimes to forms that are not useful.

Layout for marks: identify the energy source, the path and the load, then give the input and output energy of each component.

The questions for this section are with you now: the energy chain in a coal-fired power station, why transmission lines use very high voltages, the meaning of load shedding, ways to save electricity at home, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a torch in a power cut, a loop with no gaps, and energy going in with something useful coming out — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Torch in a Power Cut

Load shedding starts and the house goes dark. You grab a torch, slide the switch, and there is light. What is going on inside?

The torch is a circuit, and a circuit is a system for moving energy. It has three main parts. The cells are the energy source: they store energy in chemicals. The metal strips and wires are the path. The bulb is the load: it turns the energy into light.

When the switch is on, the cells push tiny electric charges around the loop. The charges pick up energy in the cells, carry it to the bulb, drop it off as light and heat, and come back for more. Round and round.

The charges never get used up. It is like a bicycle chain going round and round, carrying the push from the pedals to the wheel. What runs out, eventually, is the energy stored in the chemicals in the cells. That is when the torch goes dim.

Keep the torch in mind and try the questions for this part: the three parts of a circuit, what the cells do, and what gets used up.

## Subtopic: A Loop with No Gaps

For a circuit to work, the path has to be a complete loop, from one end of the cell, through the bulb, and back to the other end. No gaps anywhere. That is a closed circuit.

Make a gap anywhere and the charges cannot jump across the air. Everything stops, all around the loop, not just at the gap. That is an open circuit.

A switch is just a clever gap-maker. On: two bits of metal touch and close the loop. Off: they pull apart and leave a gap.

If a torch will not work, there is a gap somewhere. Check each part in turn: Are the cells flat? Are the springs and contacts rusty? Is the bulb broken? Is a wire loose?

The path must be made of conductors, like copper. Plastic and rubber are insulators; they are wrapped around wires to keep the charges in and keep your fingers safe.

Hold the loop in mind and try this part's questions: what a closed circuit is, what a switch does, and why one gap stops everything.

## Subtopic: Energy Goes In, Something Useful Comes Out

Every gadget takes in electrical energy and turns it into something else. A bulb gives out light and heat. A kettle gives out heat. A fan gives out movement and a bit of sound. A speaker gives out sound.

The part you want is the useful energy. The rest, usually heat, is wasted. An old-style bulb is terrible at this: only about five parts in a hundred become light, and the rest is heat. An LED lamp turns much more of its energy into light, so it saves money and electricity.

And energy is never destroyed. It just changes form. All of it goes somewhere.

The electricity for your home mostly comes from big coal power stations in Mpumalanga. Burning coal boils water, steam spins turbines, and generators make electricity, which travels along huge power lines to your house.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A circuit is a system that transfers energy from a source, through conductors, to a load. It only works when it is a closed loop; a gap anywhere stops everything. Charges are not used up; energy is transferred. Each component turns electrical energy into other forms, some useful and some wasted, but energy is always conserved.

Hold the torch, the loop and the useful energy, and take the final questions of the lesson: closing a circuit, the energy changes in a bulb, and what really runs out in a cell.
