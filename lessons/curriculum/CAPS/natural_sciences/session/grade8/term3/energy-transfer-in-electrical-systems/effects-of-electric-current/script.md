# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines the three main effects of an electric current: the heating effect, the magnetic effect and the chemical effect, and how each is put to use or guarded against. The anchor is a scrapyard on the outskirts of a city. A crane swings a large flat disc over a heap of old cars and scrap metal. The operator presses a button and a tangle of steel leaps up and clings to the disc; the crane swings across to a waiting truck, the operator releases the button, and the steel crashes down. There is no permanent magnet in the disc, only a coil of copper wire wrapped around iron. When the current flows, it becomes a powerful magnet; when the current stops, the magnetism disappears. The governing idea is that an electric current always produces effects that let us use the energy it carries: it heats the conductors it flows through, it produces a magnetic field around them, and when it passes through certain liquids it causes chemical changes. The heating effect in filaments, elements, fuses and short circuits, the magnetic effect and the making and uses of electromagnets, and the chemical effect in electrolysis and electroplating are examinable, and they lead to electromagnetism and electrochemistry in Grade 9 and in Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: The Heating Effect

Whenever a current flows through a conductor that has resistance, the conductor heats up. The electrons drifting through the conductor collide with its vibrating atoms and transfer energy to them, so the atoms vibrate more strongly, which means the temperature rises. This is the heating effect of an electric current.

The amount of heat produced depends on the current and the resistance. A larger current produces much more heat: doubling the current through the same resistance produces four times as much heat each second. A larger resistance, for the same current, also produces more heat. The longer the current flows, the more heat is produced in total.

The heating effect is used in many appliances. Kettles, urns, stoves, ovens, irons, toasters, hair dryers, heaters, electric blankets and geysers all contain heating elements, coils or strips of high-resistance alloy, usually nichrome, through which a large current flows. A geyser element is a metal tube containing a nichrome coil, packed in an insulating powder, immersed in the water. A hot glue gun and a soldering iron use the same effect.

The filament bulb uses the heating effect to produce light. Its filament, a fine coil of tungsten, is heated to about 2 500 degrees Celsius, hot enough to glow white. Tungsten is used because it has the highest melting point of any metal, about 3 400 degrees. The glass bulb is filled with an unreactive gas such as argon, because in air the hot tungsten would react with oxygen and burn out almost immediately.

The heating effect is unwanted in many other places, because it wastes energy and can be dangerous. Cables, transmission lines, motors, computers and cellphone chargers all get warm in use. Engineers use thick, low-resistance wires and include fans, cooling fins and vents to remove the heat. A laptop or phone charger placed on a bed or under a pillow can overheat because the bedding traps the heat.

The questions for this section are with you now: what causes the heating effect, how the current and resistance affect the heat produced, five appliances that use the heating effect, why tungsten and argon are used in filament bulbs, and where the heating effect is unwanted.

## Subtopic: Fuses and Short Circuits

The heating effect can also protect us. A fuse is a safety device containing a short, thin piece of wire with a low melting point, often enclosed in a glass or ceramic cartridge. The fuse is connected in series in the live side of a circuit, so all the current passes through it. While the current is normal, the fuse wire warms only slightly. If the current rises above the value the fuse is rated for, the wire heats up enough to melt, breaking the circuit and stopping the current before the wires of the circuit can overheat and start a fire. The fuse is said to have blown.

Fuses are rated in amperes, the largest current they can carry without melting. Common ratings are 3, 5, 10 and 13 amperes. The correct fuse for an appliance has a rating just above the normal current the appliance draws. A two-kilowatt kettle on 230 volts draws about nine amperes, so it needs a fuse of 10 or 13 amperes; a small radio drawing less than one ampere needs a 3 ampere fuse. Using a fuse with a rating that is too high is dangerous, because a fault current could flow without the fuse blowing. Fuses are found in some plugs, inside many appliances, in car fuse boxes, and in older household distribution boards.

A short circuit happens when current finds a path with almost no resistance, bypassing the components that normally limit it. Examples are bare live and neutral wires touching because of damaged insulation, water getting into a plug or an appliance, or a metal object such as a paper clip bridging the terminals of a battery. Because the resistance of the path is so low, a very large current flows. The heating effect of that current is huge: wires glow, insulation melts and burns, sparks fly, and fires can start.

Modern South African homes use circuit breakers instead of fuses on their distribution boards. A circuit breaker uses the heating effect, through a bimetallic strip that bends when overloaded, and the magnetic effect, through an electromagnet that snaps the contacts open in a sudden short circuit, to switch off the current. Unlike a fuse, it can be reset by flicking its switch back on once the fault is fixed.

The questions for this section are with you now: how a fuse works, what the rating of a fuse means, how to choose the correct fuse, what a short circuit is and why it is dangerous, and how a circuit breaker differs from a fuse.

## Subtopic: The Magnetic Effect and Electromagnets

In 1820 the Danish scientist Hans Christian Ørsted noticed during a lecture that a compass needle near a wire swung round whenever a current was switched on in the wire. He had discovered that an electric current produces a magnetic field around the conductor it flows through. This is the magnetic effect of a current. The magnetic field around a straight wire forms circles around the wire; reversing the current reverses the direction of the field.

The magnetic field of a single straight wire is weak. It becomes much stronger when the wire is wound into a coil, called a solenoid, because the fields of all the turns add together. If a piece of soft iron, such as an iron nail or bolt, is placed inside the coil, the field becomes far stronger still, because the iron itself becomes magnetised and adds its own field. A coil of insulated wire wound around an iron core is called an electromagnet.

An electromagnet can be made in class by winding about fifty turns of thin insulated copper wire around a large iron nail and connecting the ends of the wire to a cell through a switch. When the switch is closed, the nail picks up paper clips or pins; when it is opened, they drop off. Its strength can be investigated by counting the paper clips it can lift.

Three factors increase the strength of an electromagnet: more turns of wire on the coil, a larger current through the coil, and the presence of a soft iron core. Soft iron is used because it magnetises strongly while the current flows and loses almost all of its magnetism as soon as the current stops. Steel would remain magnetised and would not release its load.

The great advantage of an electromagnet over a permanent magnet is that it can be switched on and off, and its strength can be controlled by changing the current. Electromagnets are used in scrapyard cranes, in electric doorbells, in relays that let a small current switch a large one, in circuit breakers, in speakers and headphones, in electric motors and generators, in the locks of security gates, and in the huge magnets of MRI scanners in hospitals.

The questions for this section are with you now: Ørsted's discovery, what an electromagnet is and how to make one, the three factors that increase its strength, why soft iron is used for the core, the advantages of an electromagnet over a permanent magnet, and five uses.

## Subtopic: The Chemical Effect

When an electric current passes through certain liquids, it causes chemical reactions. This is the chemical effect of a current. Liquids that conduct electricity and are decomposed by it are called electrolytes; they include solutions of salts, acids and bases in water, and some molten compounds. Pure water conducts very poorly, but salty or acidified water conducts well. The process of using a current to decompose a substance is called electrolysis.

In electrolysis, two conducting rods or plates, called electrodes, are dipped into the electrolyte and connected to a cell or a battery. The electrode connected to the positive terminal is called the anode, and the one connected to the negative terminal is called the cathode. When current flows, new substances form at the electrodes.

In the electrolysis of acidified water, bubbles of gas appear at both electrodes. Hydrogen gas forms at the negative electrode and oxygen gas at the positive electrode, and the volume of hydrogen is twice the volume of oxygen, because each water molecule contains two hydrogen atoms for every oxygen atom. The hydrogen can be tested with a lit splint, which it extinguishes with a squeaky pop, and the oxygen with a glowing splint, which it relights.

In the electrolysis of copper chloride solution, a pink-brown coating of copper metal forms on the negative electrode, and chlorine gas, with a sharp smell like swimming pool water, bubbles off the positive electrode. The blue colour of the solution fades as the copper is removed from it.

The chemical effect is used in industry. Electroplating coats an object with a thin layer of metal: the object is made the negative electrode in a solution of the coating metal, and the metal is deposited on it. Taps and car parts are chrome-plated, cutlery is silver-plated, and jewellery is gold-plated, to look attractive and resist corrosion. Copper is purified by electrolysis to make the very pure copper needed for electrical wires. Aluminium is produced by the electrolysis of aluminium oxide dissolved in a molten mineral; the Hillside smelter at Richards Bay uses this process and needs enormous amounts of electricity. Recharging a cellphone battery is also a chemical effect of current, reversing the reaction that supplies energy.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying only bulbs and heaters get hot; every conductor with resistance gets hot when current flows. Two: replacing a blown fuse with a higher rating or a piece of wire. Three: using a steel core in an electromagnet that must switch off; soft iron is used. Four: saying pure water conducts well; it is the dissolved substances that make water conduct.

Layout for marks: name the effect, explain its cause, and give a use and, where relevant, a danger.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of electrolysis, electrolyte and electrode, the products of electrolysis of water and of copper chloride, electroplating and other uses, and the four exhibits.

## Subtopic: A Glowing Wire and a Melting Fuse

Every time current flows through a wire, the wire warms up. The moving electrons bump into atoms and pass on energy, so the atoms jiggle faster and the wire gets hot. That is the heating effect.

We use it all the time. The coil in a kettle, the element in a stove and the wires in a toaster are designed to get really hot. An old-style bulb's filament gets so hot, about 2 500 degrees, that it glows white.

We also use it for safety. A fuse is a thin wire that melts if the current gets too big. The melted wire leaves a gap, the circuit stops, and the house wires are saved from overheating. Never replace a blown fuse with a bigger one or a bit of wire.

The danger is a short circuit: when current finds a shortcut with almost no resistance, like two bare wires touching. A huge current flows, wires get red hot, and a fire can start.

Keep the glowing wire in mind and try the questions for this part: why wires heat up, how a fuse protects a circuit, and what a short circuit is.

## Subtopic: A Nail That Becomes a Magnet

Wrap a long piece of insulated wire around an iron nail fifty times. Connect the ends to a battery. Now the nail picks up paper clips. Disconnect it, and the clips fall off. You have made an electromagnet.

Current flowing in a wire makes a magnetic field around it. Wind the wire into a coil and the fields add up. Put iron inside and it gets much stronger.

Three ways to make it stronger: more turns of wire, more current, and an iron core. And the best part is that you can switch it on and off.

That is how a scrapyard crane picks up old cars and drops them where it wants. Doorbells, speakers, motors and the locks on security gates all use electromagnets.

Hold the nail in mind and try this part's questions: how to make an electromagnet, three ways to make it stronger, and why you can switch it off.

## Subtopic: Bubbles in Salty Water

Put two metal rods into salty water and connect them to a battery. Bubbles start to fizz off both rods. The current is breaking substances apart. That is the chemical effect, and it is called electrolysis.

With acidified water, you get hydrogen gas at the negative rod and oxygen gas at the positive rod, twice as much hydrogen as oxygen.

Factories use this to coat things with shiny metal. Hang a tap in a special solution, pass a current, and a thin layer of chrome sticks to it. That is electroplating. Silver cutlery, gold jewellery and very pure copper wire are made this way too.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A current always heats the wire it flows through; we use it in kettles, toasters and bulbs, and in fuses for safety, and it is dangerous in short circuits. A current makes a magnetic field; a coil around soft iron is an electromagnet, stronger with more turns, more current and an iron core, and it can be switched off. A current through certain liquids causes chemical change, electrolysis, used for electroplating and purifying metals.

Hold the glowing wire, the magnetic nail and the bubbles, and take the final questions of the lesson: name the effect in each appliance, how to strengthen an electromagnet, and what forms at each rod in electrolysis.
