# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains electric current as the flow of charge, shows how it is measured, and examines resistance, the property of a component that opposes the current and in doing so transfers useful energy. The anchor is a simple and memorable experiment: a pencil-lead dimmer. A circuit is made with two cells, a small bulb and two wires ending in crocodile clips. One clip is fixed to one end of a long strip of pencil graphite; the other clip is slid along the graphite. When the clips are close together the bulb glows brightly; as they are moved apart the bulb dims; at the far end it barely glows. Nothing about the cells or the bulb has changed. Only the length of graphite the charges must pass through has changed. The governing idea is that electric current is the rate of flow of charge around a circuit, measured in amperes with an ammeter connected in series, and that resistance opposes the current: the greater the resistance in a circuit, the smaller the current for the same cells. Resistance depends on the material, length, thickness and temperature of a conductor, and the energy transferred in a resistor is often exactly the useful output we want. The meaning and unit of current, the use of an ammeter, the factors affecting resistance and the effect of resistance on current are examinable, and they lead to Ohm's law in Grade 10 Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: Electric Current as Flowing Charge

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge past a point in a circuit. In metal wires, the moving charges are electrons. When a circuit is closed, the energy source pushes the free electrons in the conductors so that they drift steadily around the circuit. The more charge that passes a point every second, the larger the current.

Current is measured in amperes, symbol A, often shortened to amps, named after the French scientist André-Marie Ampère. A current of one ampere means that one coulomb of charge passes a point every second, which is about six billion billion electrons per second. Smaller currents are measured in milliamperes, symbol mA, where one thousand milliamperes make one ampere.

Some typical currents give a feel for the unit. A small LED draws about twenty milliamperes. A torch bulb draws a few hundred milliamperes. A two-kilowatt kettle on the 230 volt mains draws about nine amperes. A geyser draws more than ten amperes. Standard South African wall sockets are rated for a maximum of sixteen amperes.

In a single loop of components, there is only one path, so the same charges pass through every component, one after the other. Charge cannot pile up anywhere or disappear, so the current is the same at every point in a single loop: before the bulb, after the bulb and through the cell. The bulb does not use up the current; it uses the energy carried by the charges.

The brightness of a bulb is a useful rough indicator of current: in the same circuit, a brighter bulb means a larger current through it and a dimmer bulb a smaller current.

Current is sometimes compared to the flow of water in a closed system of pipes driven by a pump. The pump is like the cell, giving energy to the water; the flow of water past a point each second is like the current; and narrow sections of pipe that make it harder for the water to flow are like resistors. The comparison is not perfect, but it helps.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of current, the unit and its relationship to charge per second, typical currents, why the current is the same everywhere in a single loop, and what the brightness of a bulb tells you.

## Subtopic: Measuring Current with an Ammeter

Current is measured with an ammeter. In a circuit diagram, an ammeter is shown as a circle with the letter A inside it.

Because current is the flow of charge through a point, the ammeter must be placed in the path of the current, so that all the charges flowing in that part of the circuit pass through it. This is called connecting the ammeter in series. To do this, you break the circuit at the point where you want to measure the current and connect the ammeter into the gap, so that it becomes part of the loop.

An ammeter has a very low resistance, so that putting it into the circuit hardly changes the current it is measuring. For the same reason, an ammeter must never be connected directly across a cell or across a component, because the low resistance would allow a very large current to flow through it, which could damage the meter and drain or damage the cell.

An ammeter has a positive terminal, usually red, and a negative terminal, usually black. The positive terminal must be connected to the side of the circuit leading back to the positive terminal of the cell, so that conventional current enters the meter at its positive terminal. If it is connected the wrong way round, an analogue meter's needle swings backwards off the scale, and a digital meter shows a minus sign.

Many ammeters have more than one range. Start on the largest range, then switch to a smaller range if the reading is very small, to get a more precise reading without risking damage.

A useful experiment is to place an ammeter at different points in a single-loop circuit containing a cell, a switch and two bulbs: before the first bulb, between the bulbs, and after the second bulb. The readings are the same at every point, confirming that current is not used up.

A multimeter, common in workshops and labs, combines an ammeter, a voltmeter and a resistance meter, with a dial to choose the function.

The questions for this section are with you now: the symbol of an ammeter, why it must be connected in series, why it has a low resistance, how to connect its terminals correctly, and what the readings show at different points in a single loop.

## Subtopic: What Affects Resistance

Resistance is the opposition a component offers to the flow of current. In a metal, it is caused by the drifting electrons colliding with the vibrating atoms of the metal. Each collision slows the electrons and transfers some of their energy to the atoms, which vibrate more, so the metal heats up. Resistance is measured in ohms, symbol the Greek letter omega.

Four factors affect the resistance of a wire or a strip of conductor.

Material. Different materials have different resistances for the same size and shape. Silver and copper have very low resistance, aluminium a little higher, iron higher still, and alloys like nichrome much higher. Graphite, the carbon in pencils, conducts but has a much higher resistance than metals. Insulators such as plastic and glass have an enormous resistance, so that almost no current flows through them.

Length. A longer wire has more resistance than a shorter wire of the same material and thickness, because the electrons must travel through more material and suffer more collisions. Doubling the length doubles the resistance. That is what the pencil-lead dimmer shows: moving the clips apart increases the length of graphite in the circuit, increasing the resistance and decreasing the current, so the bulb dims.

Thickness. A thicker wire has less resistance than a thinner wire of the same material and length, because there are more paths side by side for the electrons. Doubling the cross-sectional area halves the resistance.

Temperature. For metals, resistance increases as temperature increases, because the atoms vibrate more and the electrons collide with them more often. A filament bulb has a much lower resistance when it is cold than when it is glowing hot, which is why bulbs most often fail at the moment they are switched on, when a brief surge of current flows through the cold filament.

A fair investigation of the effect of length can use a nichrome wire stretched along a metre rule, with one crocodile clip fixed at zero and the other moved to 20, 40, 60, 80 and 100 centimetres, recording the current on an ammeter each time while keeping the same cells, wire material and thickness, and temperature. The current decreases as the length increases.

The questions for this section are with you now: what causes resistance in a metal, the four factors that affect resistance and how each one changes it, the explanation of the pencil-lead dimmer, and a fair test of the effect of length.

## Subtopic: Resistance Controls Current and Provides Useful Output

For the same energy source, the greater the total resistance in a circuit, the smaller the current. Adding a resistor into a single loop increases the total resistance, so the current through every component in the loop decreases, and a bulb in the loop becomes dimmer. Removing a resistor, or replacing it with one of lower resistance, increases the current and the bulb becomes brighter. Increasing the number of cells increases the push and, for the same resistance, increases the current.

This lets resistors be used to control current. A variable resistor in a circuit with a bulb makes a dimmer; with a motor, it makes a speed control. A resistor connected in series with an LED limits the current through the LED to a safe value. Resistors in electronic circuits divide and control currents and voltages in radios, computers and cellphones.

Resistance also provides useful outputs, because the energy transferred in a resistance is turned into heat, and sometimes light. That is not always waste. The heating elements of stoves, kettles, irons, hair dryers, toasters, heaters, geysers and electric blankets are resistors chosen so that a large current produces a lot of heat. The filament of an incandescent bulb is a resistor so thin and hot that it glows white. A fuse uses resistance deliberately: a thin wire in the fuse heats up when the current is too large, melts, and breaks the circuit.

In other places, resistance is unwanted, because the heat produced is wasted energy. In connecting wires, transmission lines and the windings of motors, engineers use low-resistance copper and aluminium and make the conductors thick to reduce waste.

A short circuit is a path with almost no resistance, for example when the two terminals of a cell are joined directly by a wire, or when bare live and neutral wires touch. With almost no resistance, a very large current flows, the wires and the source heat up quickly, and there is a risk of fire. Fuses and circuit breakers protect against this.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying current is used up by a bulb; the current is the same before and after it. Two: connecting an ammeter across a component instead of in series. Three: saying a thicker wire has more resistance; it has less. Four: saying resistance is always a waste; it gives us heaters and lights. Five: saying a longer wire lets more current flow; it has more resistance, so less.

Layout for marks: state the change in resistance, then the change in current, then the effect on the bulb or device.

The questions for this section are with you now: how adding or removing a resistor affects current, uses of resistors to control current, useful outputs from resistance, what a short circuit is and why it is dangerous, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a toll gate on a highway, a pencil-lead dimmer, and a glowing toaster — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Cars Through a Toll Gate

Stand at a toll gate on the N1 and count the cars going through each minute. Lots of cars per minute means a big flow; a few cars means a small flow.

Electric current is like that, but with charges instead of cars. Current is how much charge passes a point every second. It is measured in amperes, or amps. One amp is about six billion billion electrons going past every second.

To measure it you use an ammeter, and you must put it into the loop itself, like a toll gate built across the road, so every charge has to pass through it. That is called connecting it in series.

Here is a surprise. In a single loop, the current is the same everywhere: before the bulb, after the bulb, everywhere. Every car that goes into a tunnel comes out of the other end. The bulb does not use up current. It uses the energy the charges carry.

Keep the toll gate in mind and try the questions for this part: what current means, its unit, and how to connect an ammeter.

## Subtopic: A Pencil-Lead Dimmer

Take a strip of pencil lead, two cells and a bulb. Clip one wire to one end of the lead and slide the other clip along it. Close together: bright bulb. Far apart: dim bulb.

The pencil lead has resistance. Resistance makes it harder for charges to flow, like a narrow, bumpy road slowing traffic. The longer the strip of lead the charges have to cross, the more resistance, the less current, and the dimmer the bulb.

Four things change resistance. Longer wire: more resistance. Thicker wire: less resistance, like a wider road. The material: copper has very little, nichrome a lot, plastic so much that almost nothing gets through. And temperature: hot metal has more resistance than cold metal.

Hold the dimmer in mind and try this part's questions: why the bulb dims, and how length and thickness change resistance.

## Subtopic: Why the Toaster Glows

When charges push through a resistance, they bump into atoms and give them energy, and the material heats up. Sometimes that is exactly what we want. A toaster's wires are resistors that glow red hot and toast your bread. Kettles, irons and heaters work the same way. The filament of an old bulb gets so hot that it glows white.

More resistance in a loop means less current. Take a resistor out and the bulb gets brighter. Add one and it gets dimmer.

But be careful with too little resistance. If the two ends of a battery are joined with just a wire, a huge current rushes through, the wire gets hot, and it can start a fire. That is a short circuit.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Current is the flow of charge per second, measured in amperes with an ammeter connected in series. In a single loop the current is the same everywhere. Resistance opposes current; it increases with length, decreases with thickness, depends on the material and rises with temperature in metals. More resistance means less current. Resistance turns electrical energy into heat, useful in heaters and lights. A short circuit has almost no resistance and is dangerous.

Hold the toll gate, the pencil dimmer and the toaster, and take the final questions of the lesson: what an ammeter reads, what makes the bulb dim, and why a short circuit is dangerous.
