### subtopic_1 — Cells and Batteries
**Assistant:** If a big D cell and a small AA cell both give 1.5 volts, why does the voltage not depend on size?

**Tutor:** Because the voltage of a cell is set by the chemicals inside it, not by how much of them there is. A cell works by a chemical reaction between two different materials, separated by a third substance that lets charged particles move between them. In an alkaline cell, the two materials are zinc and manganese dioxide. Each material has its own tendency to give away or take in electrons, and the difference between those two tendencies sets the size of the push given to each electron that is sent round the circuit. For zinc and manganese dioxide, that push is about 1.5 volts, whether you have a gram of chemicals or a kilogram. It is a bit like the height of a waterfall: the height decides how much energy each litre of water gains as it falls, and a wider river does not make the waterfall taller. What the amount of chemicals does change is the total energy stored, and therefore how long the cell can keep supplying current. A D cell contains several times as much zinc and manganese dioxide as an AA cell, so it can run a torch for several times as long, or supply a larger current, before it goes flat. Different chemical pairs give different voltages: a lead-acid cell in a car battery gives about 2 volts per cell, a nickel-metal hydride rechargeable about 1.2 volts, and a lithium-ion cellphone cell about 3.7 volts. That is why devices are designed for a particular kind of cell, and why you should not mix different kinds in the same device. To get a higher voltage, you connect several cells in a row; to get more energy at the same voltage, you use bigger cells.

### subtopic_2 — Conducting Wires and Switches
**Assistant:** Why does a wire that is too thin for its current get hot and become a fire risk?

**Tutor:** Because a thinner wire has more resistance, so more of the energy carried by the current is transferred into heat in the wire itself. In a metal wire, the electrons drifting along collide with the vibrating metal atoms and give them energy, making them vibrate more, which means the wire heats up. In a thick wire, there are many more paths side by side for the electrons, so for the same current each electron needs to drift more slowly and the collisions transfer less energy overall. In a thin wire, the same current has to squeeze through a narrower path, so the resistance is higher and more energy is transferred to heat in every metre. Halving the cross-sectional area of a wire doubles its resistance, and doubles the heat produced for the same current. In a properly designed installation, the wires are thick enough that the heat produced is small and is carried away into the surroundings without the wire getting noticeably warm. But if someone connects a heater, a kettle and an iron all to one thin extension cord, or uses a thin cord meant for a lamp to power a two-kilowatt heater, the current is far larger than the wire was designed for. The wire heats up faster than it can cool, the PVC insulation softens and melts, and bare wires may touch, causing sparks, or the hot wire may ignite curtains, carpets or bedding. Many house fires in South Africa start this way, especially with overloaded multi-plugs and illegal connections. That is why cables are rated for a maximum current, why circuit breakers are matched to the wire thickness, and why you should never overload extension cords.

### subtopic_3 — Resistors and Other Components
**Assistant:** Why do heating elements use nichrome wire instead of copper?

**Tutor:** Because a heating element needs a high resistance so that it gets hot, and it must survive being red hot in air for years without melting or crumbling, and nichrome does both much better than copper. Copper is chosen for connecting wires precisely because its resistance is very low, so it wastes little energy as heat. That makes it poor for a heater: to get enough resistance from copper, you would need an impractically long, thin wire. Nichrome, an alloy of mostly nickel with about a fifth chromium, has a resistance about sixty times higher than copper for the same size of wire, so a compact coil of nichrome can produce plenty of heat from the current in a kettle or toaster. Nichrome also has a high melting point, around 1 400 degrees Celsius, compared with about 1 085 degrees for copper, so it can glow red hot without melting. Most importantly, when nichrome is heated in air, the chromium forms a thin, tough layer of chromium oxide on the surface that sticks firmly and protects the metal underneath from further reaction with oxygen. Copper heated in air quickly forms a crumbly black oxide that flakes off, exposing fresh copper to be oxidised, so a copper element would soon burn through. Nichrome's resistance also changes only a little as it heats, which makes the heater's power more predictable. In a kettle, the element is usually sealed inside a metal tube or under a flat plate, packed with an insulating powder, so the water is heated without touching the live wire. In a toaster or a bar heater, the bare nichrome coil glows red, which is why you should never poke a knife into a toaster.

### subtopic_4 — Drawing Circuit Diagrams
**Assistant:** Why do we still use conventional current from positive to negative if electrons actually flow the other way?

**Tutor:** Because the convention was agreed long before electrons were discovered, it works perfectly for every calculation, and changing it would cause enormous confusion in a huge body of science and engineering. In the 1700s, Benjamin Franklin and others studying electricity imagined that it was a kind of fluid that flowed from places with a surplus, which they called positive, to places with a shortage, which they called negative. So current was described as flowing from the positive terminal to the negative terminal. It was a guess, and a reasonable one. Electrons were discovered by J. J. Thomson only in 1897, and it then turned out that in metal wires the moving charges are negative electrons, which drift from the negative terminal towards the positive terminal, the opposite direction. By then, a century and a half of rules, diagrams and formulae had been written using the original direction. It turned out not to matter for most purposes: negative charges moving one way have exactly the same effect on a circuit as positive charges moving the other way. The heating, the magnetic effect and every calculation of current, voltage and resistance give the same answers either way. And in some conductors, such as the salty liquids in batteries and in your body, and in certain semiconductors, the moving charges really are positive, or both kinds move at once. So scientists kept the old convention for current direction, and simply remember that in metals, the electrons themselves drift the opposite way. In circuit diagrams, and in your exams, always draw conventional current from the positive terminal round to the negative terminal.
