### subtopic_1 — A Circuit as an Energy System
**Assistant:** If charges move slowly through a wire, why does a bulb light the instant you close the switch?

**Tutor:** Because the wire is already full of charges, and closing the switch makes all of them start moving at almost the same moment, so the ones already at the bulb begin delivering energy straight away. In a copper wire, enormous numbers of free electrons are present everywhere along its length before the switch is ever closed. They are not waiting at the cell to set off on a journey. When you close the switch, the cell sets up an electric push along the whole circuit, and that push travels through the wire at a speed close to the speed of light. Within a tiny fraction of a second, the electrons throughout the circuit, including those in the bulb's filament, are all nudged into a slow drift. The electrons inside the filament immediately start transferring energy, and the bulb lights. The drift itself is astonishingly slow. In a typical torch circuit, the electrons crawl along the wire at well under a millimetre per second, slower than a snail. An individual electron leaving the cell might take hours to travel round the whole circuit. A good comparison is a garden hose that is already full of water. When you open the tap, water comes out of the far end immediately, not because the water from the tap has rushed down the hose, but because the water already in the hose is pushed along. Another comparison is a bicycle chain: when you press the pedal, the back wheel turns at once, even though each link moves only a short distance. The energy travels quickly; the charges themselves move slowly and are never used up.

### subtopic_2 — Closed and Open Circuits
**Assistant:** How does an earth leakage unit know to switch off when someone gets a shock?

**Tutor:** It compares the current going out to the house along the live wire with the current coming back along the neutral wire, and switches off when they are not equal. In a healthy circuit, every bit of charge that flows out to an appliance along the live wire returns along the neutral wire, because the circuit is a closed loop. So the current in the two wires is exactly the same. Inside the earth leakage unit, both wires pass through a small ring of iron, wound with a coil. The currents in the live and neutral wires flow in opposite directions, so their magnetic effects on the ring cancel out exactly, and nothing happens. But if some current escapes from the loop, for example because a person touches a live wire and current flows through their body into the ground, or because damaged insulation lets current leak to an earthed metal casing, then less current returns along the neutral than went out along the live. The magnetic effects no longer cancel, a small current is produced in the coil, and this triggers a switch that opens the circuit, cutting off the supply in about thirty thousandths of a second. In South Africa, earth leakage units in homes are set to trip when the leakage reaches thirty milliamperes, a current small enough that a healthy person is very unlikely to be killed by it for such a short time. That is why they have been required in new South African electrical installations for decades, and why many lives have been saved by them. A test button on the front lets you check it works; pressing it should switch the power off.

### subtopic_3 — Input, Transfer and Output
**Assistant:** Why does a filament bulb waste so much energy as heat, while an LED does not?

**Tutor:** Because a filament bulb makes light by getting extremely hot, and most of the radiation from a hot object is invisible infrared, while an LED produces light directly, without needing to be hot. Inside a filament bulb is a very thin coil of tungsten wire. When current flows, the electrons collide with the tungsten atoms and transfer energy, heating the filament to about 2 500 degrees Celsius, so hot that it glows white. A glowing hot object gives out a spread of radiation, from infrared through visible light and a little ultraviolet. At the temperature of a filament, the peak of that spread lies in the infrared, which we cannot see but feel as heat. Only a small fraction, around five percent, falls in the visible range. To make more of the radiation visible, the filament would need to be as hot as the surface of the Sun, about 5 500 degrees, but tungsten melts at about 3 400 degrees. An LED, a light-emitting diode, works in a completely different way. It is made of special layers of semiconductor material. When electrons cross from one layer to the other, each one drops to a lower energy level and gives out its energy as a tiny packet of light of a particular colour. The LED is designed so that the colour is visible light, and very little energy is turned into heat. White LEDs use a blue LED coated with a substance that turns some of the blue into yellow and other colours, which together look white. A good LED lamp uses about a sixth of the electrical power of a filament bulb giving the same amount of light.

### subtopic_4 — Electricity from Power Station to Plug
**Assistant:** Why does transmitting electricity at very high voltage reduce the energy wasted in the wires?

**Tutor:** Because at a higher voltage, the same amount of energy per second can be delivered with a much smaller current, and the heat wasted in a wire depends strongly on the current. Every wire has some resistance, so when a current flows through it, some energy is transferred to heat in the wire. The amount of heat produced is proportional to the square of the current: double the current and the heating in the wire becomes four times as great; multiply the current by ten and the heating becomes a hundred times as great. The power delivered by an electrical supply equals the voltage multiplied by the current. So to deliver a fixed amount of power to a city, you can use a low voltage with a large current or a high voltage with a small current. If the voltage is raised a hundred times, the current needed falls to a hundredth, and the heat wasted in the transmission lines falls to a ten-thousandth. That is why Eskom's main transmission lines run at 400 000 or even 765 000 volts on the long routes from the power stations in Mpumalanga to the Western Cape and other regions. Transformers make this possible. A transformer at the power station steps the voltage up for transmission; transformers at substations and on street poles step it down again in stages to the 230 volts used in homes, which is much safer to have inside a building. Even with high-voltage transmission, a small percentage of the energy is lost as heat along the way. Very high voltages are extremely dangerous, which is why pylons are tall, the wires are far apart, and people must never climb pylons or fly kites near power lines.
