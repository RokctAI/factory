# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the components from which circuits are built and the standard symbols used to draw them. The anchor is a box of parts on a science bench: a few 1.5 volt cells in holders, lengths of red and black insulated wire with crocodile clips, a push switch, a couple of small bulbs in holders, some small striped resistors and a buzzer. Two learners are asked to build the same circuit from a written description, and they produce two different-looking arrangements; one works and one does not. When they are given a circuit diagram instead, drawn with standard symbols, they build identical circuits within minutes. The governing idea is that each component in a circuit has a specific job, conducting, switching, supplying energy or controlling current, and that a circuit diagram uses agreed symbols and straight lines to show exactly how the components are connected, so that anyone anywhere can build and understand the same circuit. Cells and batteries, conducting wires, switches, resistors, the standard circuit symbols and the rules for drawing and reading circuit diagrams are examinable, and they are used throughout the rest of this term and in Grade 9 and Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: Cells and Batteries

A cell is a device that transfers stored chemical energy into electrical energy. Inside a typical torch cell, chemicals react and push electrons out of one terminal and around the circuit to the other terminal. Every cell has two terminals: the positive terminal, usually the end with a small raised button, marked with a plus sign, and the negative terminal, the flat end, marked with a minus sign.

The push that a cell gives to the charges in a circuit is measured in volts, symbol V. Ordinary zinc-carbon and alkaline cells, the sizes called AA, AAA, C and D, each give about 1.5 volts. The size of a cell does not change its voltage; a large D cell and a small AAA cell both give 1.5 volts, but the larger one contains more chemicals and so can supply energy for longer. Rechargeable nickel-metal hydride cells give about 1.2 volts, and a lithium-ion cell, as in a cellphone, gives about 3.7 volts.

Strictly, a battery is two or more cells connected together. Connecting cells end to end, positive terminal to negative terminal, adds their voltages: two 1.5 volt cells give 3 volts, which is the arrangement in most torches, and four give 6 volts. A rectangular 9 volt battery contains six small 1.5 volt cells joined end to end inside its case. A car battery contains six lead-acid cells of about 2 volts each, giving about 12 volts. In everyday speech people call a single cell a battery, but in science the distinction is useful.

If a cell is put in the wrong way round in a group of cells, its push opposes the others, and the total voltage drops. That is why battery holders show the correct direction with plus and minus signs.

Cells contain chemicals that can be harmful to people and the environment, especially those containing mercury, cadmium or lead. They should not be thrown into ordinary rubbish or into a fire, but taken to collection points at many supermarkets and hardware stores for recycling.

The questions for this section are with you now: the energy transfer in a cell, the positive and negative terminals, the voltage of common cells, the difference between a cell and a battery, the effect of connecting cells in a row, and why cells should be recycled.

## Subtopic: Conducting Wires and Switches

Conducting wires join the components of a circuit and provide a path of low resistance for the charges. They are made of copper, because copper is an excellent conductor, is easily drawn into thin wires, bends without breaking and does not corrode easily. Aluminium, which is lighter and cheaper, is used for overhead power lines. The copper is surrounded by a layer of insulation, usually PVC plastic, which stops charges from leaking out and prevents accidental contact with other wires or with people. Different colours of insulation identify wires: in South African house wiring, the live wire is brown, the neutral blue, and the earth green and yellow striped, while older installations used red for live and black for neutral.

Thicker wires let charges flow more easily than thin wires of the same material and length, so they waste less energy as heat and can carry larger currents safely. That is why the cable to a stove or a geyser is much thicker than the cable to a bedside lamp. A wire that is too thin for the current it carries can overheat, melt its insulation and start a fire.

Crocodile clips, spring clips and terminal blocks make temporary or permanent connections between wires and components. Every connection must be clean and tight; a loose or corroded connection can stop a circuit or create heat.

A switch opens and closes a circuit. A simple push switch or toggle switch has two metal contacts that touch when the switch is on and are separated when it is off. A push-to-make switch, as in a doorbell, closes the circuit only while it is held down. A two-way switch allows a light to be turned on or off from two places, such as the top and bottom of a staircase. A switch can be placed anywhere in a single-loop circuit, because breaking the loop at any point stops the flow throughout it. In house wiring, however, switches are always placed in the live wire, so that when the switch is off, the fitting beyond it is not connected to the live supply, making it safer to change a bulb.

The questions for this section are with you now: why copper is used for wires, the purpose of insulation, the South African wire colours, why thicker wires are used for high currents, the types of switches, and why switches are placed in the live wire.

## Subtopic: Resistors and Other Components

A resistor is a component that resists, or reduces, the flow of charge in a circuit. All components and even wires have some resistance, but a resistor is made specially to have a particular, known resistance. Resistance is measured in ohms, symbol the Greek capital letter omega.

Small fixed resistors used in electronics are made from carbon or a thin metal film on a ceramic core, with coloured bands painted around them to show their resistance. A variable resistor, or rheostat, has a sliding contact or a turning knob that changes the length of resistance wire the current passes through, so the resistance can be changed. Variable resistors are used as dimmer switches, volume controls on older radios, and speed controls on toy cars and fans.

Resistors are used to control the current in a circuit, for example to protect an LED from too large a current, which would destroy it. They can also be useful in their own right, because a resistor transfers electrical energy into heat: the elements of kettles, toasters, stoves, irons, hair dryers and geysers are all resistors, made of special wire, often an alloy of nickel and chromium called nichrome, which gets hot without melting or corroding. The filament of a bulb is also a resistor, so thin and hot that it glows.

Other components you will meet include: a light-emitting diode, or LED, which gives out light when current flows through it in one direction only; a buzzer, which produces sound; a motor, which turns; a fuse, a thin wire that melts and breaks the circuit if the current becomes dangerously large; an ammeter, which measures current and is connected in the loop; and a voltmeter, which measures voltage and is connected across a component.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a resistor does, the unit of resistance, the difference between a fixed and a variable resistor with uses of each, how heating elements use resistance, and the jobs of an LED, a fuse, an ammeter and a voltmeter.

## Subtopic: Drawing Circuit Diagrams

A circuit diagram is a simplified drawing of a circuit that uses standard symbols for components and straight lines for wires. It shows how the components are connected, not what they look like or where they sit on the bench.

The standard symbols, as used internationally and in South African textbooks, include the following. A cell is drawn as two parallel lines, a long thin line for the positive terminal and a short thick line for the negative terminal. A battery is two or more cell symbols joined, or a cell symbol with a dashed line between the plates. An open switch is a line with a gap and a short line hinged up at an angle, like a gate left open; a closed switch has the hinged line lying across the gap. A light bulb is a circle with a cross inside it. A fixed resistor is a small rectangle; a variable resistor is the same rectangle with a diagonal arrow through it. A fuse is a rectangle with a line running through it from end to end. An ammeter is a circle with the letter A, a voltmeter a circle with V, and a motor a circle with M. An LED is a triangle pointing to a short bar, with two small arrows pointing outward for the light given off.

Rules for drawing a good circuit diagram: use a ruler and draw the wires as straight lines, with right-angled corners, usually forming a rectangle. Leave no gaps in the lines, except in an open switch, because a gap means the circuit is open. Draw the symbols in the correct places in the loop. Where wires join, a solid dot marks the junction. Label components with their names or values when it helps.

The direction of current in a circuit diagram is shown by an arrow on a wire. By convention, the current is drawn as flowing out of the positive terminal of the cell, around the circuit, and back into the negative terminal. This is called conventional current. It was agreed before electrons were discovered; electrons actually flow the opposite way, from negative to positive, but the convention is still used everywhere.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: drawing a gap in a wire where none was intended. Two: drawing the cell symbol with the long and short lines the wrong way round for the positive and negative terminals. Three: calling a single cell a battery in a scientific answer. Four: thinking the position of a switch in a single loop matters; it does not, as long as it is in the loop. Five: drawing wires as curved, wobbly lines instead of straight, ruled lines.

Layout for marks: rectangle, ruled lines, standard symbols, no unintended gaps, conventional current arrow from positive to negative.

The questions for this section are with you now: the symbols for a cell, battery, open and closed switch, bulb, resistor, variable resistor, fuse, ammeter, voltmeter and LED, the rules for drawing circuit diagrams, conventional current, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a box of circuit parts, a circuit drawn like a map, and building a circuit from a diagram — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Box of Circuit Parts

Open a box of circuit parts on the science bench and every piece has a job.

The cells are the pushers. Each torch cell gives a push of about 1.5 volts, whether it is big or small. Big ones just last longer. Line two up, plus to minus, and you get 3 volts. Two or more cells joined together make a battery.

The wires are the roads. They are made of copper, which lets charges through easily, and coated in plastic, which keeps the charges in and keeps your fingers safe. Thick wires carry big currents without getting hot.

The switch is the gate. Closed, the charges go through. Open, they stop.

The resistors are the speed bumps. They make it harder for the charges to flow, and they turn some energy into heat. That is useful in a kettle or a toaster, where the heating element is just a resistor that gets very hot. A variable resistor is a speed bump you can adjust, like a dimmer switch or a fan speed knob.

Keep the box of parts in mind and try the questions for this part: what each part does, the voltage of a cell, and what a battery really is.

## Subtopic: Drawing a Circuit Like a Map

Drawing every wire and bulb exactly as it looks would be slow and messy. So scientists use a kind of map with simple symbols, called a circuit diagram.

A cell is a long thin line next to a short thick line. The long one is positive. A bulb is a circle with a cross. A switch is a line with a gate that is open or shut. A resistor is a little rectangle. Add an arrow through it, and it becomes a variable resistor. A circle with an A is an ammeter, a circle with a V is a voltmeter, and a circle with an M is a motor.

Draw wires as straight lines with a ruler, with square corners, usually making a rectangle. Never leave a gap unless you mean a switch is open, because a gap means the circuit does not work.

Current is drawn flowing out of the positive end of the cell, round the loop, and back into the negative end.

Hold the map in mind and try this part's questions: the symbol for a cell, a bulb and a switch, and the rules for drawing a diagram.

## Subtopic: Building a Circuit from a Diagram

Here is a challenge. A diagram shows a rectangle with two cells, a switch, a bulb and a resistor, all in one loop. Build it.

Start at the positive end of the cells. Follow the line to the switch, then the bulb, then the resistor, and back to the negative end of the cells. Connect each part with a wire in that order. Close the switch, and the bulb lights. Take out the resistor and connect the bulb straight to the cells, and the bulb gets brighter, because the speed bump is gone and more current flows.

With a diagram, anyone anywhere in the world will build exactly the same circuit. That is the power of standard symbols.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Cells supply energy, about 1.5 volts each; two or more cells make a battery. Copper wires with plastic insulation carry the current; thicker wires carry more. Switches open and close the circuit. Resistors reduce current and can turn energy into heat; variable resistors can be adjusted. Circuit diagrams use standard symbols, ruled straight lines and no unintended gaps, with conventional current from positive to negative.

Hold the parts, the map and the build, and take the final questions of the lesson: drawing a circuit diagram, identifying symbols, and what removing a resistor does.
