### subtopic_1 — Electric Current as Flowing Charge
**Assistant:** Is it the current or the voltage that makes an electric shock dangerous?

**Tutor:** It is the current flowing through the body that does the harm, but the voltage decides how large that current can be, so both matter. Your nerves and muscles, including your heart, are controlled by tiny electrical signals. When an outside current passes through your body, it interferes with those signals. A current of about one milliampere through the body can just be felt as a tingle. Around ten milliamperes, muscles start to contract so strongly that a person may be unable to let go of the wire. Currents of about fifty to one hundred milliamperes flowing across the chest for a fraction of a second can make the heart lose its rhythm, which can be fatal. These are tiny currents compared with the nine amperes in a kettle. How much current flows through your body depends on the voltage and on your body's resistance. Dry skin has a high resistance, several thousand ohms or more, which limits the current from a low voltage, which is why touching both terminals of a 1.5 volt cell or even a 12 volt car battery is harmless. The 230 volt mains supply is a much bigger push, and wet skin, sweat or standing barefoot on a wet floor greatly lowers your resistance, so the current through your body can easily reach dangerous levels. Static shocks, though they may involve thousands of volts, deliver so little charge so quickly that the current lasts too short a time to harm the heart. That is why the earth leakage unit in a South African home is designed to cut the power when about thirty milliamperes leaks away, and why you should never touch switches or appliances with wet hands.

### subtopic_2 — Measuring Current with an Ammeter
**Assistant:** Why must an ammeter have a very low resistance, and what happens if it doesn't?

**Tutor:** Because the ammeter becomes part of the circuit it is measuring, and if it had a large resistance, it would reduce the very current it is trying to measure. When you break a circuit and insert an ammeter in series, every charge in that part of the circuit must pass through the meter. If the meter added a significant resistance, the total resistance of the circuit would increase and the current would fall, so the meter would show a reading smaller than the current that flows when the meter is not there. A good ammeter is designed so that its resistance is tiny compared with the rest of the circuit, often less than one ohm, so its effect on the current is negligible. Inside a digital ammeter, the current actually flows through a very small, precisely known resistor called a shunt, and the meter measures the tiny voltage across the shunt to work out the current. The low resistance also explains the warning never to connect an ammeter across a cell or across a component. If you connect a low-resistance ammeter directly between the terminals of a cell, you have effectively made a short circuit: there is almost no resistance in the path, so a very large current flows. This can blow the fuse inside the meter, damage its shunt, heat and drain the cell quickly and, with a powerful supply such as a car battery, even cause burns or a fire. Voltmeters are the opposite: they are connected across components and have a very high resistance, so they draw almost no current from the circuit. Mixing up the two is one of the most common mistakes in the school laboratory.

### subtopic_3 — What Affects Resistance
**Assistant:** If metals get more resistance when hot, are there materials whose resistance drops to zero when cold?

**Tutor:** Yes. Certain materials, called superconductors, lose all their electrical resistance completely when they are cooled below a particular temperature. This was discovered in 1911 by the Dutch physicist Heike Kamerlingh Onnes, who cooled mercury with liquid helium to about minus 269 degrees Celsius, just four degrees above absolute zero, and found that its resistance suddenly vanished. In an ordinary metal, the resistance falls steadily as the temperature drops, because the atoms vibrate less and the electrons collide with them less often, but it never quite reaches zero, because of impurities and imperfections in the metal. In a superconductor, something entirely different happens below the critical temperature: the electrons pair up and move through the material in a coordinated way that is not disturbed by the atoms at all. A current started in a loop of superconducting wire has been observed to keep flowing for years with no energy source, because no energy is lost as heat. Superconductors have important uses. The very strong electromagnets in hospital MRI scanners are made from superconducting wire cooled by liquid helium, so they can carry enormous currents without overheating. The giant magnets that steer particles around the Large Hadron Collider in Switzerland are superconducting, and some experimental high-speed trains float above their tracks using superconducting magnets. The main difficulty is the cold: most superconductors work only far below minus 200 degrees. Since 1986, materials have been found that superconduct at higher temperatures, some above the boiling point of liquid nitrogen, minus 196 degrees, which is much cheaper to use. Finding a superconductor that works at room temperature would transform power transmission, with no energy lost in the wires.

### subtopic_4 — Resistance Controls Current and Provides Useful Output
**Assistant:** Why does a short circuit in a house trip the circuit breaker instead of burning the house down?

**Tutor:** Because the circuit breaker detects the sudden huge current and opens the circuit within a fraction of a second, before the wires have time to overheat. In normal use, the current in a household circuit is limited by the resistance of the appliances connected to it: a kettle element, a lamp filament, a fridge motor. In a short circuit, for example when damaged insulation lets the live wire touch the neutral wire or an earthed metal box, the current bypasses the appliance completely and flows through a path of almost no resistance. With 230 volts pushing through such a tiny resistance, the current can rise to hundreds or even thousands of amperes almost instantly. If nothing stopped it, the wires would heat up very rapidly, the insulation would melt and catch fire, and sparks could ignite the surroundings. Every circuit in a South African distribution board is protected by a circuit breaker matched to the thickness of its wires. A circuit breaker contains two mechanisms. One is an electromagnet: a very large current, as in a short circuit, makes the electromagnet strong enough to pull a lever and snap the contacts apart almost immediately. The other is a bimetallic strip, which you met in the expansion lesson: a smaller but persistent overload, such as too many heaters on one circuit, gradually heats the strip until it bends and trips the switch. Unlike a fuse, which melts and must be replaced, a circuit breaker can be reset by pushing its switch back up once the fault has been fixed. If a breaker keeps tripping, it is warning that something is wrong, and the fault should be found by a qualified electrician, not hidden by using a bigger breaker.
