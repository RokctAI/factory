### subtopic_1 — The Heating Effect
**Assistant:** Why does doubling the current produce four times as much heat, not twice as much?

**Tutor:** Because doubling the current doubles two things at once: the number of electrons passing through each second, and the energy that each of them gives up as it passes through the resistance. Think of the energy transferred in a resistor as the number of charges flowing through it each second multiplied by the energy each charge loses on the way through. If you double the current through the same resistor, twice as many charges pass every second, which on its own would double the heating. But to push twice the current through the same resistance, you need twice the voltage, which means each charge is given twice the energy by the source and loses twice the energy in the resistor. Twice as many charges, each losing twice as much energy, gives four times the energy per second. Scientists write this as the heating power equals the current squared multiplied by the resistance. Triple the current and the heating becomes nine times as great; multiply it by ten and the heating becomes one hundred times as great. This square relationship has big practical consequences. It is why the huge current in a short circuit produces such intense heating so quickly, why overloading a cable with too many appliances is so dangerous, and why power companies transmit electricity at very high voltages and low currents, since halving the current in a long transmission line cuts the heat wasted in it to a quarter. It also explains why a fuse works so reliably. Below its rating, the fuse wire warms only gently, but as the current climbs above the rating, the heating rises steeply, and the thin wire reaches its melting point within a fraction of a second during a serious fault.

### subtopic_2 — Fuses and Short Circuits
**Assistant:** Why is it so dangerous to replace a blown fuse with a piece of wire or a higher-rated fuse?

**Tutor:** Because a fuse that blew was doing its job, warning that the current had become dangerously large, and bypassing it removes the only protection against the heating that could start a fire. A fuse is chosen so that it melts at a current smaller than the current that would overheat the wires it is protecting. The wiring in a lamp cord might safely carry three amperes; a three ampere fuse in the plug will melt before the cord gets dangerously hot. If the fuse blows, it means that something allowed too much current to flow: perhaps a short circuit inside the appliance, damaged insulation, water in the plug, or too many appliances on one circuit. If you replace the fuse with a thirteen ampere fuse, a fault current of, say, ten amperes would no longer blow the fuse. That ten amperes would flow through a cord designed for three, producing about eleven times the safe heating, because heating depends on the square of the current. The cord could get hot enough to melt its insulation and ignite carpets or curtains, with nothing to stop it. Replacing the fuse with a piece of wire, a nail or tin foil is even worse, because a thick piece of metal will not melt until the current is enormous, by which time the wiring may already be on fire. Many fatal house fires and electrocutions have been caused in exactly this way. The correct response to a blown fuse or a tripping circuit breaker is to switch off and unplug the appliance, find and fix the fault, ideally with the help of a qualified electrician, and then replace the fuse with one of the correct rating.

### subtopic_3 — The Magnetic Effect and Electromagnets
**Assistant:** How does an electric doorbell keep ringing for as long as you press the button?

**Tutor:** It switches itself on and off many times a second, using an electromagnet that breaks its own circuit every time it pulls. Inside a traditional electric bell is an electromagnet, a coil of wire around an iron core. Near it is a springy strip of soft iron called the armature, with a small hammer on its end, resting next to a metal bell or gong. The armature also carries a contact that touches a fixed contact screw when the armature is at rest. When you press the bell push, you close the circuit: current flows from the battery or transformer through the bell push, through the contact screw and the armature, and through the coil of the electromagnet. The electromagnet switches on and pulls the iron armature towards it, so the hammer strikes the gong. But as the armature moves, its contact moves away from the contact screw, which breaks the circuit. The current stops, the electromagnet switches off, and the spring pulls the armature back. As it returns, its contact touches the screw again, the circuit is completed, the electromagnet switches on, the armature is pulled, the hammer strikes again, and so on, many times a second, for as long as you hold down the button. That is why the sound of a traditional bell is a continuous rapid ringing rather than a single ding. The same make-and-break principle was used in old car horns and buzzers. Many modern doorbells use electronic circuits instead, but the electromagnet remains at the heart of relays, speakers and motors. A relay, for example, uses a small current in an electromagnet to pull a switch that controls a much larger current, such as the starter motor of a car.

### subtopic_4 — The Chemical Effect
**Assistant:** Why does electrolysis of water produce exactly twice as much hydrogen as oxygen by volume?

**Tutor:** Because every water molecule contains two hydrogen atoms for each oxygen atom, and equal volumes of different gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules. When water is split by electrolysis, every water molecule that is decomposed gives two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. The hydrogen atoms pair up into hydrogen molecules, each containing two hydrogen atoms, and the oxygen atoms pair up into oxygen molecules, each containing two oxygen atoms. So for every two water molecules that are split, there are four hydrogen atoms, which make two hydrogen molecules, and two oxygen atoms, which make one oxygen molecule. That gives two molecules of hydrogen for every one molecule of oxygen. In the early 1800s, the Italian scientist Amedeo Avogadro proposed that equal volumes of any gases, measured at the same temperature and pressure, contain the same number of molecules, regardless of which gas they are. That idea has been confirmed many times. So twice as many hydrogen molecules occupy twice the volume, and in a Hofmann voltameter, the tall glass apparatus often used to demonstrate electrolysis, the hydrogen collecting above the negative electrode reaches twice the height of the oxygen above the positive electrode. Historically, this result was important evidence that water is H2O, two hydrogen atoms bonded to one oxygen atom, rather than one of each. It also connects to a modern technology: hydrogen produced by electrolysis using electricity from solar or wind power, called green hydrogen, may become an important clean fuel, and South Africa, with abundant sunshine and wind, is exploring producing it for export, particularly in the Northern Cape.
