# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains what happens when static charge escapes from an object, from the tiny crackle of a jersey to the giant spark of lightning. The anchor is a summer afternoon on the Highveld. Towering clouds build up over Gauteng, the sky darkens, and suddenly a brilliant, branching line of light joins cloud to ground, followed seconds later by a deafening crack and rumble. That flash is the same process, on a vastly bigger scale, as the small blue spark and sharp sting you sometimes feel when touching a car door after sliding across the seat. The governing idea is that discharge is the loss of static charge from a charged object, either slowly and harmlessly or suddenly as a spark; that a spark occurs when the charge becomes so large that it can force its way through the air, briefly turning the air into a conductor; and that lightning is a natural discharge between charged clouds and the ground or between parts of clouds. Discharge, sparks, the cause of lightning and thunder, and the dangers of static and lightning with ways to stay safe, are examinable, and they connect to electric current and potential difference later in this term.

## Subtopic: What Discharge Is

A charged object does not stay charged forever. Discharge is the process in which a charged object loses its extra charge and returns towards being neutral. A negatively charged object discharges when its extra electrons flow away; a positively charged object discharges when electrons flow onto it to replace the ones it lost.

Discharge can happen slowly or suddenly.

Slow discharge happens when charge leaks away gradually. A charged balloon slowly loses its charge to the surrounding air, especially when the air is humid, because a thin film of water on its surface and charged particles in the air allow the charge to escape. That is why a balloon stuck to a wall eventually falls off. Touching a charged object with a conductor connected to the ground, earthing it, discharges it quickly but without a spark if the contact is made firmly.

Sudden discharge happens when a large charge escapes all at once, through a spark. Sparks happen when the charge on an object is large enough, and a conductor such as a finger, a metal object or the ground comes close enough, for the charge to jump across the gap through the air.

Earthing is the most common way of discharging objects safely. Electrical appliances with metal casings are earthed through the third, longest pin on a South African plug, so that if a fault causes the casing to become charged, the charge flows harmlessly to the ground. Workers repairing computers wear earthed wrist straps, so that any static charge on their bodies flows away gradually instead of sparking into delicate electronic parts, which can be damaged by sparks far too small to feel.

Discharge also explains why a metal object held in the hand cannot be charged by rubbing: the charge discharges continuously through the body to the ground as fast as it is produced.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of discharge, how negative and positive objects discharge, the difference between slow and sudden discharge, and why appliances and electronics workers are earthed.

## Subtopic: Sparks and Shocks

Air is normally a very good insulator, which is why charge can build up on objects in dry weather without escaping. But air has a limit. When the charge on an object is large enough, the electric force on the few charged particles that are always present in air becomes strong enough to accelerate them violently. These fast particles crash into air molecules and knock electrons off them, creating more charged particles, which in turn crash into more molecules. In a split second, a narrow channel of air becomes filled with charged particles and turns into a conductor. Charge rushes through this channel, the spark. The air in the channel is heated so suddenly that it glows, giving the flash of light, and expands so rapidly that it makes a crack of sound.

Dry air breaks down in this way when the electrical push across it reaches about three million volts for every metre of gap, or about thirty thousand volts for every centimetre. So a spark one centimetre long from your finger to a door handle means your body had been charged to tens of thousands of volts. That sounds dangerous, but static shocks from the body are usually harmless because the amount of charge involved is tiny and it flows for only a fraction of a millionth of a second.

Everyday static shocks occur when your body has been charged and you touch a conductor. Walking across a synthetic carpet in rubber-soled shoes, sliding across a car seat, or taking off a fleece jacket can charge your body. When you then reach towards a metal door handle, a tap or a car door, the charge sparks across the last few millimetres. You feel a sharp sting, sometimes hear a snap and in the dark may see a tiny blue spark. Touching the metal first with a key held firmly in your hand lets the spark jump from the key instead of your fingertip, where nerves are concentrated, so you feel almost nothing.

Sparks from static are not always harmless. Where there are flammable gases, vapours or dusts, a spark can start a fire or explosion. Petrol vapour at filling stations, gas leaks, flour and maize-meal dust in mills, coal dust in mines, and fine powders in factories are all hazards. That is why fuel tankers are earthed while unloading, why aircraft are earthed during refuelling, why engines must be switched off and smoking is banned at fuel pumps, and why mills control dust and earth their machinery.

The questions for this section are with you now: why air is normally an insulator, how a spark forms, the voltage needed per centimetre, why everyday shocks are usually harmless, and why sparks are dangerous near fuel and dust.

## Subtopic: Lightning

Lightning is a giant electrical discharge in the atmosphere. It begins inside a thunderstorm cloud, a cumulonimbus, a towering cloud that can reach more than ten kilometres high, with strong currents of air rising and falling inside it.

Within the cloud, tiny ice crystals carried upward by rising air collide with larger, soft hail pellets that are falling. In each collision, charge is transferred, much as in rubbing: in the cold upper parts of the cloud, the small ice crystals generally become positive and the heavier pellets negative. The rising air carries the light positive crystals to the top of the cloud, while the heavier negative pellets collect lower down. The bottom of the cloud therefore becomes strongly negative and the top positive.

The negative base of the cloud repels electrons in the ground beneath it, so the surface of the ground, and tall objects on it, become positively charged by induced charge separation. The charges build up until the air between them can no longer insulate. Then a faint channel of charged air, called a stepped leader, works its way down from the cloud in a series of jumps, branching as it goes. When it gets close to the ground, a streamer rises from a tall object to meet it. The moment they connect, a huge current surges along the channel, the return stroke, which is the brilliant flash we see. Several strokes may flicker along the same channel in less than a second.

A typical lightning stroke carries a current of about thirty thousand amperes, thousands of times the current in a household appliance, for a tiny fraction of a second. The channel is only a few centimetres wide, but it is heated to around thirty thousand degrees Celsius, about five times the temperature of the surface of the Sun. Most lightning, in fact, never reaches the ground; it flashes within a cloud or between clouds.

Thunder is the sound of lightning. The air in the channel is heated so quickly that it expands explosively, sending out a shock wave that we hear as a sharp crack nearby or a long rumble further away, as sound arrives from different parts of the long, crooked channel. Light travels about a million times faster than sound, so we see the flash almost instantly but hear the thunder later. Sound travels about one kilometre in three seconds, so counting the seconds between flash and thunder and dividing by three gives the approximate distance of the lightning in kilometres.

The questions for this section are with you now: how charge separates in a thundercloud, how the ground becomes charged, the stepped leader and return stroke, the current and temperature of lightning, what causes thunder, and how to estimate the distance of a storm.

## Subtopic: Lightning Safety and Protection

South Africa has some of the highest lightning activity in the world, especially over the eastern Highveld, KwaZulu-Natal and the Eastern Cape, mostly in summer afternoons. Lightning is estimated to kill more than a hundred people in South Africa each year, many of them in rural areas, outdoors in open fields or sheltering under trees, and it injures many more and kills large numbers of cattle.

Lightning tends to strike the tallest object in an area, and to travel through the best conductors. Safety rules follow from this. If you hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck, so go indoors immediately into a solid building, or into a car or minibus taxi with a metal roof and closed windows. A metal vehicle body conducts the current around the outside and into the ground, protecting the people inside. Stay indoors until about thirty minutes after the last thunder. Avoid open fields, hilltops, soccer fields and golf courses, where you may be the tallest object. Never shelter under an isolated tree, because the lightning may strike the tree and jump to you or travel through the ground. Keep away from water, metal fences and wire washing lines. Indoors, avoid baths and showers, and do not touch corded phones or plugged-in appliances during a storm, because a strike on the power or phone lines can travel into the building. If caught in the open, crouch low on the balls of your feet with your feet together and your head down, keeping contact with the ground small, and do not lie flat.

Buildings are protected by lightning conductors, invented by Benjamin Franklin in the 1750s. A pointed metal rod on the highest point of the building is connected by a thick copper or aluminium strip down the outside wall to a metal plate or rod buried deep in the ground. If lightning strikes, the current flows through the conductor to the Earth instead of through the building. The pointed tip also allows some charge to leak away gradually from the ground, reducing the build-up. Many rural schools and homes in lightning-prone areas of South Africa have been fitted with lightning protection systems for this reason. Surge protectors on plugs protect electronic equipment from sudden voltage surges caused by nearby strikes.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying lightning never strikes the same place twice; tall buildings are struck many times a year. Two: saying rubber tyres protect you in a car; the metal body does. Three: saying it is safe to shelter under a tree; it is one of the most dangerous places. Four: saying thunder causes lightning; lightning causes thunder. Five: saying static shocks are dangerous because of the high voltage; they are harmless because the charge is tiny, unless there is fuel or dust nearby.

Layout for marks: describe charge separation, then the build-up of charge, then the sudden discharge through ionised air, then the light and sound.

The questions for this section are with you now: why lightning strikes tall objects, the main safety rules, how a lightning conductor works, why a car is safe, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a zap from a car door, a giant spark in the sky, and a Highveld storm — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Zap from a Car Door

You slide across a car seat, climb out and touch the door. Zap! A sharp sting on your fingertip. What happened?

Sliding across the seat rubbed electrons onto your body, or off it, so you became charged. Air is usually a good insulator, so the charge stayed on you. But when your finger came very close to the metal door, the charge was big enough to force its way across the tiny gap of air. That sudden jump is a spark. The air in its path got so hot that it glowed and crackled.

Losing your charge like that is called discharge. It can happen suddenly, as a spark, or slowly, as charge leaks away into damp air. A balloon on a wall falls off when its charge slowly leaks away.

A static zap may be thousands of volts, but it is so tiny and so quick that it does not hurt you. A spark near petrol or fine dust, though, can start a fire. That is why tankers are earthed when they unload fuel.

Tip: touch the car door with a key first, and the spark jumps from the key instead of your finger.

## Subtopic: A Giant Spark in the Sky

Lightning is the same thing as that car door zap, just unimaginably bigger.

Inside a towering storm cloud, wind whips ice bits up and down. They crash into each other and charge gets rubbed off, just like a balloon on your hair. The bottom of the cloud ends up negative. That negative cloud makes the ground below it positive.

When there is too much charge, it forces a path through the air. A faint zigzag path creeps down from the cloud, a spark reaches up from a tall tree or building to meet it, and then, flash! A huge current rushes through. The air in that path gets hotter than the surface of the Sun.

That super-hot air expands with a bang, which is thunder. You see the flash first, because light is much faster than sound. Count the seconds until the thunder and divide by three. That is roughly how many kilometres away the lightning is.

## Subtopic: Staying Safe in a Highveld Storm

South Africa gets lots of lightning, especially on summer afternoons in Gauteng, Mpumalanga and KwaZulu-Natal. Lightning hits the tallest thing around, so here is how to stay safe.

If you can hear thunder, go inside a solid building or a car with a metal roof. The metal of the car carries the lightning around you and into the ground. Stay away from open fields, hilltops and water. Never shelter under a lone tree. Stay off wire fences and washing lines. Indoors, do not bath or use plugged-in things. Wait half an hour after the last thunder before going out.

Buildings use lightning conductors: a metal rod on the roof joined by a thick metal strip to a plate in the ground. The lightning takes the easy metal road down to the ground, instead of going through the building.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Discharge is the loss of static charge, slowly by leaking or suddenly by a spark. A spark happens when the charge is big enough to force a path through the air, which glows and cracks. Lightning is a huge spark between a charged cloud and the ground, and thunder is the sound of the heated air expanding. Lightning strikes tall objects, so stay indoors or in a metal car, avoid trees and open ground, and protect buildings with lightning conductors.
