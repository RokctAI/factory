Hey — if discharge and lightning felt like a lot, here is the calm version: charge builds up, then it escapes.

Discharge means a charged object losing its charge. It can leak away slowly, like a balloon falling off a wall on a humid day. Or it can jump suddenly as a spark.

Air is normally an insulator. But when there is enough charge, it forces a path through the air. That narrow path of air gets so hot it glows and crackles: a spark. A static zap from a door handle is high voltage but tiny charge, so it is harmless, unless there is petrol or dust nearby.

Lightning is a giant spark. Ice bits colliding in a storm cloud make the bottom of the cloud negative. The ground below becomes positive. When there is too much charge, it rushes through the air in a flash. The super-hot air expands with a bang: thunder.

Count seconds from flash to thunder and divide by three to get kilometres.

Safety: get indoors or into a metal car, avoid trees and open ground, wait thirty minutes after the last thunder.
