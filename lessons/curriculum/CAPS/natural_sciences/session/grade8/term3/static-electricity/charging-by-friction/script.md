# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains where static electricity comes from: the transfer of electrons between two materials when they are rubbed together. The anchor is a balloon rubbed briskly on dry hair on a winter morning. When the balloon is pulled away, strands of hair rise and follow it, and the balloon then sticks to a wall for minutes without any glue. Nothing visible was added to either the balloon or the hair. Something invisible moved from one to the other during the rubbing. The governing idea is that all matter contains positive and negative electric charges, normally in equal amounts; that rubbing two different insulating materials together transfers some electrons from one material to the other; and that the material that gains electrons becomes negatively charged while the material that loses them becomes equally positively charged, because charge is neither created nor destroyed, only moved. The structure of the atom as it relates to charge, charging by friction, the triboelectric series, conservation of charge and the difference between conductors and insulators in holding static charge are examinable, and they lead to electrostatics and Coulomb's law in Grade 10 and 11 Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: Atoms and Electric Charge

Electric charge is a basic property of some of the particles that make up atoms. Static electricity has been noticed for thousands of years. The ancient Greeks observed that amber, a hard yellow fossilised tree resin, attracts bits of straw and feathers after it is rubbed with fur, and the Greek word for amber, elektron, gave us the words electricity and electron. There are two kinds of charge, called positive and negative, names chosen by the American scientist Benjamin Franklin in the 1740s.

Recall the structure of the atom from Term 2. At the centre is the nucleus, containing protons, which carry a positive charge, and neutrons, which carry no charge. Around the nucleus move the electrons, which carry a negative charge. The charge of one electron is exactly equal in size to the charge of one proton, but opposite in sign.

In a normal atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons, so the positive and negative charges cancel and the atom as a whole is neutral. Since every object is made of atoms, an ordinary object such as a balloon, a ruler or your hair is normally neutral too: it contains enormous numbers of positive and negative charges, but in equal amounts.

An object becomes charged when this balance is upset. If an object gains extra electrons, it has more negative charge than positive and is negatively charged. If an object loses some electrons, it has more positive charge than negative and is positively charged. Note carefully what moves. The protons are locked tightly in the nucleus, deep inside the atom, and cannot be rubbed off. The electrons, especially the outermost ones, are held less tightly and can be transferred. So objects become charged only by gaining or losing electrons, never by gaining or losing protons. A positively charged object has not gained positive charge; it has lost negative charge.

Charge is measured in a unit called the coulomb, symbol C, named after the French physicist Charles-Augustin de Coulomb. One coulomb is a very large amount of charge for static electricity; a rubbed balloon typically carries less than a millionth of a coulomb, though even that represents many billions of extra electrons.

The questions for this section are with you now: the two kinds of charge, the charges of protons, neutrons and electrons, why ordinary objects are neutral, and why only electrons move when objects become charged.

## Subtopic: Charging by Rubbing

When two different materials are rubbed together, their surfaces come into very close contact at many points. At each point of contact, electrons can move from the atoms of one material to the atoms of the other, because one material holds on to its outer electrons more weakly than the other. Rubbing increases the number of contact points and so increases the number of electrons transferred. When the materials are separated, the electrons that moved stay where they are, and both materials are left charged. This is called charging by friction, and the charge that stays on the objects is called static electricity, static because it stays in one place rather than flowing.

The two materials always end up with opposite charges of equal size. Rub a balloon on dry hair, and electrons move from the hair to the balloon. The balloon gains electrons and becomes negatively charged. The hair loses exactly the same number of electrons and becomes positively charged. Rub a glass rod with a silk cloth and electrons move from the glass to the silk: the glass becomes positive and the silk negative. Rub a polythene or plastic rod with a woollen cloth, and electrons move from the wool to the polythene: the polythene becomes negative and the wool positive.

This illustrates the law of conservation of charge: charge cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred from one object to another. Before rubbing, the total charge of the balloon and hair together was zero. After rubbing, the balloon has a certain negative charge and the hair the same positive charge, so the total is still zero.

Charging by friction happens in everyday life all the time, especially in dry weather. On a dry Highveld winter's day, walking across a nylon carpet in rubber-soled shoes can charge your body. Pulling a woollen jersey over your head makes it crackle and your hair stand up. Plastic wrap clings to itself and to bowls. Clothes taken out of a tumble dryer stick together and crackle, because different fabrics have rubbed against each other as they tumbled. Dust is attracted to a television or computer screen that has been wiped with a dry cloth.

Static charging works poorly in humid weather, as on a summer day in Durban, because a thin film of moisture forms on surfaces and allows the extra charge to leak away almost as fast as it builds up.

The questions for this section are with you now: how rubbing transfers electrons, which material becomes positive and which negative for a balloon and hair, glass and silk, and polythene and wool, the conservation of charge, and why static is weaker in humid weather.

## Subtopic: The Triboelectric Series

Whether a material gains or loses electrons when rubbed depends on what it is rubbed against. Scientists have arranged materials in a list called the triboelectric series, from the Greek word for rubbing. Materials near one end of the list hold their electrons loosely and tend to lose them, becoming positive. Materials near the other end hold electrons tightly and tend to gain them, becoming negative.

A simplified triboelectric series, from the end that tends to become positive to the end that tends to become negative, is: human hair, glass, nylon, wool, silk, paper, cotton, rubber, polyester, polythene, and PVC.

The rule for using it is simple. When two materials from the list are rubbed together, the one nearer the positive end loses electrons and becomes positive, and the one nearer the negative end gains electrons and becomes negative. The further apart the two materials are on the list, the more charge is usually transferred.

So glass rubbed with silk becomes positive, because glass is nearer the positive end than silk. Wool rubbed with polythene becomes positive and the polythene negative. But wool rubbed with glass becomes negative, because glass is nearer the positive end than wool. The same material can become positive or negative depending on its partner. Silk, for example, charges negative against glass but positive against polythene.

The series is also useful in industry and safety. Designers of photocopiers and laser printers choose materials that charge strongly against each other, and engineers handling powders, fuels and plastics choose materials close together on the list to reduce dangerous charge build-up.

Two pieces of the same material rubbed together transfer little or no charge, because neither has a stronger hold on electrons.

The exact order varies a little between different lists, because real materials vary in their composition, cleanliness and moisture, but the general pattern is reliable and has been confirmed by careful experiments.

The questions for this section are with you now: what the triboelectric series shows, the rule for predicting which material becomes positive, examples where the same material charges differently with different partners, and why rubbing two identical materials gives little charge.

## Subtopic: Conductors, Insulators and Keeping Charge

Not all materials can hold a static charge where it is put. The difference depends on whether electrons can move freely through the material.

An electrical conductor is a material through which charge can flow easily. Metals such as copper, aluminium and iron are good conductors, because some of their electrons are free to move from atom to atom throughout the metal. The human body, damp materials and the ground are also conductors, though weaker ones. If you rub a metal rod held in your hand, any charge transferred to it immediately flows away through the metal, through your body and into the ground. That is why a metal rod held in the hand does not appear to become charged.

An electrical insulator is a material through which charge cannot flow easily, because its electrons are held tightly by their atoms. Plastics, rubber, glass, dry wood, dry paper, wool, silk and dry air are insulators. When an insulator is charged by friction, the extra charge stays on the area that was rubbed, because it cannot move away. That is why static electricity is usually produced with insulators: balloons, plastic rulers, combs, glass rods, polythene strips and synthetic fabrics.

A metal object can be charged if it is insulated from the ground, for example a metal can standing on a dry plastic cup or a metal sphere on a glass stand, and if it is charged by touching it with a charged insulator. The charge then spreads over the whole surface of the metal, because it can move freely within the conductor.

Earthing, also called grounding, means connecting a charged object to the Earth with a conductor so that the extra charge can flow away and the object becomes neutral. The Earth is so large that it can accept or supply any amount of charge without its own charge noticeably changing.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying protons move when objects are charged; only electrons move. Two: saying a positive object has gained positive charge; it has lost electrons. Three: saying rubbing creates charge; it only separates charge that was already there. Four: saying metals cannot be charged; they can, if they are insulated from the ground. Five: forgetting that both rubbed materials become charged, with equal and opposite charges.

Layout for marks: name the materials, state which one gains electrons and which loses them, and give the resulting charge on each.

The questions for this section are with you now: the difference between conductors and insulators with examples, why a metal rod held in the hand does not become charged, how a metal object can be charged, earthing, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a balloon and your hair, an electron tug of war, and a metal spoon that will not hold charge — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Balloon and Your Hair

Rub a balloon on your hair on a dry winter day. Pull it away slowly. Your hair stands up and reaches for the balloon. Then stick the balloon on the wall. It stays there. Magic? No, electrons.

Everything is made of atoms. Each atom has positive protons in the middle and negative electrons around the outside. Normally there are exactly the same number of each, so the pluses and minuses cancel out. Your hair and the balloon start off neutral.

When you rub, some electrons jump from your hair onto the balloon. Now the balloon has extra electrons, so it is negative. Your hair has lost electrons, so it is positive. The balloon gained exactly as many as your hair lost. Nothing was made or destroyed, just moved.

Only electrons move. The protons are locked deep in the middle of each atom and cannot be rubbed off. So positive means lost electrons, and negative means gained electrons.

On a humid day in Durban the trick barely works, because damp air lets the charge leak away.

Keep the balloon in mind and try the questions for this part: what moved during the rubbing, which one became negative, and why only electrons can move.

## Subtopic: An Electron Tug of War

Rubbing two things together is like a tug of war for electrons. The material that holds its electrons more tightly wins and pulls some electrons over. The winner becomes negative, the loser becomes positive.

Scientists have made a ranking list of who wins. Near the top, the losers: hair, glass, nylon, wool. Near the bottom, the winners: rubber, polythene, PVC plastic. Rub something from the top with something from the bottom, and the top one goes positive, the bottom one goes negative.

So a plastic ruler rubbed with a wool jersey becomes negative. A glass rod rubbed with silk becomes positive. The further apart they are on the list, the bigger the charge.

But it depends on the partner. Silk loses the tug of war against plastic, but wins against glass.

And two of the same thing rubbed together? A draw. Nobody wins, so hardly any charge moves.

Hold the tug of war in mind and try this part's questions: how to use the ranking list, and why the same material can go positive or negative.

## Subtopic: The Spoon That Will Not Hold Charge

Rub a plastic ruler and it picks up little bits of paper. Rub a metal spoon while you hold it, and nothing happens. Why?

Plastic is an insulator. Its electrons are stuck in place. So when it gets extra electrons, they stay right where you rubbed. Metal is a conductor. Its electrons can move around freely. So any extra charge on the spoon just flows away through the metal, through your hand and body, and down into the ground.

If you stand the spoon on a dry plastic cup, so the charge has nowhere to go, then it can hold a charge. And connecting something to the ground with metal to let its charge escape is called earthing.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Atoms contain positive protons and negative electrons in equal numbers, so objects are normally neutral. Rubbing two different insulators transfers electrons: the one that gains them becomes negative, the one that loses them becomes equally positive. Charge is never created or destroyed. The triboelectric series predicts which way electrons move. Insulators keep static charge where it is; conductors let it flow away unless they are insulated.

Hold the balloon, the tug of war and the spoon, and take the final questions of the lesson: which material goes negative, why metal held in the hand stays neutral, and what earthing means.
