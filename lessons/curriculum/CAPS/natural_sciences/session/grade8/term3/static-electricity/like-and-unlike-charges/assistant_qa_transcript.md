### subtopic_1 — Attraction and Repulsion
**Assistant:** If electrostatic forces are so much stronger than gravity, why does gravity control the Moon and planets?

**Tutor:** Because large objects are almost perfectly neutral, so their electric forces cancel out, while gravity never cancels. Gravity only ever attracts; every particle of mass pulls on every other particle, and the pulls all add up. A planet, made of trillions of trillions of atoms, has an enormous combined gravitational pull. Electric charge comes in two kinds, and in any large lump of matter the positive and negative charges are almost exactly balanced. If a planet had a tiny surplus of one kind of charge, it would attract the opposite kind from its surroundings, from the solar wind and from space, until it became neutral again. So the electric forces between the Earth and the Moon are close to zero, and gravity, though weak particle for particle, wins on the large scale. The difference in strength is staggering. Between two electrons, the electric repulsion is about a million billion billion billion billion times stronger than their gravitational attraction. On the scale of atoms and molecules, electric forces control everything: they hold electrons around the nucleus, bind atoms into molecules, and make solids solid. When you push on a table, the force stopping your hand is the electric repulsion between the electrons in your hand and those in the table. When you stand, electric forces in the floor hold you up against the gravity of the whole planet. You can see the contest directly in a simple demonstration: a rubbed balloon, carrying a tiny fraction of the charge it could have, lifts small scraps of paper against the gravitational pull of the entire Earth below them.

### subtopic_2 — Attracting Neutral Objects
**Assistant:** Why does a balloon stuck to a wall eventually fall off after a while?

**Tutor:** Because the charge on the balloon slowly leaks away, and as it does, the attraction becomes too weak to hold the balloon up against gravity and friction is no longer enough. Several things drain the charge. Air always contains some moisture and a small number of charged particles, called ions, created by natural radioactivity and cosmic rays. Positive ions drifting near the negative balloon are attracted to it and neutralise some of its extra electrons. Water molecules in the air settle on the balloon's surface and form a very thin film that lets charge creep away along the surface. The wall itself is not a perfect insulator, especially if it is painted with a water-based paint or is slightly damp, so some electrons slowly pass from the balloon into the wall. As the charge on the balloon decreases, the induced separation in the wall also decreases, so the attraction weakens on both counts. The balloon is held up partly by friction between its surface and the wall, and friction depends on how hard the balloon is pressed against the wall by the electric attraction. When that pressing force becomes too small, friction can no longer balance the weight of the balloon, and it slides or drops off. On a dry winter's day in Johannesburg a balloon may stick for many minutes or even hours; on a humid summer's day at the coast it may hardly stick at all, or fall within seconds. You can investigate this as a fair test, timing how long identical balloons rubbed the same number of times stay on the same wall on days of different humidity.

### subtopic_3 — The Electroscope
**Assistant:** How can an electroscope be charged permanently without touching it with the charged rod?

**Tutor:** By a method called charging by induction, which uses the movement of free electrons in the metal and a brief connection to the ground. Start with a neutral electroscope and a negatively charged polythene rod. Bring the rod near the cap, without touching. The negative rod repels free electrons in the metal, pushing them down to the leaf and plate, so the leaf rises, and the cap is left slightly positive. Now, keeping the rod in place, touch the cap with your finger. Your body is a conductor connected to the ground, and the electrons that have been pushed away by the rod now have an easier escape route: they flow out through your finger and body into the ground, trying to get as far from the rod as possible. The leaf falls, because the extra electrons have left it. Next, remove your finger first, while the rod is still near. The electroscope is now cut off from the ground and has fewer electrons than protons. Finally, take the rod away. The remaining electrons redistribute themselves evenly over the cap, rod, plate and leaf, and since the whole electroscope now has a shortage of electrons, it is positively charged, and the leaf rises again. Notice that the electroscope ends up with the opposite charge to the rod, and that the rod has lost none of its own charge, because it never touched anything. The order of the steps matters: if you take the rod away before removing your finger, electrons flow back up from the ground and the electroscope is left neutral. The same method is used to charge parts of some machines that generate static electricity.

### subtopic_4 — Using Electrostatic Forces
**Assistant:** Why must dust from coal power stations be removed, if it is only fine ash?

**Tutor:** Because fine ash particles are harmful to people and the environment, and the smallest ones are the most dangerous. When coal burns, the minerals it contains do not burn; they are left as ash, and coal from many South African mines has a high ash content, often around thirty percent. Much of this ash is fine enough to be carried up the chimney with the hot gases. Particles larger than about ten thousandths of a millimetre are trapped mainly in the nose and throat, but finer particles, smaller than about two and a half thousandths of a millimetre, can travel deep into the lungs and even pass into the bloodstream. Breathing them over long periods increases the risk of asthma, bronchitis, lung disease, heart disease and strokes. Communities living near the cluster of coal power stations in Mpumalanga, an area that the government has declared an air quality priority area, suffer from high levels of this kind of air pollution, especially in winter when cold still air traps pollution near the ground. Fly ash can also settle on crops and in water and can contain small amounts of toxic metals. An electrostatic precipitator in good working order can remove more than ninety-nine percent of the ash by mass from the flue gases, and fabric filters, which work like giant vacuum cleaner bags, can do even better. The collected ash is not simply wasted: some of it is sold for use in cement and concrete, where it improves strength and reduces the need for new cement. Precipitators and filters only work well if they are maintained, which is why their performance at older power stations is monitored by environmental authorities.
