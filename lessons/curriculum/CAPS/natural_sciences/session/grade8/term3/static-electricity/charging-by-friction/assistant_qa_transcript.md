### subtopic_1 — Atoms and Electric Charge
**Assistant:** How do we know protons and electrons have exactly equal and opposite charges?

**Tutor:** Because if they were even slightly different, ordinary matter would not be neutral, and the effects would be enormous and easy to detect. A glass of water contains roughly ten million billion billion protons and the same number of electrons. Suppose the charge on a proton were larger than the charge on an electron by just one part in a billion. Then every glass of water, every person and every planet would carry a large overall positive charge, and since like charges repel, objects would push each other apart with forces far greater than gravity. The Earth would repel the Moon, and stars could not form. Nothing like that is observed. Physicists have done very careful experiments to test the equality, for example by checking whether a large container of gas, with its huge number of atoms, shows any overall charge at all, or whether a beam of neutral atoms is deflected by an electric field. These experiments show that the charges of the proton and electron are equal in size to better than one part in a billion billion. Why they should be so precisely equal is a deep question. A proton is not a simple particle; it is made of three smaller particles called quarks, with charges of two thirds and minus one third, which add up to exactly plus one, while the electron is a fundamental particle with charge minus one. The fact that these add up to exactly cancel each other is one of the clues physicists use to build theories about how the fundamental particles are related. For Grade 8, the important point is simple: one proton and one electron cancel exactly.

### subtopic_2 — Charging by Rubbing
**Assistant:** Why does rubbing transfer electrons, when just touching two materials together hardly does anything?

**Tutor:** In fact simply touching does transfer some electrons; rubbing just makes far more contact and so transfers far more. Even surfaces that feel smooth are rough on a tiny scale, covered in bumps and ridges. When you press a balloon against your hair, the two surfaces actually touch only at the tips of these bumps, a tiny fraction of the apparent area. Electrons can only move between atoms that are extremely close together, at those true contact points. When you rub, you slide the surfaces across each other, bringing fresh bumps into contact again and again, so the total area where electrons can pass from one material to the other becomes much larger. Rubbing also slightly warms the surfaces and scrapes off thin layers, exposing fresh material. That is why brisk rubbing produces much more charge than gentle touching. There is a limit, though. As the balloon becomes more negative, it starts to repel further electrons, and as the hair becomes positive it holds on to its remaining electrons more strongly, so the transfer slows down. Charge also leaks away into the air, especially if it is damp. Eventually the charge stops increasing, however long you rub. Scientists are still studying exactly how electrons are transferred in some materials, especially plastics, where small fragments of material, or charged particles from water films on the surface, may be transferred along with or instead of electrons. But for metals and most everyday cases, the electron transfer model explains the observations very well, and it is the model used in school science and in engineering.

### subtopic_3 — The Triboelectric Series
**Assistant:** What makes one material hold electrons more tightly than another in the triboelectric series?

**Tutor:** It depends on how strongly the atoms or molecules at the surface of the material attract their outermost electrons, and on whether there are spaces nearby where extra electrons could settle comfortably. In some materials, the outer electrons are far from the nuclei of the atoms, or are shielded by many inner electrons, so they are held loosely and can be pulled away fairly easily. In others, the atoms have a strong pull on electrons, especially atoms of elements such as fluorine and chlorine, which are near the right-hand side of the periodic table and attract extra electrons strongly. That is why PVC, which contains chlorine, and Teflon, the non-stick coating on pans, which contains fluorine, sit at the negative end of the series and grab electrons from almost anything. Human hair, skin and glass contain molecules that hold their outer electrons less tightly, so they sit near the positive end. Surface conditions also matter. A thin layer of water, grease or dirt changes how readily electrons leave or arrive, which is why different experimenters sometimes find slightly different orders. Scientists describe the tendency of a surface to give up electrons with a quantity called its work function, which you will meet if you study physics further. For predicting what happens when two materials are rubbed together, you only need the list: the one nearer the negative end gains electrons. Engineers use triboelectric pairs deliberately in some modern devices that generate small amounts of electricity from movement, such as sensors in shoes or clothing that produce a tiny current each time two layers of different materials touch and separate.

### subtopic_4 — Conductors, Insulators and Keeping Charge
**Assistant:** Why do petrol tankers drag a chain or connect an earth cable before unloading fuel?

**Tutor:** To prevent a build-up of static charge that could produce a spark and ignite the petrol vapour. When fuel flows quickly through pipes and hoses, and when a tanker drives along with fuel sloshing in its tank, friction between the moving liquid and the surfaces it touches separates charge, in the same way that rubbing a balloon does. The tanker's metal body and tank can collect a large charge, especially because the rubber tyres insulate it from the ground. If that charge built up and then jumped as a spark to the nozzle of a hose or to a person touching the tanker, the spark could ignite the mixture of petrol vapour and air around the filling point, causing a fire or explosion. To prevent this, the tanker is earthed before any fuel is transferred. The driver clamps a heavy cable from the tanker's body to an earthing point connected to a metal rod buried in the ground, and only then connects the hoses. Any charge that builds up flows harmlessly through the cable into the Earth. Some older tankers had a chain or strap hanging down to touch the road, with the same idea, although a chain bouncing on a dry road is not a very reliable earth. The same principle is used on aircraft, which build up charge flying through dry air and are earthed by a cable when refuelled at airports such as OR Tambo, and in factories handling flour or coal dust, which can also explode if ignited by a spark. At a petrol station, you are asked not to get back into your car while filling, because sliding across the seat can charge your body.
