# Part 1 — Expert

This session investigates the forces between electric charges and how they explain a wide range of effects, from clinging balloons to industrial dust filters. The anchor is a simple experiment with two balloons hung side by side on threads from a rod. Both are rubbed on a woollen jersey and released: they swing apart and hang at an angle, pushing each other away, though nothing touches them. Bring the jersey near either balloon, and the balloon swings towards it. The governing idea is that charged objects exert forces on each other without touching: like charges repel and unlike charges attract; the force is stronger when the charges are larger and when they are closer together; and a charged object can also attract a neutral object by pulling the opposite charges within it slightly closer. The law of charges, the factors affecting the size of the electrostatic force, attraction of neutral objects by induced charge separation, the electroscope and practical applications of electrostatic forces are examinable, and they lead to Coulomb's law and electric fields in Grade 11 Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: Attraction and Repulsion

Experiments with charged objects reveal a simple rule, called the law of electric charges: like charges repel and unlike charges attract. Like charges means charges of the same kind, both positive or both negative. Unlike charges means one positive and one negative.

The two balloons show repulsion. Both were rubbed on wool, so both gained electrons and both are negative. Like charges repel, so the balloons push each other apart. The jersey, which lost electrons, is positive. Unlike charges attract, so each negative balloon is pulled towards the positive jersey.

A classic laboratory version uses rods. A polythene rod rubbed with wool becomes negative; it is hung in a paper stirrup from a thread so that it can turn freely. A second polythene rod, also rubbed with wool, is brought near one end. The hanging rod turns away: repulsion between like charges. Now a glass or acetate rod rubbed with silk, which is positive, is brought near the same end. The hanging rod turns towards it: attraction between unlike charges.

These forces act at a distance, across empty space or through air, without any contact. They belong to the same group of non-contact forces as gravity and magnetism. The electrostatic force is very strong compared with gravity. The tiny charge on a rubbed balloon can lift a strand of hair against the gravitational pull of the entire Earth.

The size of the force depends on two things. First, the amount of charge: the larger the charges on the two objects, the stronger the force. Rubbing a balloon more vigorously gives it more charge and it repels the other balloon more strongly, so the two hang further apart. Second, the distance between the charges: the closer they are, the stronger the force, and the force weakens quickly as they move apart. Doubling the distance between two small charged objects reduces the force to a quarter. That relationship was measured by Charles-Augustin de Coulomb in 1785 using a very sensitive twisting balance, and it is called Coulomb's law.

The questions for this section are with you now: the law of electric charges, the explanation of the two balloons, the hanging rod experiment, and the two factors that affect the size of the force between charges.

## Subtopic: Attracting Neutral Objects

A rubbed plastic comb picks up small scraps of paper, which are neutral. A rubbed balloon sticks to a neutral wall. A charged rod bends a thin stream of water running from a tap. How can a charged object attract something with no overall charge?

The answer is that a neutral object still contains equal amounts of positive and negative charge, and those charges can be shifted slightly. When a negatively charged balloon is brought near a wall, the negative balloon repels the electrons in the atoms and molecules at the surface of the wall, pushing them very slightly away, while attracting the positive parts slightly closer. The wall as a whole stays neutral, but its surface nearest the balloon now has a slight positive side facing the balloon and a slight negative side facing away. This separation of charge caused by a nearby charged object is called induced charge separation, or polarisation.

Now compare the forces. The positive side of each molecule is a little closer to the balloon than the negative side. Because the electrostatic force is stronger at shorter distances, the attraction between the balloon and the nearer positive side is slightly stronger than the repulsion between the balloon and the further negative side. The overall force is therefore an attraction. That is why a charged object attracts a neutral object, whichever sign of charge it carries. A positively charged rod would pull the electrons slightly closer and push the positive parts away, and the result is again an attraction.

In a conductor, like a small piece of aluminium foil, the free electrons can move right across the object, so the separation is large and the attraction is strong. In an insulator, like paper or a wall, the electrons cannot leave their own atoms or molecules, but each molecule becomes slightly lopsided, and the effect adds up across many molecules.

The stream of water bends because water molecules are naturally slightly lopsided in charge, with a positive end and a negative end, and they turn so that their opposite end faces the charged rod and are then attracted towards it.

Sometimes a scrap of paper jumps to a charged comb, sticks for a moment and then leaps away. When it touches the comb, some charge passes to it, giving it the same charge as the comb, and like charges repel.

The questions for this section are with you now: why a charged object attracts a neutral one, what induced charge separation means, why the attraction outweighs the repulsion, the bending stream of water, and why paper can jump off a comb after touching it.

## Subtopic: The Electroscope

An electroscope is an instrument that detects whether an object is charged. A gold-leaf electroscope has a metal rod passing through an insulating stopper into a glass case. At the top of the rod is a metal disc or cap, and at the bottom a thin metal plate with a very thin leaf of gold attached at its top, hanging down beside it. Gold leaf is used because it can be beaten extremely thin and light, so even a tiny force moves it. A simple school version can be made from a glass jar, a cardboard lid, a bent paper clip and two thin strips of aluminium foil from a chocolate wrapper.

When the electroscope is neutral, the leaf hangs straight down beside the plate. If a charged object touches the cap, some of its charge flows onto the metal of the electroscope and spreads through the conductor to the plate and the leaf. The plate and leaf now carry the same kind of charge, so they repel, and the leaf rises away from the plate. The more charge, the wider the leaf opens.

Even bringing a charged object near the cap, without touching it, makes the leaf open. A negative rod near the cap repels free electrons in the metal down the rod to the plate and leaf, which both become negative and repel each other. The cap becomes slightly positive. Remove the rod and the electrons return, and the leaf falls again.

The electroscope can also tell which kind of charge an object carries. First charge the electroscope with a known charge, say negative, so that the leaf is open. Bring the unknown object near the cap. If the leaf opens further, the object has the same charge as the electroscope, negative, because it pushes more electrons down to the leaf. If the leaf falls, the object has the opposite charge, positive, because it pulls electrons up from the leaf to the cap. Be careful: a neutral object also makes the leaf fall slightly, so only an increase in divergence is a reliable test for charge.

Electroscopes were among the first instruments used to study electricity, and early in the twentieth century scientists carried them up mountains and in balloons, noticing that charged electroscopes slowly lost their charge faster at greater heights. That observation led to the discovery of cosmic rays arriving from space.

Touching the cap with a finger earths the electroscope, and the leaf collapses as the charge flows through the body to the ground.

The questions for this section are with you now: the parts of a gold-leaf electroscope, why the leaf rises when charged, what happens when a charged rod is brought near without touching, how to test which kind of charge an object carries, and how to discharge an electroscope.

## Subtopic: Using Electrostatic Forces

Electrostatic attraction and repulsion are used in many technologies.

Photocopiers and laser printers use a drum coated with a special material that holds charge in the dark but lets it leak away where light falls. An image of the page is shone onto the charged drum, leaving charge only where the dark parts of the page are. Fine black toner powder, given the opposite charge, is attracted to those areas, then transferred to a sheet of paper and melted onto it by heat.

Electrostatic spray painting is used in car factories, including those in East London, Rosslyn, Durban and Port Elizabeth, now Gqeberha. The paint droplets are given a charge as they leave the spray gun, and the car body is earthed or given the opposite charge. The droplets repel one another, so they spread into a fine even mist, and they are attracted to the car body, even wrapping around to coat the back and edges. Far less paint is wasted than with ordinary spraying.

Electrostatic precipitators clean the smoke from coal-fired power stations and factories. The smoke passes between wires with a large charge, which give the ash and dust particles a charge. The charged particles are then attracted to large metal plates with the opposite charge, where they collect. The plates are shaken periodically and the dust falls into hoppers. Many coal-fired power stations in Mpumalanga use precipitators or fabric filters to remove most of the fine ash from their smoke before it leaves the chimneys.

Some air purifiers and the dust cloths sold for cleaning screens use the same principle, attracting dust with static charge. Crop sprayers can charge pesticide droplets so that they are attracted to the leaves of plants, reducing drift.

Nature uses it too. Bees build up a positive charge as they fly, and pollen grains are attracted to their bodies; some scientists have shown that bumblebees can even sense the electric charge of flowers.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying only charged objects can be attracted; neutral objects are attracted by induced separation. Two: saying attraction proves an object is charged; only repulsion proves it, because neutral objects are attracted too. Three: saying the force stays the same at any distance; it weakens quickly as distance increases. Four: saying the leaf of an electroscope rises because it is attracted to the plate; it rises because it is repelled.

Layout for marks: identify the charges, apply like repel and unlike attract, and say whether the force is stronger or weaker because of the distance.

The questions for this section are with you now: how a photocopier uses static charge, electrostatic spray painting, the electrostatic precipitator, and the four exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through two balloons on strings, a comb and scraps of paper, and paint that hugs a car — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Two Balloons on Strings

Hang two balloons side by side on strings. Rub both on a woollen jersey and let them go. They swing apart, as if they do not like each other. Now bring the jersey close, and each balloon swings towards it.

Both balloons grabbed electrons from the wool, so both are negative. Same charges push each other away. The jersey lost electrons, so it is positive. Opposite charges pull together.

That is the rule: like charges repel, unlike charges attract.

And they do it without touching, across empty air, like magnets.

Two things make the push or pull stronger. More charge: rub harder and the balloons swing further apart. Closer together: the nearer they are, the stronger the force, and it fades fast as they move apart.

Keep the two balloons in mind and try the questions for this part: the rule for charges, why the balloons push apart, and what makes the force stronger.

## Subtopic: A Comb and Scraps of Paper

Run a plastic comb through dry hair, then hold it over tiny scraps of paper. They jump up and stick to it. But the paper is not charged. How?

The comb is negative. When it comes close, it pushes the electrons in the paper a tiny bit away, to the far side, and the near side becomes slightly positive. The paper is still neutral overall, but its positive side is closer to the comb. Closer means stronger, so the pull on the near side beats the push on the far side. The paper is attracted.

That is also why a rubbed balloon sticks to a wall, and why a charged ruler can bend a thin trickle of water from a tap.

Sometimes a scrap touches the comb, picks up some of its charge, and then flies off. Now it has the same charge as the comb, so they repel.

Hold the comb in mind and try this part's questions: why neutral paper is attracted, and why some scraps jump away again.

## Subtopic: Paint That Hugs a Car

In car factories, paint is sprayed with an electric charge on each droplet, and the car body is given the opposite charge. The droplets push away from each other into a fine, even mist, and they are pulled onto the car, even curling round to paint the back of the panels. Much less paint is wasted.

Photocopiers and printers use charge to make black powder stick only where the words are. Power stations use charged plates to pull ash out of their smoke.

And you can test for charge with an electroscope: a thin leaf of metal hanging from a rod. Touch a charged object to the top, and the leaf and rod get the same charge and push apart, so the leaf lifts.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Like charges repel and unlike charges attract, without touching. The force is stronger with more charge and at shorter distance. A charged object attracts a neutral one by pulling its opposite charges slightly closer. An electroscope's leaf rises when charged because like charges repel. Electrostatic forces are used in painting, printing and cleaning smoke.

Hold the balloons, the comb and the painted car, and take the final questions of the lesson: will they attract or repel, why the paper jumps, and how a precipitator cleans smoke.
