### subtopic_1 — What Discharge Is
**Assistant:** Why is the earth pin on a South African plug longer than the other two pins?

**Tutor:** So that the appliance is connected to earth before the live and neutral connections are made, and disconnected from earth only after they are broken. When you push a plug into a socket, the longer earth pin enters first. In the standard South African three-pin plug, the large round earth pin also opens the shutters that cover the live and neutral holes in many sockets, which is a safety feature for small children poking objects into the socket. Once the earth pin is in, the metal casing of the appliance, such as a kettle, toaster or washing machine, is connected through the earth wire in the wall to a metal rod or the earth connection at your distribution board. Only then do the shorter live and neutral pins touch their contacts and allow current to flow. If something goes wrong inside the appliance, for example if a loose live wire touches the metal casing, the casing would become dangerously charged and could give a fatal shock to anyone touching it. Because the casing is earthed, a large current instead flows straight to earth, and that large current trips the earth leakage unit or blows the fuse, cutting off the supply. When you unplug the appliance, the live and neutral pins come out first, and the earth stays connected until the very end. Earthing in the house works in the same spirit as discharging a charged object, giving charge a safe path to the ground, but here it protects against current from the mains supply, which is far more dangerous than static, because the mains can keep supplying charge continuously instead of discharging in a single tiny spark.

### subtopic_2 — Sparks and Shocks
**Assistant:** Why do you get more static shocks in winter than in summer?

**Tutor:** Because winter air on the Highveld is much drier, and dry air lets charge build up on your body instead of leaking away. The amount of water vapour that air can hold depends strongly on temperature: cold air can hold far less than warm air. On top of that, the Highveld has its dry season in winter, with very little rain from May to August. So winter air in Johannesburg or Pretoria often has a very low humidity, especially indoors when heaters warm the air without adding any moisture. In humid air, a thin film of water molecules forms on the surfaces of your skin, clothes and carpets. Water with dissolved substances in it conducts electricity, so charge separated by rubbing leaks away through this film almost as fast as it is produced, and your body never becomes strongly charged. In dry air the film is missing, the surfaces are good insulators, and every step across a carpet or every slide across a car seat adds charge that stays on you. Winter clothing makes it worse: woollen jerseys, fleeces and synthetic fabrics rub against each other and against car seats and charge strongly. Your shoes matter too, because rubber and plastic soles insulate you from the floor. In summer in the same city, frequent thunderstorms keep the air more humid, and at the coast in Durban the air is humid nearly all year, so static shocks are rare there. You can reduce shocks by using moisturiser on your skin, wearing cotton instead of synthetic fabrics, using a humidifier indoors, or touching metal with a key first.

### subtopic_3 — Lightning
**Assistant:** Why does lightning zigzag and branch instead of going in a straight line to the ground?

**Tutor:** Because the stepped leader that prepares the path does not know where the ground is; it advances in short jumps, each time following whichever direction happens to be easiest to break through. Air is not perfectly uniform. It contains patches with slightly different humidity, temperature, dust and numbers of charged particles, so its ability to insulate varies from place to place. As the leader pushes down from the cloud, it travels about fifty metres at a time, pauses for a few millionths of a second, and then jumps again. At each step, the next jump starts in whatever direction the air is easiest to break down at that moment, which is often not straight down. That produces the jagged, zigzag shape. Sometimes, at a step, two directions are almost equally easy, and the leader splits and continues in both, which produces the branches. Most branches stop and fade before reaching the ground. When the tip of one branch comes within a few tens of metres of the ground, the strong electric force pulls up a positive streamer from the nearest tall or pointed object, a tree, a mast, a building or a person. The first leader and streamer to connect complete the circuit, and the huge return stroke flashes up that one channel, lighting it brilliantly. The branches that did not connect glow more faintly, because they carry much less current. High-speed cameras filming thousands of frames per second have recorded this whole sequence. They show that the visible flash actually travels upward from the ground, even though the leader came down, which is why lightning appears to strike downward to our slower eyes.

### subtopic_4 — Lightning Safety and Protection
**Assistant:** Why is a car safe in lightning, if it is not the rubber tyres that protect you?

**Tutor:** Because a closed metal car body acts as a shield, carrying the current around the outside of the passengers and into the ground, an effect called a Faraday cage. When lightning strikes a car, the huge current reaches the roof and spreads across the metal body. Metal is an excellent conductor, so the current stays in the metal skin, flowing down the body panels and the frame. Charges in a conductor push each other as far apart as possible, so they spread over the outside surface and do not pass through the space inside. From the bottom of the car, the current jumps the short gap across or around the tyres to the ground, often damaging the tyres, the electrical system and the paint along the way. The few centimetres of rubber in a tyre are no barrier at all to a discharge that has just forced its way through several kilometres of air. People inside are safe provided they are not touching metal parts connected to the outside and the windows are closed. This explains why a car with a fabric soft-top, or a car made mainly of fibreglass or plastic, offers much less protection, and why a minibus taxi or a bus with a metal body is a good shelter. Aircraft work the same way. A large passenger aircraft is struck by lightning on average about once a year, but the current flows through its aluminium skin and leaves from another point, usually a wing tip or the tail, without harming the passengers. The English scientist Michael Faraday demonstrated the shielding effect in 1836 by sitting inside a metal-covered room while large sparks were sent across its outside, with an electroscope inside showing no charge at all.
