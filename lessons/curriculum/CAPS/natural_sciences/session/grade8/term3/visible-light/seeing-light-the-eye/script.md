# Part 1 — Expert

This session follows light into the eye and explains how it is turned into the signals that the brain interprets as sight. The anchor is a walk from bright afternoon sunshine into a dark cinema. For the first moments you can barely see the seats or the people around you; after a few minutes you can make out faces and the steps; after twenty minutes you could find a dropped coin on the carpet, though everything looks grey. Walk back out into the sunshine and you are dazzled, squinting, until your eyes adjust again. Nothing about the light outside has changed; your eyes have changed how they work. The governing idea is that the eye is an organ that focuses light onto a layer of light-sensitive receptor cells, the retina; that the receptor cells, rods for dim light and cones for colour and detail, convert the energy of light into nerve impulses; and that these impulses travel along the optic nerve to the brain, which interprets them as an image. The parts of the eye and their functions, how an image is focused on the retina, the roles of rods and cones, the optic nerve and the brain, adaptation to light and dark, and common eye problems and eye care are examinable, and they connect to the nervous system and the senses in Grade 9 and Life Sciences.

## Subtopic: The Structure of the Eye

The human eye is a roughly spherical organ about two and a half centimetres across, sitting in a bony socket in the skull that protects it. Six muscles attached to the outside of the eyeball move it to look in different directions.

The tough white outer layer is the sclera, which protects the eye and keeps its shape. At the front, the sclera is replaced by the cornea, a transparent, curved window through which light enters. The cornea does most of the bending of light needed to focus it.

Behind the cornea is the iris, the coloured part of the eye, a ring of muscle that may be brown, blue, green or grey depending on the amount of the pigment melanin in it. In the centre of the iris is a hole, the pupil, which looks black because light entering it is absorbed inside the eye and hardly any comes back out. The iris controls the size of the pupil, and so the amount of light entering the eye.

Behind the pupil is the lens, a clear, flexible structure held in place by ligaments attached to a ring of muscle, the ciliary muscle. The lens fine-tunes the focus. The space between the cornea and lens is filled with a watery liquid, the aqueous humour, and the large space behind the lens is filled with a clear jelly, the vitreous humour, which keeps the eyeball firm and round.

Lining the inside of the back of the eye is the retina, a thin layer containing millions of light-sensitive receptor cells. Behind the retina is the choroid, a layer rich in blood vessels that supply the retina, and dark pigment that absorbs stray light. Near the centre of the retina, directly behind the lens, is a small yellowish area called the fovea, where receptor cells are packed most densely and vision is sharpest. Nerve fibres from all over the retina gather and leave the eye at the optic nerve, which carries signals to the brain.

The eye is protected by the eyelids, which blink to spread tears across the cornea, keeping it moist and clean, and close quickly to protect it; by the eyelashes, which catch dust; by the eyebrows, which divert sweat; and by tears, which wash away dirt and contain a substance that kills bacteria.

The questions for this section are with you now: the names and functions of the sclera, cornea, iris, pupil, lens, ciliary muscle, retina, fovea and optic nerve, why the pupil looks black, and how the eye is protected.

## Subtopic: How the Eye Forms an Image

Light from an object enters the eye through the cornea, passes through the pupil and the lens, and is focused onto the retina. To form a sharp image, all the light from each point on the object must be brought together at a single point on the retina.

The cornea and the lens are both curved, transparent structures that refract light, bending it inwards so that the rays converge. The cornea, because of its strong curve and the large change in speed as light passes from air into it, does about two thirds of the focusing. The lens does the rest, and, importantly, it can change its shape to adjust the focus.

When you look at a distant object, the ciliary muscle relaxes, the ligaments are pulled tight, and the lens is stretched thin and flat. A thin lens bends light less, which is right for the nearly parallel rays arriving from far away. When you look at a near object, such as a book, the ciliary muscle contracts, the ligaments slacken, and the elastic lens becomes fatter and more curved, bending the light more strongly, as needed for the spreading rays from a nearby object. This automatic change of focus is called accommodation. You can feel it working: look at your finger close to your face, then at the far wall, and back.

The image formed on the retina is upside down and reversed left to right, just like the image in a pinhole camera, because rays from the top of the object cross over as they pass through the eye and land at the bottom of the retina. The brain, which has learned to interpret the signals from the retina since infancy, makes us perceive the world the right way up.

The iris adjusts the pupil to control the light. In bright light, muscles in the iris make the pupil smaller, to as little as about two millimetres across, protecting the retina from too much light and sharpening the image. In dim light, the pupil widens, to as much as about eight millimetres, letting in more light. This pupil reflex happens automatically, without thought, which is why doctors shine a small torch into a patient's eye to check that the nervous system is working.

The questions for this section are with you now: the path of light through the eye, the roles of the cornea and the lens in focusing, how the lens changes shape for near and distant objects, why the image on the retina is inverted, and how the pupil responds to bright and dim light.

## Subtopic: Rods, Cones and the Optic Nerve

The retina contains two kinds of light-sensitive receptor cells, named after their shapes: rods and cones. Together there are about one hundred and twenty million rods and about six million cones in each eye.

Rods are extremely sensitive to light, able to respond to very dim light, even a few packets of light energy. They do not distinguish colours; they give black, white and grey vision. Rods are spread over most of the retina, except the fovea, and are most numerous around the edges. They are responsible for night vision and for noticing movement out of the corner of the eye. That is why, at night, you see shapes but not colours, and why a faint star can be easier to see if you look slightly to one side of it.

Cones need brighter light, but they detect colour and fine detail. There are three types of cones, each most sensitive to a different range of wavelengths, roughly red, green and blue. The brain compares the signals from the three types to produce the sensation of every colour we see. Cones are packed most densely in the fovea, which is why you see sharpest and in the fullest colour directly where you look.

When light strikes a rod or a cone, the energy of the light changes the shape of a pigment molecule in the cell. That triggers a chain of chemical changes which produces an electrical signal, a nerve impulse. The receptor cells pass their impulses to other nerve cells in the retina, which collect and process the signals and send them along the optic nerve, about a million nerve fibres in a bundle, to the brain. The impulses go to the visual area at the back of the brain, where they are interpreted as shapes, colours, depth and movement. In a real sense, we see with our brains: the eye collects and converts the light, and the brain builds the picture.

Where the optic nerve leaves the eye, there are no rods or cones at all. This is the blind spot. We do not normally notice it because the brain fills in the gap using information from the surroundings and from the other eye.

The change from bright to dark vision is called adaptation. In the dark cinema, the pupils widen within seconds, but the rods also need time to rebuild their light-sensitive pigment, which bright light bleaches. Full dark adaptation takes about twenty to thirty minutes. Walking back into sunshine, the pupils shrink and the over-sensitive rods are bleached, giving the dazzled feeling until the cones take over.

The questions for this section are with you now: the differences between rods and cones, why you see no colour in dim light, how light is converted into a nerve impulse, the role of the optic nerve and the brain, the blind spot, and dark adaptation.

## Subtopic: Eye Problems and Eye Care

Many people's eyes do not focus perfectly, but most problems can be corrected.

In short sight, or myopia, distant objects look blurred while near objects are clear, because the eyeball is slightly too long or the cornea too curved, so light from distant objects is focused in front of the retina. It is corrected with diverging lenses, which are thinner in the middle. In long sight, or hyperopia, near objects are blurred because light is focused behind the retina; it is corrected with converging lenses, thicker in the middle. With age, from about forty-five, the lens becomes stiffer and cannot fatten enough for close work, so most older adults need reading glasses.

A cataract is a clouding of the lens, which makes vision blurred and dim. It is common in older people and can be caused or worsened by too much ultraviolet light, diabetes and smoking. It is treated by a simple operation in which the cloudy lens is replaced with a clear plastic one, restoring sight.

Colour vision deficiency, often called colour blindness, is usually inherited and means that one type of cone is missing or works differently. The most common type makes it hard to tell red from green. It affects about eight in every hundred males and fewer than one in a hundred females, because the genes involved are carried on the X chromosome.

Taking care of your eyes is important. Never look directly at the Sun, even briefly or with sunglasses, because the focused sunlight can burn the retina permanently. Wear sunglasses that block ultraviolet light and a hat in bright sunlight. Use safety goggles when working with chemicals, grinding, welding or using power tools. Rest your eyes regularly when reading or using screens for long periods, by looking at something far away every twenty minutes or so. Eat a balanced diet: vitamin A, found in carrots, sweet potato, pumpkin, spinach and liver, is needed to make the light-sensitive pigment in rods, and a lack of it causes night blindness. Have your eyes tested if words on the board look blurred, if you get frequent headaches, or if you squint to see.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the pupil is a black object; it is a hole. Two: saying the lens does all the focusing; the cornea does most of it. Three: saying rods see colour; cones see colour. Four: saying the image on the retina is the right way up; it is inverted, and the brain interprets it. Five: saying the eye sends pictures to the brain; it sends nerve impulses.

## Subtopic: A Camera in Your Head

Your eye works a lot like a camera.

Light comes in through a clear front window, the cornea, which bends it. Then it goes through a hole called the pupil. The coloured ring around the pupil, the iris, makes the hole bigger in the dark and smaller in bright light, like a camera's shutter control. Then the light goes through the lens, which fine-tunes the focus, and lands on the back of the eye, on a layer called the retina. The retina is like the camera's sensor.

The lens is clever. It changes shape: fat to look at something close, like your phone, and thin to look at something far, like the board. That is called accommodation.

The picture on the retina is actually upside down, like in a pinhole camera. Your brain turns it the right way up without you even noticing.

Keep the camera in mind and try the questions for this part: the path of light through the eye, what the iris does, and how the lens focuses on near and far things.

## Subtopic: Walking into a Dark Cinema

Walk into a dark cinema from bright sunshine and at first you can hardly see. Slowly, the room appears, but in grey, not colour. Why?

The retina has two kinds of light-catching cells. Rods are super-sensitive and work in dim light, but they only see in black, white and grey. Cones need more light, but they see colour and sharp detail. There are three kinds of cones, for roughly red, green and blue.

In the cinema, only your rods are working, so you see shapes but no colours. And the rods need time to get ready after being in bright sun, about twenty to thirty minutes for full night vision. Meanwhile your pupils open wide to let in more light.

When light hits a rod or cone, it makes a tiny electrical signal, a nerve impulse.

Hold the dark cinema in mind and try this part's questions: rods and cones, and why you see in grey in the dark.

## Subtopic: The Brain Does the Seeing

Those tiny electrical signals from the rods and cones travel along a cable of nerves, the optic nerve, to the back of your brain. And there, the brain builds the picture: shapes, colours, distance and movement. So you really see with your brain. Your eyes just collect the light and turn it into signals.

Where the optic nerve leaves the eye there are no rods or cones, a blind spot. Your brain fills in the gap so cleverly that you never notice it.

Look after your eyes. Never look at the Sun. Wear sunglasses and a hat in strong sun, and safety goggles in the lab. Eat your carrots, pumpkin and spinach for vitamin A. And if the board looks blurry, get your eyes tested.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Light enters through the cornea, passes the pupil and lens, and is focused on the retina; the lens changes shape for near and far objects. The iris controls the pupil size. Rods work in dim light without colour; cones need brighter light and see colour and detail. Receptor cells turn light into nerve impulses, which travel along the optic nerve to the brain, which interprets them as sight.
