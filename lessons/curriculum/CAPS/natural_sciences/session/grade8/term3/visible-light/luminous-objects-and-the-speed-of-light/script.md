# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces light: where it comes from, how it travels and how astonishingly fast it moves. The anchor is a clear night far from city lights, in the Karoo near Sutherland. Overhead, the Moon is bright and the stars are sharp. The stars produce their own light; the Moon does not, yet it is the brightest object in the night sky. The light from the Moon left its surface just over one second ago. The sunlight it is reflecting left the Sun about eight minutes before that. And the light from some of the faint stars set out on its journey long before anyone now alive was born. The governing idea is that light is a form of energy given out by luminous objects, which travels outward from its source in straight lines, through empty space as well as through transparent materials, at a speed of about 300 000 kilometres per second in a vacuum, the fastest speed in the Universe. Luminous and non-luminous objects, the straight-line travel of light shown by experiments and by the pinhole camera, rays and beams, and the speed of light with calculations of travel times are examinable, and they lead to the wave model of light and the electromagnetic spectrum in Grade 10 and 12 Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: Luminous and Non-Luminous Objects

Light is a form of energy that our eyes can detect. Objects that give out their own light are called luminous objects, or sources of light. Objects that do not give out their own light are called non-luminous objects; we see them only because they reflect light from a luminous source into our eyes.

Natural luminous objects include the Sun and the other stars, lightning, fire, and glowing lava. Some living things are luminous too: fireflies, glow-worms, some fungi, and many deep-sea fish and jellyfish produce light by chemical reactions in their bodies, a process called bioluminescence. On some South African beaches, at night, breaking waves can glow blue because of tiny luminous plankton in the water.

Artificial luminous objects include candles, filament bulbs, LED lamps, fluorescent tubes, cellphone and computer screens, television screens, torches and the headlights of cars.

Many luminous objects give out light because they are very hot. A candle flame, the filament of a bulb, the glowing element of a stove and the surface of the Sun all emit light because their high temperature gives their particles so much energy that some of it is given out as visible light. These are called incandescent sources. Others, such as LEDs, fluorescent tubes and fireflies, give out light without being very hot.

Non-luminous objects include the Moon, the planets, people, books, trees, walls, and almost everything around us. The Moon looks bright, but it is a non-luminous rocky body that reflects sunlight; that is why it shows phases as different amounts of its sunlit side face us. A mirror is non-luminous; it only reflects light. A cellphone screen is luminous when switched on but non-luminous when off.

The questions for this section are with you now: the difference between luminous and non-luminous objects, natural and artificial examples of each, why the Moon is non-luminous, and how incandescent sources produce light.

## Subtopic: Light Travels in Straight Lines

Light travels outward from a source in all directions, and in a uniform material, such as air or water or empty space, it travels in straight lines. This is called the rectilinear propagation of light.

Evidence for this is all around us. Sunbeams streaming through gaps in clouds or through the leaves of trees in a forest, made visible by dust or mist, are straight. The beam of a torch or the headlights of a car on a misty night are straight. You cannot see around a corner. Shadows have sharp edges that match the shape of the object, which would not happen if light bent around objects.

A simple experiment demonstrates it. Cut a small hole in the same place in each of three cards, and stand them up in a row between a candle and your eye. When the holes are exactly in a straight line, you can see the flame through them. Move any one card slightly sideways, so that its hole is out of line, and the flame disappears. Light cannot bend to pass through holes that are not in a straight line.

In diagrams, light is represented by rays: straight lines with arrows showing the direction in which the light travels. A beam is a bundle of many rays. In a parallel beam, the rays stay the same distance apart, like the light from a distant source such as the Sun. In a diverging beam, the rays spread out from a point, like light from a bulb. In a converging beam, the rays come together to a point, like the light focused by a magnifying glass.

Builders and surveyors rely on the straight-line travel of light every day. A laser level projects a perfectly straight red or green line across a wall so that tiles and shelves can be fitted level, and surveyors sight along telescopes to lay out straight roads and railway lines. Astronomers measuring the position of a star assume that its light has travelled to us in a straight line.

The pinhole camera is a classic use of straight-line travel. It is a light-proof box with a tiny hole in one end and a screen of tracing paper at the other. Light from each point on a bright object passes through the pinhole in a straight line and lands on a single point of the screen. Light from the top of the object travels downward through the hole and lands at the bottom of the screen, and light from the bottom lands at the top, so the image is upside down, inverted. It is also reversed left to right. Moving the screen further from the pinhole makes the image larger but dimmer. Making the hole larger makes the image brighter but blurred, because each point of the object now lights up a small patch instead of a point.

The questions for this section are with you now: the evidence that light travels in straight lines, the three-card experiment, the meaning of rays and beams, and how a pinhole camera forms an inverted image.

## Subtopic: The Speed of Light

Light travels extremely fast. In a vacuum, the speed of light is very nearly 300 000 kilometres per second, which is three hundred million metres per second. In air it is almost exactly the same. It is slower in water and glass, about three quarters and two thirds of that speed respectively, which is important for refraction, studied later in this topic.

To get a sense of how fast that is: light could travel around the Earth's equator, a distance of about 40 000 kilometres, roughly seven and a half times in one second. A passenger jet flying at about 900 kilometres per hour would take about two days to fly around the Earth once. Sound travels at about 340 metres per second in air, almost a million times slower than light, which is why you see a lightning flash before you hear the thunder, and why at a cricket match at Newlands, viewed from the far stand, you see the bat hit the ball a fraction of a second before you hear the crack.

The time light takes to travel a distance is found from: time equals distance divided by speed. Light from the Moon, about 384 000 kilometres away, takes 384 000 divided by 300 000, which is about 1.3 seconds, to reach us. Light from the Sun, about 150 million kilometres away, takes 150 000 000 divided by 300 000, which is 500 seconds, or about 8 minutes and 20 seconds. If the Sun suddenly stopped shining, we would not know for over eight minutes. Light from the nearest star beyond the Sun takes over four years to reach us.

Astronomers use the distance light travels in one year as a unit for measuring the enormous distances between stars. This unit, the light-year, is about 9.5 million million kilometres, and you will use it in Term 4. Because light takes time to arrive, looking at distant objects always means looking into the past: we see the Sun as it was about eight minutes ago, and a star a hundred light-years away as it was a century ago.

Measuring such a speed was a great achievement. In 1676 the Danish astronomer Ole Rømer noticed that the moons of Jupiter appeared to fall behind or run ahead of their predicted schedule depending on whether the Earth was moving away from or towards Jupiter, and realised that this was because light took longer to cross the greater distance. Later, in the 1800s, scientists measured the speed of light on Earth using rapidly rotating toothed wheels and mirrors. Today it is known so precisely that it is used to define the metre.

The questions for this section are with you now: the speed of light in a vacuum, comparisons with sound and aircraft, calculating the travel time of light from the Moon and the Sun, and how the speed of light was first measured.

## Subtopic: Light as Energy and Radiation

Light is one member of a large family of radiation called the electromagnetic spectrum. All these kinds of radiation travel at the speed of light in a vacuum and carry energy, but our eyes can detect only a small part of the family, called visible light. Infrared radiation, which we feel as heat from the Sun or a fire, and ultraviolet radiation, which causes sunburn, are close relatives of visible light on either side of it. Radio waves, microwaves, X-rays and gamma rays are also members of the family.

Light carries energy. Sunlight warms the ground; solar panels on rooftops and at solar farms in the Northern Cape transfer the energy of sunlight into electrical energy; solar water heaters transfer it into heat; and green plants capture it in photosynthesis, the process that ultimately feeds almost every living thing on Earth.

Light does not need a material to travel through. Unlike sound, which needs air, water or a solid to carry it and cannot cross empty space, light crosses the vacuum of space between the Sun and the Earth. That is how we can see stars and galaxies.

Light from a source spreads out as it travels, so its brightness decreases with distance. A torch looks very bright up close and much fainter from across a field. This is why stars, which are suns like our own, look like faint points: they are so very far away.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the Moon is luminous; it reflects sunlight. Two: saying light travels instantly; it takes time, about eight minutes from the Sun. Three: saying light bends around corners in air; it travels in straight lines. Four: saying a pinhole camera image is the right way up; it is inverted. Five: saying light needs air to travel; it crosses empty space.

Layout for marks: for calculations, write time equals distance divided by speed, substitute with units, and give the answer with a unit.

The questions for this section are with you now: visible light as part of the electromagnetic spectrum, how light carries energy, why light can cross space while sound cannot, why light gets fainter with distance, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a candle in a dark room, three cards in a row, and the fastest thing there is — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Candle in a Dark Room

Light a candle in a dark room. Suddenly you can see the candle, the table, your hands and the wall. But there is a difference between them.

The candle makes its own light. It is luminous. The Sun, the stars, a light bulb, a fire and a cellphone screen are luminous too. Even some animals glow, like fireflies.

The table, your hands and the wall do not make light. They are non-luminous. You only see them because light from the candle bounces off them and into your eyes. Blow out the candle and they disappear into the dark.

The Moon tricks people. It looks bright, but it makes no light of its own. It is a big rock, lit up by the Sun, bouncing sunlight to us.

Keep the candle in mind and try the questions for this part: what luminous means, three luminous things, and why the Moon is not luminous.

## Subtopic: Three Cards in a Row

Make a small hole in three cards, stand them in a row, and look at a candle through the holes. Line them up perfectly and you see the flame. Nudge one card sideways and the flame vanishes. Light cannot bend to sneak through. It travels in straight lines.

You can see this everywhere: sunbeams through the clouds, torch beams on a misty night, sharp-edged shadows, and the fact that you cannot see round a corner.

We draw light as rays: straight lines with arrows.

A pinhole camera uses straight lines too. Light from the top of a tree goes down through a tiny hole and lands at the bottom of the screen. Light from the bottom goes up and lands at the top. So the picture is upside down.

Hold the cards in mind and try this part's questions: how we know light goes in straight lines, and why the pinhole picture is upside down.

## Subtopic: Faster Than Anything

Light is the fastest thing there is: 300 000 kilometres every second. It could zip around the Earth seven and a half times in one second.

Sound is a snail by comparison, about a million times slower. That is why you see lightning before you hear thunder, and why at a big cricket match you see the bat hit the ball a moment before you hear the crack.

Even so, light takes time over huge distances. Moonlight takes just over one second to reach you. Sunlight takes about eight minutes and twenty seconds. So when you look at the Sun, which you must never do directly, you see it as it was eight minutes ago.

And light can cross empty space, which sound cannot. It carries energy, warming the Earth and powering solar panels and plants.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Luminous objects make their own light; non-luminous objects are seen by reflected light. Light travels in straight lines, shown as rays, which explains sharp shadows and the inverted image in a pinhole camera. Light travels at about 300 000 kilometres per second in a vacuum, taking about 1.3 seconds from the Moon and 8 minutes 20 seconds from the Sun. Light is energy and crosses empty space.

Hold the candle, the cards and the speed, and take the final questions of the lesson: luminous or not, why shadows are sharp, and how long sunlight takes to arrive.
