### subtopic_1 — The Law of Reflection
**Assistant:** Why does light obey the law of reflection? What makes the two angles equal?

**Tutor:** One elegant way to understand it is that light reflected from a mirror always takes the quickest possible path between two points, and that quickest path is exactly the one with equal angles. This idea was first suggested by Hero of Alexandria about two thousand years ago and was later generalised by the French mathematician Pierre de Fermat. Imagine a lamp at point A and your eye at point B, both in front of a flat mirror. Light from A could, in principle, reach the mirror at any point and then travel to B. Which point does it actually use? Picture the image of B behind the mirror, at the same distance behind it as B is in front. The distance from any point on the mirror to B is the same as the distance from that point to B's image. So the path from A to the mirror to B has the same length as the path from A to the mirror to B's image. The shortest path from A to B's image is a straight line, and it crosses the mirror at exactly one point. If you check the angles at that point, you find that the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection. Any other reflection point gives a longer, bent path. Because light travels at constant speed in air, the shortest path is also the quickest. A deeper explanation, which you will meet in later years, treats light as a wave: the reflected wavelets from all parts of the surface reinforce one another only in the direction where the angles are equal, and cancel out in all other directions. Both explanations agree, and the law has been confirmed by countless measurements.

### subtopic_2 — Plane Mirrors and Images
**Assistant:** Why does a mirror swap left and right but not top and bottom?

**Tutor:** Strictly speaking, a mirror does not swap left and right or top and bottom; it swaps front and back, and our brains then interpret the result as a left-right swap. Stand facing a mirror on the wall. Your head is at the top and your feet at the bottom, and the image has its head at the top and feet at the bottom, so up and down are unchanged. Your right hand is towards the east, say, and the image's corresponding hand is also towards the east, directly opposite it, so east and west are unchanged too. What has changed is front and back: your nose points towards the mirror, north, while the image's nose points south, back towards you. Every point of you is reflected straight through the mirror, along the normal. That reversal of front and back produces something like a glove turned inside out: a right-handed glove reflected becomes a left-handed one. Why do we describe it as a left-right swap? Because we imagine ourselves walking around behind the mirror and turning to face back the way we came, a half-turn about a vertical axis, since that is how people turn. After that imagined turn, the hand that would be on our right is the image's left hand. If instead we imagined standing on our heads to get into the mirror's position, we would say the mirror swaps top and bottom. So the effect we call lateral inversion is really a front-to-back reversal combined with the way we picture people turning around. A mirror on the ceiling or the floor shows this clearly: lying under one, your image appears upside down relative to you.

### subtopic_3 — Regular and Diffuse Reflection
**Assistant:** Why does the sea look dark from directly above but bright and silvery near the horizon?

**Tutor:** Because the amount of light reflected by a water surface depends strongly on the angle at which you look at it. When light meets a water surface, part of it is reflected and part of it is refracted into the water. When you look straight down at the sea, at a steep angle, only about two percent of the light from the sky above is reflected from the surface into your eyes. Most of the light enters the water, where much of it is absorbed or scattered in the depths, so very little comes back. The sea therefore looks dark, and its colour is mostly the deep blue or green of light scattered back from within the water. As you look further away, towards the horizon, your line of sight meets the water at a more and more grazing angle. At grazing angles, the fraction of light reflected rises steeply, to well over half when the angle to the surface is very small. So near the horizon the sea acts much more like a mirror, reflecting the bright sky, and looks pale and silvery. The same effect makes a lake look like a mirror when you look across it from the shore, but transparent when you stand on a jetty and look straight down. Waves complicate the picture: the tilted faces of waves reflect different parts of the sky, giving a mixture of light and dark patches, and on a sunny day the faces that happen to be tilted at the right angle to reflect the Sun into your eyes produce the dazzling sparkle called sun glint. Polarised sunglasses reduce this glare by blocking much of the reflected light.

### subtopic_4 — Uses of Reflection
**Assistant:** How do the reflectors on road signs send light straight back to the driver from almost any angle?

**Tutor:** They use corner reflectors or tiny glass beads, shapes that reflect light back exactly in the direction it came from, wherever it comes from. A corner reflector is made of three flat mirrors, or three faces of a solid plastic or glass prism, all at right angles to each other, like the inside corner of a box where two walls meet the floor. A ray of light entering the corner reflects off each of the three surfaces in turn. Each reflection reverses one direction of its travel: one reverses left-right, another up-down and the third forwards-backwards. After all three, the ray is travelling in exactly the opposite direction to the one in which it arrived, back towards its source, even if it came in at an angle. The reflective panels on vehicles, bicycles and road signs contain thousands of tiny moulded corner reflectors. Other reflective materials, such as the white and yellow tape on trucks or the strips on school bags and safety jackets, use tiny glass beads embedded in a reflective backing. Light entering the front of each bead is refracted and focused onto the back, reflects off the backing, and is refracted again on the way out, emerging back towards the source. Because the driver's eyes are close to the car's headlights, the light returned to the headlights also reaches the driver's eyes, so the reflector looks brilliantly bright to the driver but much dimmer to anyone standing to the side. That is exactly what is needed for safety at night. Wearing reflective strips can make a pedestrian visible to a driver from many times further away than dark clothing, which is why they are strongly recommended for learners walking along roads early in the morning or after dark.
