# Part 1 — Expert

This session sorts materials by how they let light through and explains how shadows form, why they change size and sharpness, and how the largest shadows in the Solar System produce eclipses. The anchor is a winter afternoon at school. At assembly in the morning, each learner's shadow stretched far across the quad towards the west; at lunchtime the shadows were short and stubby; by home time they stretched long again, now towards the east. Inside the classroom, the shadow of a hand held near the window was dark and sharp-edged on the desk, but blurred and grey on the far wall. The governing idea is that materials can be transparent, translucent or opaque depending on how much light passes through them; that a shadow is a region where light from a source is blocked by an opaque object, and forms because light travels in straight lines; and that the size, shape and sharpness of a shadow depend on the size of the source and the distances between source, object and screen. Transparent, translucent and opaque materials, the formation of shadows, umbra and penumbra, the factors affecting shadow size, and the shadows that cause solar and lunar eclipses are examinable, and they connect to the Earth, Moon and Sun system in Term 4.

## Subtopic: Transparent, Translucent and Opaque

Materials are grouped by what happens to light that falls on them.

A transparent material lets almost all light pass straight through it, so that objects can be seen clearly through it. Clear glass, clean water, clear plastic, air and the cornea and lens of the eye are transparent.

A translucent material lets some light pass through, but scatters it in many directions, so that objects behind it cannot be seen clearly; you see light and blurry shapes. Frosted glass in a bathroom window, wax paper, tracing paper, thin fabric such as a sheer curtain, a lampshade, tissue paper, and the frosted bulbs of some lamps are translucent. Translucent materials are useful where light is wanted but privacy is needed, or where light should be soft and spread out without glare.

An opaque material lets no light pass through. Light falling on it is either reflected or absorbed. Wood, metal, brick, stone, cardboard, your body and thick fabric are opaque. Opaque objects cast dark shadows.

The categories depend on thickness. A very thin sheet of gold leaf lets some greenish light through, while a thick block of gold is opaque. A shallow layer of clear water is transparent, but the deep ocean is dark because water absorbs some light and the sea is very deep. Paper is translucent when thin but opaque when stacked. Some materials change: clear glass becomes translucent when frosted by sandblasting, which roughens its surface so that light is scattered.

A simple classroom test is to hold each material between a torch and a white screen. If the beam passes through almost unchanged and you can see the torch clearly through the material, it is transparent. If only a soft, diffuse glow passes through, it is translucent. If no light passes through and there is a dark shadow on the screen, it is opaque. A light meter on a cellphone can measure how much light passes through, giving a more objective comparison.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definitions of transparent, translucent and opaque with examples, why translucent materials blur objects behind them, how thickness can change the category, and a test to classify materials.

## Subtopic: How Shadows Form

A shadow forms when an opaque object blocks light from a source. Because light travels in straight lines, it cannot bend around the object, so the region behind the object, in line with the source, receives no light from that source. That dark region is the shadow. A shadow is therefore not a thing; it is an absence of light.

The shape of a shadow is the outline of the object as seen from the source, which is why shadow puppets work: a hand shaped the right way between a lamp and a wall casts the shadow of a rabbit, a dog or a bird. The shadow of a ball is a circle, and the shadow of a book held side-on is a thin rectangle.

The size of a shadow depends on distances. Moving the object closer to the light source makes the shadow larger, because the rays from the source spread out more steeply past the edges of the object. Moving the object closer to the screen, or the screen closer to the object, makes the shadow smaller and closer to the actual size of the object. In a shadow puppet show, the puppeteer moves the puppet towards the lamp to make a giant appear.

The length and direction of shadows outdoors depend on the position of the Sun. When the Sun is low in the sky, early in the morning or late in the afternoon, the rays strike the ground at a shallow angle and shadows are long. At midday, when the Sun is highest, shadows are shortest. In South Africa, which is in the Southern Hemisphere, the midday Sun is in the northern part of the sky, so midday shadows point roughly south. They are shortest in summer, when the Sun is high, and longest in winter, when it is low. Shadows point away from the Sun, so in the morning, with the Sun in the east, they point west, and in the afternoon they point east.

A sundial uses this to tell the time: a vertical stick or blade, called a gnomon, casts a shadow that moves around a marked dial as the Sun crosses the sky. A simple shadow stick in the school grounds, with the tip of its shadow marked every half hour, shows both the changing direction and the changing length of the shadow through the day.

The questions for this section are with you now: why shadows form, why a shadow has the shape of the object, how the distance from the source changes the size of the shadow, why outdoor shadows are long in the morning and short at midday, why midday shadows in South Africa point south, and how a sundial works.

## Subtopic: Umbra and Penumbra

The sharpness of a shadow depends on the size of the light source.

A very small source, called a point source, such as a small LED or a distant tiny bulb, produces a shadow with sharp edges. Every point on the screen either receives light from the source or does not; there is nothing in between. The whole shadow is uniformly dark.

A larger source, called an extended source, such as a frosted bulb, a fluorescent tube, a window, or the Sun as seen from the Earth, produces a shadow with two parts. In the middle is a completely dark region called the umbra, where light from every part of the source is blocked. Around it is a partly lit region called the penumbra, where light from some parts of the source is blocked, but light from other parts still gets through. The penumbra is darkest next to the umbra and gradually lighter towards its outer edge, which is why the shadow appears to fade at its edges.

The size of the penumbra depends on the size of the source and on the distance between the object and the screen. A hand held close to a desk casts a sharp shadow with little penumbra; held further from the desk, the umbra shrinks and the penumbra widens, so the shadow becomes blurred. Operating theatres in hospitals use many lights from different directions, in a large ring, so that the shadow of a surgeon's hand is almost all penumbra and never blocks the view of the patient.

Ray diagrams explain umbra and penumbra. Draw straight rays from the top and bottom edges of the source past the top and bottom edges of the object to the screen. The region that no ray from the source can reach is the umbra. The regions that rays from only part of the source can reach are the penumbra.

The questions for this section are with you now: the difference between a point source and an extended source, the meaning of umbra and penumbra, why the penumbra fades towards its edge, how distance affects the sharpness of a shadow, and drawing a ray diagram of a shadow from an extended source.

## Subtopic: Shadows in Space: Eclipses

The largest shadows we ever see are cast by the Earth and the Moon. The Sun is an extended source, so both cast shadows with an umbra and a penumbra stretching far out into space.

A solar eclipse happens when the Moon passes directly between the Sun and the Earth, so that the Moon's shadow falls on part of the Earth. People in the Moon's umbra see a total solar eclipse: the Moon completely covers the bright disc of the Sun, the sky darkens to twilight, stars appear, birds stop singing, the temperature drops, and the Sun's faint outer atmosphere, the corona, becomes visible as a pearly glow. Totality lasts at most a few minutes. People in the penumbra see a partial eclipse, with only part of the Sun covered. Because the Moon's umbra is small where it reaches the Earth, a total eclipse is seen only along a narrow track, and at any one place on Earth total eclipses happen on average only once every few hundred years. The next total solar eclipse visible from parts of South Africa is expected in November 2030. Never look directly at the Sun during an eclipse except through certified eclipse glasses or a pinhole projector, because it can permanently damage the retina.

A lunar eclipse happens when the Earth passes directly between the Sun and the Moon, so that the Moon moves into the Earth's shadow. Lunar eclipses can only happen at full Moon. During a total lunar eclipse, the Moon does not disappear but turns a deep coppery red, because a little sunlight is bent through the Earth's atmosphere into the umbra, and the atmosphere scatters away most of the blue light, leaving red. Lunar eclipses can be seen from the whole night side of the Earth and are perfectly safe to watch.

Eclipses do not happen every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted slightly relative to the Earth's orbit around the Sun, so usually the Moon passes just above or below the line joining the Sun and the Earth.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying a shadow is a dark object; it is a region where light is blocked. Two: saying translucent means the same as transparent; translucent scatters light. Three: saying moving an object towards a lamp makes its shadow smaller; it makes it larger. Four: saying a lunar eclipse happens when the Moon is between the Sun and Earth; that is a solar eclipse. Five: saying midday shadows in South Africa point north; they point roughly south.

Layout for marks: draw straight rays from the edges of the source past the edges of the object, then label umbra and penumbra.

The questions for this section are with you now: how a solar eclipse forms, total and partial solar eclipses, how a lunar eclipse forms and why the Moon turns red, why eclipses do not happen every month, safe viewing, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through windows, frosted glass and walls, hand shadows on a wall, and the Moon hiding the Sun — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Windows, Frosted Glass and Walls

Look at three things in your school. Through the classroom window you can see the playground clearly. Through the frosted bathroom window you see light, but only blurry shapes. Through the brick wall you see nothing at all.

Clear glass is transparent: light goes straight through, and you can see clearly. Frosted glass is translucent: some light gets through, but it is scattered, so everything looks blurry. That is perfect for a bathroom. Brick is opaque: no light gets through.

Other transparent things: clean water, clear plastic, air. Other translucent things: tracing paper, a lampshade, a thin curtain. Other opaque things: wood, metal, cardboard, you.

And thickness matters. One sheet of paper glows when you hold it up to the light. A thick stack of paper is opaque.

Each kind is useful in its own place.

Keep the three windows in mind and try the questions for this part: transparent, translucent or opaque, and why frosted glass is useful.

## Subtopic: Hand Shadows on a Wall

Shine a lamp at a wall and make a rabbit shape with your hand in between. A rabbit shadow appears. Your hand is opaque, so it blocks the light, and because light travels in straight lines, it cannot sneak round behind your hand. The dark patch left behind is a shadow.

Move your hand closer to the lamp and the rabbit becomes a giant. Move it closer to the wall and it shrinks back to normal size, and gets sharper.

Look carefully at the edges. With a big lamp, the shadow has a very dark middle, the umbra, and a fuzzy grey edge, the penumbra, where only part of the lamp is blocked. A tiny light, like a small LED, gives a shadow with sharp edges and no fuzzy part.

Outside, shadows are long in the morning and afternoon when the Sun is low, and short at midday when it is high. In South Africa, midday shadows point roughly south.

Hold the hand shadows in mind and try this part's questions: why shadows form, how to make a shadow bigger, and what the fuzzy edge is called.

## Subtopic: When the Moon Hides the Sun

Sometimes the Moon moves exactly between the Sun and the Earth. Its shadow falls on the Earth, and people in the darkest part of the shadow see the Sun completely covered. Day turns to twilight, stars come out and birds go quiet. That is a total solar eclipse. Never look straight at it without proper eclipse glasses.

Other times, the Earth moves exactly between the Sun and the Moon. The Moon slides into the Earth's shadow and turns a deep red colour. That is a lunar eclipse, and it is safe to watch.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Transparent materials let light through clearly, translucent ones let some through but scatter it, and opaque ones block it. A shadow forms where an opaque object blocks light, because light travels in straight lines. Moving an object towards the source makes its shadow larger. An extended source gives a dark umbra and a lighter penumbra. A solar eclipse is the Moon's shadow on Earth; a lunar eclipse is the Earth's shadow on the Moon.

Hold the windows, the hand shadows and the eclipse, and take the final questions of the lesson: classify a material, make a shadow bigger, and tell a solar from a lunar eclipse.
