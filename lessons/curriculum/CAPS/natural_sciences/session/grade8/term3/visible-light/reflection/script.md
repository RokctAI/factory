# Part 1 — Expert

This session investigates the reflection of light: the law that governs it, how a flat mirror forms an image, why smooth and rough surfaces reflect so differently, and how reflection is used. The anchor is a calm early morning at a dam in the Drakensberg foothills. The water is perfectly still, and the mountains, the sky and the trees appear upside down in it, as sharp as the real thing. Then a breeze ruffles the surface; the reflected mountains break up into flickering patches of light, and in a few seconds there is no image at all, just a bright, glittering surface. The water and the light are the same; only the smoothness of the surface has changed. The governing idea is that light striking a surface obeys the law of reflection, the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, measured from the normal; that a smooth surface reflects parallel rays in a single direction, forming images, while a rough surface scatters them in many directions; and that a plane mirror forms an upright, virtual image the same size as the object and as far behind the mirror as the object is in front. The law of reflection, the terms incident ray, reflected ray, normal and angles, ray diagrams, the properties of images in plane mirrors, regular and diffuse reflection, and uses of reflection are examinable, and they lead to curved mirrors and optics in Grade 10 and 11 Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: The Law of Reflection

Reflection is the bouncing of light off a surface. To describe it precisely, scientists use a few terms.

The incident ray is the ray of light travelling towards the surface. The reflected ray is the ray travelling away from the surface after reflection. The point where the incident ray strikes the surface is the point of incidence. The normal is an imaginary line drawn at right angles, ninety degrees, to the surface at the point of incidence; it is usually drawn as a dashed line. The angle of incidence is the angle between the incident ray and the normal. The angle of reflection is the angle between the reflected ray and the normal.

The law of reflection has two parts. First, the angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection. Second, the incident ray, the reflected ray and the normal all lie in the same flat plane.

Note carefully that the angles are measured from the normal, not from the surface. A ray striking a mirror at an angle of thirty degrees to the surface makes an angle of sixty degrees with the normal, so the angle of incidence is sixty degrees and the reflected ray leaves at sixty degrees to the normal on the other side. A ray that strikes the mirror straight on, along the normal, has an angle of incidence of zero and reflects straight back along its own path.

The law can be tested in class with a ray box, a plane mirror standing upright on a sheet of paper, and a protractor. Draw a line along the back of the mirror and a normal at right angles to it. Shine a narrow ray at the point where the normal meets the mirror, at several different angles of incidence such as 20, 30, 45 and 60 degrees. Mark the incident and reflected rays with dots, remove the mirror, join the dots and measure each angle from the normal. In every case the two angles are equal, within the accuracy of the measurement. Pins can be used instead of a ray box: pins placed along an incident line are viewed in the mirror, and further pins are placed in line with their images.

The questions for this section are with you now: the terms incident ray, reflected ray, normal, angle of incidence and angle of reflection, the two parts of the law of reflection, why angles are measured from the normal, and how to test the law.

## Subtopic: Plane Mirrors and Images

A plane mirror is a flat mirror. When you look into one, you see an image of yourself that appears to be behind the glass. Light does not actually go behind the mirror; the image is an illusion created by the law of reflection and the way the brain interprets light.

Light from each point on an object, such as the tip of your nose, spreads out and strikes the mirror. Each ray reflects according to the law of reflection, and the reflected rays spread out from the mirror. Some of them enter your eye. Your brain assumes that light travels in straight lines, so it traces the reflected rays backwards, in straight lines, to the point from which they appear to come. That point lies behind the mirror. There is nothing actually there, which is why the image is called a virtual image: it cannot be caught on a screen held behind the mirror, because no light reaches that place.

The image in a plane mirror has five properties. It is virtual. It is upright, the same way up as the object. It is the same size as the object. It is the same distance behind the mirror as the object is in front of it; if you stand two metres from a mirror, your image appears two metres behind it, four metres from you. And it is laterally inverted, meaning left and right are swapped: if you raise your right hand, your image raises what looks like its left hand. That is why the word AMBULANCE is printed back to front on the front of ambulances in many countries, so that it reads correctly in the rear-view mirror of a car ahead.

To see your whole body in a plane mirror, the mirror only needs to be half your height, placed correctly, whatever your distance from it.

Ray diagrams of plane mirror images are drawn by locating the image point behind the mirror at the same distance as the object and on the same normal, then drawing reflected rays from the mirror to the eye as solid lines and extending them back to the image as dashed lines, to show they are only apparent.

The questions for this section are with you now: why a plane mirror image appears behind the mirror, the meaning of a virtual image, the five properties of a plane mirror image, lateral inversion and the ambulance example, and how to draw a ray diagram for a plane mirror.

## Subtopic: Regular and Diffuse Reflection

Every surface reflects light according to the law of reflection, but what we see depends on how smooth the surface is.

A very smooth surface, such as a mirror, polished metal, still water or the screen of a switched-off phone, is flat even on a microscopic scale. When a parallel beam of light strikes it, every ray meets the surface at the same angle of incidence, so every ray is reflected at the same angle of reflection, and the reflected rays stay parallel. This is called regular, or specular, reflection. Because the rays stay organised, they carry an image of the source, and we see clear reflections. Regular reflection can also produce glare, a dazzling patch of light, when the reflected beam enters the eye directly.

A rough surface, such as paper, cloth, a painted wall, skin, wood or a ruffled water surface, is uneven on a microscopic scale, covered in tiny bumps and hollows facing in all directions. When a parallel beam strikes it, each ray meets the surface at a different angle, because the bit of surface it hits is tilted differently. Each ray still obeys the law of reflection, but the reflected rays leave in many different directions. This is called diffuse reflection, or scattering. No image is formed, but light is sent in all directions, so the surface can be seen from anywhere in the room.

Diffuse reflection is what allows us to see almost everything around us. If the pages of a book reflected regularly like a mirror, you would see a reflection of the light bulb instead of the words, and only from one particular angle. Matt paint, which reflects diffusely, is used on classroom walls and boards to reduce glare, while gloss paint and polished surfaces reflect more regularly and shine.

The same surface can change from one type to the other. A still dam reflects the mountains clearly; a breeze roughens the surface and the reflection breaks up. A wet road at night reflects headlights like a mirror, causing glare, while a dry road scatters light and is easier to see.

The questions for this section are with you now: the difference between regular and diffuse reflection, why both obey the law of reflection, why rough surfaces do not form images, why diffuse reflection allows us to see objects from any angle, and examples of each.

## Subtopic: Uses of Reflection

Reflection is used in countless ways.

Mirrors in homes and bathrooms let us see ourselves. Rear-view and side mirrors on cars and minibus taxis let drivers see behind them. A periscope uses two plane mirrors, each tilted at forty-five degrees, one above the other, to let a person see over a wall or a crowd, or a submarine crew see above the surface of the sea; the light is turned through ninety degrees at each mirror.

Road safety relies on reflection. The cat's eyes, small reflectors set into the road surface on many South African highways, and the reflective strips on road signs, number plates, school bags, bicycles and the jackets of traffic officers and construction workers are made of tiny glass beads or prism shapes that reflect light straight back towards its source, so that they shine brightly in a driver's headlights at night. These are called retroreflectors. Astronauts left retroreflectors on the Moon, and scientists still bounce laser beams off them to measure the distance to the Moon to within a few centimetres.

Kaleidoscopes use two or three mirrors at angles to produce many reflections of coloured beads, forming symmetrical patterns. Shops use large curved mirrors in corners to see around aisles. Dentists use small angled mirrors to see the backs of teeth. Solar power plants such as those in the Northern Cape use thousands of mirrors, called heliostats, to reflect sunlight onto a central receiver on a tall tower, heating it to generate electricity. The great telescopes at Sutherland, including the Southern African Large Telescope, collect the faint light of distant stars and galaxies with huge mirrors.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: measuring angles from the mirror surface instead of from the normal. Two: saying the image in a plane mirror is on the surface of the mirror; it is as far behind as the object is in front. Three: saying rough surfaces do not obey the law of reflection; every ray does, but the surface is tilted differently at each point. Four: saying a mirror image is upside down; a plane mirror image is upright but laterally inverted. Five: saying a virtual image can be caught on a screen; it cannot.

Layout for marks: draw the normal as a dashed line, mark angles from the normal, and show equal angles of incidence and reflection.

The questions for this section are with you now: how a periscope works, how retroreflectors make road signs visible at night, three other uses of mirrors, and the five exhibits.

## Subtopic: A Ball Bouncing off a Wall

Throw a tennis ball at a wall at a slant and it bounces off at the same slant on the other side. Light does the same thing when it hits a mirror.

Imagine a line sticking straight out of the mirror at right angles, like a pole. That line is called the normal. The light coming in makes an angle with the normal, the angle of incidence. The light bouncing out makes an angle with the normal, the angle of reflection. And here is the rule: the two angles are always equal. That is the law of reflection.

Always measure from the normal, the pole, not from the mirror itself.

Shine light straight at a mirror, along the normal, and it bounces straight back at you.

You can test it with a torch, a mirror and a protractor. Shine the light in at thirty degrees, and it bounces out at thirty degrees. Try forty-five: out at forty-five. Every time.

Keep the bouncing ball in mind and try the questions for this part: what the normal is, the law of reflection, and where to measure the angles from.

## Subtopic: Your Face in the Bathroom Mirror

Look in the bathroom mirror. Your face seems to be behind the glass. It is not really there, of course. Light from your face bounces off the mirror into your eyes, and your brain traces it back in straight lines to a spot behind the mirror. That pretend face is called a virtual image.

It is the same size as you, the right way up, and as far behind the mirror as you are in front. Stand one metre away and your image looks one metre behind the glass.

But left and right are swapped. Wave your right hand and your image waves its left. That is why AMBULANCE is written back to front on the front of ambulances, so it reads the right way in the mirror of the car in front.

Hold the bathroom mirror in mind and try this part's questions: the properties of a mirror image, and why ambulance is written backwards.

## Subtopic: A Still Dam and a Windy One

On a still morning, a dam reflects the mountains perfectly. The water surface is smooth, so all the light bounces off in neat, organised lines, and you see a clear picture. That is regular reflection.

Then the wind blows, ripples appear, and the picture breaks up. Now the surface is bumpy, so the light bounces off in all different directions. That is diffuse reflection. No picture, just a sparkle.

Most things, like paper, walls and your skin, are rough on a tiny scale and reflect diffusely. That is lucky: it is why you can see a page from any angle, instead of just a glare.

Reflections help us every day: car mirrors, periscopes, and the shiny strips on road signs and school bags that glow in headlights at night.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The law of reflection: the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, both measured from the normal. A plane mirror gives a virtual, upright, same-size, laterally inverted image as far behind the mirror as the object is in front. Smooth surfaces give regular reflection and images; rough surfaces give diffuse reflection and let us see objects from all directions.
