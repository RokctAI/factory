### subtopic_1 — The Structure of the Eye
**Assistant:** Why do eyes sometimes look red in flash photographs?

**Tutor:** Because the flash lights up the blood-rich layer at the back of the eye, and that red light is reflected straight back out through the pupil into the camera. When a photo is taken indoors or at night, the person's pupils are wide open because the light is dim. The camera's flash is usually very close to the lens and fires so quickly that the pupils have no time to shrink. The bright light from the flash passes through the wide pupil, the lens focuses it onto the retina, and some of it is reflected from the back of the eye. The retina itself is thin and nearly transparent, so much of the light reaches the choroid behind it, a layer full of blood vessels that supply the retina, and it is reflected back looking red, the colour of blood. Because the eye's optics send reflected light back roughly along the path it came in, and the flash is right next to the camera lens, the reflected red light enters the camera, and the pupils appear as glowing red dots. Cameras reduce red-eye by firing a few quick pre-flashes before the real one, which makes the pupils shrink, or by placing the flash further from the lens, so that the reflected light misses the lens. In many animals, such as cats, dogs, cows and antelope, the eyes glow green, yellow or blue instead, because they have a reflective layer behind the retina called the tapetum, which reflects light back through the receptor cells a second time, giving them better night vision. That is why the eyes of animals on a farm road at night shine so brightly in a car's headlights. Doctors also use the red reflex to check babies' eyes for problems.

### subtopic_2 — How the Eye Forms an Image
**Assistant:** Why can't we see clearly underwater without goggles?

**Tutor:** Because the cornea, which normally does about two thirds of the eye's focusing, stops bending light almost completely when it is surrounded by water. Refraction happens because light changes speed when it passes from one material into another. In air, light slows down a lot as it enters the cornea, because the cornea is much denser optically than air, and the strongly curved cornea bends the light sharply towards a focus. But the cornea and the fluid inside the eye are mostly water. When your eye is underwater, light passes from water into the cornea with almost no change in speed, so it is hardly bent at all. The eye loses most of its focusing power in an instant. The lens alone cannot make up for this, even at its fattest, so light from everything is focused far behind the retina, and the world looks very blurred, as if you were extremely long-sighted. Goggles or a diving mask solve the problem by trapping a layer of air in front of your eyes. Light passes from water into the flat glass, then into air, and then into the cornea from air, just as normal, so the cornea can do its usual job. Because of the flat window, objects underwater look about a third larger and closer than they really are. Animals that live underwater have eyes adapted for this. Fish have almost perfectly round lenses, very strongly curved, which do all the focusing without help from the cornea. Some diving birds and seals can change the shape of their lenses dramatically when they dive. Some sea nomad children in Southeast Asia have been found to see unusually clearly underwater by shrinking their pupils and strongly accommodating their lenses.

### subtopic_3 — Rods, Cones and the Optic Nerve
**Assistant:** How can I find my own blind spot?

**Tutor:** With a simple test using a piece of paper, which works because the brain fills in the gap so well that you never normally notice it. On a blank card or page, draw a small cross on the left side and a solid dot about eight centimetres to the right of it, at the same height. Hold the card at arm's length in front of you. Close your left eye and look directly at the cross with your right eye. Keep staring at the cross, and you will still be aware of the dot off to the side. Now slowly bring the card towards your face, still looking only at the cross. At a certain distance, usually about twenty to thirty centimetres, the dot will suddenly vanish. Move the card a little closer or further, and the dot reappears. At the moment it disappears, the image of the dot is falling exactly on the blind spot of your right retina, where the optic nerve leaves the eye and there are no rods or cones to detect light. Notice that you do not see a black hole where the dot was. Instead, you see the plain colour of the paper filling the space. Your brain fills in the gap with whatever surrounds it. If you draw the dot on a lined page, the lines will even seem to continue straight through the blind spot. In everyday life you never notice your blind spots because each eye's blind spot falls on a different part of the scene, so the other eye covers it, and because your eyes are constantly making tiny movements. The blind spot is a reminder that what we see is partly constructed by the brain rather than being a perfect photograph of the world.

### subtopic_4 — Eye Problems and Eye Care
**Assistant:** Why is looking directly at the Sun so dangerous, even for a few seconds?

**Tutor:** Because the cornea and lens focus the Sun's light onto a tiny spot on the retina, concentrating its energy enough to damage the light-sensitive cells, and the retina has no pain receptors to warn you. When you look at the Sun, the eye does exactly what it is designed to do: it focuses the incoming light to a sharp image on the retina. The image of the Sun is less than a quarter of a millimetre across, but all the sunlight entering the pupil is concentrated on that tiny area. The energy density there is enormous, like a magnifying glass focusing sunlight onto paper. The visible light, and especially the invisible infrared and ultraviolet, can cause chemical damage to the receptor cells and, at high intensity, heating. The damage is to the fovea, the centre of the retina, which gives sharp, detailed central vision. The condition is called solar retinopathy. It can happen within seconds to minutes, and because the retina cannot feel pain, you do not notice it while it is happening. Symptoms such as a blurry or dark spot in the centre of vision, distorted shapes or altered colours may appear hours later, and the damage can be permanent. The risk is highest during a partial solar eclipse, because the Sun looks less dazzling and people stare at it for longer, but the surface that remains visible is just as intense as ever. Ordinary sunglasses, exposed film, smoked glass and CDs are not safe filters. To watch the Sun safely, use certified solar eclipse glasses, or view it indirectly with a pinhole projector, letting sunlight pass through a small hole onto a sheet of paper and looking at the projected image.
