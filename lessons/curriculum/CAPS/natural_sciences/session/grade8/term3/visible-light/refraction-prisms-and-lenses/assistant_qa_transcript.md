### subtopic_1 — What Refraction Is
**Assistant:** Why does light actually slow down in glass? Do the particles get in the way?

**Tutor:** Not in the sense of light bumping into particles and being held up like a car in traffic. Between the atoms of glass there is empty space, and light crosses that empty space at its full vacuum speed. What slows the light overall is the way it interacts with the electrons in the atoms. Light is a wave of electric and magnetic fields. As it passes through glass, its electric field pushes the electrons in the atoms back and forth at the same frequency as the light. Vibrating electrons themselves give out light waves, at the same frequency, but slightly delayed. The light that emerges at each point is the combination of the original wave and all these little delayed waves from the vibrating electrons. When they add together, the result is a wave whose peaks are slightly behind where the original wave's peaks would be, at every point along its path. The overall effect is a wave that travels more slowly through the material, even though every individual piece of it moves at full speed between interactions. How much the light is slowed depends on how strongly the electrons respond, which depends on the material and on the frequency of the light. In ordinary glass, the electrons respond more strongly to violet and blue light than to red light, which is why blue and violet are slowed more and refracted more, producing dispersion in a prism. As soon as the light leaves the glass, there are no more electrons to delay it, so it immediately travels at full speed again. This is why light can regain its full speed after leaving a material, something a car slowed by sand could not do without an engine.

### subtopic_2 — Refraction in Everyday Life
**Assistant:** Can light ever get trapped inside water or glass instead of coming out?

**Tutor:** Yes. When light inside a denser material such as water or glass meets the boundary with air at a large enough angle, it cannot get out at all and is completely reflected back inside. This is called total internal reflection. When light passes from water into air, it bends away from the normal, so the angle of refraction in the air is larger than the angle of incidence in the water. As the angle of incidence increases, the refracted ray in the air bends further and further from the normal, closer and closer to skimming along the surface. At a certain angle of incidence, called the critical angle, the refracted ray would travel exactly along the surface, at ninety degrees to the normal. For water, the critical angle is about forty-nine degrees; for ordinary glass, about forty-two degrees; and for diamond, only about twenty-four degrees. If the angle of incidence is larger than the critical angle, refraction into the air is impossible, and all of the light is reflected back into the water or glass, as from a perfect mirror. You can see this if you look up at the surface of a swimming pool from under water: beyond a certain angle, the surface looks like a silvery mirror reflecting the bottom of the pool. Total internal reflection has many uses. Optical fibres trap light by repeated total internal reflection and carry internet signals across oceans. Doctors use bundles of fibres in endoscopes to see inside the body. The sparkle of a well-cut diamond comes from light being totally internally reflected many times inside it before escaping through the top. Binoculars use prisms that totally internally reflect light instead of mirrors, because no light is lost.

### subtopic_3 — Convex and Concave Lenses
**Assistant:** Why does a convex lens make things look bigger when you hold it close, but upside down when far away?

**Tutor:** Because the kind of image a convex lens forms depends on where the object is relative to the focal point of the lens. Every convex lens has a focal point on each side, at a distance called the focal length. When an object is further from the lens than the focal length, the rays of light from each point of the object are bent enough by the lens to converge again on the far side, forming a real image that could be caught on a screen. Because rays from the top of the object pass through the lens and cross over to the bottom, the real image is upside down. If you look through the lens at such an object, with your eye beyond where the image forms, you see this inverted image, which is why a distant tree seen through a magnifying glass held at arm's length appears upside down and small. When the object is closer to the lens than the focal length, the lens cannot bend the spreading rays enough to make them converge. They still spread out on the other side, but less steeply than before. Your eye traces these rays back in straight lines, and they seem to come from a point further away and higher up than the real object. You see a virtual image that is upright and larger than the object. That is how a magnifying glass works, and why you must hold it close to the page. Exactly at the focal point, the rays leave the lens parallel, and the image is formed infinitely far away, so the object looks large but blurry. A camera, a projector and your eye all use the real, inverted image formed when the object is beyond the focal point.

### subtopic_4 — Uses of Lenses
**Assistant:** Why are the biggest telescopes, like SALT at Sutherland, built with mirrors instead of lenses?

**Tutor:** Because very large lenses are too heavy, sag under their own weight, absorb some light and suffer from colour fringes, while large mirrors avoid all of these problems. A telescope's ability to see faint, distant objects depends on how much light it collects, which depends on the area of its main lens or mirror. So astronomers want them as large as possible. A lens has to be supported only around its edge, because light must pass through the middle. A large glass lens is extremely heavy, and it bends and sags under its own weight, distorting the image. The largest lens telescope ever built, at the Yerkes Observatory in the United States in 1897, has a lens of about one metre across, and that turned out to be about the practical limit. Light also passes through the whole thickness of a lens, so the glass must be flawless throughout, and some light is absorbed. And because a lens refracts different colours by different amounts, a single lens focuses each colour at a slightly different point, producing coloured fringes, an effect called chromatic aberration. A mirror reflects light from its front surface only, so the glass behind need not be perfect and it can be supported across its entire back. All colours reflect at the same angle, so there are no colour fringes. Mirrors can also be made in segments. The Southern African Large Telescope at Sutherland has a main mirror about eleven metres across, made of ninety-one hexagonal segments that are kept precisely aligned, making it one of the largest single optical telescopes in the Southern Hemisphere. Lenses are still used in the smaller parts of the instrument and in cameras that record the light.
