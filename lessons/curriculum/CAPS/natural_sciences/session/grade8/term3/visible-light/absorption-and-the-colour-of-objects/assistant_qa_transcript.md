### subtopic_1 — Light Is Reflected, Absorbed or Transmitted
**Assistant:** Why does a wet road or wet sand look darker than when it is dry?

**Tutor:** Because the water changes how light is reflected and scattered at the surface, so more light is trapped and absorbed and less comes back to your eye. Dry sand or a dry road surface is made of many small grains with rough surfaces and air gaps between them. When light hits the surface, much of it is scattered back out in all directions by the many boundaries between grain and air, so some of it reaches your eyes and the surface looks fairly light. When water fills the gaps and coats the grains, two things change. First, the boundary between grain and water bends light much less than the boundary between grain and air, because water and sand slow light by more similar amounts. So less light is scattered back at each grain surface, and more travels deeper into the material, where it is gradually absorbed. Second, light that is scattered back towards the surface from inside the wet layer often meets the water surface at a shallow angle and is reflected back down again, staying trapped and eventually being absorbed. The overall result is that a wet surface reflects much less light back to your eyes and absorbs more, so it looks darker and its colours look deeper and richer. The same effect makes wet fabric, wet hair and wet stones look darker than dry ones, and it is why polished, varnished wood shows its colour more strongly. On a wet road at night, a different effect also matters: the smooth film of water acts like a mirror, reflecting the headlights of oncoming cars forward instead of scattering them back towards you, which is why road markings become harder to see in the rain.

### subtopic_2 — Why Objects Have Colour
**Assistant:** If leaves absorb red and blue light for photosynthesis, why didn't plants evolve to be black and absorb everything?

**Tutor:** That is a question scientists still discuss, and there are several good explanations, none of which is complete on its own. One explanation is history. The earliest photosynthetic organisms lived in water, and some scientists suggest that other microbes living near the surface were already absorbing much of the green light, so the ancestors of plants evolved pigments that used the remaining red and blue parts. Once chlorophyll was established, later plants inherited it, and evolution tends to modify what already exists rather than start again. A second explanation is protection. Sunlight on a bright day in South Africa delivers far more energy than a leaf can use for photosynthesis. Excess absorbed energy is harmful: it heats the leaf and can damage the delicate machinery of photosynthesis. A black leaf would absorb even more energy, much of which it could not use, and it would overheat and need more water to cool itself. Reflecting some light, especially green, may help leaves avoid this damage. A third explanation is that leaves are not as wasteful of green light as they look. Although leaves reflect enough green light to look green to us, they still absorb most of the green light that falls on them, roughly three quarters of it, especially in thick leaves and in the lower layers of a canopy, and some of that absorbed green light is used. Green light that passes through upper leaves also reaches lower leaves, helping shaded parts of the plant. So green leaves may be a balance between capturing enough energy and avoiding too much. Interestingly, some plants that live in deep shade have very dark leaves, and some seaweeds deep in the sea are red, using different pigments to capture the light available there.

### subtopic_3 — Filters and Coloured Light
**Assistant:** How does a phone screen make yellow if it only has red, green and blue dots?

**Tutor:** By lighting the red and green dots of each pixel together and leaving the blue one off, which stimulates the colour-sensing cells in your eyes in the same way that yellow light does. Your eye has three kinds of colour-sensing cells, called cones. One kind responds most strongly to long-wavelength light, which we see as red; one to medium wavelengths, which we see as green; and one to short wavelengths, which we see as blue. Pure yellow light from the spectrum, with a wavelength of about 580 billionths of a metre, falls between the red and green regions, and it stimulates both the red-sensitive and the green-sensitive cones strongly, and the blue-sensitive cones hardly at all. Your brain interprets that particular combination of signals as yellow. Now consider red and green light mixed together, with no actual yellow wavelength present at all. The red light stimulates the red-sensitive cones and the green light stimulates the green-sensitive cones, giving almost the same pattern of signals as pure yellow light. Your brain cannot tell the difference, so you see yellow. The pixels on a screen are far too small to see individually from a normal distance, so the light from the red and green parts blends together in your eye. By varying the brightness of the red, green and blue parts of each pixel independently, the screen can produce millions of different combinations of cone signals, and so millions of colours. If you look at a phone or computer screen showing a yellow area through a strong magnifying glass, or put a drop of water on the screen, you can see the separate red and green dots lit up, with the blue dots dark.

### subtopic_4 — Black, White and Absorbing Energy
**Assistant:** Why do many people in hot desert regions wear loose black robes instead of white ones?

**Tutor:** Because a loose dark robe can keep the body about as cool as a white one, as long as there is a breeze, and black dye was often easier to obtain and protects better against ultraviolet. This puzzle was actually tested by scientists in the 1980s, who measured the heat gained by volunteers standing in the Negev desert wearing black robes, white robes, or army uniforms. The black robes did absorb more sunlight and became hotter on the outside than the white ones, as you would predict. But the amount of heat reaching the person's skin was about the same in both robes. The explanation lies in how heat moves. The dark outer surface of the black robe gets hot and warms the air just inside it. That warm air rises and flows out through the loose opening at the neck, drawing cooler air in at the bottom, a chimney effect, which is helped by any wind. So much of the extra heat absorbed by the black fabric is carried away by moving air before it reaches the skin. A loose robe also shades the whole body from direct sunlight and keeps hot sand and wind off the skin. Tight clothing would not work this way, because there would be no space for air to circulate, and a tight black shirt really does feel hotter than a tight white one in the sun. Dark fabric also blocks ultraviolet radiation more effectively than thin white fabric, protecting the skin from sunburn. The lesson is a good one for science in general: a simple rule, black absorbs more, is correct, but real situations often involve several effects at once, and only experiments can show which ones matter most.
