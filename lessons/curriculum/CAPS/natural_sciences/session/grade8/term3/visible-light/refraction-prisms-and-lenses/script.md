# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains refraction, the bending of light as it passes from one transparent material into another, and shows how prisms and lenses use it to change the direction of light, form images and magnify. The anchor is a glass of water on a kitchen table with a pencil standing in it. Seen from the side, the pencil seems to be broken at the surface of the water, the lower part shifted sideways and looking thicker. Pull it out and it is perfectly straight. Look down into a swimming pool and the bottom seems much closer than it really is; a coin at the bottom of a bucket that was hidden behind the rim appears when the bucket is filled with water. The governing idea is that light changes speed when it passes from one transparent material into another, and if it enters at an angle, this change of speed makes it change direction: it bends towards the normal when it slows down and away from the normal when it speeds up; and lenses, curved pieces of transparent material, use refraction to converge or diverge light, forming images. The meaning and cause of refraction, the direction of bending, everyday effects of refraction, refraction through prisms, convex and concave lenses, and the uses of lenses are examinable, and they lead to Snell's law and optical instruments in Grade 11 Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: What Refraction Is

Refraction is the change in direction of light as it passes at an angle from one transparent material into another. Materials through which light travels are called media; air, water, glass and plastic are examples.

The cause of refraction is a change in the speed of light. Light travels at almost 300 000 kilometres per second in air, but it slows down in denser transparent materials: to about 225 000 kilometres per second in water, about 200 000 in ordinary glass, and about 124 000 in diamond. A material in which light travels more slowly is called optically denser.

The rules of refraction use the same terms as reflection: the incident ray, the normal drawn at right angles to the surface at the point of incidence, and the angle of incidence measured from the normal. The ray after it has crossed the boundary is the refracted ray, and its angle with the normal is the angle of refraction.

When light passes from a less dense medium into a denser one, such as from air into water or glass, it slows down and bends towards the normal. The angle of refraction is smaller than the angle of incidence.

When light passes from a denser medium into a less dense one, such as from water or glass into air, it speeds up and bends away from the normal. The angle of refraction is larger than the angle of incidence.

When light strikes the boundary along the normal, at an angle of incidence of zero, it changes speed but does not change direction; it passes straight through.

A useful comparison is a car or a toy with wheels rolling at an angle from a smooth tarred road onto sand. The wheel that reaches the sand first slows down first while the other wheel keeps going at the higher speed, so the car turns. Rolling from sand back onto tar, the first wheel to reach the tar speeds up first, and the car turns the other way. Rolling straight onto the sand, both wheels slow at once and the car does not turn.

Refraction can be investigated with a ray box and a rectangular glass block on a sheet of paper. Draw around the block, shine a ray into one side at an angle, mark the ray where it enters and leaves, remove the block and join the points. The ray bends towards the normal on entering the glass and away from the normal on leaving, emerging parallel to its original direction but shifted sideways.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of refraction, why it happens, the speed of light in air, water and glass, the direction of bending when light enters and leaves a denser medium, what happens along the normal, and the glass block investigation.

## Subtopic: Refraction in Everyday Life

Refraction explains many familiar illusions, because the brain always assumes that light has reached the eye in a straight line from where it started.

The bent pencil. Light from the part of the pencil under water bends away from the normal as it leaves the water into the air. The brain traces the light back in a straight line and places the underwater part of the pencil higher and further to one side than it really is, so the pencil appears broken at the surface.

Apparent depth. Light from a coin at the bottom of a pool bends away from the normal as it leaves the water, so the brain traces it back to a point higher than the real coin. Water appears only about three quarters as deep as it really is. A pool that looks one and a half metres deep may actually be two metres deep, which is a real danger: people misjudge the depth of rivers and dams and get into difficulty. Anyone spearing a fish from a riverbank must aim below where the fish appears to be.

The appearing coin. A coin hidden from view behind the rim of an empty cup becomes visible when the cup is filled with water, because light from the coin is bent at the water surface over the rim into the eye.

Mirages. On a hot day, the road ahead seems to have shimmering puddles of water that disappear as you approach. The air just above the hot tar is much hotter and less dense than the air above it, so light from the sky travelling down towards the road is gradually bent upward as it passes into the hotter air near the surface. It reaches the eye from below, and the brain sees an image of the sky on the road, which looks like water. Mirages are common on long straight roads through the Karoo and the Kalahari.

Twinkling stars. Starlight passes through layers of air of different temperatures and densities that move about constantly. Each layer refracts the light slightly differently, so the tiny point of a star appears to jump about and flicker. Planets, which appear as small discs rather than points, twinkle much less.

A prism refracts light twice, at two faces that are not parallel, so the light is turned through a large angle. Because different colours are refracted by different amounts, a prism also disperses white light into a spectrum, as studied earlier in this topic.

The questions for this section are with you now: why a pencil looks bent in water, why pools look shallower than they are and why this is dangerous, the appearing coin, how a mirage forms, why stars twinkle, and how a prism bends light.

## Subtopic: Convex and Concave Lenses

A lens is a piece of transparent material, usually glass or plastic, with curved surfaces, that refracts light to form an image. There are two main types.

A convex lens, also called a converging lens, is thicker in the middle than at the edges. When a beam of parallel rays, such as light from the distant Sun, passes through it, each ray is refracted towards the thicker middle, and the rays converge, coming together at a single point on the other side called the principal focus, or focal point. The distance from the centre of the lens to the focal point is the focal length. A fatter, more strongly curved lens bends light more and has a shorter focal length.

A convex lens can form a real image, an image that can be caught on a screen, of a distant object. Holding a magnifying glass between a window and a wall, you can focus a small, upside-down image of the window or the view outside onto the wall. A convex lens can also act as a magnifying glass: when an object is placed closer to the lens than its focal length, and you look through the lens, you see an upright, enlarged, virtual image.

A concave lens, also called a diverging lens, is thinner in the middle than at the edges. Parallel rays passing through it are refracted towards the thicker edges, so they spread out, diverging, as if they came from a point on the same side as the light source. A concave lens always forms a smaller, upright, virtual image, and objects seen through it look smaller.

In ray diagrams, a convex lens is drawn as a vertical line with outward-pointing arrowheads at each end, and a concave lens with inward-pointing arrowheads. Rays parallel to the main axis are drawn bending at the lens to pass through the focal point for a convex lens, or to spread out as if from the focal point for a concave lens.

Because a convex lens concentrates sunlight into a small, very hot spot, it can set fire to paper or dry grass. Glass bottles and broken glass left in the veld can act as lenses on a sunny day and are a cause of veld fires.

The questions for this section are with you now: the shapes of convex and concave lenses, what happens to parallel rays passing through each, the meaning of focal point and focal length, real and virtual images, and why glass in the veld is a fire risk.

## Subtopic: Uses of Lenses

Lenses are used in many optical instruments, and in your own eye.

The human eye contains a convex lens, working with the curved cornea, to focus light onto the retina. Spectacles and contact lenses correct focusing problems. A short-sighted person, whose eye focuses light from distant objects in front of the retina, wears concave lenses, which spread the light slightly before it enters the eye so that it focuses further back, on the retina. A long-sighted person wears convex lenses, which add extra converging power so that light from near objects focuses on the retina instead of behind it.

A magnifying glass is a single convex lens used to make small objects look larger, for reading fine print, examining insects or checking the details of stamps and coins.

A camera uses a convex lens, or a combination of lenses, to form a small, real, inverted image on a light-sensitive sensor or film. The lens moves backwards and forwards to focus on near or distant objects, which is what happens when your cellphone camera focuses.

A projector uses a convex lens to form a large, real, inverted image of a small picture on a screen; the picture is placed upside down in the projector so that the image on the screen is the right way up.

A microscope uses two convex lenses with short focal lengths to magnify tiny objects such as cells hundreds of times. A refracting telescope uses a large convex lens with a long focal length to collect light from distant objects and form an image, which is magnified by a second lens, the eyepiece. Binoculars are a pair of small telescopes with prisms inside to shorten them and turn the image the right way up.

Optical fibres, thin strands of very pure glass, carry light signals for the internet over long distances, including under the sea to South Africa, by keeping the light trapped inside the fibre through repeated reflection at the boundary between the glass core and its coating.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying light bends towards the normal when it enters air from water; it bends away. Two: saying refraction happens because light bounces; that is reflection. Three: saying a ray along the normal bends; it passes straight through. Four: saying a concave lens converges light; a convex lens converges, a concave lens diverges. Five: saying a pool is as deep as it looks; it is deeper.

Layout for marks: draw the normal as a dashed line, show the ray bending towards the normal when slowing and away when speeding up, and state the reason, the change of speed.

The questions for this section are with you now: how spectacles correct short and long sight, how a magnifying glass, a camera and a projector use lenses, the lenses in microscopes and telescopes, and the five exhibits.

## Subtopic: A Pencil in a Glass of Water

Stand a pencil in a glass of water and look from the side. It looks snapped in half at the water's surface. Take it out: perfectly straight. What is going on?

Light travels fast in air but slows down in water and glass. When light crosses from one into the other at a slant, the change of speed makes it change direction. That bending is called refraction.

The rule: when light slows down, going from air into water or glass, it bends towards the normal, the line at right angles to the surface. When it speeds up, going from water into air, it bends away from the normal. If it goes straight in along the normal, it does not bend at all.

Think of a toy car rolling at a slant from smooth tar onto sand. The first wheel to hit the sand slows down first, so the car turns.

The pencil looks broken because the light from the underwater part bends as it leaves the water, and your brain thinks the light came in a straight line.

Keep the pencil in mind and try the questions for this part: what refraction is, why it happens, and which way light bends going into water.

## Subtopic: Tricks of the Water and the Air

Refraction plays tricks on your eyes.

A swimming pool looks shallower than it really is, about three quarters of its true depth. That is dangerous: always check how deep water really is before you jump in.

On a hot day, the road ahead in the Karoo looks wet, but the puddles vanish as you drive closer. That is a mirage. The hot air near the road bends light from the sky up into your eyes, so you see a patch of sky on the road that looks like water.

Stars twinkle because their light passes through moving layers of warm and cool air, each bending it a little differently.

Hold the tricky pool in mind and try this part's questions: why pools look shallow, and how a mirage forms.

## Subtopic: Spectacles, Cameras and Telescopes

A lens is a curved piece of glass or plastic that bends light on purpose.

A convex lens is fat in the middle. It brings light together to a point, the focus. Hold one in the sun and it makes a tiny, hot, bright spot. That is why broken glass in the dry veld can start a fire. Hold it close to something small and it makes it look bigger: a magnifying glass.

A concave lens is thin in the middle. It spreads light out, and makes things look smaller.

Lenses are everywhere: in your eye, in spectacles that help people see clearly, in your phone's camera, in projectors, microscopes and telescopes.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Refraction is the change in direction of light passing at an angle from one transparent material into another, caused by a change in speed. Light bends towards the normal when it slows down and away when it speeds up; along the normal it does not bend. Refraction makes pools look shallow, pencils look bent and causes mirages. Convex lenses converge light and can magnify; concave lenses diverge light. Lenses are used in eyes, spectacles, cameras and telescopes.
