# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains why objects have the colours they do, in terms of which colours of white light they absorb and which they reflect, and how coloured filters and coloured lights change what we see. The anchor is a school disco in a hall lit only by a red spotlight. A learner in a white shirt looks red. Another in a green shirt looks almost black. A red cap looks a brilliant red, and a blue poster on the wall seems to have disappeared into darkness. In ordinary daylight, all of them look completely different. The objects have not changed; the light falling on them has. The governing idea is that the colour of an opaque object is the colour of the light it reflects into our eyes: it absorbs some colours of the light falling on it and reflects the rest; a white object reflects all colours, a black object absorbs them all; a coloured filter transmits only its own colour and absorbs the others; and what we see depends both on the object and on the colours present in the light that illuminates it. Reflection, absorption and transmission of light, the colour of opaque objects, primary and secondary colours of light, coloured filters, the appearance of objects in coloured light, and the absorption of light energy by dark and light surfaces are examinable, and they lead to the wave model of light and the electromagnetic spectrum in Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: Light Is Reflected, Absorbed or Transmitted

When light falls on an object, three things can happen to it, often all at once.

Some of the light can be reflected, bouncing off the surface. Reflection is how we see non-luminous objects: light from a source is reflected from the object into our eyes.

Some of the light can be absorbed, taken in by the material. The energy of absorbed light is not destroyed; it is transferred to the particles of the material, usually warming it up. That is why objects in sunlight get warm.

Some of the light can be transmitted, passing through the material. Transparent and translucent materials transmit light; opaque materials transmit none.

For an opaque object, only reflection and absorption happen. Every bit of light that falls on it is either reflected or absorbed. The balance between the two decides how bright the object looks and what colour it appears.

A mirror or a sheet of polished aluminium reflects almost all the light falling on it. Fresh snow reflects most of the sunlight that reaches it, which is why it is so dazzling and why skiers and mountaineers wear dark glasses. A tarred road absorbs most of the light that reaches it and reflects very little, which is why it looks dark and becomes very hot on a summer afternoon. The Moon, although it looks bright in the night sky, reflects only about an eighth of the sunlight that falls on it; its surface is actually dark grey rock, about as dark as worn tar.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three things that can happen to light falling on an object, what happens to the energy of absorbed light, why we see non-luminous objects, and examples of surfaces that mainly reflect or mainly absorb.

## Subtopic: Why Objects Have Colour

White light, as the last lessons showed, is a mixture of all the colours of the spectrum. When white light falls on an opaque coloured object, the object absorbs some of those colours and reflects others. The colour we see is the colour of the reflected light.

A red apple in sunlight absorbs most of the orange, yellow, green, blue and violet light falling on it and reflects mainly red light. The red light reaches our eyes, so the apple looks red. A green leaf absorbs most of the red and blue light and reflects mainly green; that is why leaves look green, and it is also why plants use red and blue light for photosynthesis, absorbing the energy of those colours, while the reflected green light is the part they do not use. A yellow banana reflects red, orange, yellow and some green light, which together look yellow, and absorbs mainly blue and violet.

A white object, such as a sheet of paper or a white shirt, reflects all the colours of white light about equally, so the reflected light is still white. A black object absorbs all the colours of the light falling on it and reflects very little, so almost no light reaches our eyes from it and it looks black. Grey objects reflect all colours equally, but only partly.

What makes a material absorb particular colours is the substances in it, called pigments or dyes. Chlorophyll is the green pigment in leaves; haemoglobin is the red pigment in blood; melanin is the brown pigment in skin, hair and eyes; carotene gives carrots and pumpkins their orange colour. Paints, inks and fabric dyes contain pigments chosen to absorb and reflect particular colours.

Some colours change as pigments change. Leaves of trees such as the plane trees lining Johannesburg streets turn yellow and orange in autumn, because the green chlorophyll breaks down and the yellow and orange carotene pigments, present all along, are revealed. So the colour is not a property of the object alone. It depends on which colours are present in the light falling on the object and which of those colours the object reflects.

The questions for this section are with you now: why a red apple looks red, why leaves look green and what this means for photosynthesis, why white objects look white and black objects black, and the role of pigments.

## Subtopic: Filters and Coloured Light

A coloured filter is a transparent sheet of coloured glass, plastic or gel that transmits light of its own colour and absorbs the other colours. A red filter placed in a beam of white light transmits red light and absorbs orange, yellow, green, blue and violet, so the light emerging from it is red. A green filter transmits green and absorbs the rest, and a blue filter transmits blue.

Filters can only remove colours; they cannot add any. If red light from a red filter is shone onto a green filter, the green filter absorbs it, and no light passes through: the result is darkness. Two filters of different primary colours placed one behind the other usually let almost no light through.

The primary colours of light are red, green and blue. Mixing these three in the right proportions can produce white light and every other colour that the human eye can see. Red and green light together make yellow; red and blue make magenta, a pinkish purple; green and blue make cyan, a greenish blue. These three are the secondary colours of light. All three primaries together make white. Cellphone, computer and television screens use this: each pixel has tiny red, green and blue elements that glow at different brightnesses to produce every colour.

Coloured lights change the appearance of objects. An object can only reflect colours that are present in the light falling on it. In red light, there is only red to reflect. A white shirt reflects the red and looks red. A red cap reflects the red and looks red, just as in daylight. A green shirt absorbs red and has nothing else to reflect, so it looks black. A blue poster also absorbs red and looks black. That is exactly what happens at the disco.

In blue light, a white object looks blue, a blue object looks blue, and red, yellow and green objects look very dark. In green light, a white object looks green, and red or blue objects look black. Yellow objects, which reflect both red and green, look red in red light and green in green light.

The same idea explains why shopping for clothes under the coloured lighting of some shops can be misleading, and why stage designers choose the colours of costumes and lights together. Butchers sometimes use slightly reddish lights over meat to make it look fresher.

The questions for this section are with you now: how a coloured filter works, what happens when light passes through two filters of different colours, the primary and secondary colours of light, and the appearance of white, red, green and blue objects in red, green and blue light.

## Subtopic: Black, White and Absorbing Energy

Absorbed light transfers energy to the material, warming it. Dark surfaces absorb more of the light falling on them than light-coloured surfaces, so they warm up faster in sunlight.

A black car parked in the sun on a summer day in Upington becomes much hotter inside than a white car beside it. A black T-shirt feels hotter in the sun than a white one. Tar roads become hot enough to soften on very hot days. This is why houses in hot regions are often painted white, and why many people in the Karoo and Northern Cape traditionally used light-coloured walls and roofs: white surfaces reflect most of the sunlight and stay cooler. Some cities around the world encourage white or reflective roofs to reduce heating in summer.

Dark surfaces are used where heat is wanted. Solar water heaters on South African roofs have panels with black-coated pipes or plates, behind glass, to absorb as much sunlight as possible and heat the water. A pot painted black on the outside, placed inside a glass-covered box, can cook food using only sunlight, in a solar cooker. Black clothing helps in cold weather in the sun, and many animals in cold places have dark skin under their fur to absorb warmth.

A simple investigation tests this. Fill two identical cans with the same volume of water at the same starting temperature. Paint one black and one white, or wrap them in black and white paper. Place them side by side in the sun, and record the temperature of the water in each every five minutes. The water in the black can warms faster. The independent variable is the colour of the surface; the dependent variable is the temperature; and the volume of water, the starting temperature, the size and material of the can, and the amount of sunlight are kept the same.

Good absorbers of radiation are also good emitters. A black surface at night also gives out its heat faster than a white one, which is why a black car also cools faster once the Sun has set.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying an object has its colour built in regardless of the light; the colour depends on the light falling on it. Two: saying a red filter turns light red by adding red; it removes the other colours. Three: saying a green object looks green in red light; it looks black. Four: saying black objects reflect black light; black is the absence of reflected light. Five: saying the primary colours of light are red, yellow and blue; for light they are red, green and blue.

Layout for marks: name the colours in the incoming light, state which the object absorbs and which it reflects, and conclude what colour it appears.

The questions for this section are with you now: why dark surfaces get hotter in sunlight, uses of white and black surfaces, a fair test of the effect of colour on heating, why good absorbers are good emitters, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a red apple in sunlight, a disco with a red spotlight, and a black car on a hot day — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Red Apple in Sunlight

Hold a red apple in the sunlight. Sunlight is white light, a mixture of all the colours. When it hits the apple, the apple soaks up, or absorbs, almost all the colours: orange, yellow, green, blue and violet. It bounces back, or reflects, mainly red. Only the red light reaches your eyes. So the apple looks red.

A leaf soaks up red and blue light and bounces back green. That is why leaves are green, and why plants use the red and blue light for food-making.

A white sheet of paper bounces back all the colours, so it looks white. A black shirt soaks up all the colours and bounces back almost nothing, so it looks black. Black is not a colour of light; it is what you see when hardly any light comes back.

So the colour of something is the colour of light it bounces back to you.

Keep the apple in mind and try the questions for this part: why the apple looks red, why paper looks white, and what black really means.

## Subtopic: A Disco with a Red Spotlight

At a school disco, the hall is lit only by a red spotlight. Now there is only red light to bounce back.

A white shirt bounces back all colours, but only red is there, so it looks red. A red cap bounces back red, so it looks red. A green shirt soaks up red and has nothing else to bounce back, so it looks black. A blue poster does the same and looks black.

A red filter, the coloured plastic over the spotlight, works by letting red through and soaking up all the other colours. Filters only take colours away; they never add any.

Fun fact: the primary colours of light are red, green and blue. Your phone screen mixes tiny red, green and blue dots to make every colour, including white.

Hold the disco in mind and try this part's questions: what a white shirt looks like in red light, what a green shirt looks like, and how a filter works.

## Subtopic: A Black Car on a Hot Day

Two cars stand side by side in the sun in Upington: one black, one white. After an hour, the black one is much hotter inside. Why?

The black car soaks up almost all the sunlight that hits it, and that light energy turns into heat. The white car bounces most of the sunlight away, so it stays cooler.

That is why houses in hot places are often painted white, and why the panels of solar water heaters on roofs are black: to soak up as much sunlight as possible to heat the water.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Light falling on an object can be reflected, absorbed or transmitted. An opaque object's colour is the colour of light it reflects; it absorbs the rest. White reflects all colours; black absorbs them all. Filters transmit their own colour and absorb the rest. In coloured light, an object can only reflect colours that are present, so it may look black. Dark surfaces absorb more light and heat up faster.

Hold the apple, the disco and the black car, and take the final questions of the lesson: what colour a shirt looks under coloured light, how filters work, and why solar panels are black.
