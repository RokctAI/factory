# Part 1 — Expert

This session shows that ordinary white light is a mixture of colours, and explains how a glass prism and falling raindrops separate those colours into a spectrum. The anchor is a late afternoon summer storm passing over Pretoria. The rain moves off to the east, the Sun breaks through low in the west behind you, and a vivid arch of colour appears across the dark clouds ahead: red on the outside, then orange, yellow, green, blue and violet on the inside. Sometimes a fainter second arch appears above it with the colours reversed. The governing idea is that white light is made up of a continuous range of colours, the visible spectrum; that when light passes from one transparent material into another at an angle it changes direction, which is refraction; and that each colour is refracted by a slightly different amount, so the colours spread apart, which is dispersion. The composition of white light, the order of the colours in the spectrum, the use of a prism to produce a spectrum by refraction and dispersion, and the formation of rainbows are examinable, and they lead to the wave model of light, wavelength and the electromagnetic spectrum in Grade 10 and 12 Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: White Light Is a Mixture

Sunlight, and the light from a filament bulb or a white LED, looks white or colourless. For centuries, people believed that white light was pure and that colours were added to it by glass, water or other materials. In 1666, the English scientist Isaac Newton tested this idea with a series of careful experiments using glass prisms in a darkened room, with sunlight entering through a small hole in the window shutter.

Newton found that a prism spread the narrow beam of sunlight into a band of colours on the opposite wall. He then made a small hole in a screen to let through only one colour, say green, and passed it through a second prism. The green light was bent again, but it stayed green; no new colours appeared. So the prism was not creating colours; it was separating colours that were already there. Finally, he placed a second prism, upside down, in the path of the full spread of colours, and the colours recombined into white light. His conclusion was that white light is a mixture of all the colours of the spectrum.

The band of colours produced is called the visible spectrum. It is continuous: the colours blend smoothly into one another with no sharp boundaries. By tradition, seven colours are named, in order: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo and violet. A common way to remember the order is the name ROY G BIV, made of their first letters. Many scientists today list only six, combining indigo with blue and violet, because indigo is hard to tell apart. Newton chose seven partly because he liked the comparison with the seven notes of a musical scale.

A colour wheel or Newton's disc shows the mixing in reverse. A circular card painted in segments with the colours of the spectrum, spun rapidly on a pencil or a motor, appears whitish grey, because the eye cannot follow the separate colours and blends them together.

The questions for this section are with you now: what Newton's prism experiments showed, the evidence that the prism separates rather than creates colours, the order of the colours in the spectrum, and why a spinning colour disc looks whitish.

## Subtopic: Splitting Light with a Prism

A prism is a block of transparent material, usually glass or clear plastic, with flat polished faces. The most common has a triangular cross-section.

When a beam of light passes from air into glass at an angle, it slows down and changes direction, bending towards the normal, an imaginary line drawn at right angles to the surface. When it leaves the glass back into air, it speeds up and bends away from the normal. This change of direction at a boundary between two transparent materials is called refraction. With a triangular prism, both bends turn the light in the same direction, towards the thicker base of the prism, so the beam is deviated through a large angle.

The key to the spectrum is that the different colours travel through glass at slightly different speeds, so they are refracted by slightly different amounts. Violet light slows down the most in glass and is bent the most. Red light slows down the least and is bent the least. The other colours are in between, in order. So as the white light passes through the prism, the colours fan out, red bent least at one edge and violet bent most at the other. This separation of white light into its colours by refraction is called dispersion.

Scientists describe the different colours of light by their wavelength, the distance between successive peaks of the light wave. Red light has the longest wavelength in the visible spectrum, about 700 billionths of a metre, and violet the shortest, about 400 billionths of a metre. In glass, shorter wavelengths are slowed more, which is why violet is bent the most.

The spectrum can be produced in class by shining a ray box or a strong torch beam through a narrow slit onto one face of a triangular prism and catching the emerging light on a white screen. A CD or DVD held at an angle in sunlight also shows spectrum colours, though by a different process, the spreading of light by the many fine grooves on the disc. Soap bubbles and oil films on wet roads show colours by yet another effect.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a prism is, the meaning of refraction, why a triangular prism bends light towards its base, the meaning of dispersion, which colour is bent most and least, and how to produce a spectrum in class.

## Subtopic: How Rainbows Form

A rainbow is a natural spectrum produced by sunlight shining on millions of falling raindrops. Each raindrop acts like a tiny prism.

When sunlight enters a spherical raindrop, it is refracted at the front surface, and dispersion begins to separate the colours. The light travels to the back of the drop, where some of it is reflected from the inside surface. It travels back to the front of the drop and is refracted again as it leaves, spreading the colours further. Overall, the light comes back out of the drop roughly in the direction it came from, turned through a large angle. Red light comes out at an angle of about 42 degrees from the direction directly away from the Sun, and violet at about 40 degrees.

Because of these angles, you only see a rainbow when the Sun is behind you and the rain is in front of you. The rainbow is a circle centred on the point directly opposite the Sun, called the antisolar point, which is where the shadow of your head falls. Every drop that lies at about 42 degrees from that point sends red light to your eye, and every drop at about 40 degrees sends violet. So the red band is on the outside of the arc and violet on the inside. The ground usually cuts off the lower part of the circle, so we see an arch. From an aeroplane, or with the spray from a hosepipe or a waterfall, a complete circular rainbow can sometimes be seen.

Rainbows are most common in the early morning or late afternoon, when the Sun is low. If the Sun is higher than about 42 degrees above the horizon, the whole rainbow circle lies below the horizon and cannot be seen from the ground. So in summer at midday, no rainbow appears. Afternoon rainbows appear in the east; morning rainbows appear in the west.

A fainter secondary rainbow often forms outside the primary one, at about 51 degrees. It is produced by light that is reflected twice inside each drop. The extra reflection reverses the order of the colours, so the secondary bow has red on the inside and violet on the outside. The sky between the two bows looks noticeably darker. The constant spray of the Victoria Falls, called Mosi-oa-Tunya, the smoke that thunders, produces rainbows on almost every sunny day, and even faint lunar rainbows by the light of the full Moon.

You can make your own rainbow on a sunny afternoon. Stand with your back to the Sun, set a garden hosepipe to a fine mist, and spray it in front of you, slightly upward. A rainbow arc appears in the spray, at the same angle as in the sky. Move your head and the rainbow moves with you, because each person sees light from a different set of drops; no two people ever see exactly the same rainbow. That is also why you can never reach the end of a rainbow.

The questions for this section are with you now: how a raindrop separates sunlight into colours, why the Sun must be behind you, why red is on the outside of the primary bow, why rainbows are seen in the morning and late afternoon, and how a secondary rainbow differs.

## Subtopic: Beyond the Visible Spectrum

The visible spectrum is only a tiny part of the full range of electromagnetic radiation. Beyond the red end of the spectrum lies infrared radiation, with wavelengths longer than red light, which our eyes cannot see but our skin feels as warmth. It was discovered in 1800 by William Herschel, who placed thermometers in the different colours of a spectrum from a prism and found that the temperature was highest just beyond the red end, where there was no visible light at all. Television remote controls, night-vision cameras and thermal imaging cameras use infrared.

Beyond the violet end lies ultraviolet radiation, with wavelengths shorter than violet. It causes sunburn and skin damage and is the reason for wearing sunscreen and hats, especially in South Africa, which has some of the highest ultraviolet levels and skin cancer rates in the world. Ultraviolet also makes some substances glow, which is how fluorescent paint, banknote security marks and washing powder brighteners work. Bees and many birds can see ultraviolet, and many flowers have ultraviolet patterns, invisible to us, that guide bees to the nectar.

Further along are radio waves and microwaves on the long-wavelength side, and X-rays and gamma rays on the short-wavelength side. All of them travel at the speed of light in a vacuum.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying a prism adds colour to white light; it separates colours already present. Two: saying red is bent most; violet is bent most. Three: saying you can see a rainbow by looking towards the Sun; the Sun must be behind you. Four: saying the colours of a primary rainbow are violet on the outside; red is on the outside. Five: saying the spectrum has exactly seven separate colours with sharp edges; it is continuous.

Layout for marks: name refraction and dispersion, state which colour is bent most, and explain using the different speeds of the colours in glass or water.

The questions for this section are with you now: infrared and ultraviolet and where they lie relative to the visible spectrum, Herschel's discovery, uses and dangers of ultraviolet, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a prism on a sunny windowsill, raindrops with the Sun at your back, and the colours we cannot see — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Prism on a Sunny Windowsill

Put a glass prism on a sunny windowsill and a strip of rainbow colours appears on the wall or the floor. Where did the colours come from?

They were in the sunlight all along. White light is not one colour. It is a mixture of all the colours, squashed together so that we see white. Isaac Newton proved this with prisms over three hundred years ago. He split sunlight into colours, then used a second prism to put them back together into white again.

The colours always come out in the same order: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet. Remember it as ROY G BIV.

Why does a prism split the light? When light goes into glass at a slant, it slows down and bends. That bending is called refraction. But each colour slows down by a different amount. Violet slows down the most and bends the most. Red slows down the least and bends the least. So the colours fan out. That spreading is called dispersion.

A raindrop can do the same job, as you will see next.

Keep the windowsill prism in mind and try the questions for this part: what white light is made of, the order of the colours, and which colour bends most.

## Subtopic: Raindrops with the Sun at Your Back

A rainbow is millions of tiny prisms working together: raindrops.

Sunlight goes into each drop and bends, bounces off the back of the drop, and bends again as it comes out. Each colour comes out at a slightly different angle, so the colours separate.

Here is the trick to finding one: stand with the Sun behind you and the rain in front of you. That is why rainbows show up in the late afternoon in the east, after a storm has passed, or in the morning in the west. At midday the Sun is too high, and there is no rainbow.

Red is always on the outside of the bow and violet on the inside. Sometimes a second, fainter rainbow appears above it, with the colours the other way round.

Hold the rainbow in mind and try this part's questions: where the Sun must be, which colour is on the outside, and why there are no midday rainbows.

## Subtopic: Colours We Cannot See

The rainbow carries on past the colours we can see.

Just past red is infrared. You cannot see it, but you can feel it as warmth from the Sun or a fire. Your TV remote talks to the TV with infrared.

Just past violet is ultraviolet. You cannot see it either, but it gives you sunburn, which is why sunscreen and hats matter so much in sunny South Africa. Bees can see it, and many flowers have hidden ultraviolet patterns that guide bees to their nectar.

Pocket summary of the lesson. White light is a mixture of colours: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo and violet. A prism separates them by refraction and dispersion; violet bends most and red least. Rainbows form when sunlight is refracted, reflected and dispersed by raindrops, with the Sun behind you; red is on the outside of the primary bow. Infrared lies beyond red and ultraviolet beyond violet.

Hold the prism, the rainbow and the hidden colours, and take the final questions of the lesson: the order of the spectrum, how a rainbow forms, and what lies beyond each end.
