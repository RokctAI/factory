### subtopic_1 — White Light Is a Mixture
**Assistant:** If white light contains all colours, why does mixing all paint colours give a muddy brown, not white?

**Tutor:** Because mixing light and mixing paint are opposite processes: light colours add together, while paints take colours away. When you mix coloured lights, for example by shining a red, a green and a blue spotlight onto the same white wall, each light adds its own colour to what reaches your eye. Red, green and blue light together stimulate all three kinds of colour-sensing cells in your eye, and you see white. This is called additive mixing, and it is exactly how a cellphone or television screen works: each tiny dot, or pixel, is made of red, green and blue parts, and by lighting them in different combinations the screen can make every colour, including white. Paint works the other way. A paint pigment looks coloured because it absorbs some colours from the white light falling on it and reflects the rest. Yellow paint, for example, absorbs mainly blue light and reflects red and green, which together look yellow. Blue paint absorbs mainly red and yellow and reflects blue and some green. When you mix paints, each pigment absorbs its own set of colours, so the mixture absorbs the colours absorbed by all of them. Mix many paints together and almost every colour is absorbed by one pigment or another, so very little light is reflected, and the result is a dark, muddy brown or near black. This is called subtractive mixing. Printers use it deliberately, with cyan, magenta, yellow and black inks. So both facts are true: white light contains all the colours, and mixing all paints gives dark mud, because paints remove light rather than add it.

### subtopic_2 — Splitting Light with a Prism
**Assistant:** Why does a flat window pane not split sunlight into colours, if glass disperses light?

**Tutor:** Because in a flat sheet of glass with parallel faces, the two refractions cancel each other out, so the colours come out travelling in the same direction they went in. When white light enters the first face of a window pane at an angle, it is refracted and the colours do begin to separate slightly, violet bending a little more than red. But when the light reaches the second face, which is parallel to the first, each colour is refracted back by exactly the amount it was bent on the way in. Violet, which was bent most on entering, is bent back most on leaving; red, bent least, is bent back least. All the colours therefore leave the glass travelling parallel to their original direction, just shifted sideways by a tiny amount. Because the colours are now parallel again, they overlap almost completely and still look white. In a thick slab you might notice a very faint coloured fringe at the edges of a bright beam, but in a thin window pane the sideways shift is far too small to see. A triangular prism is different because its two faces are not parallel. The first refraction bends the light towards the base, and the second, at the sloping face, bends it further towards the base instead of bending it back. The dispersion produced at the first face is increased at the second, so the colours leave in clearly different directions and spread further apart the further they travel. That is why the shape of a prism matters, and why cut diamonds, with many angled faces, flash with colours, a quality jewellers call fire, while a flat sheet of the same material would not.

### subtopic_3 — How Rainbows Form
**Assistant:** Why does the sky look darker between the primary and secondary rainbows?

**Tutor:** Because raindrops in that band of sky send almost no light towards your eye, while the drops inside the primary bow and outside the secondary bow do. Light that enters a raindrop, reflects once inside it and leaves can come out at many different angles, but none of it comes out at more than about 42 degrees from the antisolar point, the point directly opposite the Sun. Most of it comes out at angles a little less than that maximum, and it piles up most strongly right at the maximum, which is why the primary bow is bright and why the sky inside the primary bow is noticeably brighter than the sky outside it. Light that reflects twice inside a drop behaves in the opposite way: it can only come out at angles greater than about 51 degrees, piling up at that minimum to form the secondary bow, and lighting the sky outside it. Between about 42 and 51 degrees, neither the once-reflected nor the twice-reflected light can reach you, so the sky in that band is darker than the sky on either side. This dark band was described around the year 200 by the Greek philosopher Alexander of Aphrodisias, and is called Alexander's dark band in his honour. Next time you see a double rainbow over the Highveld, look between the bows and you will see it. The secondary bow is fainter than the primary because some light escapes from the drop at each reflection, so after two reflections less remains. Very occasionally, faint third and fourth bows formed by three and four reflections exist, but they appear on the same side of the sky as the Sun and are almost never seen.

### subtopic_4 — Beyond the Visible Spectrum
**Assistant:** Why can we see only the range from red to violet and not infrared or ultraviolet?

**Tutor:** Because the light-sensitive cells in our eyes are built to respond to that range, and that range is where sunlight reaching the ground is strongest and most useful for seeing. The cells at the back of the eye that detect light contain special pigment molecules. When light of the right energy strikes one of these molecules, it changes shape, triggering a nerve signal to the brain. Each type of pigment responds well to a particular band of wavelengths. Our three types of colour-sensing cells together cover roughly 400 to 700 billionths of a metre, from violet to red. Infrared light has too little energy per packet to trigger the pigments, and ultraviolet light, which has more energy, is mostly absorbed by the lens at the front of the eye before it reaches the retina, which also protects the retina from damage. This range is not an accident. The Sun gives out the most energy in the visible range, and the atmosphere lets visible light through easily while blocking much of the ultraviolet and absorbing parts of the infrared. Eyes that evolved to use the most plentiful light that reaches the ground ended up sensitive to this band. Other animals have slightly different ranges. Bees, many birds and some butterflies can see near ultraviolet, which helps them find flowers and recognise each other. Some snakes, such as pythons, have pit organs on their faces that sense infrared, letting them detect the body heat of prey in complete darkness. People who have had the lens of the eye removed in cataract surgery, without an ultraviolet-blocking replacement, have reported being able to see a little into the ultraviolet.
