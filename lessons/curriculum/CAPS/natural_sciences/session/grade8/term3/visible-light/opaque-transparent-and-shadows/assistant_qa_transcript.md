### subtopic_1 — Transparent, Translucent and Opaque
**Assistant:** Why does frosted glass scatter light when it is made of the same glass as a clear window?

**Tutor:** Because frosting roughens the surface into millions of tiny bumps and pits, each tilted at a different angle, so light passing through is refracted in many different directions instead of all in the same direction. When light passes through a smooth, flat sheet of glass, every ray in a beam meets the surface at the same angle and is refracted by the same amount, so the rays stay in the same arrangement and keep carrying a clear image of whatever is behind the glass. Frosted glass is made by sandblasting one surface with fine grit, or by etching it with acid, which leaves a surface covered in microscopic irregularities. Each tiny facet is tilted differently, so each ray that passes through is bent by a different amount in a different direction. Rays that started out side by side, carrying neighbouring parts of an image, end up travelling in completely different directions, and the image is scrambled. Plenty of light still gets through, which is why frosted glass is bright, but the information about where each ray came from is lost, so you see only light and vague shapes. The glass material itself has not changed; only its surface has. You can show this in a simple way: put a drop of water or a strip of clear sticky tape on frosted glass, and that spot becomes almost clear. The water or tape fills in the bumps and leaves a smooth outer surface, so the light is no longer scattered. The same scattering explains why many translucent things look white, such as clouds, milk, sugar, snow and frosted bulbs: they contain many tiny surfaces or particles that scatter light of all colours in all directions.

### subtopic_2 — How Shadows Form
**Assistant:** How did Eratosthenes use shadows to measure the size of the Earth over two thousand years ago?

**Tutor:** He compared the length of shadows at the same moment in two cities a known distance apart, and used the difference in the angle of the Sun to work out the Earth's circumference. Around 240 years before the common era, Eratosthenes was the chief librarian at Alexandria in Egypt. He learned that at noon on the longest day of the year, in the town of Syene, far to the south, the Sun shone straight down to the bottom of a deep well, and upright pillars cast no shadow. So the Sun was directly overhead there. At the same time in Alexandria, an upright stick did cast a short shadow. By measuring the stick and its shadow, he found that the Sun's rays were striking Alexandria at an angle of about seven degrees from the vertical, which is about one fiftieth of a full circle. Eratosthenes reasoned that because the Sun is so far away, its rays arrive parallel at both cities. The only way parallel rays could strike the ground at different angles in two places is if the ground itself is curved. The angle between the verticals at the two cities, measured at the centre of the Earth, must also be about one fiftieth of a circle. So the distance between Alexandria and Syene must be about one fiftieth of the distance all the way around the Earth. The distance was about 5 000 stadia, a unit of the time, giving a circumference of about 250 000 stadia. Depending on which length of stadion he used, this is within a few percent to perhaps fifteen percent of the true value, about 40 000 kilometres. It was an astonishing result, using only shadows, geometry and clear reasoning.

### subtopic_3 — Umbra and Penumbra
**Assistant:** Why do some solar eclipses leave a ring of Sun showing around the Moon instead of covering it completely?

**Tutor:** Because the Moon's distance from the Earth changes, and when it is near its furthest point, its umbra does not quite reach the Earth, so the Moon looks slightly too small to cover the Sun. By a remarkable coincidence, the Sun is about four hundred times wider than the Moon, but also about four hundred times further away, so the two look almost exactly the same size in our sky. But the Moon's orbit is not a perfect circle. Its distance varies between about 356 000 and 407 000 kilometres. When the Moon is near its closest point during an eclipse, it looks slightly larger than the Sun, its dark umbra reaches the Earth's surface, and people in the umbra's path see a total eclipse. When the Moon is near its furthest point, it looks slightly smaller than the Sun. Its umbra, which narrows to a point behind the Moon like a cone, ends in space before reaching the Earth. Beyond the tip of the umbra is a region called the antumbra, where an observer sees the Moon silhouetted entirely within the Sun's disc, with a bright ring of sunlight all around it. This is called an annular eclipse, from the Latin word for ring, and the dramatic ring is sometimes called a ring of fire. Because some of the Sun's bright surface is always visible, the sky does not get fully dark and the corona cannot be seen, and it is never safe to look at an annular eclipse without proper eclipse glasses. Over many millions of years, the Moon is slowly moving away from the Earth, about four centimetres a year, so in the far future total eclipses will no longer be possible, and all central eclipses will be annular.

### subtopic_4 — Shadows in Space: Eclipses
**Assistant:** Why does the Moon turn red during a total lunar eclipse instead of disappearing completely?

**Tutor:** Because the Earth's atmosphere bends some sunlight around the edge of the Earth into its shadow, and on the way the atmosphere scatters out most of the blue light, leaving mainly red light to fall on the Moon. If the Earth had no atmosphere, its umbra would be completely dark, and during a total lunar eclipse the Moon would almost vanish from the sky. But the Earth is surrounded by a thin layer of air. Sunlight passing through the atmosphere around the edge of the Earth, the ring where it is sunrise or sunset for people on the ground, is refracted, bent slightly inward, as it passes from the vacuum of space into the denser air and out again. That bending directs some sunlight into the umbra, onto the Moon. As sunlight travels the long path through the atmosphere, air molecules scatter the shorter wavelengths, blue and violet, much more strongly than the longer wavelengths, red and orange. That scattering is why the daytime sky is blue. By the time the light emerges on the far side, most of the blue has been scattered away, and the light that reaches the Moon is reddish, the same light that makes sunsets red. In effect, the eclipsed Moon is lit by the light of all the sunrises and sunsets on Earth at that moment. The exact colour varies from eclipse to eclipse, from bright copper to dark brick red, depending on how much dust, cloud and volcanic ash is in the Earth's atmosphere. After large volcanic eruptions, eclipses can be so dark that the Moon nearly disappears. Astronomers rate the darkness of lunar eclipses on a simple scale, and the colour tells them about the state of our own atmosphere.
