### subtopic_1 — Luminous and Non-Luminous Objects
**Assistant:** How do fireflies and deep-sea fish make light without getting hot?

**Tutor:** They use a chemical reaction that releases its energy directly as light, instead of first making something hot enough to glow. In a candle flame or a bulb filament, energy is first turned into heat, and the very hot material then gives out light, along with a lot of infrared radiation, which is why such sources are hot. Living things that glow use a different method called bioluminescence. Inside special cells, a substance called luciferin reacts with oxygen, helped by an enzyme called luciferase. In the reaction, the luciferin molecule is left in an excited state with extra energy, and as it settles back down, it releases that energy as a tiny packet of visible light. Almost all of the energy comes out as light and very little as heat, which is why it is sometimes called cold light. A firefly's light is far more efficient than an old filament bulb. Animals use bioluminescence for many purposes. Fireflies flash in patterns to attract mates, each species with its own code. Deep-sea anglerfish dangle a glowing lure in front of their mouths in the dark depths to attract prey. Some squid and fish light up their undersides so that, seen from below against the faint light from the surface, they cast no dark silhouette, which hides them from predators. Many jellyfish flash when disturbed, startling attackers. On the South African coast, at certain times of year, tiny single-celled organisms called dinoflagellates bloom in such numbers that breaking waves and splashing footprints glow blue at night, because every disturbance triggers a flash in millions of them at once.

### subtopic_2 — Light Travels in Straight Lines
**Assistant:** If light travels in straight lines, how can we see objects that are behind us in a mirror?

**Tutor:** Because the light still travels in straight lines, but it changes direction when it reflects off the mirror. Straight-line travel means that light goes straight as long as it stays in the same uniform material. When it hits a surface, it can bounce off in a new direction, which is reflection, or bend as it enters a different material, which is refraction. Between those events, it travels straight again. When you look in a mirror at something behind you, light from the object travels in a straight line to the mirror, reflects off it, and then travels in another straight line into your eye. Your brain assumes that light always reaches your eye in a straight line from where it came, so it traces the light back along the last straight section, and you see the object as if it were behind the mirror. That is why a reflection seems to be on the other side of the glass. A periscope, used in submarines and sometimes in crowds to see over people's heads, uses two mirrors to change the direction of light twice, so you can see over a wall. Fibre-optic cables, which carry internet signals under the sea to South Africa, guide light around bends by reflecting it from the inside walls of a very thin glass fibre thousands of times, in straight zigzag sections. So straight-line travel and seeing round corners are not in conflict: light only ever goes round corners by being reflected or refracted, never by curving through empty air on its own. On very large scales, gravity can bend the path of light, as Einstein predicted, but that only matters near enormous masses such as stars and galaxies.

### subtopic_3 — The Speed of Light
**Assistant:** How did Ole Rømer work out the speed of light just by watching Jupiter's moons?

**Tutor:** He noticed that the timing of a regular event seemed to drift, and realised that the drift was caused by the time light took to cross a changing distance. Jupiter's moon Io orbits Jupiter about every 42.5 hours, and once each orbit it passes into Jupiter's shadow and disappears, an eclipse. These eclipses should happen at perfectly regular intervals, like the ticks of a clock, and astronomers of the 1670s used tables of them to help with navigation. Rømer, working at the Paris Observatory, observed that when the Earth was moving away from Jupiter in its orbit around the Sun, the eclipses happened later and later than predicted, and when the Earth was moving towards Jupiter, they happened earlier and earlier. Over half a year, the total difference added up to many minutes. He explained this by saying that light is not instantaneous. When the Earth is on the far side of its orbit from Jupiter, the light from Io has an extra distance to travel, the width of the Earth's orbit, so the news of each eclipse reaches us later. From the delay of roughly twenty-two minutes he measured, and the best estimate of the size of the Earth's orbit at the time, the Dutch scientist Christiaan Huygens calculated a speed of about 220 000 kilometres per second. That is too low, mainly because the timing was not precise and the size of the orbit was not well known; the true delay is about 16.7 minutes. But it was the first evidence that light has a finite speed, and it was within about a quarter of the correct value, a remarkable result for the 1670s, using nothing more than a telescope, a clock and careful reasoning.

### subtopic_4 — Light as Energy and Radiation
**Assistant:** Why can light cross empty space when sound cannot?

**Tutor:** Because sound is a vibration of particles of a material, while light is a wave of electric and magnetic fields that needs no particles at all. Sound travels when vibrating particles push on neighbouring particles, which push on their neighbours, passing the vibration along, like a ripple through a crowd. In a vacuum there are no particles to push, so sound cannot travel. A famous experiment shows this: an electric bell ringing inside a sealed glass jar becomes fainter and fainter as the air is pumped out, until it cannot be heard at all, even though you can still see the hammer striking the bell. You can see it because light passes through the vacuum, but you cannot hear it because sound cannot. Light is different. In the 1860s the Scottish physicist James Clerk Maxwell showed that a changing electric field creates a magnetic field, and a changing magnetic field creates an electric field, so the two can keep creating each other and travel through space together as a self-sustaining wave. He calculated the speed of such a wave from measurements of electricity and magnetism, and found it came out equal to the measured speed of light, which led him to conclude that light is exactly such an electromagnetic wave. Because it consists of fields rather than moving particles of a material, it can travel through a complete vacuum. That is why sunlight reaches us across 150 million kilometres of nearly empty space, why we can see distant galaxies, and why astronauts on the Moon, with no air, had to talk to each other by radio, an electromagnetic wave, even when standing side by side.
