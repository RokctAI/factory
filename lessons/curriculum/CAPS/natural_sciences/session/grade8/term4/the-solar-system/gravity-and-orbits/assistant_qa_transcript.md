### subtopic_1 — Gravity Holds the Solar System Together
**Assistant:** If the Earth pulls on me and I pull on the Earth equally, why do I fall and the Earth doesn't move?

**Tutor:** The Earth does move, but by an amount far too tiny to notice, because its mass is so enormous compared with yours. When you jump off a step, the gravitational pull of the Earth on you and the pull of you on the Earth are exactly equal in size and opposite in direction. Newton showed that forces always come in such pairs. But the same force produces very different effects on objects of very different mass. A force gives a small mass a large acceleration and a large mass a tiny acceleration. Your mass might be about 50 kilograms. The Earth's mass is about six million billion billion kilograms, more than a hundred thousand billion billion times greater. So while you accelerate towards the Earth at about ten metres per second every second, the Earth accelerates towards you at a rate so small that, during your fall, it moves less than the width of an atom's nucleus. For all practical purposes, you fall and the Earth stays still. The same principle applies to the Earth and the Sun. Strictly, the Earth does not orbit the exact centre of the Sun. Both the Earth and the Sun orbit their common centre of mass, the balance point between them. Because the Sun is so much more massive, that balance point lies deep inside the Sun, very close to its centre. Jupiter, being much more massive than Earth, pulls the Sun around a balance point that lies just outside the Sun's surface. Astronomers use this wobble of stars, caused by orbiting planets tugging on them, to discover planets around other stars, measuring the tiny back-and-forth motion of the star in its light.

### subtopic_2 — How an Orbit Works
**Assistant:** Why doesn't the Moon fall down onto the Earth if Earth's gravity is pulling it?

**Tutor:** It is falling, all the time, but it is also moving sideways so fast that it keeps missing. The Moon travels around the Earth at about one kilometre per second. If there were no gravity, it would fly off in a straight line into space. Earth's gravity bends its path, pulling it towards the Earth. In one second, the Moon moves about one kilometre sideways and, because of the Earth's pull, falls about one and a third millimetres towards the Earth compared with where a straight line would have taken it. Over that one kilometre, the curve of its circular orbit around the Earth also drops away by about one and a third millimetres. So the fall exactly matches the curve, and the Moon stays at the same distance, about 384 000 kilometres. That is the balance that makes an orbit. Newton used exactly this calculation to test his idea of universal gravitation. He knew that an apple near the Earth's surface falls about five metres in its first second. The Moon is about sixty times further from the centre of the Earth than the apple. If gravity weakens with the square of the distance, the Moon should fall sixty times sixty, that is 3 600 times, less in one second: five metres divided by 3 600, about one and a third millimetres. That matched the Moon's observed motion. The same force that pulls the apple down holds the Moon in its orbit. Interestingly, the Moon is very slowly moving away from the Earth, by about four centimetres a year, because of the tides it raises in the Earth's oceans, which transfer energy to the Moon's orbit.

### subtopic_3 — Orbital Speed and Distance
**Assistant:** Why do satellites in low orbit fall back to Earth eventually, but the Moon doesn't?

**Tutor:** Because satellites in low orbit are still inside the very top of the Earth's atmosphere, which drags on them and slowly steals their speed, while the Moon orbits far beyond any atmosphere. The atmosphere does not end suddenly; it gets thinner and thinner with height. At 400 kilometres, where the International Space Station orbits, the air is about a billion times thinner than at sea level, but the station is moving at nearly eight kilometres per second, so even that thin air produces a small drag force. Each orbit, the station loses a little speed. With less speed, it cannot quite keep missing the Earth, and it sinks into a slightly lower orbit, where the air is thicker and the drag is greater. Without help, it would spiral down and burn up within a year or two. That is why the space station regularly fires rocket engines to boost itself back up. The amount of drag also depends on the Sun: when the Sun is very active, it heats and puffs up the upper atmosphere, increasing drag, and many satellites have been lost early during strong solar activity. Higher satellites, such as the navigation satellites at about 20 000 kilometres and the communication satellites at about 36 000 kilometres, experience almost no drag and can stay in orbit for thousands of years or longer. The Moon, at 384 000 kilometres, is in effectively empty space and has orbited the Earth for billions of years. So it is not that low satellites are pulled harder; it is that they are slowly braked. In empty space, with nothing to slow it, an orbit would last forever.

### subtopic_4 — Why the Solar System Is a Disc
**Assistant:** Why does a spinning cloud flatten into a disc instead of a ball?

**Tutor:** Because spin resists collapse in the directions around the axis, but does nothing to stop collapse along the axis, so the cloud keeps falling in from the top and bottom while staying wide around its middle. Imagine a slowly turning, roughly round cloud of gas and dust, with its axis of spin pointing up and down. Gravity pulls every part of the cloud towards the centre. Material near the top and bottom, close to the axis, has very little sideways motion around the axis, so nothing stops it falling straight in towards the middle. Material out at the sides is moving around the axis. As it falls inwards, it must speed up, because of a law called the conservation of angular momentum, the same law that makes a spinning skater speed up when she pulls in her arms. Faster spin makes the material tend to swing outward, like water flung from a spinning bicycle wheel, and this resists further collapse towards the axis. So the cloud collapses quickly from top and bottom, but slowly from the sides. Particles also collide with one another as they fall, and these collisions cancel out up-and-down motions, while the overall spin around the axis cannot be cancelled. The result is a thin, rapidly rotating disc around a dense centre. This happens wherever matter collapses while spinning. Rings around Saturn, discs around young stars, the swirling discs of matter around black holes and the shape of spiral galaxies like our Milky Way are all flattened by the same physics. Telescopes such as ALMA in Chile have photographed discs around young stars with rings and gaps that are thought to be where new planets are forming.
