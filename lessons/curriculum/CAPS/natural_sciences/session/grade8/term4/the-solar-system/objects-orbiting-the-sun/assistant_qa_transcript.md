### subtopic_1 — The Eight Planets
**Assistant:** Why are the inner planets rocky and the outer planets mostly gas and ice?

**Tutor:** Because of the temperature in different parts of the disc of gas and dust from which the Solar System formed, about 4.6 billion years ago. When the Sun formed, it was surrounded by a flat, spinning disc of leftover gas and dust. Close to the young Sun, the disc was hot. Only materials with high melting points, such as rock-forming minerals and metals like iron and nickel, could exist there as solid grains; water, methane and ammonia were present only as gases. So the solid material available near the Sun was limited to rock and metal, and there was not much of it. Over millions of years, these grains stuck together into larger and larger bodies, forming the small rocky planets Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars. Further out, beyond a boundary sometimes called the frost line or snow line, somewhere in the region of today's asteroid belt, the disc was cold enough for water, ammonia and methane to freeze into solid ice. Because these substances are far more abundant than rock-forming materials, the solid cores that formed out there grew much larger, many times the mass of the Earth. Those massive cores had strong enough gravity to pull in and hold enormous amounts of the hydrogen and helium gas that made up most of the disc, growing into the giants Jupiter and Saturn. Uranus and Neptune, further out where the disc was thinner and growth was slower, collected less gas and are made mostly of ices, which is why they are sometimes called ice giants. Meanwhile, the young Sun's strong wind blew much of the remaining gas out of the inner Solar System, so the rocky planets kept only thin atmospheres.

### subtopic_2 — Moons
**Assistant:** How could a moon like Europa have liquid water if it is so far from the Sun and so cold?

**Tutor:** Because Europa is heated from the inside by tides caused by Jupiter's enormous gravity, not by sunlight. Europa's surface is extremely cold, around minus 160 degrees Celsius, and covered by a crust of ice. But Europa's orbit around Jupiter is not a perfect circle; it is slightly stretched, partly because of regular tugs from the neighbouring moons Io and Ganymede. As Europa moves closer to Jupiter and then further away during each orbit of about three and a half days, the strength of Jupiter's gravitational pull on it changes. That stretches and squeezes the whole moon, flexing it slightly like a rubber ball being squeezed in a hand. This constant flexing produces friction inside the moon, which generates heat, a process called tidal heating. Scientists calculate that this heat is enough to keep a layer of water liquid beneath the ice crust, a global ocean estimated to be tens of kilometres deep, containing perhaps twice as much water as all of Earth's oceans. Evidence comes from the cracked, jumbled patterns on Europa's surface, from measurements of Europa's magnetic field by the Galileo spacecraft, which suggested a salty, conducting ocean, and possibly from plumes of water vapour spotted by the Hubble Space Telescope. Because liquid water, energy and the right chemical elements are thought to be the main ingredients for life, Europa is one of the most interesting places to search for life beyond Earth. The Europa Clipper spacecraft, launched in 2024, is on its way to study it in detail. The same tidal heating, much stronger on Io, which is closer to Jupiter, melts rock and drives Io's hundreds of active volcanoes.

### subtopic_3 — Asteroids and Dwarf Planets
**Assistant:** How do scientists know the Vredefort Dome was made by an asteroid and not by a volcano?

**Tutor:** By finding features in the rocks that can only be produced by the colossal, almost instantaneous shock of a high-speed impact, which no volcano or ordinary geological process can create. When an asteroid strikes the ground at around twenty kilometres per second, it delivers a shock wave with pressures of many hundreds of thousands of times atmospheric pressure, far higher than anything produced inside the Earth's crust by volcanoes or earthquakes. These shocks leave distinctive fingerprints. In quartz grains, the shock produces very fine, parallel planes of damaged crystal called shocked quartz, visible under a microscope. Under extreme pressure, quartz can also turn into special high-pressure forms such as coesite and stishovite. In the rocks of Vredefort, geologists have found shocked quartz and these high-pressure minerals. They have also found shatter cones, cone-shaped fracture patterns in the rocks that form when a shock wave passes through, and that point back towards the centre of the impact. Vredefort also contains unusual veins of melted and re-solidified rock, called pseudotachylite, formed as rocks were melted by friction and shock in seconds. Finally, the structure itself fits an impact: the dome is the central uplift, where rocks from deep underground rebounded upward after the impact, like the central peak in a splash of water, surrounded by rings of folded rock. Erosion over two billion years has removed the original crater, which may have been around 250 to 300 kilometres across. Because of its scientific importance as the oldest and largest well-preserved impact structure on Earth, the Vredefort Dome was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2005.

### subtopic_4 — Comets, the Kuiper Belt and the Oort Cloud
**Assistant:** Why does a comet's tail point away from the Sun even when the comet is moving away from it?

**Tutor:** Because the tail is not trailing behind the comet like smoke from a moving train; it is being pushed outward by the Sun's light and the solar wind, which always stream away from the Sun. As a comet approaches the Sun, sunlight heats its icy nucleus, and frozen gases such as water, carbon dioxide and carbon monoxide turn directly into gas, carrying dust with them. This material forms the glowing coma around the nucleus. Two forces then push it away from the Sun. First, the pressure of sunlight itself, tiny but steady, pushes on the dust grains. Second, the solar wind, a continuous stream of charged particles flowing outward from the Sun at hundreds of kilometres per second, carries along the electrically charged gas from the coma. As a result, most comets show two tails. The ion tail, made of charged gas, is straight, narrow and often bluish, and it points almost exactly away from the Sun, because the solar wind is so fast. The dust tail, made of tiny grains reflecting sunlight, is broader, yellowish-white and gently curved, because the dust is pushed more weakly and the grains lag behind along the comet's orbit. Because both are blown away from the Sun, when the comet passes the Sun and heads back out to the cold outer Solar System, its tails lead in front of it. The tails can be tens of millions of kilometres long, yet the material in them is so thin that it is closer to a vacuum than anything we can make on Earth. In 1910, the Earth passed through the tail of Halley's Comet with no effect at all.
