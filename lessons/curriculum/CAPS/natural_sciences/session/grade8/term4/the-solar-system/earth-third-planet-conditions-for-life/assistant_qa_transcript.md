### subtopic_1 — The Third Planet
**Assistant:** If the Earth is closest to the Sun in January, why is it winter in the Northern Hemisphere then?

**Tutor:** Because the seasons are controlled by the tilt of the Earth's axis, not by the distance, and the tilt affects the two hemispheres in opposite ways. The Earth's axis is tilted at about twenty-three and a half degrees and keeps pointing in the same direction in space as the Earth orbits. In early January, the Southern Hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun. The Sun rises high in our sky at midday, its rays strike the ground more directly, concentrating the energy on a smaller area, and the days are long. That is our summer. At the same time, the Northern Hemisphere is tilted away from the Sun: the Sun stays low, its rays are spread over a larger area at a slant, and the days are short, so it is winter in Europe and North America. The distance between the Earth and the Sun only changes from about 147 to 152 million kilometres, about three percent, which changes the sunlight reaching the Earth by about seven percent. That effect is real but much smaller than the effect of the tilt. It does make Southern Hemisphere summers receive slightly more sunlight than Northern Hemisphere summers, but our large oceans absorb much of that extra energy, so the difference is barely noticeable. If distance caused the seasons, both hemispheres would have summer at the same time, which is not what we observe. Australians and South Africans celebrate Christmas in summer, while people in Canada celebrate it in snow, which is perhaps the easiest everyday evidence that tilt, not distance, controls the seasons.

### subtopic_2 — Liquid Water and the Habitable Zone
**Assistant:** Have scientists found other planets in habitable zones around other stars?

**Tutor:** Yes. Since the 1990s, astronomers have discovered more than five thousand planets orbiting other stars, called exoplanets, and dozens of them lie in their stars' habitable zones. They are found mainly in two ways. In the transit method, a telescope measures the brightness of a star very precisely, and a planet passing in front of it blocks a tiny fraction of its light at regular intervals; the size of the dip gives the planet's size and the timing gives its orbit. In the wobble method, astronomers detect the star moving slightly back and forth as the planet's gravity tugs on it. One famous example is the TRAPPIST-1 system, a small, cool red dwarf star with seven Earth-sized planets, three or four of them in its habitable zone. Another is Proxima Centauri b, which orbits in the habitable zone of the nearest star to the Sun. But being in a habitable zone does not mean a planet has water or life, as our own Moon shows. A planet also needs the right size to hold an atmosphere, and red dwarf stars often blast their planets with strong flares that could strip atmospheres away. So far, no life has been found on any exoplanet. Telescopes such as the James Webb Space Telescope are now analysing the starlight that passes through some exoplanets' atmospheres, looking for gases such as water vapour, carbon dioxide and methane, which could tell us more about whether they could support life.

### subtopic_3 — The Atmosphere and the Ozone Layer
**Assistant:** If carbon dioxide keeps the Earth warm, why is adding more of it a problem?

**Tutor:** Because the natural amount gives a comfortable temperature, and adding more strengthens the effect and warms the planet beyond the conditions that ecosystems and people are adapted to. The natural greenhouse effect is essential: without it, the average surface temperature would be about minus eighteen degrees instead of about fifteen. For thousands of years, the carbon dioxide in the air stayed at about 280 parts per million, or 0.028 percent. Since the Industrial Revolution, burning coal, oil and gas and clearing forests have raised it to about 420 parts per million, about fifty percent more. More carbon dioxide absorbs more of the heat radiated from the ground and sends more of it back, so the Earth's average temperature has risen by more than one degree Celsius. That sounds small, but averaged over the whole planet it is a large change in stored energy. It leads to more frequent heat waves, changes in rainfall patterns, more intense droughts and floods, melting glaciers and ice sheets, rising sea levels as water warms and expands, and oceans that become more acidic as they absorb carbon dioxide, harming coral reefs and shellfish. South Africa, already water-scarce, is expected to see drier conditions in the west and more extreme rainfall events. Venus is an extreme warning of what a huge amount of carbon dioxide can do, although the Earth is not expected to become like Venus. So the issue is not that carbon dioxide is bad in itself, but that the balance has been changed, which is the difference between the natural greenhouse effect and the enhanced greenhouse effect.

### subtopic_4 — The Magnetic Field and a Stable Home
**Assistant:** Does the Earth's magnetic field ever flip?

**Tutor:** Yes. Over the Earth's history, the magnetic field has reversed its direction many times, so that a compass that points north today would have pointed south at those times. We know this from rocks. When lava cools to form basalt, tiny magnetic mineral grains in it line up with the Earth's magnetic field at that moment and are then locked in place as the rock hardens. Scientists studying the ocean floor discovered stripes of rock on either side of the mid-ocean ridges, where new crust forms, alternately magnetised in the normal and reversed directions, like a barcode recording the history of the field. Reversals happen irregularly, on average a few times every million years, but sometimes there are long gaps; the last full reversal was about 780 000 years ago. During a reversal, which may take a few thousand years, the field becomes weaker and more complicated, but it does not disappear completely, and there is no evidence that past reversals caused mass extinctions. The field's strength has decreased by about nine percent over the past two centuries, and there is a region of weaker field over the South Atlantic, stretching across southern Africa, called the South Atlantic Anomaly. Satellites passing over it receive more radiation and sometimes experience electronic glitches. Scientists at the South African National Space Agency's magnetic observatory in Hermanus have been monitoring the field there for decades. A weakening field does not necessarily mean a reversal is coming soon; the field naturally fluctuates. The molten iron in the outer core keeps churning, and the field will keep protecting us, as it has for billions of years.
