# Part 1 — Expert

This session surveys the many kinds of objects that orbit the Sun: the planets and their moons, the asteroids, the dwarf planets, and the icy bodies of the Kuiper Belt and the Oort Cloud from which comets come. The anchor is the Vredefort Dome in the Free State, about a hundred kilometres southwest of Johannesburg. The gentle ring of hills around the town of Parys is all that remains of the largest confirmed impact structure on Earth, made about two billion years ago when an asteroid perhaps ten kilometres or more across slammed into the ground. That asteroid was a fragment of rock left over from the formation of the Solar System, orbiting the Sun until its path crossed the Earth's. The governing idea is that the Solar System consists of the Sun and everything held in orbit by its gravity: eight planets, more than two hundred known moons, at least five recognised dwarf planets, and countless asteroids, comets and smaller bodies, arranged in distinct zones from the hot rocky inner region to the distant icy edge. The planets in order and their main features, the difference between inner and outer planets, moons, asteroids and the asteroid belt, dwarf planets and the reclassification of Pluto, the Kuiper Belt and Oort Cloud, comets, and meteors and meteorites are examinable, and they lead to the formation of the Solar System and planetary science.

## Subtopic: The Eight Planets

A planet is a large body that orbits the Sun, is massive enough for its own gravity to pull it into a nearly round shape, and has cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit of other objects of similar size. This definition was agreed by the International Astronomical Union in 2006. There are eight planets. In order from the Sun they are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune. A sentence to remember the order is: My Very Energetic Mother Just Served Us Nachos.

The four inner planets, Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars, are called the terrestrial or rocky planets. They are relatively small, dense, and made mainly of rock and metal, with solid surfaces. Mercury is the smallest planet and the closest to the Sun, with almost no atmosphere and extreme temperatures from about 430 degrees in the day to minus 180 at night. Venus is almost the same size as Earth but has a thick atmosphere of carbon dioxide that traps heat, making it the hottest planet, about 465 degrees, hot enough to melt lead. Earth is the only planet known to have liquid water on its surface and life. Mars, the red planet, is about half the size of Earth, cold and dry, with a thin carbon dioxide atmosphere, red iron oxide dust, the largest volcano in the Solar System, Olympus Mons, and evidence that water once flowed on its surface.

The four outer planets, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune, are called the giant planets. They are much larger, less dense, and made mainly of hydrogen, helium and, in Uranus and Neptune, ices of water, ammonia and methane. They have no solid surface that a spacecraft could land on. Jupiter is the largest planet, more than eleven times wider than Earth and more massive than all the other planets combined; its Great Red Spot is a storm larger than the Earth that has raged for centuries. Saturn is famous for its spectacular rings, made of countless pieces of ice and rock, and is so low in density that it would float in a large enough ocean of water. Uranus is tilted on its side, so it rolls around the Sun. Neptune, the furthest planet, is a deep blue world with the fastest winds measured in the Solar System. All four giant planets have ring systems, though only Saturn's are bright.

The inner planets are separated from the outer planets by the asteroid belt.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of a planet, the eight planets in order, the differences between the inner rocky planets and the outer giant planets, and one key feature of each planet.

## Subtopic: Moons

A moon, or natural satellite, is a natural body that orbits a planet, a dwarf planet or an asteroid. The Earth has one Moon, about a quarter of the Earth's diameter and about 384 000 kilometres away. Mercury and Venus have no moons. Mars has two small, potato-shaped moons, Phobos and Deimos, which may be captured asteroids. The giant planets have many moons each, Jupiter and Saturn with dozens upon dozens, and new small moons are still being discovered, so the totals keep changing; Saturn currently has the most known moons, well over a hundred.

Some moons are worlds in their own right. Jupiter's four largest moons, discovered by Galileo in 1610 and called the Galilean moons, are Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto. Io is the most volcanically active body in the Solar System. Europa has a smooth icy crust over a global ocean of liquid salty water, which scientists think could possibly support simple life. Ganymede is the largest moon in the Solar System, larger than the planet Mercury. Saturn's largest moon, Titan, has a thick nitrogen atmosphere and lakes and rivers of liquid methane and ethane. Saturn's small moon Enceladus sprays jets of water from an ocean beneath its icy surface.

Moons are kept in orbit around their planets by the planet's gravity, just as planets are kept in orbit around the Sun by the Sun's gravity. Our Moon always shows the same face to the Earth, because it rotates once on its axis in exactly the same time as it takes to orbit the Earth, about 27 days.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of a moon, which planets have no moons, the moons of Mars, the Galilean moons of Jupiter and a feature of each, Titan and Europa, and why our Moon always shows the same face.

## Subtopic: Asteroids and Dwarf Planets

Asteroids are rocky or metallic bodies, smaller than planets, that orbit the Sun. Most are found in the asteroid belt, a wide ring between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter. They range in size from pebbles to hundreds of kilometres across. There are millions of them, but they are so widely spread out that spacecraft have flown through the belt without coming anywhere near one, and their total mass is less than that of our Moon. The asteroid belt is thought to be made of material that never formed into a planet, because the strong gravity of nearby Jupiter kept stirring it up.

Some asteroids have orbits that cross the Earth's orbit. These near-Earth asteroids are tracked carefully by astronomers, because an impact by a large one could cause a regional or even global disaster. The Vredefort impact two billion years ago and the impact in Mexico about 66 million years ago that contributed to the extinction of the dinosaurs are examples from the distant past. In 2022 the DART spacecraft deliberately crashed into a small asteroid and successfully changed its orbit, testing a possible way to defend the Earth.

A dwarf planet is a body that orbits the Sun and is massive enough to be nearly round, but has not cleared its orbit of other objects. Five dwarf planets are officially recognised: Ceres, the largest object in the asteroid belt, about 940 kilometres across; and Pluto, Eris, Haumea and Makemake, which lie beyond Neptune.

Pluto was discovered in 1930 and was called the ninth planet for 76 years. As astronomers discovered more and more icy bodies beyond Neptune, including Eris, which is about the same size as Pluto, they had to decide whether to call them all planets. In 2006 the International Astronomical Union agreed on the definition of a planet, and because Pluto shares its orbital region with many other Kuiper Belt objects, it was reclassified as a dwarf planet. The New Horizons spacecraft flew past Pluto in 2015, revealing mountains of water ice, plains of frozen nitrogen and a thin atmosphere.

The questions for this section are with you now: what asteroids are and where most are found, why the asteroid belt never formed a planet, the danger of near-Earth asteroids and the Vredefort Dome, the definition of a dwarf planet, five dwarf planets, and why Pluto was reclassified.

## Subtopic: Comets, the Kuiper Belt and the Oort Cloud

Beyond Neptune lies the Kuiper Belt, a vast, doughnut-shaped region extending from about 30 to about 50 times the Earth's distance from the Sun. It contains enormous numbers of small icy bodies, including the dwarf planets Pluto, Haumea and Makemake, made of frozen water, methane and ammonia mixed with rock. The Earth's average distance from the Sun, about 150 million kilometres, is called one astronomical unit, so the Kuiper Belt lies between about 30 and 50 astronomical units.

Much further out, possibly extending from a few thousand to around a hundred thousand astronomical units, a significant fraction of the way to the nearest stars, is thought to be the Oort Cloud, a huge spherical shell of perhaps trillions of icy bodies surrounding the whole Solar System. It has never been observed directly, because its objects are far too small, faint and distant, but its existence is inferred from the orbits of long-period comets that fall in towards the Sun from all directions.

A comet is a small icy body, a few kilometres across, often described as a dirty snowball, which travels on a long, stretched orbit around the Sun. Far from the Sun, a comet is just a frozen nucleus of ice, dust and rock. As it approaches the Sun, the heat makes ice turn directly into gas, releasing dust. This forms a huge glowing cloud around the nucleus, the coma, which can be larger than the Sun itself, and long tails that always point away from the Sun, pushed by the pressure of sunlight and the solar wind. Short-period comets, which return within two hundred years, come mostly from the Kuiper Belt; Halley's Comet returns about every 76 years and will next be seen in 2061. Long-period comets come from the Oort Cloud and may take thousands or millions of years to complete one orbit.

When a comet or asteroid leaves behind a trail of dust and small fragments, these pieces, called meteoroids, may enter the Earth's atmosphere at tens of kilometres per second. Friction with the air heats them until they glow, producing a streak of light called a meteor, or shooting star. Most burn up completely. Any piece that survives and lands on the ground is called a meteorite. The largest single meteorite known, the Hoba meteorite, an iron mass of about sixty tonnes, lies where it fell in northern Namibia.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying Pluto is the ninth planet; it is a dwarf planet. Two: saying a comet's tail streams out behind it; the tail points away from the Sun. Three: saying shooting stars are stars; they are meteoroids burning in the atmosphere. Four: saying the asteroid belt is crowded with rocks close together; they are very widely spaced. Five: confusing meteor and meteorite; a meteorite reaches the ground.

Layout for marks: name the object, say what it orbits, where it is found, what it is made of, and one distinguishing feature.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Kuiper Belt and the Oort Cloud and their distances, the structure of a comet and why its tail points away from the Sun, short- and long-period comets, the difference between meteoroid, meteor and meteorite, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a family around the Sun, rocks and little worlds, and visitors with glowing tails — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Family Around the Sun

The Sun has a big family, all held in orbit by its gravity.

First, the eight planets. In order from the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune. Remember: My Very Energetic Mother Just Served Us Nachos.

The first four are small and rocky, with solid ground: Mercury, tiny and scorched; Venus, wrapped in thick clouds and the hottest of all; Earth, our watery home; and Mars, the red, dusty planet.

The last four are giants made mostly of gas and ice, with no ground to stand on: Jupiter, the biggest, with a storm bigger than Earth; Saturn, with beautiful rings; Uranus, rolling on its side; and Neptune, blue and windy.

Many planets have moons going round them. Earth has one. Mars has two tiny ones. Jupiter and Saturn have dozens and dozens. Some moons, like Jupiter's Europa, may even hide oceans under their ice.

Keep the family in mind and try the questions for this part: the planets in order, rocky or giant, and which planet has the most famous rings.

## Subtopic: Rocks, Ice and Little Worlds

Between Mars and Jupiter is a wide belt of space rocks called asteroids. There are millions of them, but they are spread so far apart that spacecraft fly straight through.

Every now and then, one hits Earth. Two billion years ago, a giant one smashed into what is now the Free State and made the Vredefort Dome, the biggest known impact scar on Earth.

Some objects are round like planets but share their part of space with lots of other bits. These are called dwarf planets. Ceres is in the asteroid belt. Pluto, far beyond Neptune, used to be called the ninth planet, but since 2006 it is called a dwarf planet.

Hold the rocks and little worlds in mind and try this part's questions: where the asteroid belt is, what a dwarf planet is, and why Pluto is not a planet any more.

## Subtopic: Visitors with Glowing Tails

Far beyond Neptune is the Kuiper Belt, full of icy chunks. Much, much further out, the Oort Cloud is thought to surround the whole Solar System like a giant bubble of ice.

Sometimes one of those icy chunks falls in towards the Sun. As it warms up, its ice turns to gas and it grows a glowing head and long tails. It is a comet. The tails always point away from the Sun, blown by sunlight and the solar wind. Halley's Comet comes back about every 76 years; the next visit is in 2061.

A shooting star is not a star. It is a tiny bit of space dust burning up as it rushes into our air. If a piece lands on the ground, it is called a meteorite.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Sun is orbited by eight planets: four rocky inner planets and four giant outer planets. Many planets have moons. Asteroids, mostly between Mars and Jupiter, are rocky leftovers. Dwarf planets, such as Ceres and Pluto, are round but have not cleared their orbits. Beyond Neptune lie the Kuiper Belt and, much further, the Oort Cloud, home of comets, whose tails point away from the Sun.

Hold the family, the rocks and the comets, and take the final questions of the lesson: the order of the planets, what Pluto is, and which way a comet's tail points.
