# Part 1 — Expert

This session asks why life, as far as anyone knows, exists on only one world: the third planet from the Sun. The anchor is a comparison of three neighbours. Venus, the second planet, is almost the same size as the Earth, yet its surface is hotter than a pizza oven, at about four hundred and sixty degrees Celsius, under a crushing atmosphere of carbon dioxide. Mars, the fourth planet, is about half the Earth's width, and its thin air and frozen ground average roughly minus sixty degrees. Between them, the Earth has liquid water over seventy-one percent of its surface, a breathable atmosphere and millions of species of living things. The governing idea is that life as we know it needs a combination of conditions, and the Earth has them all at once: a distance from the Sun that allows liquid water, the right size to hold an atmosphere, an atmosphere that keeps temperatures moderate and shields the surface, a magnetic field that deflects harmful particles, and a steady supply of energy from a stable star. The Earth's position as the third planet, the conditions that support life, the role of water, the atmosphere and the ozone layer, the magnetic field, and comparisons with Venus and Mars are examinable, and they lead to the study of the biosphere and environmental change in Grade 10 Life Sciences and Geography.

## Subtopic: The Third Planet

The Earth is the third planet from the Sun, after Mercury and Venus, at an average distance of about 150 million kilometres. That distance is so useful in astronomy that it has its own name: one astronomical unit, abbreviated AU. Light from the Sun takes about eight minutes and twenty seconds to cover it. The Earth's orbit is very nearly a circle; its distance from the Sun varies only between about 147 million kilometres in early January and 152 million kilometres in early July. That small change does not cause the seasons. In fact, the Earth is closest to the Sun during the Southern Hemisphere summer, but the difference in distance is far too small to matter; the seasons are caused by the tilt of the Earth's axis, about twenty-three and a half degrees, which changes how directly the Sun's rays strike each hemisphere through the year.

The Earth is the largest of the four inner, rocky planets, with a diameter of about 12 742 kilometres. It is the densest planet in the Solar System, with an average density of about five and a half grams per cubic centimetre, because it has a large iron and nickel core. The structure has layers: a thin rocky crust, from about five kilometres thick under the oceans to about seventy under high mountains; a thick mantle of hot rock that flows very slowly; a liquid outer core of molten iron and nickel; and a solid inner core, as hot as the surface of the Sun but kept solid by enormous pressure.

The Earth spins on its axis once in about twenty-four hours, giving day and night, and orbits the Sun once in about three hundred and sixty-five and a quarter days, giving the year. It has one natural satellite, the Moon, about a quarter of the Earth's diameter, which is unusually large compared with its planet. The Moon's pull steadies the tilt of the Earth's axis, which keeps the climate more stable over long periods than it would otherwise be, and it raises the ocean tides.

Seen from space, the Earth is a blue planet with swirling white clouds, brown and green continents and white polar ice. No other planet in the Solar System looks like it, because no other has oceans of liquid water at its surface.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Earth's position and distance from the Sun, the astronomical unit, the cause of the seasons, the layers of the Earth, and what the Moon does for the Earth.

## Subtopic: Liquid Water and the Habitable Zone

Every living thing known on Earth needs liquid water. Water dissolves the substances that cells use, carries them around inside organisms, takes part in photosynthesis and many other chemical reactions, and makes up most of the mass of living cells; a human body is about sixty percent water. Life probably began in water, and every known organism, from bacteria in hot springs to elephants in the Kruger National Park, depends on it.

Water can only stay liquid within a range of temperatures, from zero to one hundred degrees Celsius at normal atmospheric pressure. A planet's temperature depends mostly on how far it is from its star and on its atmosphere. Too close, and water boils away; too far, and it freezes solid. The region around a star where a planet could have liquid water on its surface is called the habitable zone, sometimes nicknamed the Goldilocks zone, because it is not too hot and not too cold, but just right. In our Solar System, the habitable zone stretches from somewhere outside the orbit of Venus to around the orbit of Mars, and the Earth sits comfortably inside it.

Being in the habitable zone is necessary but not enough. The Moon is the same distance from the Sun as the Earth, yet it has no liquid water at its surface and no air, and its temperature swings from about one hundred and twenty degrees in sunlight to about minus one hundred and seventy at night. The Moon is too small; its gravity is too weak to hold an atmosphere, and without the pressure of an atmosphere liquid water simply evaporates into space. Mars sits near the outer edge of the zone, and though it once had rivers and lakes, which carved valleys still visible today, it lost most of its atmosphere and its water froze or escaped.

The Earth's water moves endlessly in the water cycle, powered by the Sun: evaporating from oceans and dams, condensing into clouds, falling as rain and snow, and flowing back through rivers and underground water. Water also moderates the climate. The oceans absorb heat in summer and release it in winter, so coastal towns such as Durban and Gqeberha have milder temperature ranges than inland towns at the same latitude.

South Africa is a water-scarce country, with an average rainfall of about four hundred and fifty millimetres a year, well below the world average. That makes the value of liquid water, which the Earth's position gives it, very concrete: dams such as the Gariep, the Vaal and the Theewaterskloof store it, and careful use of it is a national priority.

The questions for this section are with you now: why life needs liquid water, the temperature range for liquid water, the meaning of the habitable zone, why the Moon has no liquid water although it is in the zone, and how oceans moderate climate.

## Subtopic: The Atmosphere and the Ozone Layer

The Earth is wrapped in a thin layer of gases, the atmosphere, held in place by gravity. Dry air is about seventy-eight percent nitrogen, twenty-one percent oxygen and just under one percent argon, with about 0.04 percent carbon dioxide and variable amounts of water vapour. Most of the air lies within about sixteen kilometres of the surface; if the Earth were the size of an apple, the atmosphere would be thinner than its skin.

The atmosphere supports life in several ways. It contains the oxygen that animals and plants use for respiration, and the carbon dioxide that plants use for photosynthesis. Nitrogen is a building block of proteins; bacteria in soil and in the roots of legumes such as beans and rooibos convert it into forms that plants can use.

The atmosphere keeps the surface temperature moderate. Some gases, mainly water vapour and carbon dioxide, absorb heat radiated from the warm ground and send part of it back down. This natural greenhouse effect keeps the average surface temperature at about fifteen degrees Celsius. Without it, the average would be about minus eighteen degrees, and the oceans would freeze. The atmosphere also spreads heat around the planet through winds, and it reduces the difference between day and night temperatures, acting like a blanket at night.

Venus shows what happens when the greenhouse effect runs away. Its atmosphere is about ninety-six percent carbon dioxide and so thick that the pressure at the surface is about ninety times that on Earth. The trapped heat makes Venus the hottest planet in the Solar System, hotter even than Mercury, which is closer to the Sun. Mars shows the opposite problem: its atmosphere is less than one percent as thick as Earth's, so it holds little heat, and water cannot stay liquid on its surface for long.

High in the atmosphere, between about fifteen and thirty-five kilometres up, lies the ozone layer. Ozone is a form of oxygen whose molecules contain three oxygen atoms instead of two. The ozone layer absorbs most of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet radiation, which damages living cells, causes sunburn and skin cancer and harms crops and plankton. In the 1980s scientists discovered that chemicals called CFCs, then used in fridges and spray cans, were destroying ozone and causing a seasonal ozone hole over Antarctica. In 1987 countries signed the Montreal Protocol to phase them out, and the ozone layer is slowly recovering. South Africans need sun protection anyway: the high sunshine levels and, in Gauteng, the high altitude give strong ultraviolet levels, which is why the UV index is often very high in summer.

The atmosphere also burns up most small meteoroids before they reach the ground; they are seen as shooting stars.

The questions for this section are with you now: the composition of dry air, three ways the atmosphere supports life, the natural greenhouse effect and what Venus shows about it, and the role of the ozone layer.

## Subtopic: The Magnetic Field and a Stable Home

The liquid outer core of the Earth is made of molten iron and nickel, and as it churns, it generates electric currents, which in turn produce the Earth's magnetic field. That field reaches tens of thousands of kilometres into space, forming a protective region called the magnetosphere. The Sun constantly blows out a stream of charged particles called the solar wind, and occasionally hurls out huge bursts during solar storms. The magnetic field deflects most of these particles around the Earth. Without it, the solar wind would gradually strip away the atmosphere and the surface would receive much more harmful radiation. Scientists think this is part of what happened to Mars, whose core cooled and whose global magnetic field faded billions of years ago.

Some charged particles are guided down towards the magnetic poles, where they collide with gases high in the atmosphere and make them glow, producing the aurorae: the southern lights near Antarctica and the northern lights in the Arctic. The magnetic field is also what makes a compass needle point north, and animals such as some birds, turtles and fish use it to navigate.

Life also needs a steady source of energy. The Sun is a stable, middle-aged star that has shone with fairly constant brightness for billions of years and will continue for billions more, giving life time to develop. Plants and other producers capture its energy by photosynthesis, which feeds almost every food chain on Earth.

Putting it all together, the conditions on Earth that support life are: a distance from the Sun in the habitable zone, allowing liquid water; enough mass and gravity to hold an atmosphere; an atmosphere containing oxygen, carbon dioxide and nitrogen that moderates temperature through a natural greenhouse effect; an ozone layer that blocks most ultraviolet radiation; a magnetic field that deflects the solar wind; and a stable star providing a steady supply of energy.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the Earth is hottest in summer because it is closer to the Sun; the seasons are caused by the tilt of the axis, and the Southern Hemisphere summer actually falls when the Earth is nearest the Sun, a distance change far too small to matter. Two: saying the greenhouse effect is entirely bad; the natural greenhouse effect keeps the Earth warm enough for liquid water, and the problem is the extra warming from added greenhouse gases. Three: saying the ozone hole causes global warming; the ozone layer is about ultraviolet radiation, a separate issue. Four: saying any planet in the habitable zone must have life; the Moon is in the zone and is lifeless. Five: saying Venus is hottest because it is nearest the Sun; Mercury is nearer, and Venus is hotter because of its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere.

Layout for marks: list the condition, then explain how it supports life, then give a comparison with Venus, Mars or the Moon.

The questions for this section are with you now: how the magnetic field forms and protects life, what happened to Mars, the full list of conditions that support life, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a planet that is just right, a blanket and a sunscreen, and an invisible shield — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Just Right

Count out from the Sun: Mercury, Venus, then us. The Earth is the third planet, about 150 million kilometres from the Sun. Sunlight takes about eight minutes to get here.

Our neighbours are not so lucky. Venus, next door on the inside, is hotter than a pizza oven. Mars, next door on the outside, is freezing, with air too thin to breathe. The Earth sits in between, in a zone that is not too hot and not too cold, so water can stay liquid. Scientists call it the habitable zone, or the Goldilocks zone.

Why does water matter so much? Because every living thing needs it. Your body is about sixty percent water. Plants need it to make food. Without liquid water, life as we know it cannot exist.

But being the right distance is not enough. The Moon is just as far from the Sun as we are, and it is dry, airless and dead. It is too small. Its gravity is too weak to hold on to air, and without air, water just escapes into space.

The Earth is also big enough to hold on to its air, and that is the next part of the story.

Keep the Goldilocks idea in mind and try the questions for this part: the Earth's position, what the habitable zone is, and why the Moon has no life.

## Subtopic: A Blanket and a Sunscreen

The air around the Earth does two big jobs that keep us alive.

First, it is a blanket. Some gases in the air, like carbon dioxide and water vapour, trap heat near the ground. This keeps the Earth at about fifteen degrees on average. Without that blanket it would be about minus eighteen, and the oceans would freeze. Venus has far too thick a blanket, almost all carbon dioxide, so it cooks. Mars has hardly any blanket at all, so it freezes.

Second, high up there is a layer of ozone that works like sunscreen. It soaks up most of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet rays, the ones that burn your skin and damage living cells. Some chemicals once used in fridges and spray cans were wearing holes in it, so the world agreed to ban them, and it is slowly healing.

And of course the air has oxygen for us to breathe and carbon dioxide for plants to make food.

Hold the blanket and the sunscreen in mind and try this part's questions: what the greenhouse effect does, why Venus is so hot, and what the ozone layer blocks.

## Subtopic: An Invisible Shield

Deep inside the Earth there is a core of hot, liquid iron, churning around. That moving iron makes the Earth a giant magnet, with a magnetic field stretching far out into space.

The Sun is always blowing out a stream of tiny charged particles, called the solar wind. The magnetic field acts like an invisible shield, pushing most of them away around the Earth. Without it, the solar wind would slowly blow our air away. That is probably what happened to Mars long ago, when its core cooled and its shield faded.

Near the poles, some particles sneak in and make the sky glow green and red. Those are the southern and northern lights.

Finally, the Sun itself is steady. It has been shining for billions of years and will keep going for billions more, giving plants a reliable source of energy.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Earth is the third planet, about 150 million kilometres from the Sun, in the habitable zone where water can be liquid. Life needs liquid water. The Earth is big enough to hold an atmosphere, which gives oxygen and carbon dioxide and keeps the temperature mild through the natural greenhouse effect. The ozone layer blocks most ultraviolet rays. The magnetic field, made by the liquid iron core, shields us from the solar wind. And the Sun is a steady source of energy.

Hold the Goldilocks zone, the blanket and the shield, and take the final questions of the lesson: listing the conditions for life, explaining what happened to Mars, and why Venus is hotter than Mercury.
