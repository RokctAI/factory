# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains how gravity keeps the planets, moons, asteroids and comets in stable orbits, and why the Solar System is shaped like a flat disc. The anchor is a learner swinging a tennis ball tied to a string around her head on a school field. As long as she holds the string, the ball travels in a steady circle. The moment she lets go, the ball does not keep circling, nor does it fly outward directly away from her; it shoots off in a straight line along the direction it was moving at that instant. The string was providing a constant pull towards the centre, turning the ball's straight-line motion into a circle. For the planets, there is no string. Gravity does the pulling. The governing idea is that every object with mass attracts every other object with mass; that the Sun's enormous gravity pulls each planet towards it, while the planet's sideways speed carries it forward, so that it continually falls around the Sun in a stable orbit; that planets closer to the Sun move faster and take less time to complete an orbit; and that the Solar System is disc-shaped because it formed from a flattened, spinning cloud of gas and dust. Gravity as a force of attraction between masses, the factors affecting its strength, orbits as a balance between falling and moving sideways, the relationship between distance and orbital period, and the disc shape of the Solar System are examinable, and they lead to Newton's law of universal gravitation in Grade 11 and 12 Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: Gravity Holds the Solar System Together

Gravity is a force of attraction between any two objects that have mass. Every object attracts every other object: you attract the Earth, the Earth attracts you, and the book on your desk attracts your pencil. Between everyday objects, the force is far too weak to notice; it becomes important only when at least one of the objects is enormous, like a planet or a star.

The strength of the gravitational force depends on two things. First, the masses of the objects: the greater the masses, the stronger the force. The Sun, with 333 000 times the mass of the Earth, exerts a far stronger pull than any planet. Second, the distance between them: the force becomes weaker as the distance increases, and it weakens quickly. If the distance between two objects is doubled, the force between them falls to a quarter; if it is tripled, the force falls to a ninth. This is called an inverse square relationship.

Gravity is what we feel as weight. Your weight is the pull of the Earth's gravity on your mass. On the Moon, which has much less mass than the Earth, gravity is only about a sixth as strong, so an astronaut with a mass of 90 kilograms weighs only as much as a 15 kilogram mass would weigh on Earth, and can leap high in a bulky spacesuit. On Jupiter's cloud tops, gravity is about two and a half times as strong as on Earth.

Isaac Newton realised in the 1680s that the same force that makes an apple fall to the ground also keeps the Moon in orbit around the Earth and the planets in orbit around the Sun. Before Newton, people thought that the heavens followed different rules from the Earth; his law of universal gravitation showed that one law applies everywhere.

The Sun's gravity reaches far out into space, holding not only the planets but also the dwarf planets of the Kuiper Belt and, very weakly, the icy bodies of the distant Oort Cloud, perhaps a hundred thousand times further from the Sun than the Earth is.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of gravity, the two factors that affect its strength, the effect of doubling the distance, weight as the pull of gravity, why astronauts weigh less on the Moon, and Newton's great insight.

## Subtopic: How an Orbit Works

An orbit is the curved path of an object around another object under the influence of gravity. To understand why a planet orbits the Sun instead of falling straight into it or flying off into space, Newton imagined a cannon on top of a very high mountain, firing cannonballs horizontally.

A cannonball fired slowly curves down and hits the ground not far from the mountain. Fired faster, it travels further before landing. Because the Earth is round, the ground curves away beneath a fast-moving cannonball. If it were fired fast enough, about eight kilometres per second near the Earth's surface, ignoring air resistance, the ground would curve away beneath it exactly as fast as it fell. It would keep falling towards the Earth but never reach the ground, travelling all the way around the planet and back to the mountain. It would be in orbit.

An orbiting object is therefore constantly falling towards the body it orbits, while moving sideways fast enough to keep missing it. Gravity pulls it towards the centre, changing the direction of its motion, while its sideways speed carries it forward. If gravity suddenly disappeared, the object would fly off in a straight line, like the tennis ball when the string is released. If the object had no sideways speed, gravity would pull it straight in.

The International Space Station orbits the Earth about 400 kilometres up, at about 28 000 kilometres per hour, completing an orbit in about ninety minutes. The astronauts inside float, although gravity there is still about ninety percent as strong as on the ground, because they and the station are falling around the Earth together, so nothing presses them against the floor.

Most orbits are not perfect circles but ellipses, slightly stretched circles, as the German astronomer Johannes Kepler discovered in the early 1600s from careful observations of Mars. The Earth's orbit is very nearly circular; its distance from the Sun varies by only about three percent during the year. Comets have very stretched elliptical orbits. Because the Earth is slightly closer to the Sun in early January than in early July, the seasons are not caused by changing distance, but by the tilt of the Earth's axis.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of an orbit, Newton's cannon, why an orbiting object does not fall to the ground, what would happen if gravity disappeared, why astronauts in the space station float, and the shape of orbits.

## Subtopic: Orbital Speed and Distance

Because gravity is stronger closer to the Sun, planets closer to the Sun must move faster to stay in orbit, and they also have shorter paths to travel. Both effects mean that inner planets complete their orbits much more quickly than outer planets.

The time a planet takes to complete one orbit of the Sun is its orbital period, or its year. Mercury, the closest planet, orbits at an average speed of about 47 kilometres per second and completes its year in just 88 Earth days. Venus takes 225 days. The Earth travels at about 30 kilometres per second, over 100 000 kilometres per hour, and takes 365 and a quarter days, which is why a leap day is added every four years. Mars takes about 687 days, nearly two Earth years. Jupiter takes about 12 Earth years, Saturn about 29, Uranus about 84, and Neptune, moving at only about 5.4 kilometres per second, takes about 165 Earth years. Since its discovery in 1846, Neptune has completed only about one orbit.

Kepler found a precise rule linking the period of a planet to its distance from the Sun: the further the planet, the longer its year, in a way that grows faster than the distance. A planet four times as far from the Sun as another takes eight times as long to orbit. Newton later showed that this rule follows directly from his law of gravity.

The orbits of the planets are stable over billions of years. A planet does not spiral into the Sun because its speed carries it forward; it does not fly away because gravity holds it. If the Earth suddenly moved more slowly, gravity would pull it into a smaller orbit closer to the Sun; if it moved faster, it would swing out to a larger orbit, and above a certain escape speed it would leave the Solar System altogether.

The same rules apply to moons orbiting planets, and to the artificial satellites orbiting the Earth. Communication and weather satellites placed about 36 000 kilometres above the equator take exactly one day to orbit, so they stay above the same point on the Earth; that is why a satellite dish on a South African roof can point at a fixed spot in the northern sky.

The questions for this section are with you now: why inner planets move faster, the meaning of orbital period, the periods of Mercury, Earth, Jupiter and Neptune, the Earth's orbital speed, why orbits are stable, and why satellite dishes can point at one place in the sky.

## Subtopic: Why the Solar System Is a Disc

The orbits of all eight planets lie very nearly in the same flat plane, called the plane of the ecliptic, which is the plane of the Earth's orbit. All the planets travel around the Sun in the same direction, which is also the direction in which the Sun itself spins. The asteroid belt and the Kuiper Belt also lie roughly in this plane. Seen from far above, the Solar System would look like a flat disc, with the Sun at the centre.

This shape is a clue to how the Solar System formed. About 4.6 billion years ago, a huge cloud of gas and dust in space began to collapse under its own gravity, perhaps triggered by the shock wave from a nearby exploding star. The cloud was turning very slowly. As it collapsed, it spun faster and faster, just as a figure skater spins faster when she pulls her arms in. A spinning cloud cannot easily collapse along the direction perpendicular to its spin, because spin resists that, but it can collapse freely along the axis of the spin. So the cloud flattened into a rotating disc, called the solar nebula, with most of the material gathering at the centre to form the Sun. The planets formed from the material in the disc, so they ended up orbiting in the same plane and in the same direction as the disc was spinning.

A pizza maker spinning a ball of dough in the air shows the same effect: the spinning ball flattens into a disc. Astronomers have photographed flattened discs of gas and dust around young stars elsewhere in our galaxy, some with gaps where planets appear to be forming, which supports this explanation.

Because the planets all lie close to one plane, they always appear along a narrow band across the night sky, the same band in which the Sun and Moon appear to move, through the constellations of the zodiac. On some evenings you can see several bright planets lined up along this band.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying there is no gravity in space; astronauts float because they are falling around the Earth. Two: saying planets need an engine or a push to keep moving; nothing slows them in empty space. Three: saying gravity only acts on Earth; every mass attracts every other mass. Four: saying outer planets move faster because they have further to go; they move slower. Five: saying the seasons happen because the Earth is closer to the Sun in summer; South Africa's summer comes when the Earth is closest, but the Northern Hemisphere's summer comes when it is furthest, so distance cannot cause the seasons.

Layout for marks: name gravity as the pull towards the Sun, name the sideways speed, and say that the two together produce a curved orbit.

The questions for this section are with you now: the evidence that the Solar System is a disc, how a spinning collapsing cloud formed a disc, why the planets orbit in the same direction, discs around other young stars, why planets appear along one band in the sky, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a ball on a string, a cannon on a mountain, and a spinning pizza base — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Ball on a String

Tie a tennis ball to a string and swing it round your head. It goes round and round in a circle. Now let go. The ball does not keep circling. It flies off in a straight line.

The string was pulling the ball inwards all the time, turning its straight-line motion into a circle. For the planets there is no string. Instead, there is gravity.

Gravity is a pull between any two things that have mass. The bigger the masses, the stronger the pull. The further apart, the weaker it gets: double the distance and the pull drops to a quarter.

The Sun is gigantic, so its gravity is huge. It pulls on every planet, and that pull is the invisible string that keeps them going round. Without it, the Earth would fly off into space in a straight line.

On the Moon, gravity is only a sixth as strong as on Earth, so astronauts can bounce around easily.

Keep the ball on a string in mind and try the questions for this part: what gravity is, what makes it stronger, and what would happen without it.

## Subtopic: A Cannon on a Mountain

Imagine a cannon on top of a very tall mountain, firing a ball sideways. Fire it slowly and it falls to the ground nearby. Fire it faster and it lands further away. Fire it fast enough, about eight kilometres every second, and the Earth's round surface curves away beneath it as fast as it falls. It keeps falling, but it keeps missing the ground. It goes all the way round. It is in orbit.

That is what an orbit is: falling and missing, forever.

Planets close to the Sun feel a stronger pull, so they have to go faster. Mercury zooms round in 88 days. Earth takes a year. Neptune, far away, crawls round in 165 years.

Hold the cannon in mind and try this part's questions: what an orbit is, and why Mercury's year is so short.

## Subtopic: A Spinning Pizza Base

Watch a pizza maker spin a ball of dough in the air. It flattens into a disc. The Solar System did the same.

Long ago, a huge cloud of gas and dust started to shrink under its own gravity. As it shrank, it spun faster, and the spinning flattened it into a disc. The Sun formed in the middle, and the planets formed in the disc around it. So today all the planets go round the Sun in the same direction, on almost the same flat plane, like marbles rolling around on a big plate.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Gravity is a pull between masses; it is stronger for bigger masses and weaker at greater distances. A planet orbits because gravity pulls it towards the Sun while its sideways speed carries it forward, so it keeps falling and missing. Planets closer to the Sun move faster and have shorter years. The Solar System is a flat disc because it formed from a spinning, flattening cloud.

Hold the string, the cannon and the pizza, and take the final questions of the lesson: what keeps a planet in orbit, which planet has the shortest year, and why the Solar System is flat.
