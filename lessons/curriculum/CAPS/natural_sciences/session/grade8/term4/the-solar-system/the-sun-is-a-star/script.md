# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the Sun as a star: an enormous ball of hot gas that produces its own heat and light by nuclear reactions deep in its core. The anchor is a summer day on a beach in Durban. The sand is too hot to walk on, the sea sparkles, and anyone staying out too long without sunscreen will be burnt by evening. All that warmth and light has travelled 150 million kilometres across empty space to reach the beach. It left the Sun's surface about eight minutes ago, but the energy itself was released in the Sun's core many thousands of years earlier, when hydrogen nuclei were squeezed together to form helium. The governing idea is that the Sun is a star, a sphere of hot gas held together by its own gravity, which releases energy by nuclear fusion, converting hydrogen into helium in its core; that this energy travels outward and is radiated into space as light and heat; and that this energy sustains almost all life on Earth. The Sun as a star, its size and composition, nuclear fusion of hydrogen into helium, the transfer of the Sun's energy to Earth, and the importance of the Sun for life are examinable, and they lead to the life cycles of stars and nuclear physics in later grades.

## Subtopic: The Sun Is a Star

A star is a huge, luminous ball of hot gas that produces its own energy by nuclear reactions in its core. The Sun is a star, the nearest one to Earth. The thousands of stars we see at night are suns too, many of them larger and brighter than ours, but so far away that they look like tiny points of light.

The Sun is enormous compared with the Earth. Its diameter is about 1.4 million kilometres, about 109 times the diameter of the Earth. More than a million Earths could fit inside it. Its mass is about 333 000 times the mass of the Earth, and it contains about 99.8 percent of all the mass in the Solar System; all the planets, moons, asteroids and comets together make up the tiny remainder.

The Sun is made almost entirely of the two lightest elements: about three quarters hydrogen and about a quarter helium by mass, with small amounts of heavier elements such as oxygen, carbon, neon and iron. It is not solid or liquid. It is so hot that its atoms have lost some or all of their electrons, forming a glowing, electrically charged gas called plasma.

The visible surface of the Sun, called the photosphere, has a temperature of about 5 500 degrees Celsius. The temperature rises steeply towards the centre: the core is about 15 million degrees Celsius, and the pressure there is hundreds of billions of times greater than the air pressure at the Earth's surface.

Compared with other stars, the Sun is quite ordinary: a medium-sized, yellowish-white star. Astronomers classify it as a yellow dwarf. Some stars are a hundred times wider and many thousands of times brighter; others are much smaller, cooler and redder. The Sun is about 4.6 billion years old, and it is roughly halfway through its life as a stable hydrogen-burning star.

Because of its enormous mass, the Sun's gravity dominates the Solar System, holding the planets, dwarf planets, asteroids and comets in orbit around it.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of a star, the size and mass of the Sun compared with the Earth, the composition of the Sun, the meaning of plasma, the temperatures of its surface and core, and why the Sun is described as an ordinary star.

## Subtopic: Nuclear Fusion in the Core

The Sun produces its energy by nuclear fusion, the joining together of the nuclei of light atoms to make heavier nuclei. In the Sun's core, hydrogen nuclei, which are single protons, are fused in a series of steps to form helium nuclei. Overall, four hydrogen nuclei combine to make one helium nucleus.

Fusion can only happen under extreme conditions. Hydrogen nuclei are all positively charged, so they repel one another strongly. To fuse, they must collide at enormous speeds, so that they get close enough for the very strong but very short-range nuclear force to bind them together. That requires temperatures of millions of degrees, which make the nuclei move extremely fast, and enormous pressure and density, which make collisions frequent. The Sun's core provides both, because the weight of all the layers above squeezes and heats it.

In every fusion reaction, the mass of the helium nucleus produced is very slightly less than the total mass of the four hydrogen nuclei that formed it, about seven tenths of a percent less. That missing mass has been converted into energy, according to Albert Einstein's famous equation, E equals m c squared, energy equals mass multiplied by the speed of light squared. Because the speed of light is so large, a tiny mass becomes a huge amount of energy.

Every second, the Sun fuses about 600 million tonnes of hydrogen into about 596 million tonnes of helium. The difference, about four million tonnes of mass every second, is converted into energy. Even at that rate, the Sun has enough hydrogen in its core to keep shining steadily for roughly another five billion years.

Nuclear fusion is very different from chemical burning. A fire on Earth rearranges atoms, combining fuel with oxygen, and releases energy from chemical bonds. Fusion changes the nuclei themselves, turning one element into another, and releases millions of times more energy per kilogram of fuel. The Sun is not on fire; there is no oxygen burning in it.

Scientists around the world are trying to build fusion reactors on Earth to generate electricity, but achieving and containing the temperatures needed is extremely difficult.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of nuclear fusion, the reaction in the Sun's core, why extreme temperature and pressure are needed, where the energy comes from, how much hydrogen the Sun fuses each second, and why the Sun is not burning like a fire.

## Subtopic: Energy from the Sun Reaches Earth

The energy released by fusion in the core must travel outwards through the Sun before it can escape into space. In the inner part of the Sun, the energy is carried by radiation, but the plasma is so dense that each packet of light is absorbed and re-emitted in a random direction countless times. Energy takes a very long time, estimated at tens of thousands to over a hundred thousand years, to work its way out of this region. In the outer third of the Sun, the energy is carried by convection: huge currents of hot plasma rise to the surface, cool, and sink again, like water boiling in a pot. The tops of these convection currents give the surface of the Sun a grainy appearance, called granulation.

From the photosphere, the energy is radiated into space in all directions as electromagnetic radiation: mostly visible light, infrared radiation, which we feel as heat, and ultraviolet radiation. This radiation travels through the vacuum of space at the speed of light and reaches the Earth, 150 million kilometres away, about 8 minutes and 20 seconds later.

Only a tiny fraction, about one two-billionth, of the Sun's total output falls on the Earth, but it is still an enormous amount of energy: in about an hour and a half, the Earth receives more energy from the Sun than all of humanity uses in a whole year.

Almost all life on Earth depends on this energy. Green plants and algae capture sunlight in photosynthesis, making food that feeds almost every food chain. The Sun's energy warms the land, the sea and the air, drives the winds and ocean currents, and powers the water cycle by evaporating water that falls as rain. Coal, oil and natural gas are the remains of ancient living things, so the energy in them was originally captured from sunlight millions of years ago. Solar panels and solar water heaters, increasingly common on South African homes and at solar farms in the Northern Cape, capture the Sun's energy directly.

The Sun's radiation can also be harmful. Its ultraviolet rays cause sunburn, skin ageing, eye damage and skin cancer; South Africa has one of the highest rates of skin cancer in the world, so sunscreen, hats, sunglasses and shade at midday matter. The ozone layer, high in the atmosphere, absorbs much of the most dangerous ultraviolet radiation.

The questions for this section are with you now: how energy travels outward through the Sun, how it crosses space to Earth, how long it takes, why the Sun's energy is essential for life, how fossil fuels store ancient sunlight, and the dangers of ultraviolet radiation.

## Subtopic: The Sun's Surface and Activity

The Sun is not a calm, unchanging ball. Its surface and atmosphere are constantly active.

Sunspots are dark patches on the photosphere, often larger than the whole Earth. They look dark only because they are cooler than their surroundings, at about 3 500 to 4 500 degrees Celsius; on their own, they would glow brightly. They form where strong magnetic fields poke through the surface and block the convection that brings up heat. The number of sunspots rises and falls in a cycle of about eleven years. Galileo and other astronomers noticed in the early 1600s that sunspots move across the Sun's face, showing that the Sun rotates, about once every 25 days at its equator.

Solar flares are sudden, powerful explosions near sunspots, releasing in minutes as much energy as billions of nuclear bombs. Coronal mass ejections are vast clouds of plasma blasted out into space. The Sun also continuously blows out a stream of charged particles called the solar wind.

The Sun's outer atmosphere, the corona, is extremely thin but very hot, over a million degrees Celsius. It is normally invisible against the bright surface, but it can be seen as a pearly glow during a total solar eclipse.

When charged particles from the Sun reach the Earth, most are deflected by the Earth's magnetic field. Some are guided towards the polar regions, where they collide with gases in the upper atmosphere and make them glow, producing the aurora, the shimmering curtains of green and red light seen near the poles. Strong solar storms can damage satellites, disrupt radio communications and GPS, and induce currents in long power lines. In South Africa, the South African National Space Agency in Hermanus monitors space weather and issues warnings for Africa.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the Sun is on fire; it releases energy by nuclear fusion, not burning. Two: saying the Sun is not a star; it is the nearest star. Three: saying sunspots are holes or cold; they are cooler regions, still thousands of degrees. Four: saying the Sun's light is produced at the surface; the energy comes from fusion in the core. Five: saying it is safe to look at the Sun briefly; it can damage the eyes permanently.

Layout for marks: state the reactants and product of fusion, where it happens, the conditions needed, and that mass is converted into energy.

The questions for this section are with you now: what sunspots are and why they look dark, the sunspot cycle, solar flares and the solar wind, the corona, the aurora and space weather, and the five exhibits.

## Subtopic: A Giant Ball of Glowing Gas

The Sun is a star. It is the same kind of thing as the stars you see at night, just much, much closer.

It is huge. You could line up 109 Earths across its face, and over a million Earths could fit inside it. It holds almost all the mass in the whole Solar System; everything else, all the planets and moons together, is a tiny crumb by comparison.

The Sun is not solid. It is a giant ball of glowing gas, mostly hydrogen with some helium, so hot that it is a special glowing gas called plasma. The surface is about 5 500 degrees. The middle is about 15 million degrees.

And it is an ordinary, middle-sized star, about four and a half billion years old, roughly halfway through its life.

Without it, the Earth would be a frozen, dark rock.

Keep the glowing ball in mind and try the questions for this part: why the Sun is a star, how big it is, and what it is made of.

## Subtopic: Squeezing Hydrogen into Helium

How does the Sun make so much energy? It is not burning like a fire. There is no oxygen there to burn anything.

Deep in its middle, the Sun's own enormous weight squeezes the hydrogen so hard and heats it so much that the tiny centres of the hydrogen atoms smash together and stick. Four hydrogen centres join up to make one helium centre. That is called nuclear fusion.

Here is the magic part. The helium weighs a tiny bit less than the four hydrogens that made it. That missing bit of mass turns into a huge amount of energy. Every second, the Sun turns about four million tonnes of mass into energy, and it has enough hydrogen to keep going for about another five billion years.

Hold the squeezing in mind and try this part's questions: what fusion is, what hydrogen becomes, and why the Sun is not on fire.

## Subtopic: Eight Minutes to Your Skin

The energy made in the Sun's middle takes thousands of years to struggle out to the surface. Then it shoots across space as light and heat, at the speed of light, and reaches your skin on the beach about eight minutes and twenty seconds later.

That energy keeps us alive. Plants use sunlight to make food, and every food chain starts there. Sunlight warms the Earth, drives the wind and the rain, and powers solar panels. Even coal is ancient sunlight, trapped by plants millions of years ago.

But the Sun's ultraviolet rays can burn your skin and damage your eyes, so use sunscreen, wear a hat, and never look straight at the Sun.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Sun is a star: a huge ball of hot hydrogen and helium plasma, 109 times wider than Earth, holding almost all the Solar System's mass. In its core, nuclear fusion turns hydrogen into helium, converting a little mass into a lot of energy. That energy reaches Earth as light and heat in about eight minutes and sustains almost all life.
