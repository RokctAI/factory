# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains what makes a good place and time for observing the sky, and why South Africa is home to some of the world's important observatories. The anchor is a comparison of two nights. On the first, a learner in a Johannesburg suburb steps outside on a hazy summer evening with the full Moon up and streetlights glowing: she can count perhaps twenty or thirty stars. On the second, she is on a farm in the Karoo, on a clear, dry winter night with no Moon and no lights for tens of kilometres: after twenty minutes her eyes have adjusted, and she sees thousands of stars, the Milky Way bright enough to show its dark dust lanes, and the misty patches of the Magellanic Clouds. The sky above both places is the same. What changed were the viewing conditions. The governing idea is that good observing needs darkness, clear and dry air, a steady atmosphere, height above sea level and a clear horizon, and for radio astronomy, radio silence; that light pollution, cloud, moisture, dust, moonlight and turbulence all spoil the view; and that South Africa's dry interior and southern position give it outstanding sites, used by observatories at Sutherland, in the Karoo, at Hartebeesthoek and in Cape Town. Good viewing conditions, the causes of poor conditions, South African observation sites, and practical observing tips are examinable, and they complete this term's study of Planet Earth and Beyond.

## Subtopic: What Makes a Good Viewing Site

Several conditions decide how well the sky can be seen from a site.

Darkness comes first. Faint stars and galaxies can only be seen against a dark sky. Artificial light from streetlights, buildings, sports fields and cars scatters off particles and molecules in the air and makes the whole sky glow. This is called light pollution. From the centre of a large city, only the brightest stars and planets are visible, while from a truly dark site, thousands of stars can be seen with the naked eye.

Clear, dry air is next. Clouds block the view completely, and water vapour, haze and dust absorb and scatter starlight, dimming it. Water vapour particularly absorbs infrared radiation. Dry regions with many cloudless nights a year are therefore preferred.

Height above sea level helps. At a higher altitude, there is less air above the telescope, so less of the light is absorbed and scattered, and much of the moisture and dust in the lower atmosphere lies below the site.

A steady atmosphere matters for sharp images. Moving pockets of air at different temperatures bend starlight in slightly different directions from moment to moment. This is what makes stars twinkle and blurs the images in telescopes. Astronomers call the steadiness of the air the seeing; sites where the air flows smoothly over the land, often on mountaintops with steady winds, have the best seeing.

Finally, a site should have a clear view of the horizon in all directions, a good number of clear nights, and, for southern sky objects, a position far enough south. Southern sites can see the centre of the Milky Way high overhead and the Magellanic Clouds, which are invisible from most of the Northern Hemisphere.

The questions for this section are with you now: the conditions for a good viewing site, the meaning of light pollution, why dry air and altitude help, why stars twinkle and what seeing means, and the advantage of a southern site.

## Subtopic: Things That Spoil the View

Light pollution is the fastest-growing problem. More than eighty percent of the world's people live under light-polluted skies, and a large fraction can no longer see the Milky Way from home. Light pollution wastes energy, since light shining upward lights nothing useful, and it also harms wildlife: insects swarm around lights, and some birds and sea turtles are confused by artificial light at night. It can be reduced by using shielded light fittings that point downward, using only as much light as needed, choosing warmer-coloured lights, and switching lights off when they are not needed.

The Moon itself spoils the view of faint objects. Around full Moon, moonlight scatters through the atmosphere and brightens the whole sky, hiding faint stars and the Milky Way. The best nights for faint objects are around new Moon, when the Moon is not in the night sky. The Moon is, however, a wonderful target in its own right, especially near first quarter, when shadows along the line between light and dark show its craters and mountains in sharp relief.

Weather and the atmosphere limit observing. Clouds, rain and humidity stop observation with optical telescopes; dust and smoke from veld fires make the sky hazy; strong turbulence blurs the image. Objects close to the horizon are seen through much more air than those overhead, so they appear dimmer, redder and more blurred; astronomers prefer to observe objects when they are high in the sky.

For radio telescopes, the problem is not light but radio interference from cellphones, radio and television transmitters, aircraft, satellites, and electrical equipment such as microwave ovens, motors and electric fences. Radio observatories are therefore placed in remote, sparsely populated areas, protected by law.

The questions for this section are with you now: the causes and effects of light pollution, how it can be reduced, how the Moon's phase affects observing, why objects near the horizon look poorer, and what interferes with radio telescopes.

## Subtopic: South Africa's Observatories

South Africa has a long history of astronomy and some of the best observing sites in the world.

The Royal Observatory at the Cape of Good Hope was founded in 1820 in what is now the suburb of Observatory in Cape Town. Astronomers there mapped the southern sky, and Thomas Henderson measured the distance to Alpha Centauri in the 1830s. Today the old site is the headquarters of the South African Astronomical Observatory, the SAAO, but city lights and frequent winter cloud now make Cape Town a poor place for research observing.

So in the 1970s, the main telescopes moved to a site on a hill near Sutherland in the Northern Cape, about 1 800 metres above sea level. Sutherland has dry, clear air, very dark skies far from large towns, steady seeing and many clear nights. It is one of the coldest towns in South Africa in winter, which is part of the price of its altitude. It hosts SALT, the largest single optical telescope in the Southern Hemisphere, as well as many smaller telescopes from South Africa and other countries, and it is also a popular destination for visitors who come to see the dark sky.

In the Karoo near Carnarvon, the MeerKAT radio telescope and the South African part of the Square Kilometre Array are located in a protected radio-quiet zone, chosen because the area is remote, sparsely populated and has very little radio interference. The Hartebeesthoek Radio Astronomy Observatory, west of Johannesburg, sits in a valley sheltered by hills that block radio interference from the city. Near Bloemfontein, the Boyden Observatory, moved from Peru in 1927, is used today largely for education and public outreach.

Southern Africa's dark skies are also valued for tourism and for nature. The !Ae!Hai Kalahari Heritage Park in the Northern Cape, managed with the local San and Mier communities, has been recognised internationally as a dark sky sanctuary, protecting one of the darkest skies on Earth.

## Subtopic: Observing the Sky Yourself

Anyone can start observing with no equipment at all. Choose a clear night, ideally around new Moon, and go to the darkest place you can safely reach, away from direct lights. Allow your eyes at least twenty to thirty minutes to adjust to the dark; the pupils widen quickly, but the light-sensitive cells of the retina take much longer to reach full sensitivity. Even a brief look at a bright white light, such as a cellphone screen, can undo much of this adaptation. Use a torch covered with red cellophane, because dim red light disturbs dark adaptation much less than white light. Bring a star map or a sky-map app set to night mode, warm clothing, and someone to observe with. Never look at the Sun, with or without binoculars or a telescope.

Start with the Moon, the planets and the bright constellations, then the Southern Cross, the Pointers and the Milky Way. Binoculars reveal craters on the Moon, the four bright moons of Jupiter and star clusters such as the Jewel Box near the Southern Cross.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying a telescope in a city works as well as one in the countryside; light pollution washes out faint objects. Two: saying full Moon is the best time for stargazing; moonlight brightens the sky and hides faint stars. Three: saying stars twinkle because they flicker on and off; twinkling is caused by moving air in the atmosphere. Four: saying observatories are on mountains to be closer to the stars; they are high to be above much of the air, moisture and dust. Five: saying radio telescopes need dark skies; they need radio silence, and many can work in daylight.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a tale of two skies, sky thieves, and a trip to Sutherland — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Tale of Two Skies

On a hazy night in a city, with the Moon full and streetlights on, you might see only a few dozen stars. On a clear, dark night in the Karoo, with no Moon and no lights for kilometres, you can see thousands, and the Milky Way glows across the sky. It is the same sky. Only the viewing conditions changed.

Good viewing needs darkness, clear and dry air, steady air, and a high place with a wide view of the horizon. Being far south helps too, because then you can see the middle of our galaxy and the Magellanic Clouds.

## Subtopic: Sky Thieves

Some things steal the stars from us.

Light pollution is the biggest thief. Lights shining upward make the whole sky glow, so faint stars disappear. Pointing lights downward and switching off ones we do not need helps people, animals and the night sky.

A bright Moon is another thief: near full Moon, the sky is too bright for faint stars. Clouds, dust, smoke and wobbling air make stars hard to see or make them twinkle and blur.

For radio telescopes, the thieves are cellphones, radios and electrical machines, so they are built in quiet places with very few signals.

## Subtopic: A Trip to Sutherland

South Africa has some of the best skies in the world. The first observatory was built in Cape Town in 1820, but city lights and winter clouds made it hard to see faint stars there. So the big telescopes moved to Sutherland in the Northern Cape, high, dry, dark and clear. That is where SALT is.

The radio telescopes MeerKAT and the SKA are in the quiet Karoo near Carnarvon, and another radio observatory is at Hartebeesthoek near Johannesburg.

To go stargazing yourself: pick a clear night near new Moon, find a dark place, give your eyes twenty to thirty minutes to adjust, and use a red torch. Never look at the Sun.

Light pollution, moonlight, clouds, dust and turbulence spoil the view, and radio interference spoils radio astronomy. South Africa's main observatories are at Sutherland for optical telescopes, in the Karoo and at Hartebeesthoek for radio telescopes, with the SAAO headquarters in Cape Town.
