### subtopic_1 — Watching the Sky Before Telescopes
**Assistant:** Why do the zodiac constellations matter in astronomy if astrology is not a science?

**Tutor:** Because the zodiac constellations mark the path the Sun, Moon and planets appear to follow across the sky, which is a real and useful astronomical fact, even though astrology's claims about personality and fate are not supported by evidence. As the Earth orbits the Sun, the Sun appears to move slowly against the background stars, completing one circuit of the sky in a year. Its path is called the ecliptic. Because the planets orbit the Sun in nearly the same flat plane, as we learned in the Solar System lessons, they and the Moon also always appear close to the ecliptic. The constellations along this band, such as Taurus, Scorpius and Sagittarius, became known as the zodiac. Ancient Babylonian astronomers divided this band into twelve equal parts about 2 500 years ago, and their careful records of planetary positions were genuine science. Astrology, the belief that the positions of the Sun and planets at a person's birth shape their character and future, has been tested many times in controlled studies, and its predictions do no better than chance. There is also a mismatch: because the Earth's axis slowly wobbles like a spinning top, taking about 26 000 years per wobble, the dates when the Sun is in each constellation have shifted by about a month since the zodiac was defined. The Sun also passes through a thirteenth constellation, Ophiuchus, for part of each year. For astronomers, the zodiac is simply a useful guide to where to look for the planets on a given night.

### subtopic_2 — What a Constellation Is
**Assistant:** Do the constellations change over time?

**Tutor:** Yes, very slowly, because the stars are all moving through the galaxy at different speeds and in different directions, but the changes are far too small to notice in a human lifetime. Each star is orbiting the centre of the Milky Way, and nearby stars move relative to the Sun at speeds of tens of kilometres per second. Because they are so far away, this movement shifts their positions in our sky by only tiny amounts each year. Astronomers measure these shifts, called proper motions, very precisely; the Gaia spacecraft has measured them for more than a billion stars. Over tens of thousands of years they add up. Alpha Centauri, being close, has one of the larger proper motions of the bright stars; it moves across the sky by about the width of the full Moon in roughly five hundred years. In about 50 000 years, the Southern Cross will have changed shape noticeably, and the Pointers will have moved relative to it. Another, faster change is caused by the wobble of the Earth's axis, called precession, which takes about 26 000 years. It changes which point of the sky lies above the poles. About 5 000 years ago, a star called Thuban was the north pole star, and in about 12 000 years the bright star Vega will be close to the north celestial pole. Precession also means that the Southern Cross, which was visible from places like ancient Greece thousands of years ago, has since sunk below their horizon. Our ancestors saw the same sky we see, but not quite the same.

### subtopic_3 — Southern Skies and Their Stories
**Assistant:** How did people who had no writing remember so many stars and their timing?

**Tutor:** Through stories, songs, names, ceremonies and practical use, passed down from generation to generation, which turned observations into memorable knowledge. A name like isiLimela, the digging stars, carries in it both the identity of the star cluster and what its appearance means. A story like the Khoikhoi tale of the zebras, the hunter and the lion places several stars in a fixed relationship: the three zebras in a row, the arrow below, the lion beside them. Anyone who knows the story can find all of those stars, and the story's characters make the pattern easy to remember. Many such stories also link the stars to the time of year when they are visible, to rain, to the behaviour of animals or to farming tasks, so the knowledge was used and checked every year. Elders who were skilled observers taught younger people directly under the night sky, and ceremonies were tied to the stars' appearance. Researchers who have recorded southern African star lore, often working with community elders, have found that the stories are consistent with the real behaviour of the sky. Similar methods were used worldwide. Aboriginal Australians, for example, preserved detailed sky knowledge in oral traditions, some of which may be many thousands of years old. Polynesian navigators memorised the rising and setting points of more than a hundred stars as a kind of star compass, which allowed them to find tiny islands across thousands of kilometres of open ocean. Oral traditions are a powerful technology for storing knowledge, and astronomy was one of the most important things they stored.

### subtopic_4 — Finding Your Way by the Stars
**Assistant:** Does the Southern Cross method give exact south?

**Tutor:** It gives a direction that is close to true south, usually within a few degrees, which is accurate enough for finding your way on foot, but not exact. The south celestial pole is the point in the sky directly above the Earth's South Pole, around which all the stars appear to turn. The Southern Cross is not at that point; it is about thirty degrees away from it, and turns around it during the night and through the year. The method of extending the long axis of the Cross about four and a half times, and meeting it with the perpendicular line from the Pointers, gives a point close to the pole. Judging lengths and right angles by eye in the dark introduces errors of a few degrees. Also, the Cross is sometimes too low or even below the horizon in the early evening, particularly in spring from the more northern parts of South Africa, so it is not always available. A useful check is the faint star Sigma Octantis, which lies close to the south celestial pole, but at about magnitude five it is too faint to see from anywhere with light pollution. There is another important point: true south, found from the stars, is not the same as the direction shown by a magnetic compass. The compass points to the Earth's magnetic pole, and in South Africa the difference between magnetic and true north, called the magnetic declination, is roughly twenty to twenty-seven degrees west, depending on the location. So a compass and the stars give slightly different answers, and navigators correct for this.
