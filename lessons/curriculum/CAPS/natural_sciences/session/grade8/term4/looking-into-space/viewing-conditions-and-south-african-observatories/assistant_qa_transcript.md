### subtopic_1 — What Makes a Good Viewing Site
**Assistant:** Why are so many of the world's big telescopes in deserts and on mountains, like Chile and Hawaii, not in South Africa?

**Tutor:** Because the very best sites for the largest telescopes need a rare combination of extreme altitude, extreme dryness and exceptionally steady air, and a few places in the world have it, but South Africa's sites are excellent in their own right and complement them. The Atacama Desert in northern Chile is one of the driest places on Earth, and its mountains rise to between two and five kilometres above sea level, above most of the moisture in the atmosphere. Cold ocean currents and steady winds from the Pacific Ocean flow smoothly over the coastal mountains, giving very steady seeing. Mauna Kea in Hawaii rises over four kilometres above a warm ocean, above a layer of cloud that traps moisture below it. These sites host many of the world's largest optical, infrared and millimetre-wave telescopes. Sutherland, at about 1 800 metres, is lower, and its seeing is good though not quite as exceptional, but it has dark skies, many clear nights and a southern latitude similar to Chile's, and it is at a different longitude, which is very useful: when an event such as an exploding star or a gravitational wave source needs to be watched continuously, telescopes in Chile, South Africa and Australia can pass the job from one to the next as the Earth turns. For radio astronomy, which does not need dry mountain air in the same way, South Africa's Karoo is one of the best sites in the world, because it is remote, radio quiet and protected by law, which is why it was chosen for the Square Kilometre Array.

### subtopic_2 — Things That Spoil the View
**Assistant:** How is it possible to see the Milky Way from a city?

**Tutor:** Usually it is not, and that is the main point of light pollution. Studies using satellite measurements have estimated that about a third of the world's people, and most people in Europe and North America, can no longer see the Milky Way from where they live. The Milky Way's glow is fainter than the sky glow above a city, so it simply disappears. From a small town on a clear, moonless night, a faint band may be visible; from a dark rural site it is spectacular, bright enough to show its dark dust lanes; and from the darkest places, such as the Northern Cape, it is so bright that it looks almost like a cloud. Astronomers use a scale called the Bortle scale, from one for the darkest skies to nine for inner-city skies, to describe this. One simple measure of how dark your sky is: on a moonless night, after letting your eyes adapt, count how many of the stars of the Southern Cross and the Pointers you can see, or count the stars within a small, well-known pattern and compare with a star chart. In a city, you might also see the Milky Way during a large power outage, which has happened in South Africa during load shedding, when many people noticed far more stars than usual from their homes. Light pollution is unusual among environmental problems because it can be reversed instantly: if the unnecessary lights were switched off or shielded, the stars would return that same night. Some towns have adopted dark-sky lighting rules, and Sutherland's residents keep their lighting low and shielded to protect the observatory.

### subtopic_3 — South Africa's Observatories
**Assistant:** Why does Sutherland get so cold in winter?

**Tutor:** Because of its altitude, its inland position far from the sea, its dry air and its clear skies, the same conditions that make it good for astronomy. Air temperature in the lower atmosphere generally falls with height, by about six to seven degrees Celsius for every thousand metres. At about 1 450 metres, the town of Sutherland is on a high plateau, and the observatory hill is higher still, at about 1 800 metres. Being far inland, it lacks the moderating effect of the sea, which stores heat and keeps coastal temperatures milder. The most important factor on winter nights is the clear, dry air. During the day, the ground is warmed by sunlight; at night, it loses heat by radiating infrared energy into space. Clouds and water vapour absorb and return some of that radiation, acting like a blanket. Over the Karoo on a clear, dry winter night, there is very little blanket, so the ground and the air near it cool rapidly. Cold air is denser and drains into low-lying areas, making valleys even colder. As a result, Sutherland often records some of the lowest temperatures in South Africa, with winter nights well below freezing and occasional snow. Astronomers there work in heated control rooms, but the telescope domes are kept at the outside night temperature, because warm air rising from a telescope or dome would create turbulence and blur the images. So the same clear, dry, still air that makes Sutherland cold also makes it one of the best places to see the stars.

### subtopic_4 — Observing the Sky Yourself
**Assistant:** Why do my eyes need so long to adjust to the dark, and why does red light help?

**Tutor:** Because the rod cells in your retina, which give night vision, take time to rebuild a light-sensitive chemical that bright light breaks down, and red light hardly affects them. Your eye adjusts to darkness in two ways. First, the pupil widens from about two or three millimetres in bright light to about six or seven in darkness, letting in several times more light; this takes only a few seconds. Second, and much more importantly, the light-sensitive cells become more sensitive. The cone cells, which see colour, adjust within about ten minutes, but they are not very sensitive. The rod cells are far more sensitive but recover slowly: they contain a pigment called rhodopsin, which bright light bleaches, and which takes twenty to thirty minutes or more to rebuild fully. After full dark adaptation, your eyes can be thousands of times more sensitive than in daylight. A single glance at a bright white screen or a car headlight bleaches some of the rhodopsin, and you lose part of that sensitivity, needing several minutes to recover. Rod cells are much less sensitive to deep red light than to other colours, while some cone cells still respond to it. So a dim red light lets you read a star chart using your cones while barely disturbing the rods that you need for faint stars. That is why astronomers cover torches with red cellophane and set their apps to red night mode. One extra trick, called averted vision: because rods are concentrated away from the centre of the retina, faint objects are easier to see if you look slightly to one side of them rather than straight at them.
