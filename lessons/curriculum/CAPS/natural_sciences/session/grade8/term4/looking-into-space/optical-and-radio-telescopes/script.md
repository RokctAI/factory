# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains how telescopes extend our senses far beyond what the eye can do, using two kinds: optical telescopes, which collect visible light, and radio telescopes, which collect radio waves. The anchor is the Northern Cape at night. On a hill outside the small town of Sutherland stands the Southern African Large Telescope, SALT, whose mirror, made of ninety-one hexagonal pieces fitted together, is about eleven metres across, the largest single optical telescope in the Southern Hemisphere. About two hundred kilometres to the north, in the Karoo near Carnarvon, sixty-four white dishes of the MeerKAT radio telescope point silently at the same sky, collecting faint radio waves from objects that give off little or no visible light at all. And above the atmosphere, the Hubble Space Telescope circles the Earth. The governing idea is that the bigger a telescope's light-collecting area, the fainter the objects it can detect and the finer the detail it can show; that optical telescopes use lenses or mirrors to focus visible light; that radio telescopes use large dishes and arrays to collect radio waves, revealing a different, hidden Universe; and that telescopes in space escape the blurring and blocking effects of the atmosphere. Optical and radio telescopes, how they work, examples including SALT, Hubble and the SKA, and why each is useful are examinable, and they lead to the electromagnetic spectrum in Grade 10 and 11 Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: How Optical Telescopes Work

An optical telescope collects visible light from a distant object and focuses it to form a bright, magnified image. Its most important job is not magnifying, but gathering light. The pupil of the human eye is at most about seven millimetres wide, so it collects very little light from a faint star. A telescope collects light over a much larger area and concentrates it, making faint objects visible.

There are two basic designs. A refracting telescope uses a large convex lens at the front, called the objective lens, to bend, or refract, light to a focus, and a smaller lens called the eyepiece to magnify the image. Galileo used a small refractor in 1609 and 1610 to discover the four large moons of Jupiter, the phases of Venus, mountains on the Moon and the stars of the Milky Way. A reflecting telescope uses a large curved mirror, shaped like a shallow bowl, to reflect light to a focus. Isaac Newton built one of the first reflecting telescopes in 1668. Large lenses are heavy, sag under their own weight and split light into colours, so all large modern research telescopes are reflectors; a mirror can be supported from behind and reflects all colours equally.

The light-gathering power of a telescope depends on the area of its main mirror or lens, which increases with the square of its diameter. A mirror twice as wide collects four times as much light. That is why astronomers keep building larger telescopes. Larger mirrors also show finer detail, called resolution.

Today the eye at the eyepiece has been replaced by sensitive electronic cameras, similar to those in cellphones but far more sensitive and often cooled to very low temperatures. They can collect light for minutes or hours, building up images of objects millions of times fainter than the eye can see. Instruments called spectrographs split the light into a spectrum, revealing what a star or galaxy is made of, its temperature and how fast it is moving towards or away from us.

The questions for this section are with you now: the main job of an optical telescope, the difference between refracting and reflecting telescopes, why large modern telescopes are reflectors, how light-gathering power depends on diameter, and what cameras and spectrographs add.

## Subtopic: SALT and the Hubble Space Telescope

The Southern African Large Telescope, SALT, at the South African Astronomical Observatory near Sutherland, was completed in 2005. Its main mirror measures about eleven by ten metres and is made of ninety-one hexagonal mirror segments, each about one metre across, which are kept precisely aligned so that they act as a single mirror. SALT was built by South Africa together with partners in several other countries, and its design made it much cheaper than other telescopes of its size. Because it collects so much light, it is especially good at spectroscopy of faint objects: measuring the composition and motion of distant galaxies, studying exploding stars and following up objects discovered by other telescopes. Compared with a seven-millimetre eye pupil, SALT's mirror collects millions of times more light.

The Hubble Space Telescope was launched in 1990 and orbits the Earth about five hundred and thirty kilometres up, circling it about every ninety-five minutes. Its mirror is 2.4 metres across, much smaller than SALT's, but because it is above the atmosphere it gives extremely sharp images. The atmosphere's moving pockets of warm and cool air bend light slightly and constantly, which is what makes stars twinkle and blurs images taken from the ground. The atmosphere also blocks most ultraviolet and much infrared radiation, which Hubble can observe. Hubble's images have measured the expansion rate of the Universe, shown galaxies billions of light years away in the deep fields, and revealed stars being born in clouds of gas.

In 2021 the James Webb Space Telescope was launched. Its gold-coated mirror, 6.5 metres across and made of eighteen segments, unfolded in space, and it observes mainly in infrared from about 1.5 million kilometres from the Earth. Space telescopes are extremely expensive and hard to repair, so ground-based telescopes like SALT remain essential, and the two kinds work together.

The questions for this section are with you now: where SALT is and what it is used for, the structure of its mirror, the advantages of Hubble being above the atmosphere, why stars twinkle, and how the James Webb Space Telescope differs from Hubble.

## Subtopic: Radio Telescopes and the SKA

Visible light is only a small part of the electromagnetic spectrum. Many objects in space also give off radio waves, which have much longer wavelengths than light. In 1932 an American engineer, Karl Jansky, investigating radio noise for a telephone company, discovered radio waves coming from the centre of the Milky Way, and radio astronomy was born.

A radio telescope usually uses a large, curved metal dish to reflect radio waves to a receiver at its focus, where they are converted into electrical signals and analysed by computers. Because radio waves are so long, a single dish must be very large to show fine detail. Astronomers solve this by linking many dishes spread over a wide area into an array, combining their signals with powerful computers so that together they act like one enormous telescope, a technique called interferometry.

Radio telescopes reveal things optical telescopes cannot. Radio waves pass through the dust that hides the centre of the galaxy, and through clouds, so many radio telescopes can observe day and night and in cloudy weather. They detect cold hydrogen gas, which gives off radio waves at a wavelength of twenty-one centimetres, mapping the gas in galaxies; pulsars, spinning collapsed stars that send out regular pulses; jets of material from supermassive black holes; and the faint afterglow of the Big Bang.

South Africa has long been a leader in radio astronomy. The Hartebeesthoek Radio Astronomy Observatory near Johannesburg operates a 26-metre dish that once tracked spacecraft. MeerKAT, in the Karoo, was inaugurated in 2018; its sixty-four dishes, each 13.5 metres across, are spread over about eight kilometres, and it has produced some of the sharpest radio images ever made of the centre of the Milky Way. MeerKAT is now becoming part of the Square Kilometre Array, the SKA, an international project to build the world's largest radio telescope. The SKA-Mid telescope in South Africa will have about two hundred dishes, including MeerKAT's, spread across the Karoo with outlying dishes up to about 150 kilometres apart, while the SKA-Low telescope in Western Australia will have more than one hundred thousand small antennas. Partner African countries host dishes for linked observations.

The questions for this section are with you now: what radio waves are, Jansky's discovery, how a radio dish works, why arrays are used, what radio telescopes can detect that optical telescopes cannot, and South Africa's radio telescopes, MeerKAT and the SKA.

## Subtopic: Comparing the Telescopes

Each kind of telescope reveals a different picture of the same sky. Optical telescopes show stars, the shapes of galaxies and glowing gas lit by hot young stars. Radio telescopes show cold gas, magnetic fields, pulsars and black hole jets, many of them invisible to the eye. Combining observations from several kinds gives a much fuller understanding, as when astronomers observe one exploding star with SALT, MeerKAT and space telescopes at the same time.

The location of each kind matters. Optical telescopes need clear, dark, dry skies, high above sea level, away from city lights, which is why SALT is at Sutherland. Radio telescopes need radio silence, far from cellphones, radio stations, power lines and electrical equipment, which is why MeerKAT and the SKA are in the remote Karoo, protected by a law that limits radio transmissions in the area. Space telescopes avoid the atmosphere altogether, but they are expensive and limited in size.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the main job of a telescope is to magnify; its main job is to collect light, and a bigger aperture collects more. Two: saying radio telescopes listen to sounds from space; they collect radio waves, a form of electromagnetic radiation, not sound, which cannot travel through empty space. Three: saying Hubble is better because it is closer to the stars; at about 530 kilometres up it is not meaningfully closer; it gives sharper images because it is above the atmosphere. Four: saying radio telescopes only work at night; many can observe day and night and through cloud. Five: saying all large telescopes use lenses; large research telescopes use mirrors.

Layout for marks: name the telescope, state the type and what it collects, give its location, then give one advantage, for example, MeerKAT, a radio telescope array of sixty-four dishes in the Karoo, which can see through dust to the centre of the galaxy.

The questions for this section are with you now: what optical and radio telescopes each reveal, why their locations are chosen, the advantages and disadvantages of space telescopes, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a bucket for light, a mirror in the Karoo, and giant ears for radio waves — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Bucket for Light

The black circle in the middle of your eye, the pupil, is tiny. It catches only a little light, so faint stars are invisible to you.

A telescope is like a big bucket for light. It catches light over a much bigger area and focuses it. The bigger the bucket, the fainter the things you can see. A mirror twice as wide catches four times as much light.

Some telescopes use a big lens to bend light, like Galileo's. Most big telescopes use a big curved mirror instead, because big lenses are too heavy.

Keep the light bucket in mind and try the questions for this part: the main job of a telescope, lenses and mirrors, and why bigger is better.

## Subtopic: A Mirror in the Karoo

Near Sutherland in the Northern Cape stands SALT, the Southern African Large Telescope. Its mirror is about eleven metres wide, made of ninety-one six-sided pieces fitted together like a honeycomb. It is the biggest single optical telescope in the Southern Hemisphere.

High above the Earth, the Hubble Space Telescope circles our planet. Its mirror is smaller, but it sits above the air, which wobbles and blurs starlight and makes stars twinkle. So Hubble's pictures are super sharp.

Hold SALT and Hubble in mind and try this part's questions: where SALT is, what its mirror is like, and why Hubble is in space.

## Subtopic: Giant Ears for Radio Waves

Many things in space give off radio waves, a kind of invisible light. Radio telescopes catch them with big metal dishes, a bit like satellite TV dishes but much bigger.

In the Karoo, the MeerKAT telescope has sixty-four dishes working together like one giant telescope. It is part of the Square Kilometre Array, the SKA, which will be the biggest radio telescope in the world, with dishes in South Africa and antennas in Australia. Radio telescopes can see through dust and clouds, and they need quiet places with no cellphone signals.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Telescopes collect much more light than the eye; bigger telescopes see fainter things. Optical telescopes use lenses or, for big ones, mirrors to focus visible light. SALT at Sutherland is the largest in the Southern Hemisphere. Hubble is above the atmosphere, so its images are sharp. Radio telescopes collect radio waves with dishes, often in arrays, like MeerKAT and the SKA in the Karoo, and reveal things we cannot see in visible light.

Hold the light bucket, the mirror and the giant ears, and take the final questions of the lesson: comparing optical and radio telescopes, and why each is built where it is.
