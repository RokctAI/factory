### subtopic_1 — How Optical Telescopes Work
**Assistant:** If a telescope's main job is to collect light, why do people talk about magnification so much?

**Tutor:** Because magnification is the most obvious thing a small telescope seems to do, and it is easy to advertise, but for astronomy it is usually the least important property. Magnification depends on the combination of the telescope's main lens or mirror and the eyepiece, and you can change it simply by swapping eyepieces. But magnifying an image does not add any information or light to it; it only spreads the same light over a larger area, so the image becomes dimmer and blurrier. Cheap telescopes are often sold with claims of very high magnification, such as five hundred times, but with a small lens that collects little light, such images are faint and fuzzy. What really decides how much a telescope can show is its aperture, the diameter of its main mirror or lens. A larger aperture collects more light, so fainter objects become visible, and it can separate finer details, called its resolving power. A large aperture can then support higher useful magnification. For a beginner, a pair of ordinary binoculars, which collect several dozen times more light than the eye at a low magnification of seven to ten times, is a very good way to start: they show the moons of Jupiter, craters on the Moon, star clusters such as the Pleiades and the Jewel Box near the Southern Cross, and the Magellanic Clouds. Research telescopes like SALT rarely use eyepieces at all; their light goes into cameras and spectrographs, and what matters is how much light they can collect.

### subtopic_2 — SALT and the Hubble Space Telescope
**Assistant:** How do SALT's ninety-one separate mirrors act like one mirror?

**Tutor:** Each segment is a precisely shaped piece of glass-ceramic, about one metre across, and together they form part of one large spherical surface; computer-controlled actuators behind the segments keep them aligned with each other to a tiny fraction of the wavelength of light. To act as a single mirror, the segments must sit on the same imaginary curved surface with errors smaller than about a hundredth of the width of a human hair. Temperature changes, wind and the telescope's own movements constantly push them slightly out of line, so sensors measure the positions of neighbouring segment edges and motors adjust them. SALT's design is unusual and clever. Most telescopes swing their whole mirror to follow a star across the sky, which needs a very strong, expensive mount. SALT's mirror is fixed at a constant tilt; the telescope can rotate on air bearings to point in different directions, but during an observation the mirror stays still, and instead a smaller unit at the top, called the tracker, moves across the focus to follow the star's image as the Earth turns. This design, copied from a telescope in Texas, made SALT much cheaper to build than other telescopes of its size, at the cost of being able to follow each object for only part of the night. Making the main mirror spherical also means every segment has the same shape, which simplifies manufacture, and a corrector in the tracker removes the blurring that a spherical mirror would otherwise cause. Many South African engineers and technicians helped build and maintain it.

### subtopic_3 — Radio Telescopes and the SKA
**Assistant:** Can you hear the radio signals that radio telescopes detect?

**Tutor:** Not directly. Radio waves are electromagnetic waves, like light, not sound waves, and our ears cannot detect them. Sound is a vibration of particles in a material such as air, and it cannot travel through empty space at all, which is why space is silent. Radio waves, by contrast, travel through empty space at the speed of light. When you listen to a radio station, the radio set picks up radio waves and uses the information carried in them to drive a loudspeaker, which then makes sound waves in the air. In the same way, astronomers can convert the signals from radio telescopes into sound by feeding them into a loudspeaker, and this is sometimes done for public demonstrations: signals from pulsars, for example, sound like rapid regular clicks or a buzzing hum, because the pulsar's beam sweeps past the Earth tens or hundreds of times a second. Karl Jansky first noticed radio waves from the Milky Way as a faint hiss in his headphones. But astronomers do not study the signals by listening. The signals from radio telescopes are recorded as numbers by digital electronics and processed by powerful computers, which turn them into images and spectra. MeerKAT produces so much data that it needs a supercomputer on site to process it, and the full SKA will produce more data than almost any other scientific project on Earth, requiring some of the world's fastest computers. So radio astronomers mainly look at their data rather than listen to it.

### subtopic_4 — Comparing the Telescopes
**Assistant:** Why does a cellphone near a radio telescope cause problems?

**Tutor:** Because a cellphone is a radio transmitter, and even a weak phone signal is enormously stronger than the faint radio waves arriving from space. The radio waves from distant galaxies and gas clouds are incredibly weak after travelling for millions or billions of years. The total energy collected by all the radio telescopes in the history of radio astronomy is often said to be less than the energy needed to turn a single page of a book. When complete, the SKA will be sensitive enough to detect an airport radar on a planet tens of light years away. A phone, a microwave oven, an electric fence, a car's ignition, a Wi-Fi router or a faulty power line nearby would drown out the signals the astronomers are trying to study, much as a single torch shone into your eyes would make it impossible to see faint stars. For this reason, South Africa passed the Astronomy Geographic Advantage Act in 2007, which allows large parts of the Northern Cape around the MeerKAT and SKA site to be declared an astronomy advantage area, where radio transmissions are restricted. Cellphone coverage in the core area is limited, visitors must switch off their phones, and the buildings housing electronics on site are surrounded by special shielding to stop their own equipment interfering. The Karoo site was chosen partly because it is sparsely populated, with few existing transmitters, and because the dry, high plateau and the surrounding hills help block interference. The radio-quiet Karoo is a natural resource for science, just as the dark skies of Sutherland are for optical astronomy.
