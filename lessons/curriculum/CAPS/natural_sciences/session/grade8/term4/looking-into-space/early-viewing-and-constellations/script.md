# Part 1 — Expert

This session looks at how people have watched the sky since long before telescopes, and at the patterns of stars called constellations and the stories cultures have told about them. The anchor is a winter evening in a village in the Eastern Cape. In late June, just before sunrise, a small, tight cluster of faint stars rises in the east. Farmers who speak isiXhosa call it isiLimela, the digging stars, and its return to the morning sky has long marked the time to prepare the fields for planting. Across the world, the same cluster, known to astronomers as the Pleiades, was used by other farming peoples in the same way. With nothing but their eyes, people noticed which stars rose at which time of year and used them as a calendar, a clock and a compass. The governing idea is that the sky changes in regular, predictable ways because of the Earth's rotation and orbit; that people in every culture grouped the stars into patterns and told stories about them; that astronomers now divide the whole sky into eighty-eight official constellations; and that the stars in a constellation only appear close together from the Earth. Early naked-eye observation, the uses of the sky by early people, the meaning of a constellation, examples of constellations and their cultural stories, and finding south with the Southern Cross are examinable, and they lead into telescopes and observing conditions in the next sessions.

## Subtopic: Watching the Sky Before Telescopes

For almost all of human history, people studied the sky with their eyes alone. Without electric lighting, the night sky was dark and full of stars, and it was a part of daily life. Careful observers noticed several patterns.

The stars move across the sky during the night, rising in the east and setting in the west, while keeping their positions relative to one another, as if fixed on a giant turning dome. We now know this is caused by the Earth spinning on its axis once a day. In the Southern Hemisphere, the stars near the south celestial pole, the point in the sky directly above the Earth's South Pole, circle around it without setting.

The stars visible in the evening change gradually through the year, because as the Earth orbits the Sun, the night side of the Earth faces different directions in space. Orion is seen on summer evenings in South Africa, while Scorpius rides high on winter evenings. Each star rises about four minutes earlier every night, so over a year the whole sky turns once.

Early observers also noticed five bright wanderers, the planets Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn, which move slowly against the background stars. Venus is so bright as an evening or morning star that it has names in many languages; in Sesotho, the evening Venus is called Kopadilalelo, the one who asks for supper, because it appears at supper time.

People used these patterns practically. They tracked the seasons for planting and harvesting, timed ceremonies, and navigated by the stars on land and at sea. Ancient civilisations in Egypt, Mesopotamia, China, India, Central America and elsewhere kept careful records. Many early thinkers believed that the Earth was at the centre of the Universe and that everything circled it. In 1543 Nicolaus Copernicus published the idea that the Earth and planets orbit the Sun, and in 1609 and 1610 Galileo Galilei's observations with a telescope gave strong evidence that he was right, beginning the era of telescopic astronomy.

The questions for this section are with you now: why stars appear to move across the sky each night, why the stars seen in the evening change through the year, what the planets looked like to early observers, three practical uses of the sky by early people, and the change from an Earth-centred to a Sun-centred view.

## Subtopic: What a Constellation Is

A constellation is a group of stars that appears to form a pattern or picture in the night sky, together with the region of sky around it. People in every culture have joined the stars into patterns to remember them, often naming them after animals, people, gods or objects from their daily life.

Many of the constellation names used internationally today come from ancient Greek and Roman traditions; the astronomer Ptolemy listed forty-eight constellations about 1 900 years ago. The southern skies were not visible from Greece, so many southern constellations were named much later by European navigators and astronomers. One of them, Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille, observed from the Cape of Good Hope in 1751 and 1752 and named fourteen new southern constellations, including Mensa, named after Table Mountain. In 1922 the International Astronomical Union agreed on a list of eighty-eight official constellations, and later fixed their boundaries so that together they cover the whole sky, like the provinces of a map.

A smaller, well-known pattern of stars that is part of a constellation, or crosses several, is called an asterism. Orion's Belt, three bright stars in a row, is an asterism within the constellation Orion; in South Africa, the brighter stars of Orion are often seen as a pot or saucepan.

The stars of a constellation are usually not close to one another in space. They only look close because they lie in roughly the same direction from the Earth. In Orion, for example, the bright red star Betelgeuse is several hundred light years away, while other stars in the same constellation are well over a thousand light years away. Seen from a planet around another star, the familiar patterns would look completely different.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of a constellation, the number of official constellations, the difference between a constellation and an asterism, why the stars in a constellation are not really grouped together, and Lacaille's contribution from the Cape.

## Subtopic: Southern Skies and Their Stories

The Southern Cross, called Crux, is the smallest of the eighty-eight constellations, but one of the most famous, a kite of four bright stars with a fainter fifth. Beside it are the two Pointers, Alpha and Beta Centauri. In Setswana and Sesotho tradition, the bright stars of the Cross and the Pointers have been seen as giraffes, dithutlwa, the Pointers as male giraffes and the stars of the Cross as female giraffes; their position low in the evening sky in late summer was linked to the season for certain tasks.

The Pleiades, a cluster of more than a thousand young stars in the constellation Taurus, of which about six or seven are visible to the naked eye, has a central place in many southern African calendars. In isiXhosa it is isiLimela, and in Setswana Selemela; its appearance in the eastern sky before dawn in June signals the start of the agricultural year. Among the Khoikhoi, a well-known story tells that the Pleiades are the daughters of the sky god, who sent their husband to hunt three zebras, the stars of Orion's Belt. He shot an arrow, which fell short, and is seen as the stars below the Belt; afraid to fetch it because a lion, the red star Betelgeuse, was watching the zebras, and ashamed to return home without food, he still sits in the cold night sky.

Scorpius, high overhead in South African winter, has a curved tail of bright stars and the red star Antares at its heart. The Milky Way itself, as the |xam San told it, was made by a girl who threw ashes and embers into the sky to light the way home.

These stories are not only myths. They are ways of remembering the positions and timing of stars accurately, passing on knowledge across generations without writing, and linking the sky to farming, hunting, travel and values.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Southern Cross and the Pointers, the Pleiades and their role in southern African calendars, the Khoikhoi story of Orion's Belt, Scorpius and Antares, and why cultural star stories were important.

## Subtopic: Finding Your Way by the Stars

In the Northern Hemisphere, the star Polaris lies almost exactly above the North Pole, so travellers find north by finding it. There is no bright star above the South Pole, so people in the Southern Hemisphere use the Southern Cross and the Pointers to find south.

The method is as follows. First, find the Southern Cross, and identify its long axis, the line from the top star through the brightest star at its foot. Extend that line about four and a half times the length of the Cross. Second, find the Pointers and draw a line between them; from its middle, draw a line at right angles. Where these two lines meet is close to the south celestial pole. From that point, drop straight down to the horizon: that direction is south. Because the stars turn around the south celestial pole during the night, the Cross may be upright, on its side or upside down, but the method still works.

Other cultures navigated in similar ways. Polynesian sailors crossed thousands of kilometres of the Pacific Ocean by memorising the rising and setting points of many stars, and travellers across Africa used stars to journey by night in the cool hours.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the stars move across the sky because they orbit the Earth each night; the apparent movement is caused by the Earth spinning on its axis. Two: saying the stars in a constellation are close together in space; they only lie in the same direction from the Earth. Three: saying there is a bright south pole star like Polaris; there is not, so we use the Southern Cross and the Pointers. Four: saying the same stars are seen every evening of the year; the evening sky changes with the seasons because the Earth orbits the Sun. Five: saying cultural star stories have no scientific value; they encoded careful observations used for calendars and navigation.

Layout for marks: describe the observation, explain its cause, then give a use, for example, the Pleiades rise before dawn in June because of the Earth's orbit, and farmers used this to begin planting.

The questions for this section are with you now: why the Southern Hemisphere has no pole star, the steps for finding south using the Southern Cross and the Pointers, other examples of star navigation, and the five exhibits.

## Subtopic: A Calendar in the Sky

Long before clocks, calendars and telescopes, people watched the sky with just their eyes. The sky was dark and full of stars every night.

They noticed that the stars rise in the east and set in the west during the night. That is because the Earth is spinning. They also noticed that different stars show up in different seasons: Orion on summer evenings, Scorpius on winter evenings. That is because the Earth is travelling around the Sun.

So the stars became a calendar. When the little cluster of stars called isiLimela, the Pleiades, appears just before sunrise in June, farmers know it is time to get ready for planting.

Keep the sky calendar in mind and try the questions for this part: why stars move across the sky, why we see different stars in different seasons, and how people used the stars.

## Subtopic: Join-the-Dots Pictures

People in every culture joined the stars into pictures, like a join-the-dots puzzle. These star pictures are called constellations. Today astronomers use eighty-eight official ones that cover the whole sky.

Each culture has its own stories. The Khoikhoi saw three zebras in Orion's Belt, with a hunter's arrow falling short below them and a lion watching. In Setswana and Sesotho tradition, the Southern Cross and its Pointers are giraffes.

Here is the trick: the stars in a constellation are not really near each other. Some are much further away than others. They just happen to be in the same direction from where we stand.

Hold the join-the-dots idea in mind and try this part's questions: what a constellation is, how many there are, and why the stars in one are not really close together.

## Subtopic: A Compass Made of Stars

In the north, people use a star above the North Pole to find north. We have no bright star above the South Pole, so we use the Southern Cross.

Find the kite-shaped Cross and its two bright Pointers. Make the long line of the Cross about four and a half times longer. Draw a line straight out from the middle of the Pointers. Where the two lines meet, drop straight down to the horizon. That way is south.

A constellation is a pattern of stars; there are eighty-eight official ones, and cultures have their own stories about them. The stars in a constellation are not really close together. The Southern Cross and the Pointers help us find south.
