# Part 1 — Expert

This session leaves the Solar System behind and looks at the vast star system it belongs to: the Milky Way Galaxy. The anchor is a winter night in the Karoo, far from city lights, after the Moon has set. Across the sky, from horizon to horizon, stretches a faint, glowing band, patchy and split by dark lanes, brightest and widest towards the constellation Sagittarius, high overhead. The |xam San people of the Karoo told that a girl threw wood ashes and embers into the sky so that people could find their way home at night; the ashes became this band and the embers became stars. Through even a small pair of binoculars, the glow breaks up into countless faint stars. The governing idea is that the Sun is one ordinary star among hundreds of billions in a huge spiral-shaped system of stars, gas and dust held together by gravity; that we live inside its flat disc, so we see it edge-on as a band across the sky; and that the whole galaxy rotates, carrying the Sun around its centre. The nature of a galaxy, the shape and size of the Milky Way, the Sun's position within it, and the role of gravity in holding it together are examinable, and they prepare the ground for distances in light years and for the galaxies beyond our own later in this topic.

## Subtopic: What a Galaxy Is

A galaxy is an enormous collection of stars, together with gas, dust and dark matter, held together by gravity. Galaxies are the largest single structures made of stars in the Universe. Each star in a galaxy is moving in an orbit around the galaxy's centre, just as each planet in the Solar System orbits the Sun, but on a vastly larger scale.

Our galaxy is called the Milky Way. The name comes from the ancient Greeks and Romans, who saw the band of light as a stream of spilt milk; the word galaxy itself comes from the Greek word for milk. Many cultures have their own names and stories. To the |xam it was the path of ashes and embers, and other South African communities have their own names and stories for it. Across cultures, people noticed that the band was in the same place among the stars every year and used it to tell time and seasons.

In 1610 the Italian scientist Galileo Galilei turned one of the first telescopes to the Milky Way and discovered that its glow was made of a vast number of faint stars, too dim and too close together to be seen separately with the naked eye. That was the first proof that the band is made of stars.

The Milky Way contains somewhere between one hundred billion and four hundred billion stars; astronomers cannot count them directly and estimate the number from the galaxy's mass and brightness. It also contains huge clouds of gas, mostly hydrogen, and dust, from which new stars are still forming. The dark lanes we see in the band are not gaps in the stars but clouds of dust that block the light of the stars behind them. The dark patch near the Southern Cross, known as the Coalsack, is one of these dust clouds.

The Sun is one of those hundreds of billions of stars. It is not special in size or brightness; many stars are larger and brighter, and most are smaller and dimmer. Most of the stars we see at night with the naked eye are relatively nearby members of the Milky Way.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of a galaxy, what holds a galaxy together, the origin of the name Milky Way, what Galileo discovered, the number of stars in the galaxy, and the cause of the dark lanes.

## Subtopic: The Shape of the Milky Way

The Milky Way is a spiral galaxy. Seen from above, it would look like a giant, slowly turning pinwheel: a bright, bulging centre surrounded by a flat disc in which several curved arms of stars, gas and dust wind outward. Astronomers now know that the central part is stretched into a bar shape, so the Milky Way is more precisely a barred spiral galaxy.

The disc is enormous but very thin. It is about one hundred thousand light years across, while the main part of the disc is only about one thousand light years thick. A light year, which we study fully later in this topic, is the distance light travels in one year, about 9.5 trillion kilometres. If the Milky Way's disc were shrunk to the size of a dinner plate, it would be thinner than a sheet of paper in most places, with a small bulge in the middle.

The spiral arms are the brightest parts of the disc because they contain many young, hot, blue stars and glowing clouds of gas where stars are being born. The arms are not solid objects: stars move into and out of them as they orbit, like cars moving through a slow traffic jam on a highway.

At the very centre of the galaxy lies a supermassive black hole called Sagittarius A star, with a mass about four million times that of the Sun. Astronomers discovered it by tracking the orbits of stars that race around an invisible object at the centre at thousands of kilometres per second. In 2022, the Event Horizon Telescope collaboration released the first image of the shadow of this black hole.

The galaxy is surrounded by a roughly spherical halo, containing old stars, about one hundred and fifty tight, ball-shaped groups of stars called globular clusters, and a great deal of invisible dark matter. The dark matter is detected only by its gravity, and there is several times more of it than ordinary matter. Without its pull, the outer parts of the galaxy would be rotating too fast to stay together.

The questions for this section are with you now: the type of galaxy the Milky Way is, the parts of a spiral galaxy, the size and thickness of the disc, what is found in the spiral arms, and what lies at the centre.

## Subtopic: Where the Sun Is

The Sun is located in the disc of the Milky Way, about twenty-six thousand light years from the centre, roughly halfway out towards the edge. It lies near a small arm, or spur, called the Orion Arm, between two larger spiral arms. We are not at the centre of the galaxy, any more than the Earth is at the centre of the Solar System.

Because we live inside the flat disc, we cannot see its shape from outside. When we look along the plane of the disc, in any direction, we see a huge number of stars stacked up into the distance, which appears as the bright band of the Milky Way across our sky. When we look up or down, out of the disc, we see far fewer stars and look out into the dark of intergalactic space. That is why the Milky Way appears as a band that circles the whole sky, and not as a spiral.

The centre of the galaxy lies in the direction of the constellations Sagittarius and Scorpius. In South Africa, these constellations are high overhead on winter evenings, which is why the Milky Way looks brightest and broadest in winter. However, we cannot see the centre itself with ordinary light, because thick dust clouds in the disc block it. Astronomers study the centre using infrared and radio waves, which pass through dust.

To work out the shape of a galaxy we live inside, astronomers measure the distances and directions of very many stars and gas clouds. Radio telescopes detect the radio signals given off by hydrogen gas throughout the disc, and from their speeds and directions astronomers have mapped the spiral arms. The European Space Agency's Gaia spacecraft has measured the positions and motions of more than a billion stars, producing the most detailed map of our galaxy yet.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Sun's position in the galaxy, its distance from the centre, why the Milky Way appears as a band across the sky, the direction of the galactic centre, and how astronomers map a galaxy from inside.

## Subtopic: Gravity and the Rotating Galaxy

Everything in the Milky Way is held together by gravity, the same force that holds the planets in orbit around the Sun. The combined gravity of all the stars, gas, dust and dark matter pulls each star towards the centre of the galaxy, while each star's speed carries it sideways, so that it travels in an orbit around the centre.

The Sun, carrying the whole Solar System with it, orbits the centre of the Milky Way at about two hundred and thirty kilometres per second. Even at that speed, the orbit is so large that one complete trip takes about two hundred and thirty million years, a period sometimes called a galactic year. The last time the Sun was at its present position in its orbit, the first dinosaurs were appearing on Earth. The Sun, about 4.6 billion years old, has completed roughly twenty orbits of the galaxy.

The Milky Way does not travel alone. Two small irregular galaxies, the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds, orbit it. They are easily visible with the naked eye from South Africa as two faint, misty patches in the southern sky, but they cannot be seen from most of the Northern Hemisphere. The Milky Way is also slowly pulling in and tearing apart several even smaller dwarf galaxies.

The nearest large galaxy, Andromeda, about 2.5 million light years away, is approaching the Milky Way. In about four to five billion years the two galaxies are expected to collide and merge into one giant galaxy. Because stars are so far apart compared with their sizes, almost no stars would actually crash into each other.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the Milky Way is a cloud in the Earth's atmosphere; it is the light of billions of distant stars in our galaxy. Two: saying the Sun is at the centre of the galaxy; it is about twenty-six thousand light years from the centre. Three: saying the dark lanes in the Milky Way are empty space; they are clouds of dust blocking light from stars behind them. Four: saying the Milky Way is the Universe; it is one galaxy among billions. Five: saying the stars in the galaxy are fixed and do not move; all of them, including the Sun, are orbiting the centre.

Layout for marks: define the term, give the fact with its number and unit, then explain the reason, for example, the Milky Way appears as a band because we see the disc edge-on from inside.

The questions for this section are with you now: what holds the galaxy together, the Sun's orbital speed and galactic year, the Magellanic Clouds, the future of the Milky Way and Andromeda, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a pinwheel of stars, a view from inside the plate, and a merry-go-round — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Pinwheel of Stars

On a dark night far from town lights, you can see a faint, glowing band across the sky. That is the Milky Way. The San people said a girl threw ashes and embers into the sky to light the way home. Look at it through binoculars and the glow turns into thousands and thousands of tiny stars.

A galaxy is a huge group of stars, gas and dust, all held together by gravity. Our galaxy is the Milky Way, and it has hundreds of billions of stars. Our Sun is just one of them, an ordinary star.

If you could fly far above it and look down, the Milky Way would look like a giant pinwheel: a bright middle with curved arms spiralling out. That is why we call it a spiral galaxy. It is very flat, like a dinner plate, about one hundred thousand light years across.

The dark patches in the band are not holes; they are clouds of dust hiding the stars behind them.

Keep the pinwheel in mind and try the questions for this part: what a galaxy is, the name of our galaxy, and its shape.

## Subtopic: Inside the Plate

Here is the strange part: we live inside the plate. The Sun is in the flat disc, about halfway from the middle to the edge.

When you look along the plate, you see stars piled up far into the distance, so they look like a glowing band across the sky. When you look up out of the plate, there are far fewer stars. That is why we see the Milky Way as a band, not as a pinwheel. It is like standing in a big crowd: you see heads in every direction around you, but look up and you see sky.

The middle of the galaxy is in the direction of Sagittarius and Scorpius, which are high over South Africa on winter evenings. That is when our Milky Way is brightest. At the very centre is a giant black hole, about four million times the mass of the Sun.

Hold the crowd picture and try this part's questions: where the Sun is, why the Milky Way looks like a band, and when it is brightest from South Africa.

## Subtopic: A Merry-Go-Round of Stars

The galaxy is not standing still. It turns like a huge merry-go-round. Gravity pulls every star towards the middle, and each star moves sideways fast enough to keep going round, just like the planets around the Sun.

The Sun races around the centre at about two hundred and thirty kilometres per second, carrying the Earth with it. Even so, one trip around takes about two hundred and thirty million years.

From South Africa you can also see two small neighbouring galaxies with your naked eye: the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds, two misty patches orbiting our galaxy.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A galaxy is a huge group of stars, gas and dust held together by gravity. The Milky Way is a spiral galaxy with hundreds of billions of stars, about one hundred thousand light years across and very flat. The Sun is in the disc, about twenty-six thousand light years from the centre, so we see the disc edge-on as a band across the sky. Gravity holds it together, and the Sun orbits the centre about once every two hundred and thirty million years.

Hold the pinwheel, the plate and the merry-go-round, and take the final questions of the lesson: what holds the galaxy together, how long the Sun takes to go round, and which galaxies orbit ours.
