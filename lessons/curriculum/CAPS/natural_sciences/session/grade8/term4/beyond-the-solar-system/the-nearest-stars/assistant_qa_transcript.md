### subtopic_1 — The Sun, Our Nearest Star
**Assistant:** If the Sun is just an average star, why does it look so different from the stars at night?

**Tutor:** Only because of distance. The Sun is about 150 million kilometres away; the next nearest star is about 270 000 times further away. Brightness falls with the square of the distance, so if the Sun were moved as far away as Alpha Centauri, it would look about 270 000 times 270 000 times fainter, roughly seventy-five billion times fainter. It would then appear as an ordinary bright star, a little fainter than Alpha Centauri A, which is slightly more luminous than the Sun. Its disc, now about half a degree across, would shrink to a point far too small for any eye to see as a disc. Even the largest telescopes see almost all stars as points; only a few very large, nearby stars, such as Betelgeuse, have been imaged as tiny discs using special techniques. The Sun's daytime appearance also depends on the Earth's atmosphere. Sunlight scattered by air molecules makes the whole sky blue and drowns out the stars, which are still there during the day; a total solar eclipse, when the Moon blocks the Sun, briefly darkens the sky enough for bright stars and planets to appear. Astronauts on the Moon, which has no air, saw a black sky with the Sun blazing in it. So the Sun is a star seen up close, and the stars are suns seen from very far away. This idea was proposed by some ancient Greek thinkers and argued strongly by Giordano Bruno in the 1500s, but it was only proved in 1838, when astronomers first measured the distance to a star and showed that it must be giving off about as much light as a sun.

### subtopic_2 — Alpha Centauri, the Nearest Neighbours
**Assistant:** How did astronomers first measure the distance to Alpha Centauri?

**Tutor:** By a method called parallax, and one of the first successful measurements was made at the Cape of Good Hope. Hold up a finger at arm's length and look at it with one eye closed, then the other. The finger seems to jump against the background, because each eye sees it from a slightly different position. The closer the finger, the bigger the jump. Astronomers do the same with stars, using the Earth's orbit as the two eyes. They measure a nearby star's position against much more distant background stars, and six months later, when the Earth is on the opposite side of the Sun, about 300 million kilometres away from its first position, they measure it again. A nearby star seems to shift very slightly. Knowing the width of the Earth's orbit and the angle of the shift, they can calculate the distance using geometry. The shifts are extremely small: for Alpha Centauri, the nearest bright star, the parallax angle is about three quarters of a second of arc, roughly the angle covered by a two-rand coin seen from about six kilometres away. In 1832 and 1833, Thomas Henderson, working at the Royal Observatory at the Cape of Good Hope, made measurements of Alpha Centauri that showed this tiny shift. Friedrich Bessel in Germany published the first stellar parallax, for a star called 61 Cygni, in 1838, and Henderson published his result for Alpha Centauri shortly afterwards in 1839. Today, the Gaia spacecraft measures parallaxes for over a billion stars, with a precision thousands of times better than these first efforts.

### subtopic_3 — How Far Away Are the Stars?
**Assistant:** Could we ever travel to Alpha Centauri?

**Tutor:** Not with any spacecraft we have today, though scientists have proposed ways that tiny probes might reach it within a human lifetime. Our fastest probes, moving at tens of kilometres per second, would take tens of thousands of years, much longer than all of recorded human history. The difficulty is energy: speeding up even a small spacecraft to a meaningful fraction of the speed of light needs enormous energy, and a rocket must carry its own fuel, which itself needs fuel to accelerate. One proposal, called Breakthrough Starshot, suggests building thousands of tiny probes, each weighing only a few grams, attached to very thin, very light sails. A huge array of powerful lasers on the Earth would push on the sails with light for a few minutes, accelerating them to about one fifth of the speed of light. At that speed, the trip would take about twenty years, and the probes' signals would take another 4.24 years to return. But there are huge engineering challenges: building the lasers, making sails that would not melt, surviving collisions with dust at that speed, and sending a signal home from such tiny probes. Crewed travel is far harder still; at the speeds of today's spacecraft, a crew would need a ship that could support many generations of people. For now, we explore the nearest stars with telescopes, which is how the planet Proxima Centauri b was found, by detecting the tiny wobble its gravity causes in its star.

### subtopic_4 — Comparing Stars
**Assistant:** Why can't I see the colours of most stars with my eyes?

**Tutor:** Because most stars look too faint for the colour-sensing cells in your eyes to work, so you see them with cells that see only shades of grey. The retina has two main kinds of light-sensitive cells. Cone cells detect colour but need fairly bright light. Rod cells are far more sensitive and work in dim light, but they do not distinguish colours. At night, when you look at faint stars, you are mainly using your rods, so the stars look white. Only the brightest stars send enough light to stimulate the cones, and those are the ones whose colours you can see with the naked eye: the orange-red of Antares in Scorpius, high over South Africa in winter, the red of Betelgeuse and the blue-white of Rigel in Orion in summer, and the golden yellow of Alpha Centauri. Binoculars and telescopes collect more light and make star colours easier to see; a famous example is the pair of stars Albireo, in the northern constellation Cygnus, with one golden and one blue star side by side. Photographs show colours more strongly because a camera can collect light over many seconds. Strangely, there are no green stars to our eyes, even though stars like the Sun give off the most energy in the green part of the spectrum. A star gives off a broad range of colours, and when the eye combines them, a star that peaks in green looks white or yellowish. That is also why the Sun, seen from space, is white, and only looks yellow through the atmosphere, which scatters some of its blue light away.
