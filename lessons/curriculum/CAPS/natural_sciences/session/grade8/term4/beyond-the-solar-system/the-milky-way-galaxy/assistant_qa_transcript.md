### subtopic_1 — What a Galaxy Is
**Assistant:** If the Milky Way has hundreds of billions of stars, why can I see only a few thousand with my eyes?

**Tutor:** Because almost all of those stars are too far away and too faint for the human eye to see one by one. On a perfectly dark, clear night, far from city lights, a person with good eyesight can see roughly two thousand five hundred stars at once in their half of the sky, and perhaps six thousand to nine thousand over the whole sky across the year. Nearly all of these are within a few thousand light years of the Sun, a tiny neighbourhood in a galaxy one hundred thousand light years across. How bright a star looks depends on how much light it gives off and how far away it is. Light spreads out as it travels, so its brightness falls with the square of the distance: a star twice as far away looks four times fainter, and a star a hundred times further away looks ten thousand times fainter. The vast majority of the galaxy's stars are also small, dim red dwarfs, which cannot be seen with the naked eye even when they are close; Proxima Centauri, the nearest star of all, needs a telescope. Dust in the disc blocks much of the light from distant regions too. The unresolved light of millions of distant stars blends together into the soft glow of the Milky Way band, which is why Galileo needed a telescope to show that it is made of stars. In cities, light pollution brightens the sky background so much that only the brightest few dozen stars may be visible, and most people living in Johannesburg or Cape Town today have never seen the Milky Way at all.

### subtopic_2 — The Shape of the Milky Way
**Assistant:** How do astronomers know the Milky Way is a spiral if nobody has ever seen it from outside?

**Tutor:** They use several kinds of evidence, much as you could work out the layout of a forest while standing inside it. First, they look at other galaxies. Many nearby galaxies, seen from outside, are flat spirals, and the Milky Way's band across the sky is exactly what you would expect to see from inside such a flat disc. Second, they use radio telescopes to map the hydrogen gas in the disc. Cold hydrogen gives off radio waves at a wavelength of twenty-one centimetres, which pass easily through the dust that blocks visible light. Because the gas is moving, the wavelength is slightly shifted, by the Doppler effect, depending on how fast it is approaching or receding. From these shifts and the known rotation of the galaxy, astronomers worked out the distances of gas clouds in different directions, and the clouds turned out to be concentrated in curved arms. Third, they measure distances to young stars and star-forming regions, which mark the spiral arms in other galaxies. The Gaia spacecraft has measured precise distances to more than a billion stars by measuring the tiny shifts in their positions as the Earth orbits the Sun. Infrared surveys, which see through dust, have revealed the bar of stars at the centre. Putting all of this together gives a map of a barred spiral with several major arms, though astronomers still debate exactly how many arms there are and how they are shaped, because mapping a galaxy from inside is genuinely difficult. South Africa's MeerKAT radio telescope has produced some of the sharpest radio images of the galactic centre.

### subtopic_3 — Where the Sun Is
**Assistant:** How can there be a black hole at the centre of our galaxy if black holes swallow everything near them?

**Tutor:** Because a black hole's gravity is no stronger than that of any other object with the same mass, except very close to it. A black hole is a region where so much mass is squeezed into so small a space that nothing, not even light, can escape from within a boundary called the event horizon. Sagittarius A star, at the centre of the Milky Way, has about four million times the mass of the Sun, and its event horizon is about twenty-four million kilometres across, which would fit inside the orbit of Mercury. Outside that tiny region, its gravity behaves like that of any other mass. Stars that orbit it simply travel in orbits, just as planets orbit the Sun; they are not sucked in unless they happen to pass very close. In fact, astronomers discovered the black hole by watching the orbits of stars near the centre over many years. One star, called S2, orbits it every sixteen years, coming within about seventeen light hours of it and reaching speeds of several thousand kilometres per second. From the size and speed of these orbits, Newton's and Einstein's laws give the mass of the central object, and the only thing that could pack so much mass into so small a space is a black hole. Reinhard Genzel and Andrea Ghez shared the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics for this work. The Sun, twenty-six thousand light years away, is in no danger at all; it orbits the centre of the galaxy, which is held together mainly by the gravity of all the stars and dark matter, not by the black hole, which is a tiny fraction of the galaxy's total mass.

### subtopic_4 — Gravity and the Rotating Galaxy
**Assistant:** What will happen to the Earth when the Milky Way and Andromeda collide?

**Tutor:** Almost certainly nothing dramatic to the Earth itself, although the night sky will change completely, and in any case the Sun will have changed the Earth much more by then. Andromeda is approaching the Milky Way at about one hundred and ten kilometres per second, and measurements of its sideways motion by the Hubble Space Telescope and Gaia suggest that the two galaxies will pass close to each other or collide in about four to five billion years; some recent studies suggest there is a real chance they will miss on the first pass, so it is not certain. If they do collide, the stars will almost never hit each other, because the distances between stars are enormous compared with their sizes. If the Sun were the size of a grain of sand, the nearest star would be another grain more than ten kilometres away. Instead, the gravity of each galaxy will pull on the other, flinging stars into new orbits and long streams. Over a few billion years, the two will settle into a single large galaxy, probably a smooth, egg-shaped elliptical galaxy, nicknamed Milkomeda. Gas clouds will collide and bursts of new star formation will occur. The Sun might be moved to a new orbit, further from the centre. The two central black holes will eventually sink to the middle and merge. By then, however, the Sun will be much brighter than today, and in about a billion years from now it will already be hot enough to boil away the Earth's oceans, long before the collision, so any future observers will need to be watching from somewhere else.
