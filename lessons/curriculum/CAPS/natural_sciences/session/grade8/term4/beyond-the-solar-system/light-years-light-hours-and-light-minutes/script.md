# Part 1 — Expert

This session builds the units astronomers use to measure the enormous distances in space: the light second, light minute, light hour and light year. The anchor is a message to Mars. Engineers at a control centre send a command to a rover on Mars telling it to stop before a rock. The command travels as a radio wave at the speed of light, yet when Mars is far away, on the other side of the Sun, the signal takes more than twenty minutes to arrive, and the rover's reply takes another twenty minutes to come back. The rover cannot be steered like a remote-control car; it must be told what to do in advance and must make its own decisions. Even light, the fastest thing there is, takes time to cross space. The governing idea is that distance equals speed multiplied by time, so a distance can be given as the time light takes to travel it; that light travels about 300 000 kilometres every second; and that light minutes and hours suit distances in the Solar System, while light years suit the distances between stars and galaxies. The speed of light, the meaning of light seconds, light minutes, light hours and light years, conversion between these and kilometres, and the use of each unit for suitable distances are examinable, and they lead to wave speed calculations and the electromagnetic spectrum in Grade 10 and 11 Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: The Speed of Light

Light travels through empty space at about 300 000 kilometres per second, written as three times ten to the power five kilometres per second. More precisely, it is 299 792 kilometres per second, but 300 000 is accurate enough for nearly all calculations at this level. Nothing travels faster than light; it is the speed limit of the Universe.

That speed is almost impossible to picture. In one second, light could travel around the Earth at the equator about seven and a half times. A beam of light could cross South Africa from Cape Town to Musina, about 1 600 kilometres, in about five thousandths of a second. Sound in air, by comparison, travels about 340 metres per second, nearly a million times slower, which is why you see lightning before you hear the thunder.

All the kinds of electromagnetic radiation travel at this same speed in empty space: visible light, radio waves used for cellphones and spacecraft communication, infrared, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays. So the signals from spacecraft, the light from stars and the radio waves collected by radio telescopes all travel at the speed of light.

The relationship that connects speed, distance and time is: distance equals speed multiplied by time. If light travels for a certain time, multiplying that time by the speed of light gives the distance. For example, in 5 seconds light travels 5 times 300 000, which is 1 500 000 kilometres. Rearranged, time equals distance divided by speed, so the time light takes to cross a known distance can also be found. The Moon is about 384 000 kilometres away; light takes 384 000 divided by 300 000, about 1.3 seconds, to cross that distance.

The questions for this section are with you now: the speed of light in kilometres per second, why nothing can beat it, comparisons with the speed of sound, which kinds of radiation travel at the speed of light, and the relationship between distance, speed and time.

## Subtopic: Light Seconds, Minutes and Hours

Because light covers a fixed distance in a fixed time, we can use the time it takes as a unit of distance. One light second is the distance light travels in one second, about 300 000 kilometres. The Moon is about 1.3 light seconds from the Earth.

One light minute is the distance light travels in one minute. There are sixty seconds in a minute, so a light minute is 300 000 times 60, which is 18 million kilometres. The Sun is about 150 million kilometres from the Earth; dividing 150 million by 18 million gives about 8.3 light minutes, about eight minutes and twenty seconds.

One light hour is the distance light travels in one hour: sixty light minutes, or 18 million times 60, which is about 1 080 million kilometres, just over a billion kilometres. Saturn is about 1.3 light hours from the Sun, and Neptune about 4.2 light hours.

These units make Solar System distances much easier to compare and remember. Mars ranges from about three light minutes from the Earth at its closest, to about twenty-two light minutes when it is on the far side of the Sun. That is why the Mars rover control signal can take more than twenty minutes. Jupiter is about forty-three light minutes from the Sun. Voyager 1, launched in 1977 and now the most distant human-made object, is about twenty-three and a half light hours away, nearly a whole light day; its radio signals, sent by a transmitter about as powerful as a fridge light bulb, take almost a day to reach the dishes of NASA's Deep Space Network.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of light second, light minute and light hour, converting each to kilometres, the light distances of the Moon, the Sun, Mars and Neptune, and why a Mars rover cannot be steered live.

## Subtopic: The Light Year

The distances to the stars are so large that even light hours become clumsy, so astronomers use the light year. One light year is the distance light travels in one year. It is a unit of distance, not of time, even though its name contains the word year.

To calculate it, multiply the speed of light by the number of seconds in a year. A year has about 365.25 days, each of 24 hours of 3 600 seconds, which is about 31.6 million seconds. Multiplying 300 000 kilometres per second by 31.6 million seconds gives about 9.5 trillion kilometres, that is, nine and a half followed by twelve zeros. So one light year is about 9 500 000 000 000 kilometres.

Light years suit the distances between stars. Proxima Centauri, the nearest star after the Sun, is about 4.24 light years away, which is about 40 trillion kilometres. Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky, is about 8.6 light years away. Betelgeuse in Orion is several hundred light years away. The centre of the Milky Way is about 26 000 light years from the Sun, and the galaxy's disc is about 100 000 light years across. The Andromeda Galaxy is about 2.5 million light years away, and the most distant galaxies seen by telescopes are billions of light years away.

The unit also tells us how long ago the light we see left its source. Light from a star 100 light years away left it 100 years ago. Light from the Andromeda Galaxy, which can just be seen with the naked eye from a very dark site, left it about 2.5 million years ago, when early human ancestors were living in Africa. If a star exploded today, someone 100 light years away would only see the explosion in 100 years' time.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of a light year, why it is a distance and not a time, how to calculate it, its value in kilometres, the distances of Proxima, Sirius, the galactic centre and Andromeda, and what a light year tells us about the past.

## Subtopic: Choosing and Using the Units

Scientists choose a unit that keeps numbers manageable. Kilometres suit distances on Earth and to the Moon; light minutes and light hours suit the Solar System; light years suit stars and galaxies. Using kilometres for star distances is like measuring the distance from Johannesburg to Durban in millimetres: correct, but clumsy.

To convert a light distance into kilometres, multiply the time in seconds by 300 000. For example, Jupiter is about 43 light minutes from the Sun: 43 times 60 is 2 580 seconds, and 2 580 times 300 000 is about 774 million kilometres. To convert kilometres to a light time, divide by 300 000 to get seconds, then divide by 60 for minutes or by 3 600 for hours. For example, Saturn is about 1 430 million kilometres from the Sun; dividing by 300 000 gives about 4 770 seconds, and dividing by 3 600 gives about 1.3 hours.

Layout for marks in calculations: write the formula, distance equals speed times time; substitute the values with units, with the time converted to seconds; calculate; and give the answer with the correct unit. Watch for the most common mistake, forgetting to convert minutes or hours into seconds before multiplying by 300 000 kilometres per second.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying a light year is a length of time; it is a distance. Two: multiplying 300 000 by the number of minutes without converting to seconds; the speed is per second. Three: saying we see stars as they are now; we see them as they were when the light left them. Four: saying sound or radio signals from space arrive instantly; radio waves travel at the speed of light, which still takes time, and sound cannot travel through empty space at all. Five: saying the Sun is eight light years away; it is about eight light minutes away.

The questions for this section are with you now: choosing a suitable unit, converting light minutes to kilometres, converting kilometres to light hours, the steps for a calculation, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a message to Mars, a year of travelling light, and a time machine in the sky — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Message to Mars

Light is the fastest thing there is. It travels about 300 000 kilometres every second. In one second it could go around the Earth more than seven times.

Even so, space is so big that light takes time to cross it. Light from the Moon takes about one and a bit seconds to reach us. Light from the Sun takes about eight minutes.

So we can measure distance by how long light takes to travel it. The Sun is eight light minutes away. A light minute is how far light goes in one minute: eighteen million kilometres.

When Mars is far away, a radio message takes more than twenty minutes to get there. That is why engineers cannot drive a Mars rover like a toy car. It has to think for itself.

Keep the Mars message in mind and try the questions for this part: the speed of light, what a light minute is, and how far away the Sun is in light minutes.

## Subtopic: A Year of Travelling Light

Imagine a beam of light travelling for a whole year without stopping. The distance it covers is called one light year. It is about nine and a half trillion kilometres.

The word year can trick you. A light year is not a time. It is a distance, like a kilometre, just much, much longer.

The nearest star after the Sun is about 4.24 light years away. The middle of our galaxy is about twenty-six thousand light years away. The Andromeda Galaxy is about two and a half million light years away.

Hold the travelling beam in mind and try this part's questions: what a light year is, whether it is a time or a distance, and how far the nearest star is.

## Subtopic: A Time Machine in the Sky

Because light takes time to reach us, we always see things as they were when the light left them. You see the Sun as it was eight minutes ago. You see the nearest star as it was more than four years ago. If you look at the Andromeda Galaxy, you see light that left it two and a half million years ago. Every telescope is a kind of time machine.

Pick the right unit for the job: kilometres on Earth, light minutes and light hours in the Solar System, light years for stars and galaxies.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Light travels about 300 000 kilometres per second. Distance equals speed times time. A light minute is about 18 million kilometres, a light hour about 1 080 million kilometres, and a light year about 9.5 trillion kilometres. The Moon is about 1.3 light seconds away, the Sun about 8 light minutes, Neptune about 4 light hours, and the nearest star about 4.24 light years. A light year is a distance, and looking far into space means looking back in time.

Hold the Mars message, the travelling beam and the time machine, and take the final questions of the lesson: converting between light time and kilometres, choosing units, and why we see the past.
