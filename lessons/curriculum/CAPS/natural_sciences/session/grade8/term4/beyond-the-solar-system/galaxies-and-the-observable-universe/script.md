# Part 1 — Expert

This session looks beyond the Milky Way to the billions of other galaxies and to the size of the Universe we can observe. The anchor is a photograph. In 1995, astronomers pointed the Hubble Space Telescope at a tiny patch of sky in which no stars or galaxies had ever been seen, a patch smaller than a grain of sand held at arm's length, and collected its faint light for ten days. Almost every speck of light in the resulting image, the Hubble Deep Field, turned out to be a whole galaxy, about three thousand of them, some of them billions of light years away. Since that tiny patch is typical, the whole sky must contain an enormous number of galaxies. The governing idea is that the Milky Way is just one of billions of galaxies spread through the Universe; that galaxies come in different shapes and are gathered into groups and clusters; that the Universe is expanding; and that because light travels at a finite speed, there is a limit to how far we can see, which defines the observable Universe. The existence of billions of galaxies, the main types of galaxies, the Local Group, the expansion of the Universe, and the size of the observable Universe are examinable, and they connect with the Big Bang and the history of the Universe in later grades.

## Subtopic: Billions of Galaxies

For most of history, people thought the stars of the Milky Way made up the entire Universe. Astronomers had seen faint, fuzzy patches in the sky, called nebulae, some with spiral shapes, but no one knew whether they were clouds inside our galaxy or separate galaxies far beyond it. In 1920, astronomers held a famous public debate on the question, without settling it.

In 1923, the American astronomer Edwin Hubble used the largest telescope of the time to find individual stars of a known type in the Andromeda Nebula. Measuring their brightness showed that they were far too distant to be part of the Milky Way. Andromeda was a separate galaxy, now known to be about 2.5 million light years away. Overnight, the known Universe grew enormously.

Today astronomers estimate that the observable Universe contains hundreds of billions of galaxies, and some estimates are as high as about two trillion, counting the many small, faint ones. Each of those galaxies contains millions to trillions of stars. The total number of stars in the observable Universe is greater than the number of grains of sand on all the beaches of the Earth.

Deep field images make this concrete. The Hubble Deep Field, and later the Hubble Ultra Deep Field, which showed about ten thousand galaxies in a patch of sky about a tenth of the width of the full Moon, revealed galaxies in every direction. In 2022 the James Webb Space Telescope, observing in infrared, released its first deep field, showing thousands of galaxies in a patch of sky the size of a grain of sand at arm's length, in just twelve and a half hours of observing. Wherever we look deeply enough, the sky is full of galaxies.

Only a few galaxies can be seen with the naked eye. From South Africa, the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds are easy to see on a dark night, and the Andromeda Galaxy can be seen low in the northern sky in spring as a faint smudge, the most distant object most people can see without a telescope.

The questions for this section are with you now: what astronomers once thought the Universe was, Hubble's discovery about Andromeda, the estimated number of galaxies, what deep field images show, and the galaxies visible to the naked eye from South Africa.

## Subtopic: Kinds of Galaxies

Galaxies are classified mainly by their shape. Edwin Hubble sorted them into types that are still used today.

Spiral galaxies, like the Milky Way and Andromeda, have a central bulge and a flat, rotating disc with spiral arms. The arms contain plenty of gas and dust and many young, hot, blue stars, so new stars are still forming. About two thirds of spirals have a bar of stars across their centre, as the Milky Way does; these are called barred spirals.

Elliptical galaxies are smooth, rounded or egg-shaped collections of stars with no disc and no spiral arms. They contain mostly old, reddish stars and very little gas and dust, so few new stars form in them. They range from small dwarf ellipticals to the largest galaxies known, giant ellipticals with trillions of stars, often found at the centres of large clusters of galaxies. Many giant ellipticals are thought to have formed when spiral galaxies collided and merged.

Irregular galaxies have no regular shape. They are usually small and rich in gas, with active star formation. The Large and Small Magellanic Clouds are irregular galaxies, distorted by the gravity of the Milky Way.

Galaxies also differ greatly in size. Dwarf galaxies may contain only a few million stars and be a few thousand light years across; the Milky Way, with hundreds of billions of stars, is about 100 000 light years across; and Andromeda, the largest member of our neighbourhood, is somewhat larger. At the centre of most large galaxies lies a supermassive black hole.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three main types of galaxies, the features of each, which type the Milky Way belongs to, which galaxies form most new stars, and the range of galaxy sizes.

## Subtopic: Groups, Clusters and the Expanding Universe

Galaxies are not scattered evenly through space; gravity gathers them together. The Milky Way belongs to a small group of more than fifty galaxies called the Local Group, about ten million light years across. Its two largest members are Andromeda and the Milky Way, followed by the Triangulum Galaxy; most of the rest are small dwarf galaxies, including the Magellanic Clouds.

Larger collections of hundreds or thousands of galaxies are called clusters. The nearest large cluster, the Virgo Cluster, about 54 million light years away, contains more than a thousand galaxies. Clusters and groups in turn are linked into superclusters, and on the largest scales, galaxies are arranged in a vast web of long filaments and sheets surrounding huge, nearly empty regions called voids, like the walls of bubbles in a foam.

In 1929 Edwin Hubble made a second great discovery. The light from distant galaxies is shifted towards the red end of the spectrum, a sign that they are moving away from us; and the further away a galaxy is, the faster it is moving away. This does not mean we are at the centre. The whole Universe is expanding: space itself is stretching, carrying the galaxies further apart, like raisins in a loaf of raisin bread rising in the oven, where every raisin moves away from every other raisin, and those further apart separate faster. Within groups and clusters, gravity is strong enough to hold galaxies together, which is why Andromeda is approaching us rather than moving away.

If the Universe is expanding, it must have been smaller, denser and hotter in the past. Tracing the expansion backward leads to the Big Bang, the beginning of the Universe's expansion from an extremely hot, dense state about 13.8 billion years ago.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Local Group and its largest members, clusters and superclusters, the cosmic web, Hubble's discovery that the Universe is expanding, the raisin bread model, and the age of the Universe.

## Subtopic: The Size of the Observable Universe

Because light travels at a finite speed and the Universe has a finite age, there is a limit to how far we can see. We can only see objects whose light has had time to reach us since the Universe became transparent. The region of space from which light has reached us is called the observable Universe. It is like standing in a thick forest or on a ship at sea: you can see only as far as your horizon, though the forest or the sea continues beyond it.

The most distant galaxies seen by the James Webb Space Telescope are seen as they were only about three hundred million years after the Big Bang; their light has travelled for more than thirteen and a half billion years. But during that time the Universe has expanded, so those galaxies are now much further away than thirteen and a half billion light years. Taking the expansion into account, the edge of the observable Universe is now about 46 billion light years away in every direction, giving it a diameter of about 93 billion light years.

The whole Universe may be much larger than the observable Universe, perhaps infinite; we simply cannot see beyond our horizon. And because we are at the centre of our own observable Universe, every observer in every galaxy is at the centre of theirs, just as every ship at sea is at the centre of its own horizon.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the Milky Way is the whole Universe; it is one of billions of galaxies. Two: saying the Universe is expanding away from the Earth as its centre; every galaxy sees the others moving away. Three: saying the Big Bang was an explosion at one point in space; it was the beginning of the expansion of space itself, everywhere. Four: saying the observable Universe is about 13.8 billion light years across; the Universe is about 13.8 billion years old, but because of expansion the edge is now about 46 billion light years away. Five: saying all galaxies are spirals; there are spiral, elliptical and irregular galaxies.

Layout for marks: state the fact, give the number with its unit, then explain the reason, for example, the edge of the observable Universe is about 46 billion light years away because space has expanded while the light was travelling.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of the observable Universe, why there is a limit to how far we can see, its size, why it is larger than 13.8 billion light years, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a speck of dust in the sky, spirals, eggs and blobs, and the edge of what we can see — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Universe Full of Galaxies

Hold a grain of sand at arm's length and look at the tiny patch of sky it covers. Astronomers pointed a giant space telescope at a patch like that, one that looked completely empty. When they collected its faint light, they found thousands of galaxies, each with millions or billions of stars.

That is when it really sinks in: our Milky Way is just one galaxy among billions. There may be hundreds of billions of galaxies, maybe even two trillion. There are more stars out there than grains of sand on all the beaches on Earth.

About a hundred years ago, people thought the Milky Way was everything. Then Edwin Hubble showed that the fuzzy patch called Andromeda was a whole separate galaxy, far away.

Keep the grain of sand in mind and try the questions for this part: how many galaxies there are, what Hubble discovered, and which galaxies you can see without a telescope.

## Subtopic: Spirals, Eggs and Blobs

Galaxies come in three main shapes.

Spirals are flat pinwheels with curved arms, full of gas and new stars. Our Milky Way is a spiral.

Ellipticals are smooth and shaped like eggs or balls. They have old, reddish stars and hardly any new ones forming. The biggest galaxies of all are ellipticals.

Irregulars have no neat shape at all, like the Magellanic Clouds that you can see from South Africa.

Galaxies also live in families. Ours is part of the Local Group, more than fifty galaxies held together by gravity. And all the galaxy families are moving apart, because the Universe is getting bigger, like raisins moving apart in rising bread dough.

Hold the three shapes in mind and try this part's questions: the types of galaxies, which type ours is, and what our group is called.

## Subtopic: The Edge of What We Can See

The Universe began expanding about 13.8 billion years ago. Light has only had that long to travel, so we can only see so far. The part of the Universe we can see is called the observable Universe. It is like standing in the middle of the sea: you see only to the horizon, but the sea goes on.

Because space has kept stretching while the light was on its way, the edge of what we can see is now about forty-six billion light years away in every direction, so the observable Universe is about ninety-three billion light years across. The whole Universe may be even bigger.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Milky Way is one of hundreds of billions of galaxies. Galaxies are spiral, elliptical or irregular. They gather in groups and clusters; ours is the Local Group. The Universe is expanding, and it began about 13.8 billion years ago. The observable Universe is the part we can see, about 93 billion light years across.

Hold the grain of sand, the three shapes and the horizon, and take the final questions of the lesson: the expanding Universe, its age, and the size of the observable Universe.
