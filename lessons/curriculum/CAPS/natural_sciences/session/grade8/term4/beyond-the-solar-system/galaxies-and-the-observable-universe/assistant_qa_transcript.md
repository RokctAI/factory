### subtopic_1 — Billions of Galaxies
**Assistant:** If all the galaxies are moving away from us, doesn't that mean we are at the centre of the Universe?

**Tutor:** No. In an expanding Universe, every galaxy sees all the others moving away from it, so every observer would think they are at the centre, and none of them is. Think about a loaf of raisin bread rising in the oven. Pick any raisin and imagine you are standing on it. As the dough expands, every other raisin moves away from you. A raisin that started one centimetre away might end up two centimetres away, while one that started three centimetres away ends up six centimetres away. So the more distant raisin moved away faster, three centimetres in the same time instead of one. That is exactly the pattern Hubble saw: the more distant a galaxy, the faster it recedes. But if you stood on any other raisin, you would see the same pattern. No raisin is special. The one difference with real space is that the raisin bread has an edge and a centre, while the Universe as far as we know has neither: the expansion is happening everywhere at once. Another helpful picture is a balloon with dots drawn on its surface. As it is inflated, every dot moves away from every other dot, and there is no centre on the surface. In both pictures, the galaxies themselves are not rushing through space; the space between them is stretching. Groups held together by gravity, such as the Local Group, and the galaxies, stars and planets within them, do not expand. The Earth is not getting bigger, and neither is the Solar System. The expansion shows up only over the enormous distances between groups and clusters of galaxies.

### subtopic_2 — Kinds of Galaxies
**Assistant:** What does a deep field image actually look like, and how can astronomers tell that the specks are galaxies and not stars?

**Tutor:** A deep field looks like a black background scattered with thousands of small, smudged, coloured shapes: spirals, ovals, blobs, streaks and tiny red dots, with only a handful of sharp, bright points crossed by spikes. Astronomers distinguish galaxies from stars in several ways. Stars in our own galaxy are so far away that even large telescopes see them as points of light; they appear sharp, and in Hubble and Webb images bright stars show distinctive spikes caused by the telescope's structure. Galaxies, though far more distant, are so huge that many appear as extended smudges with visible shapes, such as spiral arms or egg-like outlines. Deep fields are chosen in directions away from the plane of the Milky Way, where there are very few stars in the way, so most objects in them are galaxies. Astronomers also spread the light from each object into a spectrum. A star's spectrum shows the features of a single star, while a galaxy's spectrum is the combined light of billions of stars and glowing gas. In addition, the spectra of distant galaxies are shifted strongly towards the red, by an amount that tells how far the light has travelled. The faintest, reddest specks in the James Webb deep fields are galaxies whose light left them when the Universe was only a few hundred million years old. The colours in deep field images are partly adjusted by scientists, often representing different infrared wavelengths, but the shapes and the redshifts are real measurements.

### subtopic_3 — Groups, Clusters and the Expanding Universe
**Assistant:** What was there before the Big Bang?

**Tutor:** Nobody knows, and it is possible that the question does not have a meaningful answer, although scientists are actively studying it. The Big Bang theory describes how the Universe has expanded and cooled from an extremely hot, dense state about 13.8 billion years ago. It is supported by strong evidence: the expansion measured by Hubble and many astronomers since; a faint glow of microwave radiation that fills the whole sky, called the cosmic microwave background, which is the cooled leftover heat from when the Universe was about 380 000 years old; and the amounts of the lightest elements, hydrogen and helium, which match what the hot early Universe would have produced. But the theory does not describe the very first instant, because our current laws of physics, Einstein's general relativity for gravity and quantum theory for the very small, give conflicting answers under such extreme conditions. Some scientists think time itself began with the Big Bang, in which case asking what came before is a little like asking what is north of the North Pole. Others have proposed ideas in which our Universe arose from a previous phase, or is one of many universes, or began after a brief period of extremely rapid expansion called inflation, of which there is some indirect evidence. None of these ideas has yet been confirmed by observation. It is also important to know that the Big Bang was not an explosion of matter into empty space from a single point; it was the expansion of space itself, happening everywhere at once. This is one of the great open questions in science, and it is possible that a learner in a classroom today will help to answer it.

### subtopic_4 — The Size of the Observable Universe
**Assistant:** If the observable Universe has an edge, what is beyond it?

**Tutor:** Almost certainly more Universe, much like the part we can see, but we cannot observe it, because its light has not yet reached us. The edge of the observable Universe is not a wall or a boundary in space. It is a horizon, set by the distance light can have travelled to us in the 13.8 billion years since the Universe began expanding, together with the stretching of space during that time. Someone living in a galaxy near the edge of our observable Universe would see a sky much like ours, with galaxies in every direction, and would be at the centre of their own observable Universe, which would include regions we cannot see. Observations show that, on very large scales, the Universe looks much the same everywhere and in every direction, so there is no sign that our region is special or that the Universe stops just beyond what we can see. Measurements of the geometry of space, from the cosmic microwave background, show that it is very nearly flat, which means the whole Universe is at least many times larger than the observable part, and may be infinite. There is another strange consequence of expansion: the most distant galaxies are now moving away from us so fast, because of the stretching of space between us, that light they give off today will never reach us. Because the expansion is speeding up, driven by something called dark energy, more and more galaxies will slip beyond our view over billions of years. Astronomers in the very far future, in the merged Milky Way and Andromeda, may see only their own galaxy and darkness beyond.
