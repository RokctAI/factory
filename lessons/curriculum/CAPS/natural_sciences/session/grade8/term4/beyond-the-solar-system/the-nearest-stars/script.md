# Part 1 — Expert

This session meets the two nearest stars to the Earth: the Sun and the Alpha Centauri system. The anchor is the Southern Cross, the small kite of four bright stars that appears on many sky maps and on the flags of several southern countries, and that every South African can learn to find. Beside it shine two bright stars called the Pointers, because a line through them points to the Cross. The brighter of the two Pointers, low in the south on autumn evenings, looks like one golden star. It is in fact a family of three stars, and one of its members is the nearest star to us after the Sun. Its light, arriving tonight, left it more than four years ago. The governing idea is that the Sun is a star, and the nearest one by far; that every other star is a distant sun; and that even the nearest of them is so far away that ordinary units such as kilometres become clumsy, which is why astronomers measure distances by the time light takes to travel them. The Sun as our nearest star, the Alpha Centauri system as the nearest stars beyond the Sun, the enormous distances between stars, and the comparison of stars by size, temperature and brightness are examinable, and they lead directly to light years and to galaxies in the next sessions.

## Subtopic: The Sun, Our Nearest Star

The nearest star to the Earth is the Sun. That sentence surprises people who think of stars as tiny points of light at night, but the Sun is a star in every way: a huge ball of hot gas, mostly hydrogen and helium, that produces its own heat and light by nuclear reactions in its core, where hydrogen is fused into helium. It looks so different from the stars at night only because it is so much closer.

The Sun is about 150 million kilometres from the Earth, and its light takes about eight minutes and twenty seconds to reach us. We say the Sun is about eight light minutes away. If the Sun suddenly disappeared, we would not know for eight minutes; the sunlight already on its way would keep arriving.

Compared with other stars, the Sun is middle-sized and of middle temperature. Its diameter is about 1.4 million kilometres, about 109 times that of the Earth, and its surface temperature is about 5 500 degrees Celsius. Astronomers classify it as a yellow dwarf star. Some stars are hundreds of times wider and thousands of times brighter; but most stars in the galaxy, the red dwarfs, are smaller, cooler and much dimmer than the Sun.

Because the Sun is so close, it is the only star whose surface we can study in detail. We can see sunspots, cooler dark patches on its surface, and flares and huge loops of glowing gas. Never look at the Sun directly, even briefly, or through binoculars or a telescope: its light can permanently damage the retina. Astronomers use special filters or project its image onto a screen.

The questions for this section are with you now: why the Sun is a star, what makes it shine, its distance in kilometres and in light minutes, its size and temperature compared with other stars, and how to observe it safely.

## Subtopic: Alpha Centauri, the Nearest Neighbours

The nearest stars to the Sun belong to the Alpha Centauri system, in the southern constellation Centaurus. To the naked eye, Alpha Centauri appears as a single bright, golden star, the third brightest star in the night sky and the brighter of the two Pointers to the Southern Cross. It cannot be seen from most of Europe or North America; it is a southern sky treasure, visible from South Africa all year.

Through a telescope, Alpha Centauri splits into two stars, called Alpha Centauri A and Alpha Centauri B, which orbit each other about every eighty years. Alpha Centauri A is a yellow star a little larger and brighter than the Sun; Alpha Centauri B is a slightly smaller, more orange star. Both are about 4.37 light years from us, which means their light takes about four years and four months to reach the Earth.

The third member is a small, dim red dwarf called Proxima Centauri, from the Latin word for nearest. It is about 4.24 light years away, which makes it the nearest star to the Sun of all. Proxima is far too faint to see without a telescope; it has only about an eighth of the Sun's mass. It was discovered in 1915 by Robert Innes, the director of the Union Observatory in Johannesburg, who noticed that it moves across the sky together with Alpha Centauri A and B. Proxima orbits the other two at a distance of about thirteen thousand times the Earth-Sun distance, taking hundreds of thousands of years for each orbit.

In 2016, astronomers discovered a planet orbiting Proxima Centauri, called Proxima Centauri b, slightly more massive than the Earth and lying in the star's habitable zone. Whether it has an atmosphere or water is not known; Proxima produces powerful flares that may have stripped any atmosphere away.

The questions for this section are with you now: the nearest star system to the Sun, the members of the Alpha Centauri system, which is the nearest star, their distances in light years, how to find Alpha Centauri in the sky, and who discovered Proxima.

## Subtopic: How Far Away Are the Stars?

The distance to Proxima Centauri is about 40 trillion kilometres, that is forty followed by twelve zeros. Numbers like this are almost impossible to imagine, so a scale model helps.

Shrink the Sun to the size of a soccer ball, about twenty-two centimetres across, placed on a field in Johannesburg. On that scale, the Earth is a grain of sand about two millimetres across, about twenty-four metres away, and Neptune is about seven hundred metres away. The nearest star, another soccer ball and its companions, would be about six thousand five hundred kilometres away, roughly as far as Cairo in Egypt. Between them there is almost nothing at all. Space between stars is overwhelmingly empty.

Another way to grasp the distance is by travel time. The Voyager 1 spacecraft, one of the fastest objects people have ever launched, moves at about seventeen kilometres per second, fast enough to cross South Africa in under two minutes. Even so, it would take about seventy-five thousand years to travel as far as Proxima Centauri, and Voyager is not even heading in that direction.

Light, the fastest thing there is, travels about 300 000 kilometres per second. It crosses from the Sun to the Earth in about eight minutes, but takes about 4.24 years to reach us from Proxima. This is why astronomers describe star distances by the time light takes to cover them: the Moon is about 1.3 light seconds away, the Sun about eight light minutes, Neptune about four light hours, and Proxima Centauri about 4.24 light years. Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky, is about 8.6 light years away.

Because light takes time to travel, we always see stars as they were when the light left them. We see Alpha Centauri as it was more than four years ago, and more distant stars as they were hundreds or thousands of years ago. Looking out into space is looking back in time.

The questions for this section are with you now: the distance to Proxima Centauri in kilometres, the scale model with the Sun as a soccer ball, the travel time for a spacecraft, the speed of light and the light distances of the Moon, Sun, Neptune and Proxima, and why looking at stars is looking into the past.

## Subtopic: Comparing Stars

Stars differ in colour, temperature, size and brightness. The colour of a star tells us its surface temperature, just as a piece of metal heated in a fire glows red, then orange, then yellow-white, then bluish-white as it gets hotter. Red stars are the coolest, with surface temperatures of about three thousand degrees Celsius; yellow stars like the Sun and Alpha Centauri A are about five and a half thousand degrees; blue-white stars are hottest, from ten thousand to more than thirty thousand degrees. On a clear night you can see colours with the naked eye: Antares in Scorpius and Betelgeuse in Orion are red, Rigel in Orion is blue-white.

How bright a star looks from the Earth depends on two things: how much light it actually gives off, and how far away it is. A dim star nearby can look brighter than a very powerful star far away. The Sun is the brightest object in our sky only because it is so close. Alpha Centauri A looks bright partly because it is near. The other Pointer, Beta Centauri, looks almost as bright as Alpha Centauri, yet it is about ninety times further away; it gives off thousands of times more light than the Sun.

Stars also vary enormously in size. Red dwarfs like Proxima are smaller than the Sun; giant stars such as Betelgeuse are so large that, placed where the Sun is, they would swallow the orbits of Mercury, Venus, the Earth and Mars.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the Sun is not a star; it is a star, and the nearest one. Two: saying the brightest stars at night are the nearest; brightness depends on both the light given off and the distance. Three: saying Alpha Centauri is one star; it is a system of three stars. Four: saying a light year is a measure of time; it is a distance, the distance light travels in a year. Five: saying red stars are the hottest because red means hot; red stars are the coolest, and blue-white stars are the hottest.

Layout for marks: name the star, give its distance with the unit, then state one property, for example, Proxima Centauri, about 4.24 light years away, a small red dwarf, the nearest star to the Sun.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a star's colour tells us, the two factors that decide how bright a star looks, the example of Beta Centauri, the range of star sizes, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through our own star, the pointer next door, and a very long trip — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Our Own Star

Here is a surprise: the nearest star is the Sun. It is a giant ball of hot gas that makes its own heat and light, just like the stars you see at night. It only looks so big and bright because it is so close.

The Sun is about 150 million kilometres away. Its light takes about eight minutes to reach us, so we say it is about eight light minutes away.

The Sun is a middle-sized yellow star. Some stars are much bigger and brighter, but most stars are smaller and dimmer.

Never look straight at the Sun, not even for a moment, and never through binoculars. It can damage your eyes forever.

Keep the idea that the Sun is a star in mind and try the questions for this part: the nearest star, how far its light travels, and how to stay safe.

## Subtopic: The Pointer Next Door

Find the Southern Cross, the little kite of four stars in the southern sky. Next to it are two bright stars called the Pointers. The brighter one is Alpha Centauri.

It looks like one star, but it is really three. Two of them, Alpha Centauri A and B, are like our Sun and circle each other. The third, Proxima Centauri, is a small, dim red star that is the nearest star to us after the Sun, about 4.24 light years away. It was discovered from Johannesburg in 1915.

That means the light you see tonight from Alpha Centauri left it more than four years ago.

Hold the Pointers in mind and try this part's questions: what Alpha Centauri really is, the name of the nearest star, and how far it is.

## Subtopic: A Very Long Trip

Imagine the Sun shrunk to a soccer ball in Johannesburg. The Earth would be a grain of sand about twenty-four metres away. The nearest star would be another soccer ball about six and a half thousand kilometres away, about as far as Cairo. In between: almost nothing.

Our fastest spacecraft would take about seventy-five thousand years to get there. Light, the fastest thing there is, takes just over four years.

Stars also come in colours. Red stars are the coolest, yellow stars like the Sun are in the middle, and blue-white stars are the hottest. A star can look bright because it really is powerful, or just because it is close.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Sun is a star, and the nearest one, about eight light minutes away. The nearest stars after the Sun are in the Alpha Centauri system, three stars about 4.2 to 4.4 light years away, with Proxima Centauri the closest. Space between stars is mostly empty and the distances are enormous. Star colour shows temperature, and how bright a star looks depends on how much light it gives and how far away it is.

Hold the soccer ball, the Pointers and the long trip, and take the final questions of the lesson: the scale of star distances, the colours of stars, and why a star can look bright.
