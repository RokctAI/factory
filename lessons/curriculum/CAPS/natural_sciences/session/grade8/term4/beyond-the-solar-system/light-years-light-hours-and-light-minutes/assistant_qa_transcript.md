### subtopic_1 — The Speed of Light
**Assistant:** How did scientists first measure the speed of light if it is so fast?

**Tutor:** The first good estimate came from watching Jupiter's moons, and later measurements used spinning mirrors and wheels on Earth. In the 1670s, the Danish astronomer Ole Rømer was timing the eclipses of Io, one of Jupiter's moons, which disappears into Jupiter's shadow every forty-two and a half hours. He noticed that the eclipses came earlier than predicted when the Earth was on the same side of the Sun as Jupiter, and later when the Earth was on the far side, by up to about twenty minutes in total. He realised that the light from Io had further to travel when the Earth was further from Jupiter, and that the extra delay was the time light took to cross the width of the Earth's orbit. Using the size of the orbit as then known, Christiaan Huygens calculated a speed of about 220 000 kilometres per second, of the right size though too low, because the orbit had been underestimated and the delay overestimated. In 1849, the French physicist Hippolyte Fizeau sent a beam of light through the gaps of a rapidly spinning toothed wheel to a mirror about eight kilometres away and back. At a certain speed of the wheel, the returning light was blocked by the next tooth, and from the wheel's speed he calculated the speed of light at about 313 000 kilometres per second. Later experiments with rotating mirrors became much more accurate. Today the speed of light in a vacuum is defined as exactly 299 792 458 metres per second, and the metre itself is defined as the distance light travels in 1 divided by 299 792 458 of a second.

### subtopic_2 — Light Seconds, Minutes and Hours
**Assistant:** If light from the Sun takes eight minutes, does that mean the sunset I see has already happened?

**Tutor:** Yes, in a sense, though the details are interesting. When you watch the Sun touch the horizon, you are seeing light that left the Sun about eight minutes and twenty seconds earlier. During those eight minutes the Earth has kept turning, so the Sun's true direction has moved on by about two degrees, roughly four times the width of the Sun's disc. But that is not quite the whole story, because the light leaving the Sun is also coming from where the Sun was relative to us, and the Earth's own motion slightly changes the apparent direction, an effect called aberration. For sunset, there is another, larger effect: the Earth's atmosphere bends, or refracts, the sunlight, lifting the image of the Sun by about half a degree near the horizon. So when you see the bottom of the Sun just touching the horizon, the real Sun is already about half a degree below it, as if it has already partly set. The atmosphere also squashes the Sun into an oval shape at sunset because the lower edge is bent upward more than the upper edge. In practical terms, if the Sun suddenly stopped shining, the sky would stay bright for eight minutes and twenty seconds before going dark, and the Earth would keep orbiting the spot where the Sun had been for the same time, because changes in gravity also travel at the speed of light, as gravitational wave detectors have confirmed. So you always see the Sun as it was, a little in the past.

### subtopic_3 — The Light Year
**Assistant:** If the Andromeda Galaxy is 2.5 million light years away, how do we know the distance?

**Tutor:** By finding stars in Andromeda whose true brightness we know and comparing it with how bright they look. Distances in space are measured with a chain of methods, each one checked against the one before. For nearby stars, parallax, the small shift in a star's position as the Earth orbits the Sun, gives the distance directly by geometry. That works only out to a few thousand light years. For greater distances, astronomers use standard candles, objects whose true light output is known. The most famous are Cepheid variable stars, giant stars that brighten and dim regularly over days or weeks. In 1912, the American astronomer Henrietta Leavitt discovered that the longer a Cepheid's period of brightening and dimming, the more light it actually gives off. So measuring a Cepheid's period tells astronomers its true brightness, and comparing that with how bright it looks, using the rule that brightness falls with the square of distance, gives its distance. Nearby Cepheids were calibrated with parallax. In 1923, Edwin Hubble found Cepheids in Andromeda and showed that it was far beyond the edge of the Milky Way, proving that it was a separate galaxy. Modern measurements put it at about 2.5 million light years. Each light year is about 9.5 trillion kilometres, so the distance is about 24 billion billion kilometres, a number so large that light years are by far the most sensible unit.

### subtopic_4 — Choosing and Using the Units
**Assistant:** Why can't anything travel faster than light?

**Tutor:** Because of how space, time, energy and mass are connected, as described by Albert Einstein's theory of special relativity in 1905. Experiments have shown that the speed of light in a vacuum is the same for every observer, no matter how fast they are moving. That seems impossible by everyday logic: if you are in a car moving at 100 kilometres per hour and throw a ball forward at 20, the ball moves at 120 relative to the road. But light does not add up that way. If you shine a torch forward from a fast spacecraft, the light leaves at 300 000 kilometres per second relative to you and also at 300 000 relative to someone standing still. For this to be true, time and distance themselves must change for moving objects: moving clocks run slower and moving objects become shorter in the direction of motion, effects measured in experiments and allowed for in the GPS satellites in our cellphones. A consequence is that as an object with mass speeds up towards the speed of light, it takes more and more energy to make it go even slightly faster. Reaching the speed of light would need an infinite amount of energy. In particle accelerators such as the Large Hadron Collider, protons are pushed to 99.999999 percent of the speed of light, but never beyond it. Light itself, made of particles called photons that have no mass, always travels at exactly that speed. So the speed of light is not just a record; it is built into the structure of the Universe.
