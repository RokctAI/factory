### subtopic_1 — Decomposers: Nature's Recyclers
**Assistant:** Why do mummies and frozen mammoths survive for thousands of years when a dead bird rots in weeks?

**Tutor:** Because decomposers are living organisms, and like all living things they need water, a suitable temperature and, for most of them, oxygen. Remove any one of those and decomposition slows almost to a stop. A dead bird lying on damp ground in summer offers bacteria and fungi everything they want: moisture in its tissues, warmth, air, and a rich supply of protein and fat. They multiply rapidly, the enzymes they release break the tissues down, and within weeks only feathers and bones remain. Now consider the cases that survive. Egyptian mummies were deliberately dried with a salt called natron, which drew the water out of the body; without water, bacterial cells cannot grow or divide, and the body is preserved. Bodies have also been preserved naturally in hot deserts for the same reason. Mammoths found in the frozen ground of Siberia were kept at temperatures far below freezing, where the water is ice and the enzymes of micro-organisms are too cold to work. Bodies preserved in peat bogs in northern Europe survive because the waterlogged bog has almost no oxygen and is acidic, conditions in which few decomposers can live. Each case removes a condition the decomposers need. This is exactly the science behind food preservation: we dry fruit and make biltong, we freeze meat, we can and vacuum-pack food, and we pickle in acid or salt, all to stop the same decomposers that rot a dead bird from rotting our food. The ability to stop decomposition and the ability to speed it up in a compost heap come from the same knowledge.

### subtopic_2 — Micro-organisms in Food
**Assistant:** If bacteria turn milk sour when it goes off, why is sour yoghurt safe while sour milk can make us sick?

**Tutor:** Because the difference lies in which bacteria are doing the work and whether the process was controlled. When yoghurt is made, the milk is first heated to about eighty-five degrees, which kills most of the bacteria already in it, including any harmful ones. Only then is a starter added, containing two particular kinds of lactic acid bacteria that are known to be safe. Kept warm, these multiply quickly, and within hours they produce so much lactic acid that the yoghurt becomes too acidic for most harmful bacteria to grow. The good bacteria get a head start and then make conditions hostile to the competition. Milk that goes off by accident has no such control. Whatever bacteria happened to be in the milk or fell into it from the air, a dirty jug or someone's hands grow together, and they may include bacteria that produce toxins or cause disease. Some of the souring is also due to lactic acid bacteria, which is why spoiled milk and yoghurt can smell a bit alike, but there is no guarantee that harmful ones have been kept out. Traditional amasi relies on the lactic acid bacteria naturally present winning that race, which they usually do in clean containers that have been used for amasi before and carry the right bacteria; the acidity they create protects the amasi. This is why hygiene matters so much in traditional fermentation, and why the heating step and the clean starter are the heart of the yoghurt method rather than optional extras. The rule is that controlled fermentation by known organisms produces safe food, while uncontrolled spoilage is a gamble.

### subtopic_3 — Micro-organisms in Medicine: Penicillin and Beyond
**Assistant:** Why would a mould produce a substance that kills bacteria in the first place?

**Tutor:** Because in the soil and on decaying material, moulds and bacteria compete for the same food, and a chemical weapon gives the mould an advantage. Penicillium lives on rotting fruit, bread and other organic matter, exactly the kind of food that bacteria also feed on. If the mould releases a substance that stops bacteria from building their cell walls, the bacteria nearby burst or stop growing, and the mould has more food and space for itself. That is what Fleming saw on his dish: a clear zone around the mould where the bacteria could not survive. Many micro-organisms have evolved such weapons, which is why soil turned out to be the richest source of antibiotics. Streptomycin, the first drug that worked against TB, came from a soil bacterium found in the 1940s, and many of the antibiotics in use today were found by testing soil organisms for substances that kill bacteria. The same competition explains antibiotic resistance. Bacteria have been living alongside antibiotic-producing moulds and bacteria for millions of years, and some have evolved ways to survive, such as enzymes that break penicillin down. When people use antibiotics on a massive scale, those resistant bacteria are favoured and their numbers grow. So the very arms race that produced penicillin in nature is now being played out in hospitals. Scientists continue to search soils, seas and even insects for new antibiotics, because the old ones lose ground every year. The lesson for each of us is to use antibiotics only for bacterial infections when they are prescribed, and to finish the course, so that this weapon stays useful.

### subtopic_4 — More Useful Microbes and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why do farmers plant beans in a field before maize instead of just adding fertiliser?

**Tutor:** Because beans bring their own nitrogen factory, and using it is cheaper and kinder to the soil and rivers than buying all the nitrogen in a bag. Maize needs a great deal of nitrogen to make the proteins in its leaves and grain, and without enough it grows short and yellow. The air is about seventy-eight percent nitrogen, but that nitrogen gas is very unreactive, and neither maize nor any plant can use it directly. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria can. In the roots of beans, groundnuts, soya, cowpeas and other legumes, they live inside small swellings called root nodules, converting nitrogen gas into nitrogen compounds that the plant uses to build proteins; the plant feeds them sugar in return. When the bean crop is harvested, its roots and leftover stems are left in the soil, and as decomposers break them down, the nitrogen compounds are released into the soil for the next crop. Maize planted afterwards finds more nitrogen waiting for it. This practice is called crop rotation, and farmers in South Africa and around the world have used it for centuries; intercropping, growing beans and maize together, is a traditional version. Factory-made fertiliser does work, but it costs money, its manufacture uses a lot of energy, and fertiliser that washes off fields into rivers feeds the algal blooms that strip oxygen from water and kill fish, which we met in the ecology lessons. So legumes and their bacteria offer a way to feed the soil that also reduces pollution. Many farmers use both, applying less fertiliser after a legume crop than they otherwise would.
