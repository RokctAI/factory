### subtopic_1 — What Makes Something a Micro-organism
**Assistant:** Why can a school microscope show bacteria but never a virus, however good the lenses are?

**Tutor:** Because a light microscope is limited not only by the quality of its glass but by the light itself. To see two points as separate, the light used must have a wavelength small enough to fit between them. Visible light has wavelengths of roughly four hundred to seven hundred nanometres, and that sets a hard limit: a light microscope cannot separate details much smaller than about two hundred nanometres, no matter how perfect the lenses or how many are stacked together. A bacterium about two thousand nanometres long is well above that limit, so it shows up, although only as a small dot or rod with little detail. A yeast cell or a protist is larger still and shows its nucleus and other structures. Most viruses are between twenty and three hundred nanometres across, at or below the limit, so at best they would be an unresolvable blur and in practice they are invisible. The electron microscope solves this by replacing light with a beam of electrons, which behave like waves with a far shorter wavelength, and by using magnets instead of glass lenses to focus them. That allows magnifications of hundreds of thousands of times and reveals the shapes of individual viruses, the protein coats, even the spikes on a coronavirus. This is why viruses were suspected long before they were seen: in the 1890s scientists knew something smaller than any bacterium was passing through filters and causing disease in tobacco plants, but nobody saw a virus until electron microscopes were built in the 1930s.

### subtopic_2 — Viruses and Bacteria
**Assistant:** If viruses cannot feed or grow on their own, should we call them living things at all?

**Tutor:** Scientists genuinely disagree, and the disagreement is useful because it forces us to say what we mean by living. The usual checklist of life processes includes feeding, respiring, growing, excreting, responding, moving and reproducing, and a living thing is normally made of at least one cell. A bacterium ticks every box: it is a cell, it takes in food, releases energy by respiration, grows, and divides by itself. A virus fails most of the list. It is not a cell, it has no way of releasing energy, it does not grow, and outside a host it does nothing at all; a virus particle on a door handle is closer to a chemical than to an organism. Yet a virus does have one key property of life: it carries genetic instructions and, inside a host cell, those instructions are copied and passed on, sometimes with changes, which means viruses evolve. That is why new strains of influenza appear every year and why the coronavirus produced new variants during the pandemic. So the honest answer is that viruses sit on the boundary. Many textbooks call them non-living particles that become active only inside living cells; others treat them as the simplest form of life. For Grade 8 the examinable points are clear whichever label you prefer: a virus is not a cell, it consists of genetic material in a protein coat, and it can reproduce only inside a living host cell. Writing those three facts earns the marks; arguing about the label does not lose them, provided the facts are right.

### subtopic_3 — Protists and Fungi
**Assistant:** Why are fungi not classified as plants, when mushrooms grow in the ground like plants do?

**Tutor:** Because the features that matter for classification are structure and feeding, not where something grows, and on both counts fungi are very different from plants. Plants have chlorophyll and make their own food by photosynthesis; fungi have no chlorophyll and cannot photosynthesise at all, which is why mushrooms grow happily in dark forests and in the dark cellars of mushroom farms. Plants take in carbon dioxide and build sugars; fungi release digestive enzymes onto dead wood, leaves or food and absorb the dissolved nutrients, which is closer to how animals feed, except that the digestion happens outside the body. Plant cell walls are made of cellulose; fungal cell walls are made of chitin, the same tough substance found in the outer skeletons of insects. Plants store food as starch; fungi store it as glycogen, as animals do. And the body plan is different. A plant has roots, stems and leaves; a fungus is mostly a hidden network of thin threads, the hyphae, running through its food, with the mushroom being only the temporary spore-producing part that pushes up into the air. When scientists compare the genetic material of living things, fungi turn out to be more closely related to animals than to plants. So fungi are placed in a kingdom of their own. In practical terms this matters because their role in ecosystems is different: plants are producers, while most fungi are decomposers, and together with bacteria they are responsible for returning the nutrients in dead material to the soil.

### subtopic_4 — Comparing the Four Groups
**Assistant:** Why do doctors worry when people take antibiotics they do not need?

**Tutor:** Because every unnecessary dose gives bacteria practice at surviving antibiotics, and the survivors are the ones that multiply. In any large population of bacteria, a few individuals happen to carry small differences that let them withstand a particular antibiotic slightly better. When the antibiotic is used, the ordinary bacteria die and those few survive; with the competition removed, they divide every twenty minutes or so and soon form a new population that the antibiotic can no longer kill. This is called antibiotic resistance. It happens faster when antibiotics are used unnecessarily, for example for a cold or flu, which are caused by viruses that antibiotics cannot affect, because the drug does nothing to the virus but still acts on the harmless bacteria living in the body, selecting resistant ones. It also happens when a patient stops a course of treatment early because they feel better: the most easily killed bacteria are gone, but the tougher ones are still there and can recover. South Africa has seen this with tuberculosis. TB normally needs about six months of treatment, and when people stop early, resistant forms called multidrug-resistant TB can develop, which needs longer, harder and far more expensive treatment. Resistance can also spread from one kind of bacterium to another. That is why the rules are simple: take antibiotics only when a health worker prescribes them for a bacterial infection, take every dose, and finish the course. Knowing the four groups is the first defence, because knowing that a disease is viral tells you immediately that antibiotics are the wrong tool.
