Hey — after a lesson about diseases, it is easy to think every microbe is out to get you. Here is the calm version: most of them are working for you, in three jobs.

Job one, the clean-up crew. Bacteria and fungi rot dead leaves, dung and bodies, and give back carbon dioxide to the air and minerals to the soil, so plants can grow again. That is decomposition. They need water, warmth and air, which is why a compost heap gets hot.

Job two, the cooks. Bacteria turn milk sugar into acid, and that acid makes yoghurt and amasi thick and sour. Yeast, a fungus, makes carbon dioxide bubbles, and that makes bread rise. Remember the yoghurt order: heat the milk, cool it down, add the bacteria, keep it warm.

Job three, the doctors. In 1928 Fleming spotted a mould killing bacteria on a dish. That mould gave us penicillin, the first antibiotic. It breaks bacterial walls, so it never works on viruses.

Bonus helpers: bacteria in bean roots feeding the soil with nitrogen, and microbes in a cow's stomach that digest grass.

Open the practice set and give each microbe its job. You've got this!
