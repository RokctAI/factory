# Part 1 — Expert

This session opens the study of micro-organisms, the living things too small to see without a microscope, and sorts them into four groups: viruses, bacteria, protists and fungi. The anchor is a single drop of water from a garden pond, which under a school microscope turns out to be crowded with life, and a single bacterium on a kitchen counter that, given warmth, food and moisture, can become more than a million bacteria in under seven hours. The governing idea is that micro-organisms are classified by their structure: whether they are cells at all, whether their cells have a nucleus, and whether they are made of one cell or many threads. The size units, the four groups with their structure and examples, how each reproduces and how to tell them apart are all examinable, and the groups return in Grade 10 Life Sciences as the foundation of biodiversity and classification.

## Subtopic: What Makes Something a Micro-organism

A micro-organism, also called a microbe, is a living thing so small that it can only be seen properly with a microscope. The word means small organism, and the size is the only thing the four groups have in common; in structure they are very different.

Micro-organisms are measured in units smaller than the millimetre. One millimetre contains one thousand micrometres, and one micrometre contains one thousand nanometres. A typical bacterium is about one to five micrometres long; a common gut bacterium is about two micrometres, so about five hundred of them laid end to end would span one millimetre. A human hair is about seventy micrometres wide, which is about thirty-five such bacteria side by side. Yeast cells are a little larger, around five to ten micrometres. Viruses are much smaller, mostly between twenty and three hundred nanometres, so that hundreds of them could fit across a single bacterium.

These sizes decide which microscope is needed. A school light microscope uses glass lenses and light and magnifies up to about four hundred times, and research light microscopes reach about a thousand times; that is enough to see protists clearly, yeast cells, mould threads and bacteria as tiny dots and rods. Viruses are smaller than the wavelength of visible light allows a light microscope to show, so they can only be seen with an electron microscope, which uses beams of electrons and magnifies hundreds of thousands of times.

The history follows the instruments. In the 1670s Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, a Dutch cloth merchant who ground his own lenses, looked at pond water and scrapings from his teeth and described tiny moving creatures he called animalcules; he was the first person to see bacteria and protists. Two centuries later Louis Pasteur showed that micro-organisms cause fermentation and food spoilage, and Robert Koch showed in 1882 that a particular bacterium causes tuberculosis. Viruses were not seen until electron microscopes were built in the 1930s.

Micro-organisms live almost everywhere: in soil, where a teaspoon may hold billions of bacteria; in water, air, food and dust; on our skin and inside our gut; in hot springs and deep ocean vents. Most are harmless and many are essential. Only a small minority cause disease, and those are called pathogens, the subject of the next session.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of a micro-organism, converting between millimetres, micrometres and nanometres, and why viruses need an electron microscope.

## Subtopic: Viruses and Bacteria

Viruses are the smallest and strangest group. A virus is not a cell. It consists of genetic material, either DNA or RNA, wrapped in a coat of protein, and some viruses also have an outer envelope. A virus cannot feed, grow or reproduce on its own. It can reproduce only by entering a living host cell and taking over that cell's machinery to make copies of itself; the copies then burst out or bud off, often destroying the cell, and infect more cells. Outside a host a virus is inactive, like a seed that never germinates unless it lands in the right soil. For this reason many scientists describe viruses as being on the boundary between living and non-living. Examples are the human immunodeficiency virus that causes AIDS, the influenza virus, the measles virus, the rabies virus, and the coronavirus that caused the COVID-19 pandemic. Viruses infect plants and bacteria too; the tobacco mosaic virus was the first to be discovered.

Bacteria are single cells, and they are the simplest true cells. A bacterial cell has a cell wall, a cell membrane and cytoplasm, but no nucleus; its genetic material, a single loop of DNA, lies free in the cytoplasm. Some bacteria have a whip-like flagellum for swimming. Bacteria are grouped by shape: round cells are called cocci, rod-shaped cells are called bacilli, and spiral cells are called spirilla. Many cocci live in chains or clusters.

Bacteria reproduce by binary fission: the cell copies its DNA, grows, and splits into two identical daughter cells. Under ideal conditions some bacteria divide every twenty minutes. That means one bacterium becomes 8 after one hour, 64 after two hours and 512 after three hours, and after twenty divisions, which takes under seven hours, there are 1 048 576 bacteria from a single cell. This is why food left out in a warm kitchen spoils quickly and why a small infection can become serious overnight. Growth stops when the food runs out, waste builds up or conditions change, which is why refrigeration, salting, drying and cooking all preserve food: they remove the warmth, water or living bacteria that the doubling needs. Some bacteria also form tough spores that survive boiling and drying.

Bacteria feed in many ways. Most absorb food from their surroundings, living on dead material or inside other organisms; some make their own food by photosynthesis, as cyanobacteria in ponds and the sea do.

The questions for this section are with you now: why a virus is not a cell, the three bacterial shapes, and the doubling of bacteria by binary fission.

## Subtopic: Protists and Fungi

Protists are mostly single-celled organisms whose cells do have a nucleus, which separates them from bacteria. They are a varied group that does not fit neatly into the plant or animal kingdoms. Amoeba moves and feeds by pushing out finger-like extensions of its cytoplasm called pseudopodia, which flow around food particles and engulf them. Paramecium is covered with tiny hairs called cilia that beat like oars to move it and sweep food into a groove. Euglena has a long flagellum for swimming and also has chloroplasts, so it can make food by photosynthesis in light and absorb food in the dark; it behaves like both a plant and an animal. Plasmodium is a parasitic protist that causes malaria. Many algae, such as the diatoms with glass-like shells that drift in the sea, are protists and are among the most important producers on Earth, forming the base of marine food chains and producing a large share of the world's oxygen. A drop of pond water under the microscope is often swarming with protists, and some, like large amoebae, are just big enough to see as specks with the naked eye.

Fungi include both micro-organisms and much larger organisms, but all share a body plan and a way of feeding. Fungal cells have a nucleus and a cell wall made of chitin, not cellulose, and fungi have no chlorophyll, so they cannot photosynthesise. Instead they release digestive enzymes onto their food and absorb the dissolved nutrients; most feed on dead material and are decomposers, while some are parasites. Yeast is a single-celled fungus that reproduces by budding, a small new cell growing out of the parent and breaking off. Moulds such as the black bread mould Rhizopus and the blue-green Penicillium are made of long, thin threads called hyphae, which branch and spread through their food in a network called a mycelium. Moulds reproduce by spores, released in their millions from tiny stalks, which is why a single mouldy orange soon has neighbours. The mushrooms in a forest are the spore-producing structures of fungi whose hyphae run unseen through the soil or wood; the mushroom itself is not a micro-organism, but the fungus is in the same kingdom as yeast and mould.

The questions for this section are with you now: how protists differ from bacteria, how amoeba, paramecium and euglena move, and the structure and feeding of yeast and moulds.

## Subtopic: Comparing the Four Groups

The four groups can be told apart by three questions, asked in order. First, is it a cell at all? If not, it is a virus. Second, if it is a cell, does it have a nucleus? If not, it is a bacterium. Third, if it has a nucleus, does it absorb food through threads or budding cells with chitin walls? If so, it is a fungus; if it is a single cell that moves with pseudopodia, cilia or flagella, or is a single-celled alga, it is a protist.

A comparison table sets this out. Viruses: not cells, protein coat and genetic material, twenty to three hundred nanometres, reproduce only inside host cells, examples HIV and influenza. Bacteria: single cells with a cell wall but no nucleus, about one to five micrometres, reproduce by binary fission, shapes cocci, bacilli and spirilla, examples the TB bacterium and the bacteria used to make yoghurt. Protists: mostly single cells with a nucleus, usually larger than bacteria, reproduce mainly by dividing in two, examples amoeba, paramecium, euglena and Plasmodium. Fungi: cells with a nucleus and chitin walls, single-celled yeasts or thread-like moulds, reproduce by budding or spores, feed by absorbing digested food, examples yeast, Penicillium and bread mould.

The groups matter for medicine as much as for classification. Antibiotics such as penicillin attack structures that bacterial cells have and human cells do not, such as the bacterial cell wall, so they work against bacteria but have no effect on viruses, which have no cell wall and live inside our own cells. Antifungal medicines are different again, and malaria needs its own drugs aimed at Plasmodium. Knowing which group causes a disease tells a doctor which kind of treatment can work.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: calling a virus a very small bacterium; it is not a cell at all. Two: saying bacteria have a nucleus; their DNA lies free in the cytoplasm. Three: calling all fungi micro-organisms; mushrooms are visible, though the group includes microscopic yeasts and moulds. Four: saying all micro-organisms cause disease; most are harmless or useful. Five: treating a viral infection such as flu with antibiotics; antibiotics act on bacteria only.

Layout for marks: name the group, give its structure in one sentence, its method of reproduction, and a named example.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three sorting questions, the comparison table, and why antibiotics work on bacteria but not on viruses.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through an invisible zoo, four families with four very different houses, and one cell that becomes a million — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Invisible Zoo

Take one drop of water from a pond and put it under a microscope. What looked like clear water becomes a zoo: blobs that ooze along, little slippers covered in hairs rowing in circles, green specks with tails, and tiny dots and rods everywhere. Every one of them is alive. They are micro-organisms, living things too small to see with your eyes alone.

How small? Think of a millimetre, the smallest mark on your ruler. Cut it into a thousand pieces; each piece is a micrometre. A typical bacterium is about two micrometres long, so about five hundred of them in a row would fit across that millimetre mark. One of your hairs is about as wide as thirty-five bacteria side by side. Viruses are far smaller again; cut a micrometre into a thousand pieces to get nanometres, and most viruses are a few tens to a few hundreds of those.

That is why we need different microscopes. The school microscope, using light and glass lenses, can show the blobs, the hairy slippers, yeast and bacteria. Viruses are too small even for that. To see a virus, scientists need an electron microscope, which uses a beam of electrons instead of light.

The first person to see the invisible zoo was a Dutch cloth seller called Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, who made his own lenses in the 1670s and was amazed to find tiny animals in pond water and in the gunk from his own teeth.

And the zoo is everywhere: in soil, water, air, on your skin and inside your gut. Most of it is harmless, and a lot of it is helpful. Only a few kinds make us sick.

Keep the pond drop in mind and try the questions for this part: what a micro-organism is, how small a micrometre is, and why viruses need a special microscope.

## Subtopic: Four Families, Four Houses

The invisible zoo has four families, and the easiest way to tell them apart is to ask what kind of house they live in.

Viruses have no house at all. A virus is not even a cell; it is a set of instructions wrapped in a protein coat. It cannot eat, grow or do anything on its own. It has to break into a living cell and hijack it, forcing the cell to make copies of the virus, like a burglar who moves in and uses your kitchen to print more burglars. HIV, flu, measles and the virus behind COVID-19 are all viruses.

Bacteria have a one-room house: a single cell with a wall around it but no nucleus inside, so their instructions just float loose. They come in three shapes: balls, rods and spirals. TB is caused by a rod-shaped bacterium, and the bacteria that make yoghurt are rods and balls.

Protists have a house with a separate office: a single cell with a proper nucleus. The amoeba oozes along by pushing out blobs of itself. The paramecium rows with thousands of tiny hairs. The euglena swims with a tail and can make its own food from sunlight like a plant. And one protist, Plasmodium, causes malaria.

Fungi have houses made of threads. Mould on old bread is a mat of fine threads that spread through the food and digest it from the outside. Yeast is a fungus that lives as single round cells and makes new ones by budding, like a bubble growing a smaller bubble. Mushrooms are fungi too, but they are big enough to see, so the mushroom itself is not a micro-organism.

No house, one room, a room with an office, a house of threads. Virus, bacterium, protist, fungus.

Hold the four houses in mind and try this part's questions: why a virus is not a cell, the three bacteria shapes, and which family yeast belongs to.

## Subtopic: One Becomes a Million

Here is the scary bit about bacteria. They multiply by splitting in two. One becomes two, two become four, four become eight. In warm, damp food, some bacteria can split every twenty minutes. So one bacterium on a chicken leg left on the kitchen counter becomes 8 after an hour, 64 after two hours, and 512 after three hours. After twenty splits, which is under seven hours, there are more than a million, all from one.

That is why we keep food in the fridge, cook meat properly, and do not leave leftovers lying around on a hot day. Cold slows the splitting down. Cooking kills the bacteria. Drying and salting, like biltong, take away the water they need. Every way of keeping food safe is a way of stopping the doubling.

And it is why the family matters when you are sick. Antibiotics are medicines that break the walls of bacteria, so they can cure bacterial diseases like TB. But a virus has no wall and hides inside your own cells, so antibiotics do nothing against flu or a cold. Taking them for a virus is useless, and it helps bacteria learn to resist them.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Micro-organisms are living things too small to see without a microscope, measured in micrometres and nanometres. There are four families. Viruses are not cells and can only copy themselves inside a host. Bacteria are single cells without a nucleus, shaped like balls, rods or spirals, and split in two very fast. Protists are single cells with a nucleus, like amoeba, paramecium, euglena and malaria's Plasmodium. Fungi have threads or budding cells and feed by digesting food from the outside, like mould and yeast. Most micro-organisms are harmless or helpful.

Hold the invisible zoo, the four houses and the doubling chicken leg, and take the final questions of the lesson: how many bacteria after three hours, why the fridge works, and why antibiotics cannot cure flu.
