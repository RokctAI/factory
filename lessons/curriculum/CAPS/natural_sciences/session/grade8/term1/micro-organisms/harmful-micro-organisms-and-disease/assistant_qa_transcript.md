### subtopic_1 — Pathogens and How Diseases Spread
**Assistant:** If I have already had a disease once, why can I sometimes catch it again?

**Tutor:** Because immunity depends on the immune system recognising the exact pathogen it met before, and some pathogens change or are simply too many to remember. When a pathogen gets into the body, white blood cells make antibodies shaped to fit particular molecules on that pathogen's surface, and some of those cells stay in the body as memory cells. If the same pathogen returns, the memory cells recognise it and respond so quickly that it is destroyed before it causes illness. This is why most people get measles or chickenpox only once. But the protection is only as good as the match. The influenza virus changes the molecules on its surface a little every year, so the antibodies made last year fit poorly, and people catch flu again; this is also why the flu vaccine is updated every year. The common cold is caused by more than a hundred different viruses, so beating one gives no protection against the rest. Some pathogens hide from the immune system: the TB bacterium can survive inside white blood cells, and HIV attacks the very cells that organise the immune response. The malaria parasite changes its surface while it lives in the blood and spends much of its time hidden inside cells, so people in malaria areas build only partial immunity after many infections. Immunity can also fade over years, which is why some vaccines need boosters. So having a disease once helps against that exact pathogen, but how much it helps depends on how stable the pathogen is and how well it hides.

### subtopic_2 — Tuberculosis and Cholera
**Assistant:** Why is cholera treated mainly with sugar and salt water rather than with medicine to kill the bacteria?

**Tutor:** Because cholera kills by dehydration, not by the bacteria destroying organs, so the urgent task is to replace what the body is losing. The cholera bacterium stays in the gut and releases a toxin that makes the cells lining the intestine pump salts out into the gut, and water follows the salts. A severely ill person can lose many litres of fluid a day in watery diarrhoea. Losing that much water and salt thickens the blood, drops blood pressure and upsets the balance the heart and kidneys need, and that can kill within hours. Killing the bacteria does not stop the toxin that is already acting, and the illness usually clears by itself within a few days if the patient is kept alive. Plain water alone is not absorbed well enough, and it does not replace the salts. The discovery behind oral rehydration solution was that the gut absorbs salt much better when sugar is present, because the two are carried into the cells together, and water follows them back into the body. So a simple mixture of clean water, sugar and salt in the right proportions can be absorbed even while the diarrhoea continues. The proportions matter: too much salt or sugar draws more water into the gut and makes things worse, which is why a level-teaspoon recipe is given. This treatment has saved millions of lives because it is cheap and can be made at home. Antibiotics can shorten severe illness and reduce spreading, and drips are used when a patient is too weak to drink, but rehydration is what saves life.

### subtopic_3 — HIV and AIDS, and Malaria
**Assistant:** Why can a mosquito spread malaria but not HIV, when both are in the blood?

**Tutor:** Because the two pathogens have completely different relationships with the mosquito, and a mosquito does not work like a syringe. When a mosquito bites, it does not inject the blood of the last person it fed on. It has separate channels in its mouthparts: one sucks blood up into its gut, and another injects saliva, which stops the blood clotting. Blood goes one way only, into the gut, where it is digested. The malaria parasite has evolved to use this route. Once in the mosquito's gut, Plasmodium goes through a stage of its life cycle there, reproduces, and its offspring migrate to the mosquito's salivary glands, ready to be injected with the saliva at the next bite. The mosquito is a genuine host for part of the parasite's life cycle, which takes about one to two weeks inside it. HIV has no such stage. It can only reproduce inside particular human white blood cells, which mosquitoes do not have, so any HIV taken in with blood is simply digested and destroyed in the gut. It never reaches the saliva. Studies of HIV in areas with many mosquitoes confirm this: the pattern of infection follows sexual contact and blood exposure, not mosquito bites, and children and elderly people who are bitten as often as anyone else do not get HIV that way. This is a good example of why knowing the biology of the pathogen matters for prevention. Nets prevent malaria; nets do nothing against HIV, and condoms and safe handling of blood do.

### subtopic_4 — Preventing the Spread of Disease
**Assistant:** How can vaccinating most people protect someone who has not been vaccinated?

**Tutor:** Because a pathogen survives in a community only if each infected person passes it on to at least one new person before recovering. Think of a pathogen trying to spread through a school. If nobody is immune, one sick learner might infect several classmates, each of whom infects several more, and the disease sweeps through. Now suppose most learners have been vaccinated. The sick learner still coughs or touches surfaces, but most of the people the germs reach are immune, so the pathogen hits a dead end again and again. On average each case now produces fewer than one new case, so the outbreak shrinks and dies out. The few learners who could not be vaccinated, perhaps because they are too young or have a weakened immune system from an illness or treatment, are protected because the pathogen never manages to reach them through the wall of immune people around them. That is herd immunity. How many people need to be immune depends on how infectious the disease is. For measles, one of the most infectious diseases known, about nineteen in every twenty people need to be immune, which is why measles outbreaks appear quickly when vaccination rates drop. For less infectious diseases the threshold is lower. Herd immunity is also why vaccination is treated as a community responsibility: each person who is vaccinated protects themselves, and also closes one more route by which the pathogen could reach a newborn baby or a sick neighbour. The same chain logic explains why treatment prevents spread: a patient who is no longer infectious is another dead end.
