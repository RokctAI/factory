# Part 1 — Expert

This session turns to the small minority of micro-organisms that cause disease, and to the four diseases named in the curriculum: tuberculosis, AIDS, malaria and cholera. The anchor is the idea that every infectious disease needs a route from one person to the next, and that each of these four uses a different route: TB travels through the air, cholera through water and food, HIV through blood and body fluids, and malaria through the bite of a mosquito. The governing idea is that if you know the micro-organism and its route, you know how to block it. The causes, routes, symptoms, treatments and methods of prevention of each disease are examinable, and so is the general reasoning about why prevention works, which leads directly to the study of the immune system and disease in Grade 10 and 11 Life Sciences.

## Subtopic: Pathogens and How Diseases Spread

A pathogen is a micro-organism that causes disease. Pathogens are found in all four groups: viruses such as HIV and influenza, bacteria such as those that cause TB and cholera, protists such as Plasmodium which causes malaria, and fungi such as those that cause athlete's foot and thrush. A disease caused by a pathogen that can pass from one person to another is called an infectious or communicable disease. Diseases such as diabetes or most cancers are not caused by pathogens and cannot be caught; they are non-communicable.

A pathogen causes harm in two main ways. Some damage or destroy the body's cells directly as they multiply, as a virus does when it bursts the cells it has taken over, or as the malaria parasite does when it breaks open red blood cells. Others release poisonous substances called toxins; the cholera bacterium's toxin makes the gut pour water out of the body. The symptoms of disease, such as fever, cough, diarrhoea and tiredness, come partly from this damage and partly from the body's own defence as the immune system fights back.

Every infectious disease needs a route of transmission, a way to get from one host to the next, and the routes fall into a few groups. Airborne or droplet transmission: tiny droplets from a cough, sneeze or even speech carry the pathogen to the next person's lungs, as in TB, flu and COVID-19. Water-borne and food-borne transmission: pathogens in the faeces of an infected person contaminate drinking water or food, as in cholera and typhoid. Transmission through blood and body fluids: the pathogen passes in blood, semen, vaginal fluid or breast milk, as in HIV and hepatitis B. Vector-borne transmission: an animal, usually an insect, carries the pathogen from one person to another, as the Anopheles mosquito carries malaria; the animal that carries it is called a vector. Direct contact with skin or contaminated objects spreads others, such as ringworm, a fungal infection.

The body's first defences are barriers: unbroken skin, mucus and tiny hairs in the airways, and acid in the stomach. When a pathogen gets past them, white blood cells attack it, some by engulfing it, others by making antibodies that stick to that particular pathogen and mark it for destruction. Once the body has fought off a pathogen, it often remembers it and can respond quickly next time; this is immunity. Vaccines use that memory by presenting the immune system with a harmless form or part of a pathogen, so that it is ready before the real pathogen ever arrives.

## Subtopic: Tuberculosis and Cholera

Tuberculosis, called TB, is caused by a rod-shaped bacterium. It usually infects the lungs, though it can affect other parts of the body. It spreads through the air: when a person with active lung TB coughs, sneezes or talks, droplets carrying the bacteria are released, and people sharing the same air, especially in crowded, poorly ventilated rooms, taxis and homes, breathe them in. Many people who are infected carry the bacteria in an inactive form and do not become ill; the disease becomes active when the immune system is weakened, for example by HIV, poor nutrition or other illness. The symptoms of active TB are a cough lasting more than two weeks, sometimes with blood, fever and night sweats, weight loss, tiredness and chest pain. TB is diagnosed by testing a sample of sputum, the mucus coughed up from the lungs.

TB is curable. Treatment is a combination of antibiotics taken every day for about six months, free at South African clinics. The danger lies in stopping early: the patient feels better after a few weeks, but bacteria remain, and stopping allows resistant forms to develop, known as multidrug-resistant TB, which takes much longer and is much harder to treat. South Africa has one of the highest rates of TB in the world, closely linked to its HIV epidemic, and TB is one of the leading causes of death in the country. Prevention includes the BCG vaccine given to babies at birth, opening windows and improving ventilation, covering coughs, early testing of anyone with a lasting cough, and making sure every patient completes treatment.

Cholera is caused by a comma-shaped bacterium that lives in the gut. It spreads through water or food contaminated with the faeces of an infected person, which happens where sewage is not properly treated or separated from drinking water, or where people cannot wash their hands. The bacterium releases a toxin that makes the lining of the intestine pour water and salts into the gut, causing sudden, severe watery diarrhoea, often described as rice-water stools, along with vomiting and leg cramps. A person can lose litres of fluid in a day, and severe dehydration can kill within hours.

The main treatment for cholera is to replace the lost water and salts, using oral rehydration solution: the World Health Organization's home recipe is one litre of clean, boiled and cooled water with six level teaspoons of sugar and half a level teaspoon of salt. Severe cases need fluids through a drip and sometimes antibiotics. South Africa has had serious outbreaks: in KwaZulu-Natal in 2000 and 2001 there were more than one hundred thousand cases, and in 2023 an outbreak around Hammanskraal near Pretoria, linked to contaminated water, caused more than twenty deaths. Prevention means safe water and sanitation: boiling or treating drinking water, keeping toilets away from water sources, washing hands with soap after using the toilet and before handling food, cooking food well and washing fruit and vegetables in clean water.

## Subtopic: HIV and AIDS, and Malaria

AIDS, acquired immune deficiency syndrome, is caused by HIV, the human immunodeficiency virus. HIV attacks a particular kind of white blood cell that organises the body's immune response. Over years, without treatment, the number of these cells falls, the immune system weakens, and the body can no longer fight off infections that a healthy person would resist easily, such as TB, pneumonia and certain cancers. When the immune system has been badly damaged in this way, the person is said to have AIDS. HIV is the cause; AIDS is the late stage of untreated HIV infection.

HIV is spread through four body fluids: blood, semen, vaginal fluid and breast milk. The routes are unprotected sex with an infected person, sharing needles or contact with infected blood, and from an infected mother to her baby during pregnancy, birth or breastfeeding. HIV is not spread by hugging, shaking hands, sharing cups, plates or toilets, swimming together, coughing, or mosquito bites; the virus does not survive long outside the body and a mosquito does not inject another person's blood. Knowing what does not spread HIV matters as much as knowing what does, because fear and stigma stop people testing and seeking help.

There is no cure for HIV, but antiretroviral therapy, a daily combination of medicines, stops the virus from multiplying. A person who takes it consistently can keep the virus at levels so low that tests cannot detect it, can live a long and healthy life, and does not pass the virus on through sex. South Africa has close to eight million people living with HIV, the largest number of any country, and runs the largest antiretroviral treatment programme in the world. Prevention includes knowing your status through testing, abstaining or using condoms, not sharing needles, treatment of pregnant mothers to protect their babies, and preventive medicine for people at high risk.

Malaria is caused by Plasmodium, a single-celled protist, and it is carried by the female Anopheles mosquito, which bites mainly between dusk and dawn. When the mosquito feeds on an infected person it takes in the parasite; when it bites the next person it injects the parasite with its saliva. The parasite travels to the liver, multiplies, then invades red blood cells, multiplies again and bursts them, releasing more parasites to invade more cells. Each wave of bursting causes attacks of high fever, chills, sweating, headache and body aches, beginning about one to four weeks after the bite and easily mistaken for flu. Severe malaria can cause anaemia, brain damage and death, especially in young children. Malaria kills more than half a million people a year worldwide, most of them children under five in Africa. In South Africa malaria occurs in the low-lying north-east: parts of Limpopo, Mpumalanga and northern KwaZulu-Natal, mainly in the hot, wet months from about September to May. It is treated with antimalarial drugs, and fast diagnosis by a blood test saves lives.

Malaria is prevented by attacking the mosquito and protecting people from bites: sleeping under insecticide-treated nets, using repellent and wearing long sleeves from dusk, spraying the inside walls of houses with insecticide, removing standing water where mosquitoes breed, and taking preventive tablets when travelling to malaria areas.

## Subtopic: Preventing the Spread of Disease

Prevention works by breaking the chain between the pathogen, its route and the next host, so every method can be explained by which link it breaks.

Personal hygiene breaks contact and food routes. Washing hands with soap and water for at least twenty seconds, especially after using the toilet and before preparing or eating food, removes pathogens from the skin; soap breaks up the oily outer layer of many viruses and lifts bacteria away. Safe water and sanitation break the water route: boiling water for at least one minute kills bacteria such as the cholera bacterium, and proper toilets and sewage treatment keep faeces out of drinking water. Safe food handling, cooking food thoroughly, refrigerating leftovers and washing produce, stops bacteria multiplying.

Covering coughs, ventilation and masks break the air route. Opening windows dilutes airborne pathogens, which is why TB spreads less in well-ventilated rooms. Barrier methods and safe handling of blood break the body-fluid route: condoms, never sharing needles or razors, and gloves when helping someone who is bleeding. Vector control breaks the insect route: nets, repellents, indoor spraying and removing breeding sites.

Vaccination protects the host. A vaccinated person's immune system responds fast enough to stop the pathogen before it causes disease, and when most of a community is vaccinated the pathogen struggles to find new hosts at all, which also protects those who cannot be vaccinated; this is called herd immunity. South African children receive vaccines for TB, polio, measles, whooping cough and others in their first years.

Treatment also prevents spread. A TB patient on treatment soon stops being infectious; a person on antiretroviral therapy with an undetectable viral load does not pass HIV on sexually. Testing early and treating fully protects the community as well as the patient.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying AIDS is a virus; HIV is the virus, AIDS is the condition it causes. Two: saying mosquitoes spread HIV; they do not. Three: treating cholera only with antibiotics; replacing water and salts is what saves lives. Four: stopping TB tablets once the cough clears; the full six months are needed. Five: saying malaria is caused by the mosquito; the mosquito is the vector, and Plasmodium is the cause.

Layout for marks: name the disease, the pathogen and its group, the route, two symptoms, the treatment, and a prevention method linked to the route it blocks.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through four doors that germs use to get in, four diseases at a glance, and how to lock each door — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Germs Need a Way In

Most micro-organisms are harmless, but a few make us sick. Those are called pathogens, or germs. And here is the key idea: a germ is stuck unless it can travel from one person to the next. Every disease you can catch has a favourite door.

Door one is the air. Someone coughs or sneezes and tiny droplets full of germs float across the room into your lungs. TB and flu use this door.

Door two is dirty water and food. If toilets leak into the river or someone does not wash their hands, germs from faeces get into what people drink and eat. Cholera uses this door.

Door three is blood and body fluids. Some germs only travel inside blood, sexual fluids or breast milk. HIV uses this door.

Door four is a bite. An insect bites a sick person, picks up the germ, then bites someone else and passes it on. The insect is called a vector. The mosquito that carries malaria uses this door.

Your body has its own guards. Skin is a wall. The sticky mucus in your nose and the acid in your stomach trap and kill germs. And if a germ gets through, your white blood cells hunt it down, and afterwards they remember it, so next time they are faster. A vaccine is a practice drill: it shows your body a harmless version of the germ so that the guards are ready before the real thing arrives.

Keep the four doors in mind and try the questions for this part: what a pathogen is, which door each disease uses, and how a vaccine works.

## Subtopic: Four Diseases at a Glance

TB is a bacterium that lives in the lungs and travels through the air. The signs are a cough that lasts more than two weeks, sometimes with blood, sweating at night and losing weight. The good news: TB can be cured with free tablets from the clinic. The catch: you must take them for about six months, even after you feel better, or the germs come back tougher.

Cholera is a bacterium that travels in dirty water and food. It causes sudden, watery diarrhoea so severe that a person can dry out and die in hours. The life-saver is simple: a litre of clean, boiled and cooled water with six level teaspoons of sugar and half a level teaspoon of salt, sipped to replace what is lost. In 2023 people near Hammanskraal died of cholera because of contaminated water.

HIV is a virus that travels in blood and body fluids. It attacks the white blood cells that run your defences, and after years without treatment the defences collapse; that late stage is called AIDS. So HIV is the virus and AIDS is what it can lead to. You cannot get HIV from a hug, a handshake, a shared cup or a mosquito. There is no cure, but daily tablets called antiretrovirals keep the virus so low that people live long, healthy lives.

Malaria is caused by a tiny protist called Plasmodium, carried by a female mosquito that bites at night. The germ hides in the liver, then bursts red blood cells, giving waves of fever, chills and sweating that feel like a bad flu. In South Africa it is found in the warm, low north-east of Limpopo, Mpumalanga and northern KwaZulu-Natal.

## Subtopic: Locking the Doors

If you know the door, you know how to lock it.

Lock the air door: cover your cough with your elbow, open windows, and get tested if a cough lasts more than two weeks. Lock the water door: wash your hands with soap after the toilet and before eating, boil or treat water if you are not sure it is safe, and cook food properly. Lock the blood door: never share needles or razors, wear gloves when helping someone who is bleeding, and know your HIV status. Lock the bite door: sleep under a treated net, use repellent from dusk, and get rid of standing water where mosquitoes breed.

And keep the guards strong with vaccines. Babies in South Africa get a TB vaccine at birth and many others in their first years. When most people are vaccinated, germs cannot find new people to infect, which protects even the babies too young for the jab.

Treatment locks doors too. Someone taking TB tablets properly soon stops spreading it. Someone on antiretrovirals with an undetectable virus does not pass HIV on through sex. Test early, treat fully.
