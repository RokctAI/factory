# Part 1 — Expert

This session corrects the impression the last one may have left: that micro-organisms are mainly enemies. Most are harmless and a great many are essential. The anchor is a bowl of homemade yoghurt beside a compost heap and a dish of mould, three things that look unrelated but are each the work of micro-organisms: bacteria turning milk into yoghurt, bacteria and fungi turning garden waste into fertile soil, and a mould producing the antibiotic penicillin, which has saved many millions of lives. The governing idea is that micro-organisms carry out chemical changes that people and ecosystems depend on, in decomposition, in food and in medicine. The process of decomposition, the making of yoghurt, bread and traditional fermented foods, the discovery and action of penicillin, and further uses such as nitrogen fixation and sewage treatment are examinable, and they connect forward to biotechnology in Grade 11 and 12 Life Sciences.

## Subtopic: Decomposers: Nature's Recyclers

When a plant or animal dies, the materials in its body, carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus and many others, are locked inside complex substances such as proteins, starch and cellulose. Living plants cannot use those substances directly; they need simple minerals dissolved in soil water and carbon dioxide from the air. Decomposition is the process that turns one into the other, and it is carried out mainly by bacteria and fungi, the decomposers.

Decomposers release digestive enzymes onto dead material, break the large molecules into small ones, absorb them, and use them for energy in respiration. Respiration releases carbon dioxide into the air, where plants take it up again for photosynthesis, and the breakdown releases minerals such as nitrates and phosphates into the soil, where plant roots absorb them. In this way the same atoms of carbon and nitrogen are used again and again, passing from plants to animals to decomposers and back to plants. Without decomposers, dead leaves, dung and bodies would pile up for ever, and the soil would gradually run out of the minerals that plants need, so all food chains would eventually stop. Earthworms, millipedes and woodlice help by shredding dead material into smaller pieces, which gives the bacteria and fungi more surface to work on, but the final chemical breakdown is done by the micro-organisms.

Decomposers need the same conditions as any living cell: moisture, warmth, oxygen for most of them, and food. That is why things rot fastest in warm, damp conditions and hardly at all in dry deserts or frozen ground. Gardeners use this in a compost heap: garden waste and vegetable peelings are piled up, kept moist, and turned to let air in. The micro-organisms respire so actively that the centre of a good heap becomes hot, often reaching around sixty degrees Celsius, which also kills weed seeds and many pathogens. After a few months the waste has become dark, crumbly compost full of minerals, which improves the soil and replaces part of the need for bought fertiliser.

People use decomposition on a large scale in sewage treatment. At a sewage works the waste water is pumped through tanks where air is bubbled in, and huge populations of bacteria feed on the organic matter, breaking it down into carbon dioxide, water and minerals, so that the water released into rivers is far cleaner. When decomposition happens without oxygen, other bacteria produce methane gas; in a biogas digester, cattle dung or food waste is sealed in a tank and the methane collected for cooking or lighting, a technology used on farms and in rural homes across South Africa.

The questions for this section are with you now: what decomposers do with dead material, the conditions they need, why a compost heap gets hot, and how sewage works use bacteria.

## Subtopic: Micro-organisms in Food

People have used micro-organisms to make food for thousands of years, long before anyone knew they existed. The general process is fermentation: micro-organisms break down sugars in the food, and the products they release change its taste, texture and how long it keeps.

Yoghurt is made by two kinds of bacteria, one rod-shaped and one round, which feed on lactose, the sugar in milk, and turn it into lactic acid. The acid makes the milk proteins clump together and thicken, which gives yoghurt its texture, and it gives the sharp, sour taste. It also preserves the yoghurt, because few harmful bacteria can grow in acid. The method has fixed steps, each with a reason. Heat the milk to about eighty-five degrees Celsius to kill unwanted bacteria and change the milk proteins so that the yoghurt sets thicker. Cool it to about forty-three degrees, because the yoghurt bacteria would be killed by the higher temperature. Stir in a starter, a spoonful of live yoghurt containing the bacteria. Keep the mixture warm, at about forty-three degrees, for four to eight hours while the bacteria multiply and produce acid. Then refrigerate to slow the bacteria and stop the yoghurt becoming too sour.

South Africa has its own fermented milk with a long history. Amasi is made by leaving fresh milk in a container, traditionally a calabash or a clay pot, for a few days, during which lactic acid bacteria naturally present sour and thicken it; the whey is drained off and the thick amasi eaten with pap. Mageu, also called amahewu, is a drink made by fermenting thin maize-meal porridge, again with lactic acid bacteria, giving it a slightly sour taste. Cheese, buttermilk and sour cream are made in similar ways.

Bread uses a fungus. Yeast, a single-celled fungus, is mixed into dough with flour, water and a little sugar. In the warm dough the yeast breaks down the sugar, releasing carbon dioxide and a small amount of alcohol. The carbon dioxide forms bubbles trapped in the stretchy dough, which makes it rise; in the oven the heat kills the yeast, the alcohol evaporates, and the bubbles set into the light texture of bread. The same yeast process, with the alcohol kept, is used to make traditional sorghum beer, umqombothi, and other drinks; the chemistry of fermentation returns in the chemical reactions topic next term.

Micro-organisms are also used to make vinegar, soy sauce, and the cultures added to some yoghurts and drinks as probiotics, meaning bacteria that may help the gut. And they are a reminder that the same micro-organisms that make food can also spoil it; a carton of milk left out goes sour for the same reason that yoghurt forms, just without control.

The questions for this section are with you now: how bacteria turn milk into yoghurt, the reason for each temperature, how amasi and mageu are made, and why bread rises.

## Subtopic: Micro-organisms in Medicine: Penicillin and Beyond

Before the 1940s, a scratch that became infected or a case of pneumonia could easily kill, because there was no reliable medicine to kill bacteria inside the body. That changed with an accident. In 1928 Alexander Fleming, a Scottish scientist working in London, returned from holiday to find that one of his dishes of bacteria had been contaminated by a blue-green mould. Around the mould was a clear ring where the bacteria had died. He reasoned that the mould was releasing a substance that killed bacteria, identified the mould as a species of Penicillium, and named the substance penicillin.

Fleming could not produce penicillin in useful amounts. Around 1940 Howard Florey and Ernst Chain at Oxford found ways to purify it and showed that it cured infections in mice and then in people, and during the Second World War factories in the United States and Britain learned to grow the mould in huge tanks. By 1944 penicillin was being made in quantities large enough to treat soldiers' wounds, and after the war it became widely available. Fleming, Florey and Chain shared the Nobel Prize in 1945.

Penicillin is an antibiotic, a substance that kills bacteria or stops them from multiplying without harming the patient's own cells. It works by preventing bacteria from building their cell walls properly, so the cells burst as they grow; human cells have no cell walls, so they are unaffected. That is also why penicillin and other antibiotics have no effect on viruses. Many more antibiotics were later found in micro-organisms, especially soil bacteria; one of them, streptomycin, was the first effective drug against TB.

Micro-organisms also help make vaccines, since many vaccines are produced by growing a weakened or killed pathogen, or a harmless part of it, in large cultures. Since the 1980s bacteria have been altered by genetic engineering so that they carry the human gene for insulin, the hormone people with diabetes need; the bacteria are grown in large vats and produce human insulin, replacing insulin that used to be extracted from the organs of pigs and cattle. This is biotechnology: using living organisms, very often micro-organisms, to make useful products.

The great threat to all this is antibiotic resistance. Every use of an antibiotic kills the sensitive bacteria and leaves any resistant ones to multiply, so antibiotics that are overused or stopped early gradually lose their power. Using them only when prescribed, for bacterial infections, and finishing the course are how individuals protect a discovery that has saved a vast number of lives.

The questions for this section are with you now: the story of Fleming's discovery, how penicillin kills bacteria and why it cannot kill viruses, and two other medical uses of micro-organisms.

## Subtopic: More Useful Microbes and the Error Museum

Several other uses of micro-organisms are worth knowing, because they show how much ecosystems and farming depend on them.

Nitrogen fixation. Plants need nitrogen to make proteins, and the air is about seventy-eight percent nitrogen gas, but plants cannot use nitrogen gas directly. Certain bacteria can. Some live in the soil, and some live inside swellings called root nodules on the roots of legumes: beans, peas, groundnuts, lucerne, clover, and also acacias and rooibos. The bacteria turn nitrogen from the air into nitrogen compounds that the plant can use, and in return the plant supplies them with sugar. This is a mutualism, and it is why farmers rotate crops, planting beans or groundnuts in a field one season to enrich the soil for maize the next.

Digestion. Cattle, sheep, goats and antelope cannot digest cellulose themselves, yet they live on grass. In the first chamber of their stomach, the rumen, billions of bacteria and protists break the cellulose down, and the animal absorbs the products. Without these micro-organisms grazing animals could not survive on grass at all. Humans also carry large numbers of bacteria in the large intestine; they help digest some foods, produce vitamin K, and crowd out harmful bacteria.

Cleaning up. Some bacteria can break down oil and other pollutants, and they are used to help clean up oil spills and contaminated soil, a process called bioremediation.

So the balance sheet is clear: a few micro-organisms are pathogens, but life on Earth would be impossible without the decomposers and nitrogen fixers, and human society relies on micro-organisms for food, medicine, waste treatment and energy.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying all bacteria are harmful; most are harmless and many are essential. Two: saying yoghurt bacteria are killed when the milk is heated; the milk is heated before the bacteria are added, and cooled first so they survive. Three: saying bread rises because yeast makes oxygen; it makes carbon dioxide. Four: saying penicillin is made by a bacterium; it is made by a mould, which is a fungus. Five: saying decomposers destroy nutrients; they release them back to the soil for plants to use.

Layout for marks: name the micro-organism and its group, state the process it carries out, give the product, and explain the benefit.

The questions for this section are with you now: nitrogen fixation in root nodules, why cattle need micro-organisms to digest grass, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a clean-up crew, a team of tiny cooks, and a mould that saved millions — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Clean-Up Crew

Imagine a forest where nothing ever rotted. Every leaf that fell, every dead tree, every bit of dung would just stay there, piling higher and higher, year after year. And the soil underneath would slowly run out of food for plants, because everything good would be locked up in the pile. Soon nothing could grow.

That does not happen because of the clean-up crew: bacteria and fungi. They break dead things down into simple pieces. Some of those pieces go into the air as carbon dioxide, which plants breathe in for photosynthesis. The rest go into the soil as minerals, which plant roots suck up. So the same bits get used again and again, from plant to animal to rot and back to plant. Nature's recycling.

The crew needs what any living thing needs: water, warmth, air and food. That is why bread goes mouldy faster in a warm kitchen and why nothing rots in a dry desert.

You can put the crew to work at home. A compost heap of peels and grass cuttings, kept damp and turned now and then, gets hot inside, sometimes about sixty degrees, because the microbes are so busy. A few months later it is rich, dark compost for the garden. Towns do the same with sewage: bacteria eat the dirt in the water so it is clean enough to go back to the river. And on some farms, cow dung in a sealed tank is turned by bacteria into biogas for cooking.

Keep the clean-up crew in mind and try the questions for this part: what decomposers do, what they need, and why compost gets hot.

## Subtopic: The Tiny Cooks

Some of your favourite foods are made by micro-organisms.

Yoghurt is milk that bacteria have worked on. The bacteria eat the sugar in milk and turn it into acid, which makes the milk thick and sour. To make it at home: heat the milk to kill any unwanted germs, let it cool to warm, because too hot would kill the good bacteria, stir in a spoon of live yoghurt to bring in the bacteria, keep it warm for a few hours, then put it in the fridge.

Amasi works the same way. Milk is left in a container for a few days, the bacteria that are naturally there sour and thicken it, and it is eaten with pap. Mageu is thin maize porridge that bacteria have soured into a drink.

Bread uses a fungus called yeast. Yeast eats the sugar in the dough and gives off carbon dioxide gas. The gas makes bubbles that get trapped in the dough and puff it up. In the hot oven the yeast dies and the bubbles set, so the bread stays light and fluffy. Yeast also makes a little alcohol, which is why it is used for traditional sorghum beer, but in bread the oven drives it off.

And here is the twist: milk left out on a hot day goes sour by the same kind of process, just with whatever bacteria happen to land in it. The cooks only make good food when we control them.

Hold the tiny cooks in mind and try this part's questions: what yoghurt bacteria make from milk sugar, why the milk is cooled before adding them, and what gas makes bread rise.

## Subtopic: The Mould That Saved Millions

In 1928 a scientist called Alexander Fleming came back from holiday to a messy lab. One of his dishes of bacteria had a blob of blue-green mould growing on it. Most people would have thrown it away. Fleming looked closely and saw a clear ring around the mould where the bacteria had died. The mould was making something that killed bacteria. The mould was Penicillium, so he called the substance penicillin.

It took other scientists, Florey and Chain, about twelve more years to work out how to make enough of it, and by 1944 factories were growing the mould in giant tanks to treat wounded soldiers. Before penicillin, an infected cut could kill you. After penicillin, it was cured in days. It has saved many millions of lives.

Penicillin breaks the walls of bacteria so they burst. Your own cells have no such walls, so they are safe. A virus has no wall either, so penicillin cannot touch it, which is why antibiotics never cure a cold. Today bacteria help make other medicines too, like the insulin that people with diabetes need, made by bacteria that have been given the human instructions for it.

And there are helpers everywhere else: bacteria in the bumps on bean roots that feed the soil with nitrogen, microbes in a cow's stomach that let it live on grass, microbes in your gut that help you digest.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Most micro-organisms are helpers. Decomposers, bacteria and fungi, break down dead things and return carbon dioxide and minerals for plants. Bacteria turn milk into yoghurt and amasi by making acid; yeast makes bread rise with carbon dioxide. Fleming found penicillin in a mould in 1928, and antibiotics kill bacteria by breaking their walls, never viruses. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria feed plants, and microbes in the gut help digest food.

Hold the clean-up crew, the tiny cooks and Fleming's mouldy dish, and take the final questions of the lesson: who discovered penicillin, why it cannot cure a virus, and how bean roots feed the soil.
