Today we meet the micro-organisms we could not live without. Most microbes are harmless, and many are essential. Bacteria and fungi are the decomposers that break down dead leaves, dung and bodies, returning carbon dioxide to the air and minerals to the soil so plants can grow again; we put them to work in compost heaps, sewage works and biogas digesters. In food, bacteria turn the sugar in milk into lactic acid to make yoghurt, amasi and mageu, and the yeast fungus fills bread dough with bubbles of carbon dioxide. In medicine, a mouldy dish in 1928 led Alexander Fleming to penicillin, the first antibiotic, which kills bacteria by breaking their cell walls and has saved many millions of lives. We finish with nitrogen-fixing bacteria in bean roots, the microbes that let cattle live on grass, and why careful use of antibiotics protects them for the future.
