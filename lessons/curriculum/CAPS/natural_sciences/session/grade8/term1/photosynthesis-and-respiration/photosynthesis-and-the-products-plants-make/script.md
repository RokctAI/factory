# Part 1 — Expert

This session establishes photosynthesis as the process by which green plants build their own food, and follows that food beyond the leaf to the compounds a plant makes from it. The anchor is the word equation: carbon dioxide plus water, in the presence of sunlight and chlorophyll, gives glucose plus oxygen. The anchor investigation is the starch test on a leaf with iodine solution. Both the equation and the investigation are examinable, and so is the second half of the story: a plant converts glucose into starch for storage, cellulose for cell walls, sucrose for transport, and, with mineral salts from the soil, into proteins and oils. Every living thing on Earth, including every learner in this class, is fed in the end by this one process.

## Subtopic: The Word Equation of Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis is the process in which green plants use energy from sunlight to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water, releasing oxygen as a by-product. The name says it: photo means light, synthesis means putting together. The word equation is written with the raw materials on the left, an arrow, and the products on the right, with the conditions written above or beside the arrow. Carbon dioxide plus water gives glucose plus oxygen; conditions: sunlight and chlorophyll.

Each part of that line has a source. Carbon dioxide is a gas in the air, making up only about 0,04 percent of it, and it enters the leaf through tiny pores called stomata, mostly on the underside of the leaf. Water is absorbed by the roots from the soil and travels up the stem in xylem vessels to the leaf. Sunlight is the energy source; photosynthesis is an energy-storing process, converting light energy into chemical energy held in the glucose. Chlorophyll is the green pigment that absorbs the light energy; it is not used up, so it is a condition, not a raw material, and is never written on the left of the arrow.

The products also have destinations. Glucose is a sugar, the plant's food, and it is either used immediately for energy, stored, or built into other compounds, which is the second half of this session. Oxygen leaves the leaf through the same stomata, which is why the oxygen in the atmosphere that animals breathe comes from plants, algae and photosynthetic bacteria. Roughly, the air is 21 percent oxygen and 78 percent nitrogen, and the oxygen fraction exists because of photosynthesis over billions of years.

A distinction examiners press on: the raw materials are substances that are consumed, and they appear on the left; the conditions are requirements that are not consumed, and they sit at the arrow. Sunlight and chlorophyll are conditions. Writing "sunlight plus carbon dioxide plus water" as if light were a chemical costs the mark. Note also that photosynthesis happens only in the green parts of a plant, and only in the light; at night no photosynthesis occurs, although, as the next session shows, respiration continues day and night.

The questions for this section are with you now: the full word equation in order, raw materials against conditions, and where each ingredient comes from.

## Subtopic: Where It Happens: Leaves, Chlorophyll and Chloroplasts

The leaf is the plant's photosynthesis organ, and its structure matches the job. A leaf is broad and flat to present a large surface to the light. It is thin, so light reaches every cell and gases need travel only a short distance. It has veins, which carry xylem bringing water in and phloem carrying sugars away. Its lower surface is dotted with stomata, each pore guarded by two guard cells that open in the day to admit carbon dioxide and close at night or in drought to reduce water loss.

Inside a leaf cell, the green colour comes from chloroplasts, small structures containing the pigment chlorophyll. The chloroplast is the site of photosynthesis: that is where the light energy is captured and where glucose is assembled. Cells deeper in the leaf, and cells in roots, have no chloroplasts and cannot photosynthesise; they depend on sugar delivered from the leaves. A plant stem that is green photosynthesises a little; a brown woody trunk does not.

Chlorophyll absorbs mainly the red and blue parts of sunlight and reflects green, which is why leaves appear green to us. The pigment is why light becomes usable; without it, light passes through or warms the leaf and no glucose is made. A variegated leaf, one with white patches, demonstrates this. The white patches lack chlorophyll. When such a leaf is tested for starch, only the formerly green areas turn blue-black; the white areas stay brown. Starch was made only where chlorophyll was present, and that is direct evidence that chlorophyll is a requirement.

Two further factors control the rate. Light intensity: more light, up to a point, means faster photosynthesis, which is why a seedling in a dark cupboard yellows and dies. Carbon dioxide concentration: commercial tomato growers in greenhouses sometimes raise the carbon dioxide level to increase yield. Temperature also matters, because the process is run by enzymes that work best in a warm range; this is why maize in Mpumalanga grows in summer and not in a frosty July.

The questions for this section are with you now: leaf adaptations and the reason for each, the chloroplast as the site, and the variegated-leaf evidence.

## Subtopic: Testing a Leaf for Starch

Glucose made in the leaf does not stay as glucose for long. Within hours it is converted into starch grains inside the chloroplast, because starch is insoluble and compact and does not upset the water balance of the cell. Starch is therefore the practical marker of photosynthesis: a leaf that has photosynthesised recently contains starch, and starch is detected with iodine solution, which turns from yellow-brown to blue-black when it meets starch.

The investigation has a fixed sequence, and each step has a reason that the mark sheet rewards. First, destarch the plant by keeping it in the dark for at least 48 hours, so that any starch found afterwards was made during the experiment. Second, expose the leaf to light for several hours; a control leaf is covered with black paper or foil, or the whole plant is kept in the dark, so that the only difference between the two is light. Third, remove the leaf and boil it in water for about one minute. This kills the cells and softens the leaf so that the iodine can enter. Fourth, boil the leaf in ethanol. Ethanol removes the chlorophyll so the colour change can be seen; the ethanol turns green and the leaf becomes pale and brittle. Fifth, dip the leaf in warm water to soften it again. Sixth, spread the leaf on a white tile and cover it with iodine solution.

Safety: ethanol is highly flammable and is never heated over an open flame. The ethanol is placed in a test tube or beaker standing in the hot water that was used for the first boiling, with the Bunsen burner turned off. This is a water bath, and its use is itself an examinable point.

Results: the leaf that was in the light turns blue-black, showing starch, showing photosynthesis took place. The covered leaf stays yellow-brown, showing no starch, showing no photosynthesis without light. A variegated leaf, as described, turns blue-black only where it was green. The conclusion is written as: light and chlorophyll are necessary for photosynthesis, and starch is a product of it.

Layout of an investigation for marks: aim, hypothesis, variables with the independent variable light and the dependent variable the presence of starch, the controlled variables, method in numbered steps, results, conclusion. A conclusion that says "the leaf turned blue" without saying "therefore starch is present, therefore photosynthesis occurred" earns only the observation mark.

The questions for this section are with you now: the six steps and the reason for each, the water bath, and how the covered leaf acts as the control.

## Subtopic: From Glucose to Starch, Cellulose and More

Glucose is the first product, but a plant is not made of glucose. It converts the sugar into a family of compounds, each for a purpose. Starch is for storage: long chains of glucose, insoluble, packed into grains. Plants store starch in leaves overnight and in long-term stores: potato tubers, maize kernels, rice grains, wheat seeds, and the roots of sweet potato and cassava. The staple diet of South Africa, maize meal for pap, is largely stored plant starch. Cellulose is for structure: glucose units linked in a different way to form tough fibres, the main material of plant cell walls. Cotton is almost pure cellulose, paper is cellulose from wood, and humans cannot digest it, which is why it passes through us as fibre.

Sucrose is for transport: glucose combined with another sugar, fructose, into a sugar that dissolves well and travels in the phloem to every part of the plant. Sugar cane, grown along the KwaZulu-Natal coast and in Mpumalanga, stores sucrose in its stem, and table sugar is sucrose crystallised from that juice. Fruits are sweet with sucrose and glucose to attract animals that spread the seeds.

Fats and oils are stored energy, richer per gram than starch, which is why seeds that must germinate quickly store oil: sunflower seeds from the Free State, peanuts, avocado. Proteins need an element glucose does not contain, nitrogen. Plants absorb nitrogen as nitrates, mineral salts dissolved in soil water, and combine them with the carbon compounds from glucose to build amino acids and proteins. This is why nitrogen fertiliser, and the nitrogen fixed by legumes such as beans, matters so much to farming: no nitrates, no protein, poor growth. Glucose itself is also broken down in respiration to release energy for everything the plant does, which is next session's work.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: writing chlorophyll or sunlight as a raw material on the left of the arrow; they are conditions. Two: saying plants take in food from the soil; they take in water and mineral salts, and make their food. Three: saying plants photosynthesise at night; without light they only respire. Four: calling starch the first product; glucose is made first and starch is formed from it. Five: heating ethanol directly over the flame in the starch test; a water bath is required and the method mark is lost without it.

Layout for marks on an equation question: raw materials on the left, products on the right, conditions at the arrow, all four substances named, and the arrow pointing one way. On a compound question: name the compound, say what it is made from, say what it is for, give an example.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four compounds and their purposes, where the nitrogen in protein comes from, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a kitchen with a solar stove, a pantry test with iodine, and a sugar factory that turns one product into bricks, boxes and deliveries — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Plant's Kitchen

Think of a green leaf as a kitchen with a solar stove. Every kitchen needs ingredients, a source of energy and a cook. The two ingredients are carbon dioxide, which drifts in from the air through tiny doors underneath the leaf, and water, which the roots suck up from the soil and send up the stem like a straw. The energy is sunlight. The cook is chlorophyll, the green stuff in the leaf. The cook never gets eaten; the cook just does the work. The ingredients do get used up. That is the difference between a raw material and a condition, and it is why sunlight and chlorophyll sit beside the arrow and not in front of it.

What comes out of the kitchen? Two things. Glucose, which is sugar, the plant's food, and oxygen, a gas the plant does not need much of and lets out of the same tiny doors. So the recipe reads: carbon dioxide plus water, cooked with sunlight by chlorophyll, gives glucose plus oxygen. Say it a few times until it sounds like a recipe you know.

Here is the astonishing bit. Every breath you take is oxygen that came out of a leaf's kitchen, and every meal you eat was either a plant or an animal that ate a plant. A piece of pap is maize starch; the maize made it from air, water and sunlight in a field. The leaf is the only kitchen on the planet that cooks from air and light, and everything else is just moving that food around.

Keep the kitchen in mind and try the questions for this part: the two ingredients, the energy, the cook, and the two things that come out.

## Subtopic: The Pantry Test

A kitchen that has been cooking leaves evidence in the pantry. For a leaf, the pantry is starch. Glucose is sweet and sticky and dissolves in water, so the plant quickly packs it into starch, which is dry and does not dissolve, like turning loose sugar into a sealed packet. If you find starch in a leaf, the kitchen has been working.

The detective's tool is iodine solution. On its own it is yellow-brown, like weak tea. Touch it to starch and it goes blue-black, almost ink. Try it at home on a slice of bread or potato; both are starch, both go blue-black. Try it on an onion, which stores sugar instead, and it stays brown.

But a living leaf is waxy and green, and iodine cannot get in or be seen. So the leaf is prepared. Boil it in water for a minute to kill it and soften it. Then boil it in ethanol, which washes out the green so you can see the colour change; the ethanol goes green and the leaf goes pale. Ethanol catches fire easily, so the tube of ethanol stands in the hot water from the first step with the flame switched off, like warming a baby's bottle in a bowl of hot water. Then soften the leaf in warm water again, lay it flat on a white tile, and drop iodine on it.

To make the test fair, the plant is kept in the dark for two days first, so any starch you find was made during the test and not last week. One leaf is covered with foil as the control. The uncovered leaf goes blue-black: it cooked. The covered one stays brown: no light, no cooking, no starch.

Hold the pantry test and try this part's questions: the colour change, why ethanol is used, and why the flame is off.

## Subtopic: What the Plant Builds With Its Sugar

Glucose is only the first thing the plant makes, like a factory that produces one raw material and then turns it into everything else. Four products come from it. Starch is the pantry: glucose packed away for later, in leaves overnight and in the big stores you eat, potatoes, maize, rice, bread wheat. Cellulose is the bricks: glucose linked in tough fibres that build the walls of every plant cell. Cotton is cellulose. Paper is cellulose. You cannot digest it, which is why it is called fibre and why it keeps your gut moving.

Sucrose is the delivery van. Glucose on its own is awkward to transport, so the plant turns it into sucrose, which dissolves nicely and travels through the phloem tubes to the roots, the fruit and the growing tips. Sugar cane in KwaZulu-Natal is a stem full of sucrose, and the sugar in your tea is that same sucrose. Oils and fats are the long-life batteries: seeds like sunflower pack energy as oil because it stores more energy per gram than starch.

Then there is protein, and protein needs an ingredient sugar does not have: nitrogen. The plant gets it from the soil, dissolved as nitrates, which is what fertiliser mostly is. Air, water and sunlight make the sugar; sugar plus nitrates from the soil makes protein. That is why a farmer who gets rain and sun but no fertiliser gets a yellow, stunted crop.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Recipe: carbon dioxide plus water, with sunlight and chlorophyll, gives glucose plus oxygen. Sunlight and chlorophyll are conditions, not ingredients. Glucose is packed into starch, and iodine turns starch blue-black. Starch for storing, cellulose for building, sucrose for moving, oil for long-term energy, and with nitrates from the soil, protein. Plants do not eat from the soil; they cook from the air.

Hold the kitchen, the pantry test and the sugar factory, and take the final questions of the lesson: the four products, the nitrogen question, and what the plant actually takes from the soil.
