### subtopic_1 — The Word Equation of Photosynthesis
**Assistant:** Why is chlorophyll not a raw material if the plant cannot photosynthesise without it?

**Tutor:** Because being necessary and being consumed are different things, and the word equation records only what is consumed and produced. Carbon dioxide and water go in and are changed: their atoms end up inside the glucose and the oxygen. Chlorophyll takes part in every step, but at the end of the process the same chlorophyll is still there, ready for the next photon. A pigment molecule may be used thousands of times a second without being used up, which is exactly the behaviour of a condition. Sunlight is the same case from the energy side: it is essential, it is absorbed, but it is not a substance, and the equation lists substances on the left and right. So the correct form is carbon dioxide plus water, arrow, glucose plus oxygen, with sunlight and chlorophyll written above the arrow. The examiner is checking two things with that layout. First, that you know which four substances are involved. Second, that you understand the difference between a reactant and a requirement. Learners who write "sunlight plus carbon dioxide plus water" have the right ingredients and the wrong idea, and the mark scheme separates the two. Think of it as the difference between the flour in a loaf and the oven that baked it; the loaf contains the flour, it does not contain the oven.

### subtopic_2 — Where It Happens: Leaves, Chlorophyll and Chloroplasts
**Assistant:** Why do we say photosynthesis needs chlorophyll? Could a plant not just use the light directly?

**Tutor:** Light passing through a cell does nothing useful unless something absorbs it, and absorbing light at the right wavelengths and handing the energy on to chemical reactions is the specific job of a pigment. Chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light strongly and reflects green, which is why leaves look green, and the absorbed energy is what drives the building of glucose. Water and carbon dioxide on their own do not react to make sugar no matter how bright the sun; the pigment is the bridge between the light and the chemistry. The evidence we rely on in Grade 8 is the variegated leaf. Its white patches have cells with everything a green cell has except chlorophyll, including stomata nearby for carbon dioxide and xylem for water. After a day in the light, the iodine test shows blue-black only in the formerly green regions and brown in the white ones. Same light, same water, same gas, different pigment, different result. That is a controlled comparison inside a single leaf, and it is why the chloroplast, the structure that holds chlorophyll, is named as the site of photosynthesis. Cells without chloroplasts, in roots or in the white patches, must be fed sugar through the phloem from the cells that have them.

### subtopic_3 — Testing a Leaf for Starch
**Assistant:** Why test for starch at all? The equation says the product is glucose.

**Tutor:** Because glucose does not stay glucose for long inside a leaf, and starch is both more stable and far easier to detect. Glucose is soluble and sweet; if a leaf accumulated it, the water balance of the cells would be disturbed and the sugar would leak and be respired. So within hours the chloroplast joins glucose molecules into starch grains, which are insoluble and compact and sit quietly until the plant needs them. A leaf that has photosynthesised in the last day therefore contains starch, and a leaf that has not does not, which makes starch an excellent marker of whether the process happened. The second reason is practical: there is a simple, cheap, visible test for starch. Iodine solution is yellow-brown and goes a deep blue-black in contact with starch, and the colour change is unmistakable on a white tile. Testing for glucose directly requires a different reagent, heating, and a colour change that is harder to read. The preparation steps serve the iodine: boiling in water kills the cells and breaks the membranes so the solution can enter; boiling in ethanol dissolves out the chlorophyll so a blue-black result is not hidden behind green; and because ethanol ignites easily, the ethanol tube stands in the hot water with the burner off, a water bath. Every one of those reasons is a mark on the memorandum.

### subtopic_4 — From Glucose to Starch, Cellulose and More
**Assistant:** If plants make their own food from air and water, why do farmers need fertiliser?

**Tutor:** Because the air and water supply carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, and those three elements are all that glucose, starch, cellulose, sucrose and oils contain. A plant can build its energy stores and its cell walls from photosynthesis alone. Proteins are different. Every amino acid contains nitrogen, and some contain sulphur; chlorophyll itself contains magnesium and nitrogen; the molecules that carry energy inside cells contain phosphorus. None of those elements come from carbon dioxide or water. They are taken up by the roots as mineral salts dissolved in soil water, nitrogen chiefly as nitrates. Air is nearly 80 percent nitrogen gas, but plants cannot use the gas; it must first be converted into nitrates, which happens through lightning, through bacteria in the soil, through the root nodules of legumes such as beans and lucerne, and, on a farm, through fertiliser. A field of maize in Mpumalanga with good rain and sun but no nitrates grows pale, stunted plants, because it can make sugar but not protein, and growth is protein. So the honest statement is this: plants make their food from air and water, and they make their bodies from that food plus minerals from the soil. The soil is not a food source; it is a mineral source. That distinction is a favourite short-answer question, and the word "nitrates" is usually the mark.
