### subtopic_1 — The Word Equation of Respiration
**Assistant:** Why is energy written in the equation if it is not a substance?

**Tutor:** Because the energy is the reason the reaction matters, and a word equation is allowed to record it as long as we understand what the word means there. Glucose and oxygen are substances, and carbon dioxide and water are substances; their atoms are rearranged and conserved. Energy is not made from atoms. It was already present, stored in the chemical bonds of the glucose, placed there by photosynthesis from sunlight, and the reaction releases it in a form the cell can use. Writing "plus energy" on the right tells the reader the direction of the energy flow: out of the chemicals and into the cell's work. Leave it off and the equation describes the chemistry but not the biology, because an organism respires precisely to obtain that energy. Compare the photosynthesis equation, where sunlight is written at the arrow, not on the right, because there the energy goes in. So the convention is consistent: energy at the arrow when it is supplied, energy on the right when it is released. The caution for marks is never to call energy a product in the same breath as carbon dioxide and water. If a question asks for the products of respiration, the substances are carbon dioxide and water, and energy is released. That phrasing earns the mark and shows the examiner you know the difference.

### subtopic_2 — Breathing Is Not Respiration
**Assistant:** Why does the examiner care so much about separating breathing from respiration?

**Tutor:** Because the two words name events at completely different scales, and a learner who merges them cannot explain either properly. Breathing is an organ-level, physical process: muscles move the ribs and diaphragm, the lungs fill and empty, and gases are exchanged across the lung surface into the blood. It can be seen and counted, about fifteen times a minute at rest. Respiration is a cell-level, chemical process: inside the mitochondria of every cell, glucose and oxygen react and energy is released. It cannot be seen, it happens in every one of your trillions of cells at once, and it never pauses. The connection between them is supply and demand. Breathing supplies the oxygen that cell respiration needs and removes the carbon dioxide it produces, and the blood is the transport between the two. The test case that settles it is any organism without lungs. A fish exchanges gases through gills, an earthworm through its skin, a plant through stomata and root surfaces, and yet every cell of each one respires exactly as yours do. So breathing is one way of achieving gas exchange, and respiration is what the gas exchange is for. In an exam, a one-line definition that says "respiration is breathing" scores zero; a definition that says "the release of energy from glucose in cells" scores full marks. The vocabulary is the understanding.

### subtopic_3 — Evidence: Limewater, Thermometers and Germinating Seeds
**Assistant:** Why boil the seeds for the control? Why not just use an empty flask?

**Tutor:** Because a good control changes only the one thing being tested, and the thing being tested is whether living seeds respire. An empty flask differs from the experimental flask in two ways at once: it has no seeds and it has nothing living. If its limewater stays clear, you cannot tell whether that is because nothing was respiring or simply because there was nothing in the flask that could release gas by any means, perhaps moisture in the seed coats or microbes on the seed surfaces. Boiled seeds remove that doubt. They are the same seeds, the same mass, the same moisture, the same surface, in the same flask with the same limewater at the same temperature. The only difference is that boiling has killed them. If the flask with living seeds turns the limewater milky and the flask with boiled seeds does not, the carbon dioxide can be attributed to life, which is the claim we want to make. The same logic runs the thermometer version: boiled seeds are the fair comparison for the warm living seeds. It also matters that both flasks are kept in the dark. Seeds that have begun to green could photosynthesise in the light and absorb carbon dioxide, masking the result. Dark, sealed, living against boiled: that design earns the method marks, and the conclusion must say the carbon dioxide and heat came from respiration in living cells.

### subtopic_4 — Photosynthesis and Respiration Together
**Assistant:** If plants respire all night and give off carbon dioxide, how is the air still mostly oxygen-rich?

**Tutor:** Because the two processes are not equal in size, and the imbalance is in oxygen's favour. During the day a leaf in good light photosynthesises many times faster than it respires. It is capturing energy and building glucose far quicker than its cells are spending glucose for their own needs, so in daylight the plant releases much more oxygen than it uses and absorbs much more carbon dioxide than it releases. At night photosynthesis stops completely, the cells carry on respiring at their steady rate, and the plant does take in oxygen and release carbon dioxide, like any animal. But a night of respiration undoes only a fraction of a day of photosynthesis. The surplus glucose becomes growth, wood, fruit, seeds, and the oxygen matching that surplus stays in the air. Add up every plant, alga and photosynthetic bacterium over the planet and over billions of years, and the surplus is the 21 percent oxygen we breathe. The balance is now being disturbed from the other side: burning coal, oil and gas returns carbon that was locked away by ancient photosynthesis, faster than today's plants can absorb it, which is why atmospheric carbon dioxide has risen from about 0,028 percent to about 0,042 percent in two centuries. For the exam, the point to state is simple and precise: plants respire all the time and photosynthesise only in the light, and over a day photosynthesis exceeds respiration, so the net effect of a healthy plant is oxygen out and carbon dioxide in.
