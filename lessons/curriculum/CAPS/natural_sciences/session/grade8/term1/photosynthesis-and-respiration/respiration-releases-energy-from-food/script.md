# Part 1 — Expert

This session establishes respiration as the process by which every living organism releases energy from food. The anchor is the word equation: glucose plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide plus water plus energy. The governing facts are that respiration happens in every living cell, in plants as well as animals, by day and by night, and that it is a chemical process, not the act of breathing. Last session built glucose with photosynthesis; this session takes it apart, and the two equations turn out to be mirror images of each other. Both equations, the evidence for respiration, and the difference between breathing and respiration are examinable.

## Subtopic: The Word Equation of Respiration

Respiration is the process in which living cells break down glucose, using oxygen, to release the energy stored in it. The word equation is: glucose plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide plus water plus energy. Glucose and oxygen are the reactants, on the left. Carbon dioxide and water are the products, on the right. Energy is written on the right because it is released, but it is not a substance; it is the point of the whole process.

This is aerobic respiration, the word aerobic meaning "with air", which here means with oxygen. It happens in every living cell of every living organism: in your muscle cells, in a plant's root cells, in a bacterium, in a fungus, in a fish. There is no living cell that does not respire, and a cell that stops respiring dies within minutes, because it has no energy to maintain itself. Respiration also never switches off. Awake or asleep, in the light or in the dark, the cells are releasing energy.

The energy is used for everything a living thing does. Movement: muscle contraction is powered by it. Growth: building new cells and new proteins. Repair of damaged tissue. Keeping warm: mammals and birds maintain a constant body temperature, around 37 degrees Celsius in humans, and most of the heat comes from respiration. Transport of substances in and out of cells. Transmitting nerve impulses. Reproduction. A plant uses respiration energy to grow, to open its stomata, to move sugars through the phloem, and to absorb minerals from the soil.

Where in the cell does it happen? Mostly in small structures called mitochondria, often called the powerhouses of the cell. Cells that need a great deal of energy, such as muscle cells, have many mitochondria. The detail of how the glucose is dismantled belongs to Grade 11 Life Sciences; in Grade 8 the equation and its meaning are what the mark sheet wants.

The questions for this section are with you now: the word equation in order, where respiration happens, when it happens, and what the energy is used for.

## Subtopic: Breathing Is Not Respiration

The most common error in this topic is to use "respiration" to mean breathing. They are different things, and the examiner tests the difference directly. Breathing is a physical process: the movement of air into and out of the lungs, driven by muscles between the ribs and the diaphragm. Its purpose is gas exchange, getting oxygen into the blood and carbon dioxide out of it. Respiration is a chemical process: the reaction inside cells that releases energy from glucose. Breathing supplies the oxygen that respiration needs and removes the carbon dioxide respiration makes, but breathing itself releases no energy.

Follow the oxygen. Air is drawn into the lungs; oxygen passes into the blood; red blood cells carry it to every part of the body; it leaves the blood and enters a cell; inside the cell, in the mitochondria, it reacts with glucose. Carbon dioxide takes the reverse journey: made in the cell, into the blood, to the lungs, out with the next breath. The glucose came from digested food, carried by the blood from the small intestine. So the blood is a delivery system joining the three organ systems: digestive, which provides glucose, breathing, which provides oxygen and removes carbon dioxide, and circulatory, which carries all of it to and from the cells where respiration actually happens.

Organisms without lungs respire just as much. A fish takes oxygen from water through its gills. An earthworm absorbs it through its moist skin. An insect has tiny tubes opening along its body. A plant has no lungs and no blood; oxygen simply enters through the stomata of the leaves and through the surface of the roots, and carbon dioxide leaves the same way. All of them respire; only some of them breathe.

Breathing rate changes with the demand for energy. Run up a flight of stairs and you breathe faster and your heart beats faster, because the muscle cells are respiring faster and need oxygen delivered and carbon dioxide removed at a higher rate. The faster breathing is the response; the respiration in the muscles is the cause.

The questions for this section are with you now: the one-line difference between breathing and respiration, the route of oxygen from air to mitochondrion, and organisms that respire without breathing.

## Subtopic: Evidence: Limewater, Thermometers and Germinating Seeds

The products of respiration can be detected, and the standard investigations are examinable. Carbon dioxide is detected with limewater, which is clear and turns milky, or cloudy white, when carbon dioxide bubbles through it. Blow gently through a straw into a test tube of limewater and it turns milky within a few breaths, showing that exhaled air contains more carbon dioxide than the air you inhaled. The control is to pump room air through a second tube of limewater with a bicycle pump or a syringe; it stays clear for far longer, because air contains only about 0,04 percent carbon dioxide while exhaled air contains about 4 percent, a hundred times more.

Germinating seeds demonstrate that plants respire. Soak bean or maize seeds until they begin to sprout, place them in a sealed flask with a small tube of limewater, and in a day the limewater turns milky. The control is an identical flask of boiled, dead seeds, which leaves its limewater clear. Only living seeds produce carbon dioxide, and the seeds are in the dark, so there is no photosynthesis to confuse the result.

Heat is detected with a thermometer. Fill a vacuum flask with germinating seeds, fit a thermometer through the stopper, and set up a control flask of boiled seeds. After a day the living seeds are several degrees warmer. The energy released by respiration is partly heat, which is also why a compost heap is warm inside, why a crowded classroom heats up, and why your body is warm at all.

Water is harder to show, but breathing onto a cold mirror or window produces condensation, water vapour from the lungs, part of which is respiration's product. Oxygen use can be shown with a burning candle: a candle in a sealed jar with a living mouse would use up oxygen faster than one with no mouse, a classic experiment from history, and the modern version uses an oxygen sensor.

Layout for an investigation: aim, hypothesis, independent variable, which is living against dead seeds, dependent variable, which is the limewater or the temperature, controlled variables such as the same number of seeds and the same flask, numbered method, results in a table, conclusion that names respiration. The conclusion must say "carbon dioxide was produced, therefore the seeds were respiring"; the milky limewater is the observation, not the conclusion.

The questions for this section are with you now: the limewater test and its control, the two seed experiments, and why dead seeds are the right control.

## Subtopic: Photosynthesis and Respiration Together

Place the two word equations side by side. Photosynthesis: carbon dioxide plus water, with sunlight and chlorophyll, gives glucose plus oxygen. Respiration: glucose plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide plus water plus energy. They are reverses of each other. What one builds, the other dismantles; what one releases, the other consumes. Photosynthesis stores light energy as chemical energy in glucose; respiration releases that chemical energy for the cell to use. Energy enters living systems once, as sunlight captured by plants, and every organism that is not a plant gets its share by eating.

Plants do both. A green plant photosynthesises only in the light, but it respires all the time. In bright daylight photosynthesis is much faster than respiration, so the plant takes in more carbon dioxide than it releases and gives out more oxygen than it uses; the net effect is oxygen out, carbon dioxide in. At night photosynthesis stops, respiration continues, and the plant takes in oxygen and gives out carbon dioxide like an animal. Over a whole day a healthy plant produces far more oxygen than it consumes, which is why the atmosphere holds 21 percent oxygen. The old advice not to keep plants in a bedroom overnight is based on this but exaggerates it: a pot plant respires far less than the person sleeping beside it.

This is the carbon-oxygen cycle in miniature. Carbon dioxide from respiration, from decay, from burning wood and fuels, is taken up by plants; oxygen from photosynthesis is used in respiration and burning. The two processes have kept the atmosphere in rough balance for a very long time, and the rising carbon dioxide level of the last two centuries, from burning coal and oil faster than plants can absorb it, is a disturbance of that balance.

Energy in food is measured in kilojoules, and the amounts on food labels are respiration amounts: the energy a body can release from the food. A hundred grams of maize meal contains about 1500 kilojoules; an apple about 250; a teaspoon of sugar about 70. A Grade 8 learner needs roughly 9000 to 11 000 kilojoules a day, more when active. All of it is released by respiration.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: respiration means breathing; it is the chemical release of energy in cells. Two: plants do not respire; they respire all the time, in every cell. Three: plants respire only at night; they respire day and night and photosynthesise only in the day. Four: energy is a substance made by respiration; it is released, and is written on the right only to show that. Five: the milky limewater proves respiration; it proves carbon dioxide, and the conclusion must link the gas to living cells.

Layout for marks on a comparison question: a two-column table, process by process. Where: photosynthesis in chloroplasts of green cells, respiration in mitochondria of all cells. When: light only, against always. Energy: stored, against released. Gases: carbon dioxide in and oxygen out, against oxygen in and carbon dioxide out. Four rows, full marks.

The questions for this section are with you now: the two equations side by side, day against night in a plant, kilojoules, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a braai fire that burns without flames, a delivery truck that is not the kitchen, and a garden by day and by night — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Burning Without Flames

A braai fire needs two things: fuel, which is the wood or charcoal, and oxygen from the air. Fan it and it burns faster. Put a lid on it and it goes out. What comes off the fire? Heat and light, plus smoke, which is mostly carbon dioxide and water vapour. Fuel plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide plus water plus energy.

Now read that again, because that is the respiration equation. Your cells are burning fuel. The fuel is glucose, the sugar from the food you ate. The oxygen came in with your breath. The products are carbon dioxide, which you breathe out, and water, and energy. The only difference from the braai is that there are no flames: the burning happens slowly, in thousands of tiny controlled steps, inside little structures in your cells called mitochondria, at body temperature, so the energy comes out in a usable form instead of a blaze.

That energy is for everything. Lifting your arm. Growing a centimetre. Healing a cut. Keeping you at 37 degrees on a cold Johannesburg morning, which is why you feel warm to the touch; you are a slow fire. Every single cell is doing it, all the time, even when you are asleep. Switch it off and the cell dies.

Keep the braai in mind and try the questions for this part: the fuel, the gas that is needed, what comes out, and why there are no flames.

## Subtopic: Breathing Is Delivery, Respiration Is the Fire

Here is the mix-up that costs the most marks. People say "respiration" when they mean breathing. They are not the same. Breathing is the delivery truck. Your ribs and diaphragm pull air in; oxygen gets into your blood in the lungs; the blood drives it to every cell in your body. Then it collects the carbon dioxide rubbish and drives it back to the lungs, and you breathe it out. That is all breathing does: deliver oxygen, remove carbon dioxide.

Respiration is the fire itself, inside the cell, where glucose and oxygen finally meet and energy is released. The truck is not the kitchen. The truck feeds the kitchen.

Proof that they are different: a fish does not breathe air, but every fish cell respires. An earthworm has no lungs; oxygen soaks in through its wet skin. A tree has no lungs and no blood at all, and yet every cell in it respires, taking oxygen straight in through the pores in its leaves and the surface of its roots. All living things respire. Only some of them breathe.

And when you sprint for the taxi? Your leg muscles respire much faster, so they need more oxygen delivered and more carbon dioxide taken away. That is why you breathe hard and your heart pounds. The hard breathing is the delivery service working overtime because the fires in your legs are roaring.

Hold the truck and the fire and try this part's questions: which one is chemical, which one is physical, and who respires without breathing.

## Subtopic: Day and Night in the Garden

Last lesson the leaf was a kitchen cooking glucose from air and light. This lesson the same leaf is burning that glucose for energy. Both at once? Yes, and here is how it works. Every cell in the plant respires all the time, day and night, just as yours do. But only the green cells photosynthesise, and only when there is light.

In the daytime the kitchen is running flat out. It makes far more glucose than the plant burns, and gives off far more oxygen than it uses. Net result: the plant takes in carbon dioxide and gives out oxygen, which is what everyone remembers. At night the kitchen shuts. The fire keeps burning. The plant now uses oxygen and gives out carbon dioxide, just like an animal. Over a full day the kitchen wins by a mile, which is why the air is a fifth oxygen.

You can catch the plant respiring with germinating seeds in a sealed jar in the dark, with a little tube of limewater inside. Limewater is clear water that turns milky when carbon dioxide bubbles through it. Living seeds turn it milky in a day; boiled, dead seeds leave it clear. Put a thermometer in a flask of sprouting seeds and it reads a few degrees warmer than a flask of dead ones: the fire gives off heat, exactly like a warm compost heap.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Respiration: glucose plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide plus water plus energy. It happens in every living cell, always, with no flames. Breathing is the delivery; respiration is the fire. Plants respire too, day and night, and photosynthesise only in the light, so by day they give out oxygen overall and by night they give out carbon dioxide. Limewater goes milky for carbon dioxide; a thermometer catches the heat. And the two equations are each other backwards: one stores the Sun's energy in sugar, the other spends it.

Hold the braai, the truck and the garden at night, and take the final questions of the lesson: the equations as mirror images, what a plant does in the dark, and what milky limewater proves.
