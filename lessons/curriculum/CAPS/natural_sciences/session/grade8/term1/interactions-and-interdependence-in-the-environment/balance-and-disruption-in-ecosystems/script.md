# Part 1 — Expert

This session examines what it means for an ecosystem to be in balance, and what knocks it out of balance. The anchor is the relationship between impala and lions in a savanna reserve, with their numbers rising and falling against each other year after year without either disappearing. The governing idea is that balance in an ecosystem is dynamic: populations change constantly, but the changes stay within limits set by food, water, space, predators and disease. Disruption occurs when something pushes a population past those limits, and the disruptors are either natural, such as drought, flood, fire and disease, or human, such as habitat destruction, pollution, overharvesting and the introduction of alien species. Definitions, South African examples and the reading of a population graph are all examinable.

## Subtopic: What Balance Means

An ecosystem is in balance when the populations of its organisms remain more or less stable over time, with births and immigration roughly matching deaths and emigration. The word to hold onto is dynamic. Balance does not mean nothing changes. It means that the changes correct themselves. In a good rainy season grass grows thickly, impala have more food, more calves survive, and the impala population rises. More impala means more food for lions, so more cubs survive and the lion population rises a year or two later. More lions eat more impala, so the impala population falls; with less prey, lion cubs starve and the lion population falls; with fewer lions, the impala recover. Plotted on a graph, the two populations rise and fall in waves, the predator wave lagging behind the prey wave, and neither line reaches zero.

The limits on a population are called limiting factors: the amount of food, the water supply, the space and shelter available, the number of predators, the spread of disease and parasites, and competition with other species for the same resources. The largest population an area can support over a long period is its carrying capacity. A reserve of a given size and rainfall can carry a certain number of elephants; beyond that the trees are destroyed faster than they regrow, and the whole system degrades. The Kruger National Park has debated its elephant carrying capacity for decades.

Interdependence makes balance possible. Grass depends on dung and dead material being decomposed; herbivores depend on grass; predators depend on herbivores; the grass depends on herbivores being kept in check by predators so that it is not grazed to the roots. Remove any one link and the correction fails. Where predators have been shot out, as on many farms, herbivore numbers must be managed by people, through culling or selling live animals, because the natural correction is gone.

Reading a graph for marks: identify the axes and units, describe the trend of each line, note the lag between predator and prey peaks, and explain each rise and fall by the cause in the chain: "the lion population rises after the impala population because more prey allows more cubs to survive."

The questions for this section are with you now: dynamic balance, four limiting factors, carrying capacity, and reading the predator-prey graph.

## Subtopic: Natural Disruptions

Ecosystems have always been disturbed by natural events, and most have evolved to recover from them. Drought is the most important in South Africa, a dry country with an average rainfall of about 460 millimetres a year against a world average of about 860. In a drought the grass fails, waterholes dry, herbivores weaken and die in large numbers, predators and scavengers feast briefly and then decline, and the population graph for every species dips. When the rains return, grass regrows from seed and root, herbivore numbers recover over several seasons, and the system returns towards its previous balance. The 2015 to 2016 drought killed thousands of buffalo and hundreds of hippo in the Kruger; the populations have since recovered.

Floods do the opposite kind of damage: they drown animals, strip soil, uproot vegetation and spread disease, but they also deposit fertile silt and fill wetlands, and floodplain ecosystems such as the Pongola depend on them. Fire is a natural part of the grassland, savanna and fynbos biomes. Lightning fires remove dead grass, release minerals in the ash, and in fynbos trigger the germination of seeds that will not sprout without heat and smoke. Fire too often, or at the wrong time of year, destroys the seed bank and kills young plants before they have seeded, so fire is a disruption or a necessity depending on frequency, and that nuance is examinable.

Disease and parasites act as limiting factors and sometimes as disasters. Rinderpest, a cattle virus introduced to Africa in the 1890s, killed most of the buffalo and many antelope across the continent and reshaped the savanna for decades. Locust swarms, outbreaks of army worm, and sudden plagues of rodents after good rains all cause temporary disruption. Volcanic eruptions and earthquakes are not a South African concern, but a volcanic ash cloud can cool the whole planet for a year or two, as happened after Pinatubo in 1991.

The pattern for natural disruptions is this: they are usually temporary, the ecosystem has mechanisms to recover, and the recovery takes a time proportional to the damage: months for a grass fire, years for a drought, decades for a forest. The distinction from human disruption is that natural disruption is usually followed by recovery, while many human disruptions are permanent or continuous.

The questions for this section are with you now: how a drought moves through a food web, when fire helps and when it harms, and why natural disruptions are usually temporary.

## Subtopic: Human Disruptions

Humans disrupt ecosystems in ways that are larger, faster and more permanent than most natural events, and the mark sheet expects you to name the categories with a South African example for each.

Habitat destruction is the largest single cause of species loss. Ploughing grassland for maize, clearing bush for cattle, draining wetlands, cutting forest, building suburbs over fynbos on the Cape Flats, and opening mines all remove the homes organisms depend on. Less than three percent of the original Highveld grassland is formally protected; most has gone under the plough or the city.

Pollution poisons or smothers ecosystems. Water pollution: sewage, fertiliser run-off and industrial waste in the Vaal and Hartbeespoort systems feed algal blooms that strip oxygen from the water and kill fish; acid mine drainage from old gold mines on the West Rand carries acid and heavy metals into streams. Air pollution: sulphur dioxide and particulates from power stations and industry on the Mpumalanga Highveld damage plants and human lungs. Soil and litter: plastic bags are eaten by cattle and sea turtles, and fishing line and nets kill seabirds and seals.

Overharvesting takes organisms faster than they reproduce. Abalone, perlemoen, poached from the Western Cape coast has collapsed; West Coast rock lobster and linefish such as the seventy-four are at a fraction of their former numbers; rhino poaching for horn killed more than a thousand rhino a year in South Africa at the peak around 2014 and 2015. Illegal hunting of bushmeat and the collection of cycads and succulents for sale empty the land of species that cannot replace themselves.

Overgrazing and poor farming expose soil. Too many cattle or goats strip the grass, rain washes the topsoil away, gullies called dongas form, and the land is permanently degraded; parts of the Eastern Cape and the Karoo show the result. Alien invasive species, treated in the next section, crowd out indigenous life. Finally, climate change, from carbon dioxide released by burning coal, oil and gas, is shifting rainfall patterns and temperatures faster than many species can adjust; the 2015 to 2018 Cape Town drought, which brought the city close to Day Zero, is the kind of event expected to become more frequent.

The questions for this section are with you now: five categories of human disruption each with a South African example, why habitat destruction is the worst, and how fertiliser run-off kills fish.

## Subtopic: Alien Invaders and Reading a Case Study

An alien species is one that has been brought, deliberately or accidentally, to a region where it did not occur naturally. It becomes invasive when it spreads and causes harm, and the reason it can is that it has left behind the predators, parasites and competitors that kept it in check at home, while the local ecosystem has nothing adapted to control it. South Africa has one of the worst invasive plant problems in the world.

Water hyacinth, a floating plant from South America, covers dams such as Hartbeespoort in a solid mat, blocks light to the plants below, strips oxygen from the water as it rots, and chokes pumps and boats. Black wattle, pine and eucalyptus from Australia and Europe, planted for timber and shelter, have spread along rivers and across mountain catchments, where they use far more water than the fynbos or grassland they replace; the Working for Water programme, started in 1995, employs thousands of people to clear them precisely because the trees were drying up rivers. Lantana and the famine weed Parthenium invade bushveld and farmland; prickly pear was once so bad in the Eastern Cape that a moth and a cochineal insect were imported to control it, an early success of biological control. Among animals, the house crow in Durban, the mallard duck that hybridises with the yellow-billed duck, and smallmouth bass that have emptied Cape rivers of indigenous fish are examples.

Reading a case study for marks is a fixed procedure. One: identify the disruption and classify it, natural or human, and the category. Two: identify the organisms affected directly, by the arrows of the food web. Three: trace the effect one or two steps further. Four: state the consequence for people. Five: suggest a response and say why it would work. For water hyacinth: human introduction of an alien species; blocks light to submerged plants, which die, and reduces oxygen, which kills fish; fewer fish for anglers and blocked pumps for irrigation; mechanical removal plus a biological control weevil that eats only hyacinth, because chemicals would poison the dam.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: balance means no change; it means change within limits that correct themselves. Two: fire always destroys; in fynbos and grassland the right fire frequency is necessary. Three: a species that is harmful is alien; some indigenous species are pests and some aliens are harmless; alien means introduced, invasive means spreading and harming. Four: pollution is only litter; chemicals, sewage, fertiliser and gases are the larger problem. Five: a case study answer that stops at "the fish die" without tracing why, through light, plants and oxygen.

Layout for marks: name the disruption, classify it, trace the effect through the web, state the human consequence, propose and justify a response.

The questions for this section are with you now: alien against invasive, why invaders succeed, Working for Water, and the five-step case study method.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a seesaw that wobbles, a bush that heals itself, and a guest who will not leave — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Seesaw

Picture two children on a seesaw in the park, an impala on one end and a lion on the other. It is never perfectly level; it rocks. Good rains, lots of grass, lots of impala, and the impala end goes down. More impala means well-fed lions and more cubs, and a year later the lion end goes down and the impala end comes up, because more lions are eating more impala. Fewer impala means hungry lions and fewer cubs, so the lion end lifts, the impala recover, and round it goes. Up, down, up, down, but nobody falls off. That is what balance in an ecosystem means. Not stillness. A wobble that fixes itself.

What stops the seesaw tipping right over? The limits. Only so much grass, only so much water, only so much space and shelter, only so many animals before disease spreads and before the predators find them. Those are the limiting factors, and the biggest crowd an area can feed for a long time without being wrecked is its carrying capacity. Pack a reserve with too many elephants and they flatten the trees faster than trees can grow; the park has gone past its carrying capacity.

The seesaw works because everyone leans on everyone. Grass needs the dung and dead leaves rotted down; impala need grass; lions need impala; and the grass needs lions, because without them the impala would eat it to the roots. Take out one rider, shoot out the predators on a farm, say, and the seesaw jams. Then people have to be the lions, selling or culling animals to keep the numbers down.

Keep the seesaw in mind and try the questions for this part: why the lion wave comes after the impala wave, what a limiting factor is, and what carrying capacity means.

## Subtopic: When the Bush Fights Back

Nature knocks the seesaw itself sometimes. Drought is our big one; South Africa gets about half the world's average rain. The grass dies, waterholes dry, animals die in numbers, and every line on the graph dips. Then the rain comes back, the grass grows from seed, and over a few seasons the herds recover. The Kruger lost thousands of buffalo and hundreds of hippo in the 2015 and 2016 drought, and they came back.

Floods drown and wash away, but they also leave fertile mud and refill wetlands. Fire sounds like pure destruction, but the grassland and the fynbos actually need it. A fire clears old dead grass, puts minerals back into the soil as ash, and some fynbos seeds will not even sprout until they have felt heat and smoke. The trouble is fire too often, before the young plants have made seed, or at the wrong season. So fire is medicine in the right dose and poison in the wrong one, and an exam might ask you to say both.

Disease knocks things too. A cattle virus called rinderpest arrived in the 1890s and wiped out most of the buffalo in Africa; the bush looked different for decades afterwards. Locust swarms strip fields in a day. The point about all these natural knocks is that the bush fights back: months to recover from a grass fire, years from a drought, decades for a forest. Usually temporary, usually healable. Hold that thought, because the next kind of knock is different.

Hold the healing bush and try this part's questions: how drought travels through the food web, the right dose of fire, and why nature's knocks are usually temporary.

## Subtopic: The Guest Who Will Not Leave

Now the knocks that do not heal, the human ones. Plough the grassland for mealies, build houses on the Cape Flats, drain the wetland, dig the mine: the homes are gone and so are the animals and plants that lived there. Habitat destruction is the biggest killer of species on Earth. Pour sewage and fertiliser into the Vaal and the water turns green with algae that suck out the oxygen, and the fish float up dead. Let acid leak out of old gold mines on the West Rand and the streams go orange and lifeless. Take perlemoen off the rocks faster than it can breed and it is gone; shoot a thousand rhino a year for horn and you can watch the graph fall. Put too many goats on a hillside and the grass goes, then the soil goes, and a donga opens that never closes.

And then the guest who will not leave. Someone brings a plant or animal from another continent, and it has left behind everything that used to eat it or compete with it, while nothing here knows what to do with it. Water hyacinth from South America blankets Hartbeespoort Dam, blocks the light, rots and strips the oxygen, and jams the pumps. Black wattle and pine from Australia and Europe march down our rivers and drink far more water than the fynbos they replaced, which is why the Working for Water programme has paid thousands of people since 1995 to chop them out: it is cheaper than building another dam. Alien means it was brought here. Invasive means it is spreading and doing damage. Not every alien is invasive, and some home-grown species are pests too.

When an exam gives you a case study, walk five steps. What is the disruption and is it natural or human? Who is hit first? Who is hit next, following the arrows? What does it cost people? What should be done, and why would that work? For the hyacinth: human, alien species; blocks light so underwater plants die; less oxygen so fish die; anglers and farmers lose out; pull it out and release the weevil that eats only hyacinth, because poison would kill the dam.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Balance is a wobble that fixes itself, held by limiting factors and carrying capacity. Natural knocks, drought, flood, fire, disease, usually heal. Human knocks, habitat destruction, pollution, overharvesting, overgrazing, aliens, climate change, often do not. Alien is brought in; invasive is spreading and harming. And every case study is five steps: name it, who first, who next, what it costs, what to do and why.

Hold the seesaw, the healing bush and the unwanted guest, and take the final questions of the lesson: alien against invasive, the hyacinth chain, and the five steps.
