# Part 1 — Expert

This session traces energy through an ecosystem. The anchor chain is grass, arrow, impala, arrow, lion, with decomposers acting on every link, and the anchor figures are 10 000 kilojoules of energy stored in the grass, 1000 reaching the impala, 100 reaching the lion. The governing ideas are that a food chain always starts with a producer, that each position along it is a trophic level, that real ecosystems are food webs of many interlinked chains, and that roughly 90 percent of the energy is lost at every transfer, so chains are short and top predators are few. The arrow convention, the trophic names, the reading of a web and the energy arithmetic are all examinable.

## Subtopic: Food Chains and the Arrow

A food chain is a sequence showing how energy and food pass from one organism to the next in an ecosystem. It is written as a line of organisms joined by arrows, and the arrow always points from the organism that is eaten to the organism that eats it, because the arrow records the direction in which energy flows. Grass, arrow, impala, arrow, lion. Acacia leaves, arrow, kudu, arrow, leopard. In the sea off the Western Cape: phytoplankton, arrow, zooplankton, arrow, sardine, arrow, Cape gannet.

Every food chain begins with a producer, because producers are the only organisms that bring energy into the living world, capturing sunlight by photosynthesis. The Sun is the ultimate source of the energy, and some diagrams show it before the producer, but the Sun is not an organism and is not a link in the chain. After the producer come consumers: the first consumer is a herbivore, the next a carnivore that eats the herbivore, and so on. Most chains have three or four links; five is unusual and six almost unknown, and the reason is the energy loss treated in the final section.

Decomposers are not usually written in the line, but they act on every link. Dead grass, impala dung, a lion that dies of old age: bacteria and fungi break all of it down and return the minerals to the soil that grows the grass. A complete diagram often shows decomposers off to one side with arrows from every organism into them.

Reading a chain for marks: identify the producer, name the consumers in order, and describe the arrows correctly. A common question gives the chain and asks what the arrow represents; the answer is "the direction of energy flow" or "is eaten by", not "eats". Another gives the organisms jumbled and asks for the chain to be constructed: start from the only organism that photosynthesises, then ask of each organism who eats it.

The questions for this section are with you now: what the arrow means, why a producer must come first, and a three-link chain from a dam and one from the sea.

## Subtopic: Trophic Levels

Each position in a food chain is a trophic level, trophic meaning feeding. The producer occupies the first trophic level. The herbivore that eats it is the primary consumer, at the second trophic level. The carnivore that eats the herbivore is the secondary consumer, at the third trophic level. A carnivore that eats that carnivore is the tertiary consumer, at the fourth trophic level. Grass is trophic level one, impala two, lion three. Phytoplankton one, zooplankton two, sardine three, gannet four.

Note the difference between the two kinds of numbering. "Primary, secondary, tertiary" count consumers; "first, second, third, fourth" count trophic levels, including the producer. So the primary consumer is at the second trophic level. Examiners test exactly this offset.

An organism can occupy different trophic levels in different chains. A jackal eating a locust is a secondary consumer, at level three; the same jackal eating a lizard that ate the locust is a tertiary consumer, at level four. A baboon eating fruit is a primary consumer; eating a scorpion it is a secondary consumer. Omnivores by definition sit at more than one level. The level belongs to the position in a particular chain, not to the species.

The top carnivore in a chain is often called the apex predator: the lion in the savanna, the great white shark along the coast, the martial eagle in the sky over the Karoo. Apex predators are few in number for reasons that follow from the energy arithmetic.

Layout for marks: when asked for the trophic level, give both the name and the number where possible, "secondary consumer, third trophic level", and when asked to justify, refer to what the organism ate in that chain. "The lizard is a secondary consumer because it eats the locust, which is a herbivore."

The questions for this section are with you now: the four trophic levels named and numbered, the offset between consumer order and level number, and one organism at two levels.

## Subtopic: Food Webs

Real ecosystems are not single chains. Grass is eaten by impala, zebra, locusts and termites. Impala are eaten by lions, leopards, wild dogs and cheetahs. Locusts are eaten by lizards, birds and jackals. Lizards are eaten by snakes and eagles. Drawing every chain in an area, with the shared organisms drawn once, produces a food web: a diagram of interconnected food chains showing all the feeding relationships in a community.

A food web is read the same way as a chain. Every arrow points from food to feeder. Producers are at the bottom, usually with no arrows coming into them. Apex predators are at the top, with no arrows leaving them except to the decomposers. Each individual chain can be traced through the web by following arrows from a producer to an end point: grass to locust to lizard to eagle is one chain inside the web; grass to zebra to lion is another.

Food webs show interdependence and resilience. Because a lion eats zebra, impala, kudu, buffalo and warthog, a bad year for impala does not starve the lions; they shift to other prey. A species with only one food source is in a far more precarious position. Webs also show how a change spreads. Remove the jackals, and the locusts and rodents they ate increase; the increased locusts eat more grass; the grazers have less; and the grazers' predators feel it. Add an introduced species that nothing eats, such as the water hyacinth choking the Hartbeespoort Dam, and it spreads unchecked.

Exam questions on webs are predictable. Name a producer, a herbivore, a carnivore, an omnivore from the web. Write out one food chain from the web with at least four organisms. State the trophic level of a named organism in a named chain. Predict what would happen to population X if population Y were removed, and give the reasoning through the arrows: "If the lizards disappeared, the locust population would increase because fewer are eaten; the eagles would decline because one of their food sources is gone, or would eat more snakes." Two or three steps of reasoning, each tied to an arrow, earn the marks.

The questions for this section are with you now: how a web differs from a chain, tracing one chain through a web, and predicting the effect of removing one species.

## Subtopic: Energy Loss Along the Chain

Energy enters at the producer and is lost at every step. Of the sunlight falling on a field of grass, only about one percent is captured by photosynthesis; the rest is reflected, passes through, or warms the ground. Of the energy stored in the grass, only about ten percent ends up stored in the bodies of the impala that eat it. Of the energy in the impala, only about ten percent ends up stored in the lions. This is the ten percent rule, and it is an approximation, but a reliable one.

Where does the other ninety percent go? Four places. First, not all of the lower level is eaten: roots, bark and thorns, bones and hooves and hide. Second, not all of what is eaten is digested: dung carries energy away, which is why dung beetles can live on it. Third, most of what is digested is respired: the impala uses energy to run, breathe, keep warm, grow and repair itself, and that energy leaves as heat. Fourth, some leaves as other waste, such as urine. Only the energy built into new body tissue is available to the next level.

Use the anchor figures. Grass holds 10 000 kilojoules. The impala population feeding on it stores 1000 kilojoules, ten percent. The lions store 100 kilojoules. A fourth level would receive 10 kilojoules, and a fifth, 1 kilojoule: not enough to support a population of large animals. That is why food chains are short, why there are far fewer lions than impala and far fewer impala than blades of grass, and why a pyramid is the natural shape to draw. A pyramid of energy has the producer as a wide base and each level above it one tenth as wide. A pyramid of numbers shows the count of organisms at each level and usually has the same shape, though it can be distorted when one producer is enormous: a single acacia tree feeds thousands of caterpillars.

The arithmetic also explains human food choices. A hectare of maize fed to people directly supports about ten times as many of them as the same maize fed to cattle whose meat is then eaten, because the cattle respire away ninety percent. It is why most of the world's staple foods are plants.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: arrows drawn from the eater to the eaten. Two: the Sun written as the first organism; it is the energy source, not a link. Three: "the energy disappears"; it is transferred to the surroundings mainly as heat from respiration, and energy is never destroyed. Four: the primary consumer placed at the first trophic level; the producer is first. Five: claiming a long chain of eight animals; the ten percent rule makes it impossible.

Layout for marks on an energy question: state the rule, show the calculation as ten percent of the previous level, give the unit kilojoules, and in a "why" question name the four routes of loss with respiration as heat the largest.

The questions for this section are with you now: the ten percent calculation from 10 000 kilojoules, the four routes of loss, and why chains are short.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a relay race, a spider's web you pull on, and a bucket with a hole in it — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Follow the Sandwich

Think of a food chain as a relay race where the baton is energy. The Sun passes it to the grass, because grass is the only runner that can take it from the Sun. The grass passes it to the impala that eats the grass. The impala passes it to the lion. Grass, arrow, impala, arrow, lion. The arrow always points to the one who receives the baton, the eater, because the arrow shows where the energy goes. It does not show who is chasing whom.

The race always starts with a plant, or algae, or seaweed, because only they can take the baton from the Sun. If you are given a pile of names and told to make a chain, find the one that is green and photosynthesises and put it first. Then ask, "who eats this?", draw an arrow to that one, and ask again.

Each runner has a position name. First is the producer. Second is the primary consumer, which is always a herbivore. Third is the secondary consumer, a meat-eater. Fourth is the tertiary consumer. Those positions are called trophic levels, feeding levels. Watch the counting trap: the primary consumer is at the second level, because the producer took the first spot.

A sea version: tiny floating plants called phytoplankton, eaten by tiny floating animals called zooplankton, eaten by sardines, eaten by a gannet diving off the Cape coast. Four runners, four levels, one baton from the Sun.

Keep the relay in mind and try the questions for this part: where the arrow points, who runs first, and which level the herbivore is on.

## Subtopic: One Menu, Many Links

Nobody in the bush eats just one thing. Lions eat zebra, impala, kudu, warthog, buffalo. Impala are eaten by lions, leopards, cheetahs, wild dogs. Locusts are eaten by lizards, birds and jackals. Draw all of that at once, each animal written once with arrows from all its food to it, and the chains cross and join into a net. That net is a food web: every chain in the area, drawn on one page.

Think of it as a spider's web. Pull one strand and the whole thing shivers. Take out the jackals, and the locusts and mice they used to eat multiply; more locusts means less grass; less grass means hungry grazers; hungry grazers means hungry lions. One tug, four strands moved. That is what an exam means when it asks "what would happen if the jackals were removed?" You trace the arrows: fewer jackals, more locusts, less grass, and so on, two or three steps, each one following an arrow.

The web also shows safety in variety. A lion with five kinds of prey survives a bad impala year by eating more zebra. An animal with one food only is in trouble the moment that food fails. And something new that nothing eats, like the water hyacinth smothering Hartbeespoort Dam, spreads with no strand pulling back on it.

To find a single chain inside a web, start at a plant at the bottom and follow the arrows upward until you run out: grass to locust to lizard to eagle. That is one chain. Grass to zebra to lion is another. Same web, different routes.

Hold the spider's web and try this part's questions: what a web shows that a chain does not, tracing one route, and what happens when you pull a strand.

## Subtopic: The Leaky Bucket

Here is the question that explains the whole bush: if there is so much grass, why are there so few lions? Picture the energy as water carried in a bucket with a hole in it. The grass fills the bucket with 10 000 kilojoules. The impala eat the grass, but by the time the energy is stored in impala bodies, nine tenths has leaked out. The impala bucket holds 1000. The lions eat the impala and nine tenths leaks again. The lion bucket holds 100. One more level would have 10, and then 1. Nobody can live on that. So chains stop after three or four links, and there are fewer animals at each step up. Draw it and it is a pyramid: wide grass at the bottom, a thin point of lions at the top.

Where does the water leak? Four holes. Some of the grass is never eaten: roots and thorns. Some of what is eaten is not digested and comes out as dung, which is why dung beetles have a living. Most of the rest is burned in respiration to run, breathe and stay warm, and leaves as heat. A bit goes in urine. Only the energy that builds new body, muscle and bone and fat, is in the bucket for the next eater. The energy does not vanish; it ends up as heat in the air. Energy is never destroyed, just spread out where nobody can eat it.

The leak explains food, too. A field of maize eaten straight by people feeds about ten times as many as the same field fed to cows first, because the cows leak ninety percent. That is why pap, bread and rice feed the world.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A food chain is a relay of energy: start with a producer, arrows point to the eater. Levels: producer first, then primary, secondary, tertiary consumers. A web is all the chains in one place drawn together; pull one strand and the others move. Ten percent passes to each next level; the rest leaks as uneaten parts, dung, and mostly heat from respiration. So chains are short and the shape is a pyramid.

Hold the relay, the web and the leaky bucket, and take the final questions of the lesson: the 10 000 to 1000 to 100 count, the four holes, and why lions are rare.
