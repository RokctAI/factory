### subtopic_1 — What Balance Means
**Assistant:** If the impala and lion numbers keep going up and down, how can anyone call that balance?

**Tutor:** Because the up and down is the balance; a system that corrected itself without moving would have nothing to correct. Think of the word in the sense a cyclist uses it. A cyclist in balance is not motionless; she is constantly leaning a little one way and steering a little the other, and the result over any stretch of road is a straight line. The impala and lion populations do the same. A rise in impala is answered a season or two later by a rise in lions, which brings the impala down, which brings the lions down, which lets the impala rise again. Each movement is the correction for the one before it, and over ten or twenty years the average numbers stay roughly constant and neither population disappears. That is why the technical phrase is dynamic equilibrium: equilibrium because the long-run averages hold, dynamic because they hold through continuous change. The graph shows it as two waves, the predator wave lagging behind the prey wave, both staying between a floor and a ceiling. The floor and ceiling are the limiting factors, food, water, space, predators, disease, competition, and the ceiling for the whole community is the carrying capacity. In an exam, the mark for "balance" is earned by the phrase "populations fluctuate but remain more or less stable over time", and the explanation marks are earned by naming the cause of each rise and fall through the food web.

### subtopic_2 — Natural Disruptions
**Assistant:** How can fire be both a disruption and something fynbos needs? It sounds like a contradiction.

**Tutor:** It is a question of dose and timing, and the same is true of most natural forces. Fynbos evolved on nutrient-poor soils in a region with hot dry summers and lightning storms, so natural fires swept through roughly every ten to twenty years for millions of years. The plants adapted to that rhythm. Proteas hold their seeds in woody cones that open only after a fire; many fynbos seeds lie dormant in the soil until heat cracks their coats or chemicals in smoke signal them to germinate; some bulbs flower spectacularly only in the first spring after a burn. A fire at the natural interval clears the old, woody growth, returns minerals in the ash, lets light reach the ground, and triggers a new generation. In that dose, fire is not a disruption at all; it is maintenance. The disruption comes when the dose is wrong. Fires every three or four years, often started by people, kill young plants before they have flowered and set seed, so the seed bank in the soil runs down and the fynbos is replaced by grass or alien wattle. Fires at the wrong season, or made far hotter by alien pines and eucalyptus that burn like torches, cook the seeds instead of waking them. Too few fires, for fifty years, let the vegetation grow old and senescent and stop regenerating. So in an exam, the full answer is both halves: fire at the natural frequency is necessary to fynbos and grassland; fire too frequent, too hot or at the wrong season is a disruption. Giving one half earns one mark; giving both earns the rest.

### subtopic_3 — Human Disruptions
**Assistant:** Fertiliser helps plants grow. Why does fertiliser in a dam end up killing the fish?

**Tutor:** Because it helps the wrong plants grow, in the wrong place, and the sequence from there to dead fish has four steps that are worth writing out, since they appear again and again in case studies. Rain washes nitrates and phosphates from farmland, and sewage adds more, into rivers that feed a dam such as Hartbeespoort. Those minerals are exactly what algae and water hyacinth need, so step one is an explosion of growth, a bloom, that turns the water green or covers it in a mat. Step two: the mat blocks sunlight, so the submerged plants beneath it can no longer photosynthesise and they die; the algae themselves, when they have used up the minerals, die in their millions. Step three: all that dead material is decomposed by bacteria, and bacteria respire, using up the oxygen dissolved in the water far faster than the surface can replace it. Step four: fish, crabs, insect larvae and everything else that breathes dissolved oxygen suffocate and die, and their decay uses still more oxygen. The water becomes a foul, oxygen-free soup, which is the condition called eutrophication. So fertiliser kills fish indirectly, through algae, light, decomposition and oxygen, and the examiner awards marks for each link in that chain. The lesson for people is that minerals belong in the soil, not the river, and that sewage works and farming practices upstream decide what the dam looks like downstream.

### subtopic_4 — Alien Invaders and Reading a Case Study
**Assistant:** Why do alien plants spread so much faster here than in their own countries?

**Tutor:** Because they arrive alone. In its home region, every plant lives inside a web of enemies that has had thousands of years to find it: insects that eat its leaves, fungi that rot its roots, animals that eat its seeds, and neighbouring plants that compete for the same light and water on equal terms. All of those keep its numbers within limits, which is the balance from the first part of this lesson. When a plant is carried to a new continent, as a garden ornament, a timber crop or a seed in a ship's ballast, it is carried without that web. Nothing here recognises black wattle or water hyacinth as food, no local fungus is adapted to attack it, and the indigenous plants beside it are adapted to a poorer, slower life on our nutrient-poor soils and in our fire cycle. So the newcomer grows faster, seeds more heavily, and faces no check: an organism with no limiting factors, which is the definition of a population out of balance. The practical consequences follow. Wattle and pine along a river draw up far more water than the fynbos they replace, which is why the Working for Water programme, started in 1995, pays thousands of people to clear catchments; it genuinely does return water to the rivers. Hyacinth smothers dams. The best long-term answer is often to import the missing enemy, under strict testing, which is biological control: a weevil for hyacinth, a moth and a cochineal insect for prickly pear, a gall-forming insect for some wattles. The test case is whether the agent eats only the invader, and that justification is what the exam wants when you propose it.
