### subtopic_1 — Ecology and the Levels of Study
**Assistant:** Why do ecologists need six different levels? Is an ecosystem not enough on its own?

**Tutor:** Because different questions live at different scales, and a question asked at the wrong level cannot be answered. If you want to know whether the impala in a section of the Kruger are increasing or declining, you count a population: one species, one place, one time, and the answer is a number that changes year by year. If you want to know how lions, impala, grass and rain interact, you need the ecosystem, because the rainfall and the soil are part of the explanation and they are not alive. If you want to compare the Lowveld to the Highveld, you compare biomes, whole regions defined by climate and vegetation. And if you want to talk about the carbon dioxide in the atmosphere or the oxygen made by all the planet's plants, you are at the level of the biosphere, because gases do not respect park fences. Each level is a tool sized for a particular job. The exam tests whether you can pick the right one from the wording. "All the organisms of different species in an area" is a community; add "and their physical environment" and it becomes an ecosystem. The precise words are the marks: "same species" in a population, "non-living" in an ecosystem, "similar climate and vegetation" in a biome. Learn the hierarchy as six nested boxes and read every question for the clue that says which box it is in.

### subtopic_2 — Biotic and Abiotic Components
**Assistant:** A dead tree is not living any more. Why is it still called biotic?

**Tutor:** Because the biotic category is about origin and role, not about whether something is breathing at this moment. A dead tree is made of wood, bark and leaves, which are the tissues of a once-living organism. It is still full of the carbon compounds photosynthesis built, and that is exactly why a whole community depends on it: fungi digest it, termites and beetles tunnel through it, woodpeckers nest in it, and bacteria finally return its minerals to the soil. It is organic matter moving through the living system, and it belongs with the living things in the two-column sort. The abiotic column is for components that were never alive and are not made by life in that way: water, air, rock, sunlight, temperature, wind, the depth of a dam, the salt in sea water. Soil is a useful borderline case to think through. The mineral grains are abiotic; the humus mixed in with them is decayed plant and animal material and is biotic; the earthworms are biotic. In practice, exam questions treat "soil" as abiotic unless they specifically ask about its organic content. The rule that keeps you safe is this: ask where the thing came from. If it was ever part of an organism, it goes in the biotic column. Dung, bones, shells, a fallen feather, leaf litter: biotic. A stone, a puddle, a breeze, a sunbeam: abiotic.

### subtopic_3 — Habitats
**Assistant:** What is the difference between an organism's habitat and its ecosystem? They sound like the same place.

**Tutor:** They overlap in space and differ in scale and in point of view. The ecosystem is the whole system: every living thing in an area together with the sunlight, water, soil and air, and all the interactions among them. It is described from the outside, as a unit. The habitat is one organism's address inside that system: the particular kind of place that supplies what that organism needs. The Kruger savanna is one ecosystem, but inside it a leopard's habitat is riverine bush and rocky hills, a barbel's habitat is the muddy riverbed, a dung beetle's habitat is the dung lying on the open ground, and a hornbill's habitat is the canopy of tall trees. Four organisms, one ecosystem, four habitats. The habitat is described by the physical features that make it suitable: shelter, water, food supply, temperature, light. That is why a question asking you to describe a habitat is not answered by listing the animals that share it, and it is why a habitat is also distinct from a species' range, which is the geographical area where the species is found. In an exam, "habitat" wants the kind of place and why it suits the organism. "Ecosystem" wants the community and its environment as a whole. Keep the scale in your head: the ecosystem is the suburb; the habitat is the house.

### subtopic_4 — Adapting to Change
**Assistant:** Hibernation and aestivation both mean sleeping through a hard season. Why have two words?

**Tutor:** Because the two hard seasons are opposite problems, and the body's strategy is tuned to each. Hibernation is a response to cold. In a cold winter, food is scarce and keeping a warm body warm costs enormous energy, so an animal such as a hedgehog or certain bats lowers its body temperature, slows its heartbeat and breathing to a fraction of normal, and lives for weeks or months on stored fat. The danger it is avoiding is starving while trying to stay warm. Aestivation is a response to heat and drought. In the dry season of the Lowveld or the Karoo, water disappears and the sun would dry an amphibian out in hours. The African bullfrog digs down into mud while it is still soft, sheds several layers of skin to form a waterproof cocoon around itself, and slows its body down inside that cocoon, sometimes for many months, until rain softens the ground and it digs out. The danger it is avoiding is drying out and overheating. Same general idea, slowing down and waiting, two different triggers and two different enemies: cold and famine for hibernation, heat and drought for aestivation. The exam distinction is usually a one-mark word, and the way to remember it is that aestivation sounds like a hot word, from the Latin for summer, and hibernation sounds like winter, which it is.
