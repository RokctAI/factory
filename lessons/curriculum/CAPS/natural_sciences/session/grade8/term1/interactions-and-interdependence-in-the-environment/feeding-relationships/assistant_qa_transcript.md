### subtopic_1 — Producers: Where All Food Begins
**Assistant:** Why are producers called the base of the ecosystem when there are so few kinds of them compared with animals?

**Tutor:** Because base refers to function, not variety. Producers are the only organisms able to take energy from outside the living world, sunlight, and turn it into chemical energy stored in food. Every other organism, from a locust to a lion to a soil bacterium, is spending energy that a producer captured first. Count the kinds of animal in a patch of savanna and you may find hundreds; count where their energy came from and the answer is always the grass and the trees. Remove the lions and the ecosystem changes; remove the producers and it ends, because the consumers have nothing to eat and, in time, nothing to breathe, since the producers also release the oxygen. That is why we draw producers at the bottom of every food chain and pyramid and call them the base. There is a second sense in which they are the base: the quantity of producers sets the ceiling for everything above. A Highveld grassland that grows thick grass every summer can carry far more antelope and far more predators than a Karoo plain with sparse scrub. The exam often asks why producers must be the first organism in a food chain, and the answer is in two parts: they make their own food from inorganic substances using sunlight, and no consumer can do that.

### subtopic_2 — Consumers: Herbivores, Carnivores and Omnivores
**Assistant:** How can a skull alone tell us an animal's diet if we never saw the animal eat?

**Tutor:** Because teeth are tools shaped for a job, and the shape survives long after the animal does. Grinding tough plant material needs a large flat surface with ridges, like a millstone, so a herbivore's cheek teeth are broad and flat with ridged tops, and its front teeth are chisel-like for cropping grass or stripping leaves. Many herbivores have a gap where canines would be, because they have no use for gripping teeth. Killing and tearing meat needs the opposite: long, pointed canines to grip and puncture, and cheek teeth with sharp blades that slide past each other like scissors to slice flesh. A carnivore skull also tends to have a short, strong jaw and large muscle attachments for a powerful bite. Omnivores show a compromise: incisors for biting, canines that are present but not dagger-like, and molars that are flattish but with some cusps. A human skull is the standard example, and so is a baboon's. So a palaeontologist, a vet or a Grade 8 learner given a jawbone reads the diet from the pattern. The exam skill is to describe the teeth and then say what diet they fit, in that order: "broad flat molars and no canines, therefore a herbivore." Evidence first, conclusion second, and the mark follows.

### subtopic_3 — Decomposers: Closing the Loop
**Assistant:** Fungi and bacteria are so small. How can they possibly deal with a whole dead elephant?

**Tutor:** By being numerous, fast and chemical rather than large and mechanical, and by having help. A dead elephant is not dismantled by one organism; it passes through a sequence. Scavengers, vultures and hyenas, remove most of the flesh within days. Detritivores, chiefly fly larvae, beetles and later termites, shred and swallow the remaining soft tissue and skin. What reaches the bacteria and fungi is already in pieces with a large surface area, and that is where their size becomes a strength. A single gram of fertile soil holds hundreds of millions of bacteria and metres of fungal threads, and in warm, moist conditions bacterial populations double in less than an hour. They do not bite; they secrete enzymes that digest the material outside their cells and then absorb the products, so every surface they coat is being dissolved at once. Within weeks the soft remains are gone, and within a year or two in the savanna even the bones are crumbling as bacteria and fungi work through them. The products are carbon dioxide released to the air and mineral salts released to the soil, which the grass takes up, which an elephant eats. That is the loop, and the point for the exam is that decomposers are the organisms that complete it by returning nutrients to the environment. Scavengers and detritivores help, but only bacteria and fungi finish the job.

### subtopic_4 — Reading a Feeding Relationship
**Assistant:** Why does the arrow point from the impala to the lion? Surely the lion goes to the impala.

**Tutor:** Because the arrow records where the food and energy go, not where the animal walks, and a diagram has to choose one meaning and keep it. The whole point of a food chain is to trace energy from the Sun through the producers and along the consumers, and the arrow is drawn in the direction of that flow: grass is eaten by impala, so energy passes from grass to impala and the arrow points at the impala; impala is eaten by lion, so energy passes to the lion and the arrow points at the lion. Read every arrow as "is eaten by" or "gives its energy to" and it will always be the right way round. Learners who draw it the other way are thinking of the attack, the lion running at the impala, which is a true picture of the hunt but not what the diagram represents. The convention matters because next session we add energy figures and the arrows must point the way the kilojoules move; a reversed arrow would say energy flows from the lion back into the impala, which is absurd. The marker checks arrow direction specifically. If you are ever unsure in an exam, start from the producer, which must be first, and ask of each organism "who eats this?"; draw the arrow to that eater. Done consistently, it also keeps the producer at the start and the top predator at the end, which is the shape every memorandum expects.
