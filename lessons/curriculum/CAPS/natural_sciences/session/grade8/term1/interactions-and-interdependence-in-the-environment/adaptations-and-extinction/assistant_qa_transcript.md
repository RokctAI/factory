### subtopic_1 — What an Adaptation Is
**Assistant:** How can we tell a real adaptation from a feature that just happens to be there?

**Tutor:** By asking what the feature does for survival or reproduction in that particular habitat, and whether it is inherited. The two tests work together. Take the gemsbok's pale coat. It is inherited, every gemsbok has it, and in the Kalahari a pale coat reflects more sunlight than a dark one, so the animal absorbs less heat and loses less water keeping cool; that is a measurable advantage where ground temperatures pass 60 degrees. Both tests pass, so it is an adaptation. Now consider the exact length of one gemsbok's tail or a scar on its flank. The scar is not inherited at all, and the small difference in tail length makes no detectable difference to whether it eats, escapes or breeds, so neither counts. The habit of thought to develop is to see every organism as a set of answers to the problems of its home, and to ask of each feature which problem it answers. Sometimes the answer is not obvious and biologists argue about it; sometimes a feature is a leftover from an ancestor that no longer serves a purpose. For Grade 8, the examinable standard is clear: a feature counts as an adaptation when you can name the habitat problem and explain how the feature improves the organism's chances against it. If you cannot name the problem, you have not yet shown it is an adaptation, and the mark scheme will not give you the mark for the feature alone.

### subtopic_2 — Structural, Functional and Behavioural Adaptations
**Assistant:** Hibernation seems to be both a body process and a behaviour. Which box does it go in?

**Tutor:** Both, and the honest answer in an exam is to say so and explain why, because the classification is a tool for thinking, not a law of nature. The three categories separate a part of the body from a process inside the body from an action the organism performs. Hibernation is an action in the sense that the animal seeks out a den and settles down for winter, which is behavioural; but the heart of it, the dramatic slowing of heartbeat and breathing, the fall in body temperature, the switch to living off stored fat, is a set of internal processes under physiological control that the animal does not consciously perform, which is functional. Most textbooks list it as functional or physiological for that reason, with the den-seeking as the behavioural part. The gemsbok's heat tolerance is cleaner: letting body temperature rise and cooling the blood to the brain are entirely internal processes, so functional, while feeding at night is entirely behavioural and the pale coat is entirely structural. Markers accept a reasoned classification. What they do not accept is a bare label with no reason, or a label that contradicts the definition, such as calling venom production structural when it is a process. So when a feature straddles two boxes, write a sentence like "mainly functional, because the slowing of the body is an internal process, with a behavioural element in choosing a den." That sentence shows understanding, and understanding is what the mark is for.

### subtopic_3 — Extinction: When Adaptation Fails
**Assistant:** If species have always gone extinct naturally, why is extinction today treated as a crisis?

**Tutor:** Because of the rate and the cause, not the fact. The fossil record shows that species have always disappeared and new ones have always arisen; averaged over long periods, roughly one species in a million goes extinct each year as a natural background rate, and even the mass extinctions, catastrophic as they were, were followed by millions of years of recovery in which new species evolved to fill the gaps. What is different now is that the current rate is estimated at hundreds to a thousand times that background, that it is accelerating, and that the causes are human and therefore avoidable. Habitat destruction, overhunting and overfishing, alien species, pollution and climate change are all acting at once, within decades, on species whose adaptations took hundreds of thousands of years to form and cannot change on that timescale. A natural climate shift over a hundred thousand years gives populations time to move or adapt; a sheep farm ploughed in a season does not. There is also no recovery on any timescale that matters to us. The species evolving to replace what we lose will take millions of years to appear, and in the meantime every lost species is a lost link in a food web, a lost pollinator or seed-disperser, a lost source of medicines or crop genes, and a loss that is permanent. The quagga will not return. That combination, unnatural speed, human cause, and irreversibility, is what the word crisis is pointing at.

### subtopic_4 — South Africa's Lost and Threatened Species
**Assistant:** Why can the Quagga Project not simply bring the quagga back by breeding?

**Tutor:** Because a species is more than its coat pattern, and the project is working only with what the coat pattern tells us. The quagga was a population of plains zebra that lived in the Karoo and the Free State and had stripes only on the head, neck and shoulders, with a plain brown body and white legs. When the last one died in Amsterdam in 1883, every quagga gene that differed from other plains zebras died with it, except the ones that happened to persist in the wider zebra population. The project, begun in 1987, selects plains zebras with the weakest striping and breeds them together, generation after generation, so that the striping fades towards the quagga's. After several generations it has produced animals that look very like the old photographs and skins. But looking alike is not being the same. The quagga's adaptations to the Karoo, its behaviour, its physiology, the thousands of genetic differences nobody has measured because no living quagga exists to measure them, are not being selected for, because nobody knows what they were. What the project produces is a zebra that resembles a quagga, and the researchers themselves say so, calling the animals Rau quaggas after the man who started the work. The lesson for the exam is exact: extinction is permanent; a look-alike bred afterwards is not a reversal. And the lesson for conservation is that preventing the loss of the riverine rabbit or the African penguin now is the only way to have them later.
