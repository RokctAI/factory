### subtopic_1 — Food Chains and the Arrow
**Assistant:** Why do we leave the Sun and the decomposers out of the written chain if both are essential?

**Tutor:** Because a food chain is a diagram of organisms feeding on organisms, and the two things you name are essential in different ways that the chain format cannot hold. The Sun is the source of all the energy, but it is not alive and nothing eats it; writing it as the first link would suggest that grass consumes the Sun the way an impala consumes grass, which is a category error. We say instead that the chain begins with a producer, and we note separately that producers capture their energy from sunlight. Many textbook diagrams draw the Sun beside the producer with a different kind of arrow, a ray rather than a feeding arrow, to make that difference visible. Decomposers are left out for the opposite reason: they belong everywhere, not in one place. Bacteria and fungi feed on dead grass, on impala dung and carcasses, and on dead lions; drawing them in the line would put them after the lion as if they ate only lions, which is false. So they are drawn to one side with arrows from every level into them, or simply noted as acting on all levels. The chain itself stays a straight sequence from producer to apex predator, which is the form examiners want. When a question asks you to add decomposers, place them beside the chain with arrows from each organism, and you have shown you understand both the sequence and the loop.

### subtopic_2 — Trophic Levels
**Assistant:** Why is the primary consumer at trophic level two? Primary means first.

**Tutor:** Because the two labels are counting different things, and both counts are correct in their own system. "Primary, secondary, tertiary" is a count of consumers only: the first organism that eats is the primary consumer, the organism that eats it is the secondary consumer, and so on. "First, second, third trophic level" is a count of feeding positions in the whole chain, and the producer occupies a feeding position too, since it feeds itself by photosynthesis. Producers are therefore the first trophic level, and the first consumer lands at the second level. The offset of one is permanent: primary consumer, second level; secondary consumer, third level; tertiary consumer, fourth level. Examiners use the two systems deliberately to test whether you have understood the structure rather than memorised a word, and the safest habit is to give both when asked for the trophic level: "secondary consumer, third trophic level." The second subtlety is that the level belongs to the position in a given chain, not to the species. A jackal eating a locust sits at the third level; the same jackal eating a lizard that ate a locust sits at the fourth. Omnivores shift levels with every meal, and a question that names the chain is asking for the level in that chain only. Read the arrows, count from the producer, and the level follows.

### subtopic_3 — Food Webs
**Assistant:** How do I answer "what would happen if the lizards disappeared" without just guessing?

**Tutor:** You do not guess; you follow arrows, one step at a time, and state each step as a cause and its effect. Start at the organism removed and look in two directions. Downwards, at what the lizards ate: the arrows into the lizard come from locusts, so with no lizards fewer locusts are eaten and the locust population increases. Carry that one step further: more locusts eat more grass, so the grass decreases and other grazers have less food. Upwards, at what ate the lizards: the arrows out of the lizard go to snakes and eagles, so those predators lose one food source. If they have other prey in the web, they may switch to it and increase pressure on, say, rodents; if the lizard was their only food, they decline. Write each link as a sentence with "because": "The locust population would increase because fewer locusts are eaten by lizards." Two or three such sentences, each tied to a visible arrow, earn the marks; a bare "the locusts would increase" earns one. Two cautions. First, do not invent organisms or feeding links that the web does not show; the examiner marks against the given diagram. Second, be careful with words like "always" and "disappear"; populations change in size, and a careful answer says "increase" or "decrease" rather than "die out" unless the web gives no alternative food at all. The food web is a map, and the question is asking you to read a route on it.

### subtopic_4 — Energy Loss Along the Chain
**Assistant:** If ninety percent of the energy is lost at each step, where does it actually go? Energy cannot just vanish.

**Tutor:** It does not vanish; it leaves the chain, and the law you are quoting, that energy is never created or destroyed, is exactly what tells us where to look. Take the impala eating grass. Part of the grass is never eaten at all: roots, tough stems, seeds blown away; that energy stays in the plant and eventually goes to the decomposers. Of the grass actually eaten, a portion cannot be digested, chiefly cellulose, and leaves the impala as dung still carrying its energy; the dung beetles and bacteria get that share. Of the portion digested and absorbed, most is respired: glucose plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide, water and energy, and the impala spends that energy running from lions, walking to water, chewing, pumping blood, growing hair, and above all keeping its body at about 39 degrees. Every one of those activities ends with the energy as heat in the surrounding air, where no organism can eat it. A small further amount leaves in urine. What remains, roughly a tenth, is the energy locked into new impala tissue, and that alone is available to the lion. So the ninety percent is accounted for in four destinations, with respiration as heat by far the largest, and the total energy of the system is unchanged; it has simply been converted into a form that is spread out and useless to the next level. That spreading out is why the chain is short, why the pyramid narrows, and why a hectare of maize feeds ten people as pap for every one it feeds as beef.
