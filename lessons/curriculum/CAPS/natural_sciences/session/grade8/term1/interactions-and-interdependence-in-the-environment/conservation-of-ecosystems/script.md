# Part 1 — Expert

This session closes the ecology unit by asking what can be done about the disruptions and extinctions of the last two sessions. The anchor is the African penguin colony at Boulders Beach in Simon's Town, which began with two breeding pairs in 1982, grew into a protected colony of a few thousand birds, and now depends on a network of people: rangers, scientists, a seabird rescue centre, fishing regulators and ordinary visitors who keep to the boardwalks. The governing idea is that conservation means protecting and managing ecosystems so that they keep working and keep supporting the species in them, including us, and that it is carried out at two scales: by environmentalists and organisations with legal and scientific tools, and by individuals through daily choices. The reasons for conservation, the methods of each group, sustainable use and the evaluation of a conservation plan are all examinable.

## Subtopic: Why Ecosystems Need Conserving

Conservation is the protection, careful management and wise use of natural resources and ecosystems so that they continue to function and remain available for future generations. It is not the same as locking nature away. A conserved ecosystem may still be fished, grazed, harvested or visited, provided the use does not exceed what the ecosystem can replace.

The first reason to conserve is that people depend on ecosystems for services nobody could afford to replace. Mountain catchments covered in fynbos and grassland collect rain and release it slowly into rivers; most of the water used in Gauteng and Cape Town begins as rain on protected or semi-natural mountain land. Wetlands filter pollutants and soak up floodwater. Insects, birds and bats pollinate crops; without pollinators, deciduous fruit farming in the Western Cape would collapse. Soil organisms keep farmland fertile. The sea supplies fish to commercial and small-scale fishers. These are called ecosystem services, and they are free only while the ecosystems that provide them are healthy.

The second reason is biodiversity, the variety of living things in an area, counted at three levels: the variety of genes within a species, the variety of species, and the variety of ecosystems. South Africa is one of the most biologically diverse countries on Earth. It covers about two percent of the world's land area but holds close to ten percent of the world's known plant species. The Cape Floral Region, the smallest of the world's six floral kingdoms, has about nine thousand plant species, roughly seven in every ten of which grow nowhere else; such species are called endemic. Biodiversity matters because varied ecosystems recover from disruption better than simple ones, because wild species are the source of medicines and crop genes, and because the loss of any species is permanent.

The third reason is economic and social. Nature-based tourism, from the Kruger to whale watching in Hermanus, employs hundreds of thousands of people. Traditional medicine relies on wild plants, many of which are now overharvested. Rooibos and honeybush are fynbos plants that became industries. And communities whose livelihoods rest on land and sea have the most to lose when they degrade.

The final reason is the one the last session made plain: disruption and extinction are happening faster than at any time in human history, overwhelmingly because of human activity, and what humans cause, humans can in principle reduce.

The questions for this section are with you now: a definition of conservation, three ecosystem services, the three levels of biodiversity, and why South Africa matters so much for biodiversity.

## Subtopic: Conservation by Environmentalists and Organisations

An environmentalist is a person who works to protect the environment, whether as a scientist, a ranger, an activist, a policy-maker or a volunteer in an organisation. Their methods can be grouped into five kinds.

The first is protected areas: land and sea set aside by law, where development is restricted and wildlife is managed. South Africa's first, the Sabie Game Reserve, was proclaimed in 1898 and became the Kruger National Park in 1926; the Kruger now covers about 19 500 square kilometres. National parks are managed by South African National Parks, provincial reserves by provincial agencies such as Ezemvelo KZN Wildlife and CapeNature, and roughly nine percent of the country's land is formally protected. Marine Protected Areas do the same at sea, giving fish and shellfish a place to breed undisturbed so that numbers spill over into fished areas; twenty new ones were declared in 2019.

The second is laws and regulation. The Biodiversity Act of 2004 lists threatened species and alien invasive species and controls what may be done with them. Fishing quotas and closed seasons limit how many fish may be taken and when. Permits are needed to collect protected plants such as cycads. Internationally, the CITES agreement controls trade in endangered species and their parts, including rhino horn and ivory.

The third is direct protection and rescue. Anti-poaching rangers, tracking dogs, dehorning and the moving of rhino to safer reserves helped bring the poaching of rhino down to well under half its peak of more than a thousand a year. SANCCOB, a seabird rescue centre founded in Cape Town in 1968, cleans oiled seabirds; after the Treasure oil spill of 2000 about nineteen thousand oiled African penguins were captured, cleaned and most of them released. Captive breeding programmes raise animals such as cheetah and wild dog for release into reserves, and seed banks store the seeds of threatened plants.

The fourth is restoration: repairing damaged ecosystems. Working for Water, started in 1995, employs people to clear alien invasive trees from catchments; other programmes rehabilitate wetlands, fill dongas, and replant mined dunes along the KwaZulu-Natal coast.

The fifth is research, monitoring and education. Scientists at organisations such as the South African National Biodiversity Institute assess which species are threatened and publish Red Lists. Organisations such as the Endangered Wildlife Trust, BirdLife South Africa and WWF South Africa run projects, lobby government and educate the public. Monitoring tells conservationists whether their methods are working, which is the only way to improve them.

The questions for this section are with you now: what an environmentalist is, the five kinds of method with a South African example of each, and why a Marine Protected Area can increase the catch outside it.

## Subtopic: Conservation by Individuals

Most environmental damage is the sum of millions of small choices, so individual action is not a gesture; it is where the numbers come from. The examinable actions fall under a few headings, and each should be linked to the disruption it reduces.

Use less water. South Africa is a dry country, and every litre taken from a river is a litre an ecosystem does not get. Shorter showers, fixing leaks, collecting rainwater and watering gardens in the evening all reduce the demand that leads to new dams and drained wetlands. During the Cape Town drought of 2018 residents were limited to fifty litres per person per day, and the city avoided running dry largely through individual saving.

Use less electricity. Most of South Africa's electricity comes from burning coal, which releases carbon dioxide and sulphur dioxide. Switching off lights and appliances, using efficient bulbs and solar water heaters reduces both air pollution and climate change.

Reduce, reuse, recycle, in that order. Reducing waste means buying less packaging; reusing means using bottles and bags again; recycling sends paper, glass, metal and some plastics back into manufacturing. Each step keeps waste out of landfills, rivers and the sea, where plastic kills turtles, seabirds and seals.

Buy responsibly. The SASSI programme, run by WWF South Africa since 2004, sorts seafood into green for a good choice, orange for think twice, and red for do not buy. Refusing to buy poached perlemoen, rhino horn products, wild-collected cycads or succulents removes the market that drives overharvesting.

Garden and farm with nature. Plant indigenous species, which need less water and feed local insects and birds; remove alien invasive plants; avoid unnecessary pesticides, which kill pollinators; compost garden waste.

Take part. Volunteer for beach or river clean-ups and alien-clearing days. Record sightings for citizen-science projects such as bird atlasing or nature-recording apps, which give scientists data from places they cannot reach. Join or start a school eco-club; the Eco-Schools programme runs in many South African schools. Report poaching, illegal dumping and pollution to the authorities.

Stay on the path. At Boulders Beach the boardwalks exist because penguins nest in burrows under bushes and sand, and a careless foot collapses a nest.

The pattern for marks is action, then the disruption it reduces, then how: "collecting rainwater reduces the water taken from rivers, leaving more for aquatic ecosystems."

The questions for this section are with you now: four individual actions each linked to a disruption, the SASSI colours, and why individual choices matter at national scale.

## Subtopic: Sustainable Use and Evaluating a Conservation Plan

Sustainable use means using a natural resource at a rate no faster than it can be replaced, so that it remains available indefinitely. The idea is easiest to hold as a bank account: the ecosystem is the capital, and what it regrows each year is the interest; live on the interest and the account lasts for ever; spend the capital and it shrinks until it is gone. Perlemoen poaching spends the capital; a fishing quota set below the yearly breeding rate lives on the interest.

South Africa has old and new examples of sustainable use. The Thonga fish traps at Kosi Bay have been built from reeds and sticks for centuries; they catch fish moving with the tide but let small fish through and are rebuilt by families who hold traditional rights, so the estuary keeps producing. Thatching grass, reeds and medicinal plants are harvested from some protected areas under permits that limit the amount taken. In the Makuleke region of the northern Kruger, land returned to its community in 1998 was kept as a contractual national park, with the community earning income from tourism, so that conservation and livelihoods support each other. Community conservancies and stewardship agreements on private farms follow the same idea: people protect what benefits them.

Unsustainable use is the opposite and is the root of many earlier disruptions: overgrazing, overfishing, overharvesting of medicinal bark and bulbs, and the clearing of more land than the soil can support.

Evaluating a conservation plan is a common extended question, and it has a fixed procedure. One: identify the problem and its cause. Two: state what the plan does. Three: explain how the plan removes or reduces the cause; a plan that treats only the symptom is weak. Four: identify who benefits and who bears the cost; plans that ignore the local community often fail. Five: say how success would be measured. For the African penguin, a plan to ban purse-seine fishing for sardines and anchovy close to breeding colonies scores well on step three because it targets the main cause, food shortage; it costs the fishing industry, which must be weighed on step four; and it is measured by counting breeding pairs on step five.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: conservation means no use at all; sustainable use is part of conservation. Two: only governments can conserve; individuals and communities are essential. Three: listing an action without the disruption it reduces. Four: protected areas alone are enough; species move, water flows in, pollution drifts across fences. Five: recycling is the first step; reducing comes first, then reusing, then recycling.

Layout for marks: name the action, name the cause it addresses, explain the mechanism, and for a plan add who pays and how success is measured.

The questions for this section are with you now: sustainable use and the bank account, the Kosi Bay fish traps, and the five-step evaluation of a plan.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a savings account, a team of guardians, and a list of things anyone can do on Monday — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Live on the Interest

Imagine someone gives you a savings account with a lot of money in it. Every year the bank adds a little interest. If you spend only the interest, the account stays the same size for ever, and you can keep spending every year. If you dip into the main amount, the account shrinks, the interest shrinks with it, and one day it is empty.

Nature is that account. The fish in the sea, the grass on a hillside, the trees in a forest are the main amount. What they regrow each year is the interest. Catch only as many fish as are born each year and there will always be fish. Catch more and the shoals shrink until there is nothing left to catch. That is sustainable use: taking no faster than nature can replace. Conservation is looking after the account.

Why bother? Because the account pays for things we cannot buy anywhere else. Mountain grassland and fynbos catch the rain that fills our taps. Bees and other insects pollinate the apples and pears. Wetlands clean dirty water for free. The sea feeds fishing families. These are ecosystem services, and they only keep coming while the ecosystems are healthy.

And South Africa's account is unusually rich. We have about two percent of the world's land but nearly a tenth of its plant species. The Cape fynbos alone has about nine thousand kinds of plant, and most of them grow nowhere else on Earth. Lose them here and they are lost everywhere.

Keep the savings account in mind and try the questions for this part: what sustainable use means, two free services nature gives us, and why South Africa's plants matter to the whole world.

## Subtopic: The Guardians

Some people make protecting nature their job. They are called environmentalists, and they work in teams.

Some guard places. Rangers patrol national parks such as the Kruger, which started as a small reserve in 1898. At sea, Marine Protected Areas are no-fishing zones where fish can breed in peace, and the extra fish swim out and fill the nets of fishers nearby. Protect the nursery and the whole coast benefits.

Some write and enforce rules. There are limits on how many fish you may catch, seasons when you may not fish at all, permits for collecting rare plants, and a worldwide agreement that bans trade in rhino horn and ivory.

Some rescue. Rangers and their dogs chase rhino poachers, and rhino killings have dropped to well under half of the terrible peak of more than a thousand a year. When the ship Treasure spilled oil near Cape Town in 2000, volunteers at the seabird centre SANCCOB caught and washed about nineteen thousand oily penguins, and most went back to the sea.

Some repair. Working for Water pays thousands of people to cut down thirsty alien trees so rivers can run again. Others replant mined sand dunes and rebuild damaged wetlands.

And some count and teach. Scientists count animals and plants to find out which are in trouble, then check whether the rescue plans are working. Groups such as the Endangered Wildlife Trust and BirdLife South Africa run projects and teach the public.

Guard, rule, rescue, repair, count. Five jobs, one team.

Hold the five jobs and try this part's questions: what a Marine Protected Area does, how the penguins were saved from oil, and what Working for Water does.

## Subtopic: What You Can Do on Monday

The guardians cannot do it alone, because most damage comes from millions of small choices, and so do most of the fixes. Here is a list for Monday.

Save water: short showers, fix the dripping tap, catch rain in a tank. In 2018 Cape Town got down to fifty litres per person a day, and ordinary people saving water kept the taps running. Save electricity: switch off lights and chargers, because most of our power comes from burning coal. Reduce, reuse, recycle, in that order; the best rubbish is the rubbish you never make, then the bottle you use again, then the can you send for recycling. Buy wisely: check the SASSI list before buying fish, green for go, orange for think, red for no, and never buy poached perlemoen or anything made from rhino horn. Plant indigenous plants that feed local bees and birds, and pull out alien weeds. Join a beach clean-up or a school eco-club. Report poaching or illegal dumping. And at places like Boulders Beach, stay on the boardwalk, because penguin nests are under your feet.

When an exam asks what an individual can do, give the action, then the problem it fixes, then how. "Recycle cans, which reduces mining and keeps litter out of rivers."

And when it asks you to judge a plan, walk five steps: what is the problem and its cause; what does the plan do; does it fix the cause or only the symptom; who gains and who pays; how will we know it worked?

Pocket summary of the lesson. Conservation is looking after nature's savings account so it keeps paying: clean water, pollination, fish, tourism, medicine. Live on the interest, never the capital. Guardians protect places, make rules, rescue, repair and count. Individuals save water and power, reduce, reuse and recycle, buy wisely, plant indigenous and take part. A good plan fixes the cause, counts the cost and measures success.
