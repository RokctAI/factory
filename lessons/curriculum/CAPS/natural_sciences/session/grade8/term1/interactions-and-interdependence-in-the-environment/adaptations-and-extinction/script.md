# Part 1 — Expert

This session defines adaptation and classifies adaptations into three kinds, then follows the idea to its end point: what happens to a species whose adaptations no longer fit its environment. The anchor organisms are the gemsbok of the Kalahari, which survives heat that would kill a human, and the quagga, a South African zebra that no longer exists. The governing definitions are that an adaptation is a feature that increases an organism's chance of surviving and reproducing in its habitat, that adaptations are structural, functional or behavioural, and that extinction is the permanent disappearance of a species when no living individuals remain. Definitions, the three categories with examples, the causes of extinction and the South African cases are all examinable.

## Subtopic: What an Adaptation Is

An adaptation is a characteristic of an organism that makes it better suited to survive and reproduce in its particular habitat. The characteristic may be a body part, a process inside the body, or a pattern of behaviour, and the test is always the same: does this feature help the organism obtain food, avoid being eaten, cope with the climate, find water or mates, or raise young in the place where it lives? If so, it is an adaptation.

Adaptations are inherited. An individual does not acquire an adaptation during its life; it is born with it, and it passes it to its offspring. A dassie does not learn to have rubbery footpads; it has them. This is why the gemsbok of the Kalahari is a good anchor. It lives where summer ground temperatures exceed 60 degrees Celsius and surface water may be absent for months, and every notable feature of its body and behaviour can be read as an answer to that problem. Its pale coat reflects sunlight; it allows its body temperature to rise to over 45 degrees during the day rather than sweating away water, and radiates the heat at night; a network of blood vessels in its nose cools blood on its way to the brain; it feeds at night and early morning when plants hold the most moisture, and digs up water-storing roots and melons. Structure, function and behaviour, all pointing at the same enemy: heat and drought.

Adaptations match a habitat, not an abstract standard of excellence, and that is why the same feature can be an advantage in one place and a disadvantage in another. A thick white coat is an adaptation for a polar bear and a death sentence in the Kalahari. The gemsbok's heat tolerance would be useless in a cold wet forest. Whenever a question asks you to identify an adaptation, name the feature, name the problem in the habitat, and state how the feature solves it; three parts, three marks.

Adaptations arise over many generations. Individuals with a slightly more useful version of a feature survive and breed more often, so the feature spreads, and over a very long time populations come to fit their habitats closely. That process, natural selection, is treated properly in Grade 12 Life Sciences; in Grade 8 the examinable point is that adaptations are inherited features that fit an organism to its habitat.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of an adaptation, the three-part answer, and why a feature can help in one habitat and harm in another.

## Subtopic: Structural, Functional and Behavioural Adaptations

Adaptations are grouped by what kind of thing they are. Structural adaptations are physical features of the body: its shape, size, colouring, and parts. The giraffe's long neck and tongue reach acacia leaves beyond other browsers. The cheetah's long legs, flexible spine and semi-retractable claws for grip make it the fastest land animal over short distances. The springbok's pale coat and white underbelly reflect heat, and the white hairs it raises along its back signal alarm. The bat-eared fox's enormous ears radiate heat and pick up the sound of termites underground. Among plants, the thick fleshy stems of Karoo succulents store water, the spines of euphorbias deter browsers and reduce water loss, the quiver tree's thick trunk stores water and its white powdery bark reflects light, and the welwitschia of the Namib has two long leaves that channel fog moisture to its roots and a taproot reaching deep water.

Functional adaptations, also called physiological adaptations, are processes inside the body. The gemsbok's ability to let its body temperature rise and its nasal cooling of blood are functional. A camel produces very concentrated urine and dry dung to save water. Snakes produce venom to subdue prey. Desert plants open their stomata only at night to take in carbon dioxide while losing little water. The African lungfish can breathe air and survive months in dried mud in a slimy cocoon. Hibernation and aestivation are functional, because the slowing of the body is an internal process, although the choice to do it is also behavioural.

Behavioural adaptations are things an organism does. Being nocturnal to avoid daytime heat. Migrating, as swallows and sardines do. Meerkats posting a sentry while the group forages. Weaver birds building hanging nests over water, out of reach of snakes. Dung beetles rolling and burying dung. Tortoises retreating into shade at midday. Plants cannot behave in the animal sense, but the sunflower turning its head to follow the Sun and the leaves of some acacias folding at night are sometimes classified as behavioural because they are movements rather than fixed structures.

Many adaptations fall into more than one category, and examiners accept a reasoned classification. The sentence that earns marks is a feature followed by a benefit: "the bat-eared fox has very large ears, a structural adaptation, which radiate heat and detect prey underground."

The questions for this section are with you now: one example of each kind of adaptation, with the habitat problem it solves, and the gemsbok sorted into all three.

## Subtopic: Extinction: When Adaptation Fails

A species becomes extinct when the last living individual dies and no members remain anywhere on Earth. Extinction is permanent. A species that is extinct in the wild but survives in zoos or captive breeding programmes is a related but different category, and a species whose numbers are falling towards extinction is called endangered; the International Union for Conservation of Nature ranks species from least concern through vulnerable, endangered and critically endangered to extinct in the wild and extinct, and the list is called the Red List.

Species go extinct when their environment changes faster or further than their adaptations can cope with. Adaptations fit a habitat as it was; if the habitat disappears, the climate shifts, a new predator or competitor arrives, or a disease sweeps through, a well-adapted species can find its advantages have become useless. Extinction has always happened. The fossil record shows that most species that have ever lived are now extinct, and it records several mass extinctions, episodes in which a large proportion of all species vanished in a short time. The best known, about 66 million years ago, ended the dinosaurs and is linked to an asteroid impact in what is now Mexico; the largest, about 252 million years ago, wiped out around nine tenths of marine species, and its aftermath is written in the Karoo rocks, where a single tough animal, Lystrosaurus, dominates the fossil beds for a while because almost everything else had died.

Natural causes of extinction include climate change over long periods, volcanic eruptions, asteroid impacts, sea-level change, competition from newly evolved species, and disease. Human causes are now far more important and act far faster: habitat destruction, which removes the place a species is adapted to; hunting and overharvesting, which remove individuals faster than they breed; alien species, which bring predators and competitors a species has no adaptations against; pollution; and human-driven climate change, which shifts conditions within decades rather than millennia. The present rate of extinction is estimated at hundreds to a thousand times the natural background rate, and the cause is overwhelmingly human.

Extinction matters beyond sentiment. A species lost removes a link from every food web it belonged to, and the interdependence of the earlier sessions means the effects spread. Wild species are also the sources of medicines, crops, pollinators and the genetic variety that farming relies on. And it is irreversible: once gone, a species cannot be recovered.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of extinction, two natural and three human causes, and why extinction is irreversible.

## Subtopic: South Africa's Lost and Threatened Species

South Africa has already lost species in historical times. The bluebuck, a blue-grey antelope of the southern Cape, was the first large African mammal to be exterminated by people in the modern era; the last was shot around 1800, after its grassland had been turned into farms and it had been hunted for sport and for its hide. The quagga, a zebra of the Karoo and the Free State with stripes only on its head and neck, was hunted for meat and hides and to clear grazing for sheep; the last wild quagga were shot in the 1870s and the last captive one died in Amsterdam Zoo in 1883. A breeding programme since 1987 has selected plains zebras with reduced striping to resemble it, but the quagga itself is gone. The Cape lion, a dark-maned lion of the Cape, and the Cape warthog were also lost in the nineteenth century.

Other species are close to the edge. The riverine rabbit of the Karoo, which lives only in the dense vegetation along seasonal rivers, is critically endangered because that habitat has been ploughed for crops and grazed by livestock; fewer than a few hundred adults are thought to remain. The African penguin breeds along the Cape coast and has collapsed from millions of birds a century ago to a few thousand breeding pairs, because of guano scraping, egg collecting, oil spills and above all the overfishing of the sardines and anchovies it eats. The black rhino was reduced across Africa by about 98 percent in the twentieth century by poaching for horn. The Knysna seahorse lives only in three estuaries and is threatened by pollution and development. The Pickersgill's reed frog survives in a few KwaZulu-Natal wetlands threatened by drainage. The Clanwilliam cedar of the Cederberg has been reduced by timber cutting and too-frequent fire. In every case the cause is a human disruption from last session acting on a species whose adaptations fit a habitat that is disappearing.

The pattern to recognise is that extinction and endangerment are the end result of the earlier sections: habitat destruction, overharvesting, pollution, alien species and climate change acting on populations whose adaptations cannot change as quickly as their world. Protection, treated next session, works by removing the cause.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying an animal "adapted" during its life, as if it chose or learned a body feature; adaptations are inherited. Two: naming a feature without stating the habitat problem it solves. Three: calling a functional adaptation structural because it is inside the body; the test is whether it is a process or a part. Four: saying a species in a zoo is not extinct in the wild; it can be extinct in the wild while alive in captivity. Five: treating extinction as reversible; it is permanent, and the quagga project produces zebras that resemble quaggas, not quaggas.

Layout for marks: feature, category, habitat problem, how it helps. For extinction: definition, cause named and classified as natural or human, example, consequence.

The questions for this section are with you now: the quagga and the bluebuck, three endangered South African species with their causes, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through tools built for a job, three kinds of clever, and a photograph of an animal nobody will ever see again — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Built for the Job

Every tool in a toolbox is shaped for a job. A screwdriver is useless for hammering; a hammer is useless for screws. Living things are the same. Every creature is built for the place it lives in, and the features that make it fit are called adaptations. The test for an adaptation is simple: does this help it eat, avoid being eaten, cope with the weather, find water, or raise babies, here, in this place?

Take the gemsbok, the big antelope of the Kalahari with the long straight horns. The Kalahari sand gets hotter than 60 degrees in summer and there may be no water for months. Look at the gemsbok and every feature answers that. Pale coat to bounce off sunlight. It lets its body heat up past 45 degrees in the day instead of sweating, and cools off at night. A clever set of blood vessels in its nose cools the blood heading to its brain. It eats at night and early morning, when plants are juiciest, and digs up water-filled roots and tsamma melons. Everything about it says: built for heat and thirst.

Two important things. First, you do not get adaptations by trying hard. You are born with them, and you pass them to your children. The gemsbok did not learn to have a pale coat. Second, an adaptation is only good for its own place. A polar bear's thick white coat is perfect in the Arctic and would kill it in the Kalahari. Built for the job, and only that job.

When an exam asks you about an adaptation, answer in three bits every time: the feature, the problem in the habitat, how the feature solves the problem. "Pale coat; intense sun; reflects heat so the animal stays cooler."

Keep the toolbox in mind and try the questions for this part: what makes something an adaptation, whether you can learn one, and the three-bit answer.

## Subtopic: Three Kinds of Clever

Adaptations come in three kinds, and the way to tell them apart is to ask: is it a part, a process, or a habit?

A part is a structural adaptation, something you could see or touch. The giraffe's long neck. The cheetah's long legs and gripping claws. The bat-eared fox's giant ears that work as radiators and as termite detectors. A succulent's fat water-storing stem. A euphorbia's spines. The quiver tree's thick trunk and white bark.

A process is a functional adaptation, something happening inside the body that you cannot see. The gemsbok letting its temperature rise and cooling its brain blood. A camel making super-concentrated urine to save water. A snake making venom. Desert plants opening their breathing pores only at night. A lungfish shutting its body down inside a mud cocoon until the rains return.

A habit is a behavioural adaptation, something the animal does. Coming out at night. Migrating like the swallows. The meerkat standing guard while the others dig. The weaver bird hanging its nest over water so snakes cannot reach it. The tortoise walking into the shade at noon. A dung beetle rolling its ball away from the competition.

Some adaptations are two kinds at once, and that is fine as long as you say why. Hibernating is a process inside the body and also a choice of behaviour. The gemsbok has all three: pale coat, part; heat tolerance, process; night feeding, habit. Part, process, habit. Say which, then say the job it does.

Hold the three kinds in mind and try this part's questions: sorting a list into part, process or habit, and the gemsbok's three.

## Subtopic: Gone for Good

What if the place changes faster than the creature can? Then the tool no longer fits the job. If the grassland becomes a wheat farm, if a new hunter arrives with a rifle, if the sardines are fished out, a perfectly adapted animal is suddenly adapted to a world that is not there any more. If every last one dies, the species is extinct. Not rare. Not hiding. Gone, everywhere, for ever.

South Africa has photographs and skins of animals you will never see alive. The quagga was a zebra of the Karoo with stripes only on its head and neck and a plain brown body. Farmers shot them for meat and hides and to free up grass for sheep. The last wild ones were killed in the 1870s and the last one anywhere died in a zoo in Amsterdam in 1883. There is a project breeding zebras that look like quaggas, but they are zebras in a quagga costume; the real thing cannot come back. The bluebuck went even earlier, around 1800, the first big African mammal we wiped out.

Extinction has always happened; nature does it too, slowly, through long climate changes, and sometimes fast, as when an asteroid ended the dinosaurs 66 million years ago. The Karoo rocks are full of fossils from an even bigger die-off 252 million years ago. But today extinctions are running hundreds of times faster than nature's pace, and the reasons are the human knocks from last lesson: homes destroyed, animals hunted or fished out, aliens brought in, water poisoned, climate shifting.

Not yet gone, but close: the riverine rabbit of the Karoo, whose riverside bush has been ploughed; the African penguin, starving because we took its sardines; the black rhino, poached for horn; the Knysna seahorse, in three polluted estuaries. The Red List ranks them from vulnerable to endangered to critically endangered, and the last step after that is the quagga's.

Pocket summary of the lesson. An adaptation is an inherited feature that fits a creature to its home: a part, a process or a habit. Answer in three bits: feature, problem, how it helps. When the home changes faster than the creature can, the species dies out, and extinction is for ever. South Africa lost the quagga and the bluebuck to hunting and farming, and the riverine rabbit, the penguin and the rhino are next in line unless the causes are removed.

Hold the toolbox, the three kinds and the quagga photograph, and take the final questions of the lesson: why extinction is permanent, two lost South African species, and three in danger.
