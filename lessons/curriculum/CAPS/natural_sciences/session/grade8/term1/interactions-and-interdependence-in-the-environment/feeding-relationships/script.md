# Part 1 — Expert

This session classifies the organisms of an ecosystem by how they obtain food. The anchor ecosystem is the savanna of the Kruger National Park, and the anchor cast is grass, an impala, a lion, a vulture, a chacma baboon, a dung beetle and the bacteria and fungi in the soil. The governing idea is that every organism is either a producer, which makes its own food, a consumer, which eats other organisms, or a decomposer, which breaks down dead material, and that consumers divide further into herbivores, carnivores and omnivores. The classification is examinable by definition and by example, and it is the vocabulary that food chains and food webs depend on next session.

## Subtopic: Producers: Where All Food Begins

A producer is an organism that makes its own food from simple inorganic substances, using an external source of energy. On land and in water the producers are almost always green plants and algae, which photosynthesise: carbon dioxide and water, with sunlight and chlorophyll, give glucose and oxygen. Some bacteria also produce, including photosynthetic bacteria in ponds and the microscopic phytoplankton that make the sea green and feed everything in it. Producers are also called autotrophs, from the Greek for self-feeding.

Producers are the base of every ecosystem because they are the only organisms that bring energy into the living world. Sunlight falls on grass; the grass stores part of it as chemical energy in glucose, starch and cellulose; and everything else in the ecosystem gets its energy by eating that grass or by eating something that did. Remove the producers and every consumer starves. In the savanna the producers are the grasses, the acacia and marula trees, the shrubs and the herbs. In a dam they are the reeds, the water lilies and the algae. In the ocean they are seaweeds near the shore and phytoplankton everywhere else.

Producers also make the oxygen. Their photosynthesis releases oxygen as a by-product, and it accumulates in the air and dissolves in water, where the consumers use it for respiration. So producers supply the two things every consumer needs, food and oxygen, and that double dependence is why the amount of plant life in an area sets a limit on how much animal life it can support. A Highveld grassland grows far more grass than a Karoo plain, and it carries far more grazing animals as a result.

In classification questions the test is simple: does the organism make its own food? Grass, yes: producer. An oak tree, yes: producer. A mushroom, no: it absorbs food from dead matter, so it is a decomposer, not a producer, even though it grows out of the ground and does not move. Mistaking fungi for producers is the commonest error in this part of the topic.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of a producer, three producers from different ecosystems, and why a mushroom is not one.

## Subtopic: Consumers: Herbivores, Carnivores and Omnivores

A consumer is an organism that cannot make its own food and obtains it by eating other organisms, living or recently dead. Consumers are also called heterotrophs, other-feeding. They are classified by what they eat.

Herbivores eat plants only: impala, zebra, kudu, giraffe, elephant, cattle, locusts, caterpillars, snails. Herbivores are the consumers that feed directly on producers, so they are also called primary consumers, and they are usually the most numerous large animals in an ecosystem. Herbivores have teeth suited to plants: sharp front teeth to crop grass or strip leaves, and broad, flat, ridged molars to grind tough plant material, which is why a cow spends hours chewing. Many have long digestive systems and bacteria in the gut to break down cellulose.

Carnivores eat animals only: lion, leopard, cheetah, wild dog, jackal, snakes, eagles, crocodiles, frogs, spiders and the ladybird that eats aphids. Carnivores that eat herbivores are secondary consumers; a carnivore that eats another carnivore, such as an eagle that takes a snake, is a tertiary consumer. Carnivore teeth are pointed: long canines to grip and kill, and shearing cheek teeth that work like scissors. A predator is a carnivore that hunts and kills live prey; the prey is the animal it eats. A scavenger is a carnivore that feeds on animals already dead: vultures, hyenas much of the time, and jackals when they can. Insectivores are carnivores whose prey is insects: the aardvark eats termites, bats eat moths, and the bat-eared fox eats harvester termites.

Omnivores eat both plants and animals: humans, chacma baboons, bushpigs, crows, rats, many fish. Omnivore teeth are mixed: incisors for biting, canines that are present but modest, and molars that are flat enough to grind. Your own mouth is the diagram: pointed canines at the corners, flat molars at the back.

Classification is by diet, not by size or appearance. A hippo weighs over a tonne and is a herbivore. A praying mantis is tiny and is a carnivore. The exam asks for the category and a justification: "the baboon is an omnivore because it eats fruit, roots and insects as well as small animals." Diet first, label second.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three consumer types with two savanna examples each, teeth as evidence, and predator against scavenger.

## Subtopic: Decomposers: Closing the Loop

A decomposer is an organism that feeds on dead plants and animals and on waste, breaking the organic material down into simple substances that are returned to the soil, water and air. The decomposers are chiefly bacteria and fungi. They do not eat in the animal sense; they release digestive chemicals onto dead material, break it down outside their bodies, and absorb what they need. In the process, carbon returns to the air as carbon dioxide, and nitrogen, phosphorus and other minerals return to the soil as salts that plants can take up again.

Without decomposers an ecosystem would choke on its own dead. Every leaf that fell, every animal that died and every dropping would simply pile up, and the minerals locked inside them would never reach the roots of the next generation of plants. Decomposers close the loop between consumers and producers, and the loop is the nutrient cycle that keeps soil fertile. A compost heap is a decomposer factory: layers of vegetable peelings and garden clippings, kept moist and warm, are turned into dark crumbly compost within months, and the heat you feel inside the heap is the respiration of the bacteria and fungi at work.

Several animals assist decomposition and are sometimes grouped with the decomposers as detritivores: earthworms, millipedes, woodlice, termites and dung beetles. They shred and swallow dead material and dung, speeding up the work the microbes finish. The dung beetle is a South African celebrity. In the Addo Elephant National Park the flightless dung beetle rolls balls of elephant dung and buries them as food for its larvae, so the dung is cleared and the soil is fertilised in one move; road signs there warn drivers to avoid the beetles. Termites in the savanna digest dead grass and wood with the help of microbes in their guts and are responsible for a large share of decomposition in dry regions.

Distinguish three roles that all deal with the dead. A scavenger, such as a vulture, is a consumer eating a dead animal's flesh. A detritivore, such as an earthworm, eats fragments of dead material and dung. A decomposer, a bacterium or a fungus, breaks the remaining material down chemically to its simplest form. Vultures are not decomposers; they are scavenging carnivores. Fungi are not producers; they are decomposers. The marks are in these distinctions.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of a decomposer and the two groups that do it, what decomposition returns and to where, and scavenger against detritivore against decomposer.

## Subtopic: Reading a Feeding Relationship

The three groups are linked by feeding, and the links can be drawn. The convention, which next session develops into food chains and webs, is that an arrow points from the organism that is eaten to the organism that eats it: grass, arrow, impala, arrow, lion. The arrow means "is eaten by" and, more precisely, "energy flows to". Many learners draw the arrow the other way, to show the lion attacking the impala, and lose the mark, because the arrow records the direction in which food and energy move, not the direction in which the predator moves.

Feeding relationships have named partners. In a predator-prey relationship the predator hunts and kills; the lion is the predator and the impala the prey. Interdependence runs both ways. The number of impala limits the number of lions that can be fed, and the lions in turn keep the impala population from growing beyond what the grass can support. A single species can occupy different roles at once: a baboon is prey to a leopard and predator to a scorpion.

Some relationships are not simple eating. A parasite feeds on a living host without killing it quickly: ticks on cattle, tapeworms in a dog, mistletoe drawing water from a tree. Mutualism benefits both partners: the oxpecker bird eats ticks off a giraffe, so the giraffe is cleaned and the oxpecker fed; bees feed on nectar and pollinate the flower. These are examinable as examples of interdependence, the theme of the whole topic.

Layout for marks on a classification question: state the group, give the reason from the diet, give the example. "The vulture is a consumer, specifically a scavenging carnivore, because it eats the flesh of dead animals." On an arrow question: arrow from food to feeder, labelled if required, and every organism placed once.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: fungi as producers; they are decomposers. Two: vultures as decomposers; they are scavengers. Three: arrows pointing from eater to eaten; the arrow shows energy flow to the eater. Four: classifying by size or habitat instead of diet; a hippo is a herbivore. Five: calling a decomposer "something that eats dead things"; a decomposer breaks dead material down into simple substances returned to the environment, and that return is the mark.

The questions for this section are with you now: the direction of the arrow, a parasite and a mutualism, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a kitchen, a queue of eaters, and the cleaners who come in after closing — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Kitchen, the Eaters and the Cleaners

Every ecosystem is a restaurant with three kinds of staff and customers. First, the kitchen: the producers. Green plants and algae cook their own food from air, water and sunlight. They are the only ones who can. Grass, trees, reeds, seaweed, the green scum on a dam: all kitchens. Nobody else in the whole restaurant can make food; they can only eat what the kitchen made, or eat someone who ate it.

Second, the eaters: the consumers. They cannot cook, so they eat. Some eat only from the kitchen directly, plants, and those are the herbivores: impala, cows, goats, locusts, caterpillars. Some eat only other eaters, and those are the carnivores: lions, leopards, snakes, eagles, a spider in your bathroom. And some eat from both menus, and those are the omnivores: baboons, bushpigs, crows, and you, with your pap and your chicken.

Third, after closing, the cleaners: the decomposers. Bacteria and fungi come in and break down everything that died, the fallen leaves, the carcass the vultures left, the dung. They turn it back into simple stuff in the soil that the kitchen uses to grow tomorrow's food. Helping them are the detritivores, earthworms and dung beetles and millipedes, who shred and swallow the big bits first.

One quick trap. A mushroom grows out of the ground, does not move, and looks like a plant. It is not a kitchen. It is a cleaner. Fungi cannot photosynthesise; they feed on the dead.

Keep the restaurant in mind and try the questions for this part: who can cook, who only eats, and who cleans up.

## Subtopic: Teeth Tell the Story

If you want to know what an animal eats, open its mouth. Herbivores grind plants, so they have wide, flat, ridged back teeth like millstones, and front teeth for cropping grass. Think of a cow chewing for hours, or a horse's long flat molars. Carnivores grab and tear meat, so they have long pointed canines for gripping and killing and sharp cheek teeth that slide past each other like scissors; a dog's mouth shows you the whole set. Omnivores have a mixed toolkit: some pointed, some flat. Run your tongue around your own mouth. Pointed canines at the corners, flat molars at the back. You are built for both menus.

Among the carnivores, there are different ways to get meat. A predator hunts and kills: the lion stalking the impala. A scavenger waits for something to die and then eats it: the vulture circling, the hyena at a carcass. An insectivore eats insects: the aardvark digging into a termite mound at night, a bat snatching moths under a street light. All carnivores, different job descriptions.

And the labels come from the diet, never from the size. A hippo is enormous and eats grass: herbivore. A praying mantis is tiny and eats other insects: carnivore. An elephant eats three hundred kilograms of plants a day: herbivore. If an exam shows you a skull with big flat molars and no long canines, say herbivore and say why. Teeth are the evidence.

Hold the mouths in mind and try this part's questions: which teeth say herbivore, which say carnivore, and the three kinds of meat-eater.

## Subtopic: Nothing Is Wasted

Think about what would happen in a town if nobody collected the rubbish. In the bush, millions of leaves fall, animals die, and every animal drops dung every day. Yet the bush is not knee-deep in dead stuff. Someone is cleaning, and the cleaners are the decomposers: bacteria and fungi. They ooze digestive juices onto dead material, dissolve it, and soak up what they need. What is left goes back into the soil as minerals, and into the air as carbon dioxide, which is exactly what the kitchen, the plants, needs to cook with. The loop closes. Nothing is wasted.

A compost heap at home shows it happening. Pile up vegetable peelings and grass cuttings, keep them damp, and in a few months you have dark, crumbly soil. Put your hand in the middle of a working heap and it is warm: that is millions of bacteria and fungi respiring as they eat.

The dung beetles of Addo are the stars of the show. They roll elephant dung into balls bigger than themselves, bury them, and lay eggs inside, so the dung disappears from the surface and fertilises the ground at the same time. There are road signs in the park telling drivers to steer around them, which tells you how important the cleaners are.

Three different jobs around a dead animal, and the exam loves to mix them up. The vulture tears off the meat: that is a scavenger, an eater. The maggots and earthworms chew the scraps: detritivores. The bacteria and fungi finish the job down to minerals: decomposers. Only the last group are decomposers.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Producers cook from sunlight; green plants and algae. Consumers eat: herbivores eat plants, carnivores eat animals, omnivores eat both. Decomposers, bacteria and fungi, break down the dead and return the minerals to the soil. Teeth tell you the diet, and diet gives the label, never size. Vultures are scavengers, not decomposers; mushrooms are decomposers, not producers. And the arrow in a feeding diagram points from the food to the eater, the way the energy goes.

Hold the kitchen, the teeth and the cleaners, and take the final questions of the lesson: why nothing piles up, the dung beetle's double job, and the vulture-versus-bacteria trap.
