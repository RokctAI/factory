# Part 1 — Expert

This session opens the study of ecology by fixing its vocabulary. The anchor is a single impala in the Kruger National Park, and the question is what we call the things around it, from the herd it stands in to the planet it stands on. The governing structure is a nested hierarchy: organism, population, community, ecosystem, biome, biosphere. Inside the ecosystem we separate biotic components, the living things, from abiotic components, the non-living conditions, and we fix the meaning of habitat. Finally we see how organisms adapt to the changes their habitat throws at them, daily and seasonally. Every one of these terms is a definition the mark sheet rewards for exact wording.

## Subtopic: Ecology and the Levels of Study

Ecology is the study of the interactions between living organisms and their environment, and between living organisms and one another. The word is from the Greek oikos, meaning home; ecology is the study of living things at home. Because the living world is organised at different scales, ecologists name the levels at which they work, and the levels nest inside one another like boxes inside boxes.

The organism is a single living individual: one impala. A population is all the individuals of one species living in the same area at the same time: the impala of a particular section of the Kruger. "Same species, same place, same time" is the three-part definition, and all three parts earn marks. A community is all the populations of different species living in an area and interacting: the impala, the acacia trees, the grasses, the lions, the dung beetles, the oxpecker birds, the termites and the bacteria in the soil, taken together. A community is living things only.

An ecosystem is a community together with its non-living environment, with all the interactions between them: the community of the Kruger savanna plus its sunlight, rainfall, temperature, soil and air. The word ecosystem is where the non-living world enters the definition, and that is the examinable difference between a community and an ecosystem. Ecosystems can be any size: a rock pool at Kommetjie, a wetland, a dam, a rotting log, or the whole savanna.

A biome is a very large region sharing a similar climate and a similar kind of vegetation. South Africa is unusually rich in biomes: savanna across the Lowveld and the north, grassland on the Highveld, fynbos in the Western Cape, Nama Karoo and Succulent Karoo in the arid west, forest in small patches such as Knysna, thicket in the Eastern Cape, desert in the far north-west, and the Indian Ocean coastal belt of KwaZulu-Natal. The biosphere is the sum of all the ecosystems on Earth: the thin layer of land, water and air, roughly from the ocean floor to the lower atmosphere, in which life exists. It is the largest level and there is only one.

The questions for this section are with you now: the six levels in order from smallest to largest, the three-part definition of a population, and the difference between a community and an ecosystem.

## Subtopic: Biotic and Abiotic Components

Every ecosystem has two kinds of component. Biotic components are the living things, or things that were recently alive: plants, animals, fungi, bacteria, protists, and also dead leaves, carcasses and dung, because they were living material and are still being used by living things. Abiotic components are the non-living physical and chemical conditions: sunlight, temperature, water, air and wind, soil type and soil chemistry, altitude, slope, and, in water, depth, salinity and current.

The components interact in both directions. Abiotic factors shape the biotic. Rainfall decides whether a region grows forest, savanna or Karoo scrub; the 400 millimetre rainfall line that runs roughly down the middle of South Africa separates the wetter grassland and savanna of the east from the dry Karoo of the west, and the farming changes with it from maize to sheep. Temperature sets the limits of where a species can survive. Soil type decides which plants grow; fynbos grows on nutrient-poor sandy soils where few other plants manage. Light decides how deep in a dam plants can grow.

Biotic components also shape the abiotic. Trees cast shade and lower the temperature beneath them, hold soil against erosion with their roots, and raise humidity by releasing water vapour. Earthworms and termites turn and aerate the soil. Decomposing leaves return minerals to it. Beavers are the textbook example elsewhere; in South Africa, hippos keep channels open in the St Lucia wetland and elephants knock down trees and open up savanna for grasses. A living thing is never merely a passenger in its environment.

The exam skill is sorting. Given a photograph or description of an ecosystem, list the biotic and abiotic components in two columns. Water is abiotic. Fish are biotic. The dead fish floating is biotic, because it is organic material from a living thing. The temperature of the water is abiotic. The algae are biotic. The rock is abiotic; the lichen on the rock is biotic. Students lose marks by classifying dead material as abiotic; the rule is that anything that is or was living is biotic.

The questions for this section are with you now: two columns for a dam, how rainfall shapes a biome, and one example of living things changing their non-living surroundings.

## Subtopic: Habitats

A habitat is the place where an organism lives: the specific part of an ecosystem that provides what it needs to survive. The needs are food, water, shelter, space, and the right conditions, such as temperature and light. The habitat of a barbel is the muddy bottom of a river or dam; the habitat of a rock hyrax, the dassie, is a rocky koppie with crevices; the habitat of a dung beetle is the dung of large herbivores on the savanna floor; the habitat of a leopard is the wooded river banks and rocky outcrops of the bushveld. Habitats are described in terms of their physical features, not in terms of the other animals present.

An ecosystem contains many habitats. The Kruger savanna has riverbank habitats, open-grassland habitats, rocky-outcrop habitats and the inside of a termite mound. A single tree is a set of habitats: the canopy for birds and caterpillars, the bark for lichens and beetles, the hollow trunk for owls and bees, the roots for fungi. Small habitats like these are sometimes called micro-habitats, and they are a useful idea in a school investigation: the underside of a stone, the leaf litter under a hedge, a pot plant's soil.

Do not confuse habitat with ecosystem or with the whole range of a species. The habitat is the type of place: "a leopard's habitat is dense riverine bush and rocky hills". The range is the geographic area where the species is found: "leopards occur across most of sub-Saharan Africa". Ecosystem is the community plus environment of a whole area. A question asking for a habitat wants the kind of place and the features that make it suitable.

Organisms are suited to their habitats. A dassie's rubbery foot pads grip rock; a barbel's whisker-like barbels find food in muddy water where eyes are useless; a dung beetle's shovel-shaped head and strong front legs roll dung. Suitability to habitat is the start of the idea of adaptation, which the next section takes up. When a habitat is destroyed, by clearing bush for farming, draining a wetland, or building over a dune, the organisms that depended on it either move, adapt or die, which is the single largest cause of species loss in South Africa and worldwide.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of habitat and the needs it supplies, habitat against range against ecosystem, and the micro-habitats in one tree.

## Subtopic: Adapting to Change

Habitats change. Some changes are daily: temperature falls at night, light disappears, humidity rises. Some are seasonal: the Highveld has wet summers and dry, frosty winters; the Western Cape has wet winters and dry summers; the Lowveld has a hot wet season and a cooler dry season when grass dries out and water sources shrink. Organisms survive change either by adjusting their behaviour or through features of their bodies.

Daily adaptations include being nocturnal. Many desert and Karoo animals, such as the aardvark, the bat-eared fox and most geckos, are active at night and shelter in burrows by day to avoid heat and water loss. Many plants close their stomata in the heat of the day. Dassies bask in the morning sun to warm up and retreat into shade at midday.

Seasonal adaptations are more dramatic. Migration: swallows that nest in Europe spend the southern summer in South Africa and leave in autumn; wildebeest in the Serengeti follow the rains; sardines run up the KwaZulu-Natal coast in winter following cold water. Hibernation and torpor: some bats and hedgehogs slow their bodies down in winter and live off stored fat. Aestivation is the dry-season equivalent: the African bullfrog buries itself in mud, seals itself in a cocoon of shed skin, and waits, sometimes for months, until the rains return. Deciduous trees drop their leaves in the dry or cold season to save water; the marula and the baobab stand bare through the Lowveld winter. Many grassland plants die back to underground roots and bulbs and resprout after rain or after fire; fynbos plants depend on fire, with seeds that germinate only after a burn.

Food storage and body changes also count: fat reserves laid down before winter, thicker winter coats, and in some birds a change of plumage. Plants store starch in bulbs and tubers for the next season. All of these are responses to predictable change, and the general principle for marks is: name the change, name the response, and say how the response helps survival. "Trees drop leaves in winter" is half an answer; "to reduce water loss when the roots cannot take up enough water" completes it.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: calling a community an ecosystem; the ecosystem includes the non-living environment. Two: calling a dead leaf abiotic; it was living, so it is biotic. Three: defining a population without "same species"; two species in one area are two populations. Four: describing a habitat by listing the animals that live there; a habitat is described by its physical features. Five: naming an adaptation without saying what change it answers.

Layout for marks: define, give an example, state the interaction or the benefit. Three parts, three marks, every time.

The questions for this section are with you now: a daily and a seasonal adaptation, aestivation against hibernation, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a set of nesting boxes, a stage with actors, and a home that has to cope with the weather — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: From One Buck to the Whole Planet

Start with one impala standing in the bush. That is an organism, one living thing. Zoom out a little and you see its herd and all the other impala in that part of the park: a population. A population is one kind of animal, in one place, at one time. Three parts, and if you leave one out the mark goes.

Zoom out more and you see everything alive in that area: impala, lions, grass, thorn trees, vultures, beetles, the bacteria in the soil. All of those living things together are a community. Think of your street: all the people are a population; the people plus their dogs, cats, trees and the ants in the garden are the community.

Now add the things that are not alive: the sun, the rain, the soil, the air, the heat. Living community plus non-living surroundings, all mixed together and affecting each other, is an ecosystem. A dam is an ecosystem. A rotting log is an ecosystem. The Kruger is an ecosystem.

Zoom out again and the ecosystems with the same sort of climate and plants make a biome: the savanna of the Lowveld, the grassland of the Highveld, the fynbos of the Cape, the dry Karoo. Zoom out one last time and every ecosystem on Earth, every ocean and desert and city park, makes the biosphere, the thin skin of the planet where life happens. One impala, one herd, one neighbourhood, one place with its weather, one climate region, one planet. Six boxes, each inside the next.

Keep the nesting boxes in mind and try the questions for this part: the six levels in order, and which one is the first to include the non-living things.

## Subtopic: Living Things and the Stage They Stand On

Picture a school play. There are the actors, and there is the stage with its lights and scenery. In an ecosystem the actors are the living things: plants, animals, fungi, bacteria. Scientists call them biotic, bio meaning life. The stage is everything non-living: sunlight, rain, temperature, soil, wind, rocks. Those are abiotic, a meaning not. Biotic, alive; abiotic, not alive.

One trap. A dead leaf on the ground, a bone, a pile of dung: biotic or abiotic? Biotic. They came from living things and other living things are still eating them. Only things that were never alive, like water, rock, air and heat, are abiotic.

The actors and the stage change each other. The stage decides who can act. Where rain is plentiful, trees grow; where it is scarce, you get Karoo bossies and sheep farms. Where the soil is poor sand, as in the Cape, you get fynbos and little else. But the actors also rebuild the stage. A big tree makes shade, cools the ground, and holds the soil with its roots. Termites and earthworms dig the soil and let air in. Elephants push over trees and turn woodland into grassland. Dead leaves rot and feed the soil for next year.

So when a question gives you a picture of a dam and says "list the biotic and abiotic components", draw two columns. Fish, reeds, frogs, algae, the dead fish: actors. Water, mud, sunlight, temperature, the rock: stage.

Hold the actors and the stage and try this part's questions: which column a dead fish goes in, and one way the actors change the stage.

## Subtopic: Home and the Seasons

A habitat is simply an animal's or a plant's home: the kind of place it lives in, which gives it food, water, shelter and space. A dassie's home is a rocky koppie with cracks to hide in. A barbel's home is the muddy bottom of a dam. A dung beetle's home is, well, dung. Describe a home by what the place is like, rocky, muddy, shady, wet, not by who the neighbours are. One tree alone is a block of flats with many homes: birds in the canopy, beetles under the bark, an owl in the hollow trunk, fungi in the roots.

Homes do not stay the same. Night comes and it gets cold and dark. Winter comes, or the dry season, and food and water run short. Living things have to cope, and they do it in two ways: by changing what they do, or by having bodies built for it.

Changing what they do. Hide from the heat and come out at night, like geckos and bat-eared foxes. Leave, like the swallows that fly to Europe when our autumn comes, or the sardines that follow cold water up the KwaZulu-Natal coast. Sleep through it: some bats slow right down in winter, and the African bullfrog buries itself in mud and seals itself in a skin cocoon until the rains come back, which is called aestivation, the dry-season cousin of hibernation. Bodies built for it. Marula and baobab trees drop their leaves in the dry Lowveld winter so they do not lose water. Grassland plants die back to bulbs underground and shoot again after rain. Animals grow thicker coats and store fat before winter.

The marks rule is always the same three steps: what changed, what the living thing does about it, and how that helps it survive. "The tree drops its leaves" is half marks. "The tree drops its leaves in the dry season so that it loses less water" is full marks.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Six boxes: organism, population, community, ecosystem, biome, biosphere. Biotic is alive or was alive; abiotic never was. A habitat is a home, described by the place. When the home changes, living things move, hide, sleep, or have bodies built for it, and you always say how the response helps.

Hold the nesting boxes, the stage and the home in winter, and take the final questions of the lesson: the home-not-neighbours rule, aestivation, and the three-step answer.
