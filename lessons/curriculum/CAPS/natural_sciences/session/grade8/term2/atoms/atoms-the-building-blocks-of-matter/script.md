# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the atom as the basic building block of all matter. The anchor is a thought experiment that is more than two thousand years old: take a small cube of gold and cut it in half, then cut one half in half again, and keep going. Can you go on for ever, or do you eventually reach a piece that cannot be cut and still be gold? The answer is that after about twenty-five halvings of a one-centimetre cube you reach a single gold atom, and that is the smallest particle that is still gold. The governing idea is that all matter is made of atoms, that each element consists of only one kind of atom, and that the vast variety of substances around us is built from about ninety kinds of naturally occurring atoms combined in different ways. The history of the atomic idea, the size of atoms, the link between atoms and elements, and the alphabet picture of matter are examinable, and they lead directly into the structure of the atom in the next session and into atomic theory in Grade 10 Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: The Idea of the Atom

Around 400 years before the common era, the Greek thinker Democritus, following his teacher Leucippus, argued that matter cannot be divided for ever. If you keep cutting, he said, you must eventually reach particles so small that they cannot be cut any further, and he called them atomos, meaning uncuttable. He imagined that atoms of different substances had different shapes, that they moved in empty space, and that everything we see is made of combinations of atoms. It was a brilliant guess, but it was only a guess; Democritus had no way to test it, and the more influential philosopher Aristotle rejected it in favour of the idea that matter was continuous and made of four elements, earth, air, fire and water. Aristotle's view dominated for nearly two thousand years.

The atom returned as a scientific theory in 1803, when the English teacher and chemist John Dalton used it to explain measurements. Chemists had found that when elements combine to form a compound, they always do so in the same proportions by mass: water always contains eight grams of oxygen for every gram of hydrogen, however it is made. Dalton realised that this made sense if each element consisted of atoms of a fixed mass, and compounds formed when atoms joined in fixed small whole-number ratios. His atomic theory had several parts. All matter is made of tiny particles called atoms. Atoms of the same element are identical in mass and properties, and atoms of different elements are different. Atoms cannot be created, destroyed or split in chemical reactions. Atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios to form compounds, and in a chemical reaction atoms are rearranged.

Parts of Dalton's theory have since been refined. Atoms can be split, though only by nuclear processes, not chemical reactions, and they contain smaller particles, the subject of the next session. Atoms of the same element can have slightly different masses, because they can differ in their number of neutrons. But the core of the theory stands: matter is made of atoms, each element has its own kind of atom, and chemical reactions rearrange atoms without creating or destroying them. The difference between Democritus and Dalton is the difference between philosophy and science; Dalton's idea explained measurements and could be tested.

The questions for this section are with you now: what Democritus meant by atomos, the main points of Dalton's atomic theory, and which parts of it have since been changed.

## Subtopic: How Small Is an Atom?

Atoms are almost unimaginably small. A typical atom has a diameter of roughly one tenth of a nanometre, which is one ten-billionth of a metre. Atoms differ in size, the smallest, helium, being about half of that and the largest several times bigger, but one tenth of a nanometre is a good working value.

Comparisons help. The full stop at the end of this sentence, printed on paper, is about half a millimetre across; that is about five million atoms side by side. A human hair, about seventy micrometres wide, is about seven hundred thousand atoms across. The physicist Richard Feynman offered another picture: if an apple were magnified to the size of the Earth, the atoms in the apple would be roughly the size of the original apple. And the numbers in ordinary objects are enormous. A single drop of water contains about a thousand billion billion molecules of water, each made of three atoms.

The gold-cube thought experiment can be put into numbers. A gold atom is about 0.29 nanometres across. A cube of gold one centimetre on each side, cut in half repeatedly, reaches the size of a single atom after about twenty-five cuts. Twenty-five is a surprisingly small number, because each cut halves the size, and halving twenty-five times divides by more than thirty million. After that, there is nothing left to cut that would still be gold; split the atom itself, which needs a nuclear process, and you no longer have gold at all.

Atoms are too small to be seen with any light microscope, because they are far smaller than the wavelength of light. They were inferred for more than a century from chemical evidence before anyone could image them. Since the 1980s, scanning tunnelling microscopes have been able to map individual atoms on a surface by moving an extremely fine needle across it and measuring a tiny electric current. In 1989 researchers at IBM even arranged thirty-five individual xenon atoms on a nickel surface to spell out the letters of the company's name. So atoms are no longer only a theory; they can be imaged, and even moved one at a time.

The questions for this section are with you now: the approximate size of an atom, the full stop comparison, how many halvings take a gold cube to one atom, and how atoms can be imaged.

## Subtopic: Elements Consist of One Kind of Atom

An element is a substance made of only one kind of atom. A gold ring contains only gold atoms; a piece of copper wire, only copper atoms; the helium in a balloon, only helium atoms. This is the atomic meaning of the definition met earlier, that an element cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical means: there is nothing simpler to break it into, because it contains only one kind of atom.

Atoms of different elements are different. What makes them different is their internal structure, specifically the number of protons in the nucleus, which is the atomic number; that is the subject of the next session. For now the key point is that there are 118 kinds of atom, one for each element, and about ninety of them occur in nature.

The atoms of an element may exist singly or joined in small groups. In helium, neon and the other noble gases the atoms float about as separate single atoms. In oxygen gas, the atoms are joined in pairs, each pair a molecule of two oxygen atoms; nitrogen, hydrogen and chlorine gases are similar. In a metal such as iron or copper, enormous numbers of atoms are packed together in a regular arrangement called a lattice. In sulfur, eight atoms join in a ring. In diamond, every carbon atom is joined to four others throughout the crystal. In every case, though, all the atoms are of the same kind, so the substance is an element.

The properties of an element, its colour, density, melting point and how it reacts, come from the properties of its atoms and the way they are arranged. That is why every sample of pure copper, mined in Limpopo or recycled from old wiring, has the same properties: it is made of the same atoms. And it is why, during chemical reactions, the atoms of one element never turn into atoms of another. When copper reacts with oxygen to form black copper oxide, the copper atoms are still copper atoms, now joined to oxygen atoms, and they can be recovered as copper again. Turning one element into another, the dream of the medieval alchemists who wanted to make gold from lead, is impossible by chemical means.

The questions for this section are with you now: the atomic definition of an element, examples of elements whose atoms are single, paired or in a lattice, and why chemical reactions cannot turn lead into gold.

## Subtopic: The Alphabet of Matter

There are only about ninety kinds of natural atom, yet there are millions of different substances. How can so few building blocks produce such variety? The answer is in the way they combine, and the best picture is the alphabet. English has twenty-six letters, yet those letters make hundreds of thousands of words, because the same letters can be put together in different numbers and different orders. Atoms are the letters of matter, and compounds are the words. Carbon, hydrogen and oxygen alone, combined in different numbers and arrangements, make sugar, starch, cellulose, alcohol, vinegar, fats and thousands of other substances.

A few elements dominate the things around us. In the human body about ninety-six percent of the mass comes from just four elements: oxygen about sixty-five percent, carbon about eighteen, hydrogen about ten and nitrogen about three, mostly in the form of water and the proteins, fats and carbohydrates of cells; calcium and phosphorus in bones and teeth make up most of the rest. Earth's crust is mostly oxygen and silicon, joined together in the minerals of rock and sand, followed by aluminium and iron. The Universe as a whole is mostly hydrogen and helium; the heavier elements were made inside stars, a story for Term 4.

The building-block picture also explains what happens in a chemical reaction. When paper burns, atoms of carbon and hydrogen in the paper join with oxygen atoms from the air to form carbon dioxide and water, which drift away as gas and vapour. The atoms are not destroyed; they are rearranged into new substances. Count the atoms before and after and the totals match, which is why mass is conserved in a sealed container. This idea will be developed fully in the chemical reactions topic later this term.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying atoms can be seen with a school microscope; they are far too small. Two: saying an element contains several kinds of atom; it contains only one kind. Three: saying atoms are destroyed when a substance burns; they are rearranged. Four: saying Democritus proved atoms exist; he proposed them without evidence, and Dalton's theory was based on measurements. Five: saying all atoms are the same size and mass; they differ from element to element.

Layout for marks: state the idea, then give an example. "An element contains only one kind of atom; for example, copper wire contains only copper atoms."

The questions for this section are with you now: the alphabet analogy, the four elements that make up most of the body, why mass is conserved when atoms rearrange, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through cutting a cube in half, a scale that will make your head spin, and an alphabet with ninety letters — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Cut It in Half, Again and Again

Take a little cube of gold, one centimetre on each side. Cut it in half. You have two smaller pieces of gold. Cut one of those in half. Still gold. Keep going. Can you go on for ever?

More than two thousand years ago a Greek thinker called Democritus said no. Eventually, he said, you would reach a piece so tiny that it could not be cut any more. He called it atomos, which means uncuttable, and that is where our word atom comes from. He had no way of proving it, and most people ignored him for about two thousand years.

Then in 1803 an English teacher called John Dalton brought the idea back with real evidence. He noticed that chemicals always combine in exactly the same proportions, and the only sensible explanation was that everything is made of tiny particles of fixed mass. His main ideas: everything is made of atoms; each element has its own kind of atom; atoms are not created or destroyed in chemical reactions, just rearranged; and atoms join in simple whole-number ratios to make new substances.

So how many cuts until you reach one gold atom? Only about twenty-five. Each cut halves the size, and halving twenty-five times shrinks it by more than thirty million times. After that, the next piece would not be gold at all.

## Subtopic: Smaller Than You Can Imagine

Atoms are small in a way that is hard to picture, so let us try a few comparisons.

An atom is about one tenth of a nanometre across. The full stop at the end of a sentence is about five million atoms wide. One of your hairs is about seven hundred thousand atoms wide. If you blew an apple up to the size of the whole Earth, each of its atoms would be about the size of the original apple. And a single drop of water contains about a thousand billion billion water molecules.

That is why no ordinary microscope can show an atom. Light is just too big to see something that small. For more than a hundred years scientists were sure atoms existed only from clever chemical evidence. Then, in the 1980s, a new kind of microscope was invented that feels its way across a surface with an incredibly sharp needle. With it, scientists finally made pictures of single atoms. In 1989 a team even pushed thirty-five atoms around one by one to spell out the name of their company. Atoms went from an idea to something you could arrange like beads.

## Subtopic: Ninety Letters, Millions of Words

An element is a substance made of only one kind of atom. Gold is only gold atoms. The helium in a party balloon is only helium atoms. There are 118 kinds of atom, one for each element, and about ninety are found in nature.

So how do we get millions of different substances from only about ninety kinds of atom? Think of the alphabet. Twenty-six letters make every word in the dictionary, just by joining in different numbers and orders. Atoms are the letters of matter, and the substances around you are the words. Carbon, hydrogen and oxygen alone make sugar, starch, wood, fat, vinegar and alcohol, just arranged differently.

Your own body is mostly four letters: oxygen, carbon, hydrogen and nitrogen make up about ninety-six percent of your mass. The rocks under your feet are mostly oxygen and silicon. The stars are mostly hydrogen and helium.

And when something burns or rusts or rots, the atoms are not destroyed. They are just rearranged, like letters shuffled into new words. That is why chemists can never turn lead into gold: chemistry rearranges letters, but it cannot change one letter into another.

Pocket summary of the lesson. All matter is made of atoms, tiny particles about a tenth of a nanometre across. Democritus guessed it; Dalton proved it with measurements in 1803. An element is made of only one kind of atom. About ninety natural kinds of atom combine like letters into millions of substances. In chemical reactions atoms are rearranged, never created or destroyed.

Hold the gold cube, the scale and the alphabet, and take the final questions of the lesson: what an element is in terms of atoms, the four main elements of the body, and why burning does not destroy atoms.
