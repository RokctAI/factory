# Part 1 — Expert

This session closes the atoms topic with the practical question of how mixtures are separated. The anchor is a bucket of muddy river water collected after a summer storm, carrying sand, leaves, dissolved salts and micro-organisms. By the time water from such a river reaches a tap in Gauteng, every one of those has been removed, using nothing more mysterious than differences in size, density, solubility and boiling point. The governing idea is that the substances in a mixture are not chemically joined and keep their own properties, so they can be separated by physical means, choosing a method that exploits a property in which the components differ. The main methods, hand sorting, sieving, magnetic separation, decanting, filtration, evaporation, crystallisation, distillation and chromatography, the property each one uses, the labelled apparatus, and their use in water purification, mining and recycling are examinable, and they are the practical foundation for laboratory chemistry in Grades 10 to 12.

## Subtopic: Mixtures and the Principle of Separation

A mixture contains two or more substances that are not chemically joined. Each substance keeps its own properties, and the proportions can vary. That is the key to separating them. Since the components are not bonded, no chemical reaction is needed; all that is required is to find a physical property in which they differ and use it.

The properties most often used are particle size, which lets a sieve or a filter hold back larger pieces; magnetism, which lets a magnet pull out iron or steel; density, which lets heavier particles sink or settle while lighter ones float or stay suspended; solubility, which lets one substance dissolve in a liquid while another does not; and boiling point, which lets one liquid evaporate and be collected while another is left behind.

Some mixtures are obviously mixed: you can see the separate parts of sand and gravel, or oil floating on water. These are called heterogeneous mixtures. Others look completely uniform: salt water, sugar water, air and brass all look like a single substance, because one component is spread through another as individual particles. These are called homogeneous mixtures, and a homogeneous mixture of a substance dissolved in a liquid is called a solution. The dissolved substance is the solute, and the liquid it dissolves in is the solvent. In sea water, salt is the solute and water the solvent. Separating a solution needs methods that work at the level of particles, such as evaporation, distillation and chromatography.

Often a mixture needs several methods in sequence, each removing one component. A mixture of sand, salt and iron filings can be separated by first pulling out the iron with a magnet, then adding water to dissolve the salt, then filtering to remove the sand, and finally evaporating the water to recover the salt. Planning the order is part of the skill: the magnet must come first, before the iron is wet and mixed with mud, and the filtering must come before the evaporating.

The questions for this section are with you now: why mixtures can be separated by physical means, the five properties used, the difference between heterogeneous and homogeneous mixtures, and the meaning of solute, solvent and solution.

## Subtopic: Separating Solids and Insoluble Substances

Hand sorting is the simplest method, picking out pieces by hand, as people sort stones from dried beans before cooking, or as workers sort recyclable materials on a conveyor belt. It uses differences in size, shape and colour.

Sieving separates solid particles of different sizes. A sieve has holes of a particular size; smaller particles fall through and larger ones are held back. Builders sieve sand to remove stones, cooks sieve flour to remove lumps, and farmers winnow grain from chaff using the wind, which carries off the lighter husks.

Magnetic separation removes magnetic materials, iron, steel, nickel and cobalt, from a mixture. A magnet run over a mixture of iron filings and sulfur lifts the iron out. At scrapyards and recycling plants, huge electromagnets lift steel from mixed waste.

Decanting means carefully pouring off a liquid and leaving a heavier solid that has settled at the bottom. It is quick but not perfect, since a little liquid stays with the solid and fine particles may be carried over.

Filtration separates an insoluble solid from a liquid. Fold a circle of filter paper into a cone, place it in a funnel over a beaker or flask, and pour the mixture in. The liquid passes through tiny holes in the paper, while the solid particles are too large and are held back. The solid left on the paper is called the residue; the liquid that comes through is called the filtrate. Filtration removes sand and mud from water, coffee grounds from coffee, and tea leaves from tea. It cannot remove a dissolved substance, because dissolved particles are as small as the water molecules and pass straight through.

Evaporation recovers a dissolved solid from a solution by heating it until the solvent turns to vapour and escapes, leaving the solute behind. Salt is recovered from salt water this way; in salt pans along the West Coast and in the Northern Cape, the Sun does the heating. Evaporating to dryness can scatter the solid and damage some substances, so crystallisation is often used instead: the solution is heated only until it is concentrated, then left to cool slowly, and pure crystals form as the solute comes out of solution. Sugar is purified this way in the sugar mills of KwaZulu-Natal.

## Subtopic: Separating Liquids and Dissolved Substances

Evaporation throws away the solvent; distillation keeps it. In distillation, a solution is heated in a flask until the liquid boils. The vapour rises, passes into a condenser, a long tube surrounded by a jacket of cold running water, cools and condenses back to liquid, and drips into a collecting flask as the distillate. The dissolved solids, which do not evaporate, stay behind in the flask. Distillation of sea water produces pure water; large desalination plants supply drinking water to coastal towns in dry regions, and several were built or planned along the South African coast after the Cape Town drought, although most large plants today use a different method that forces water through very fine membranes. A thermometer is placed at the opening to the condenser so that it reads the temperature of the vapour: when it reads 100 degrees, the distillate is water.

Fractional distillation separates mixtures of liquids with different boiling points. When the mixture is heated, the liquid with the lowest boiling point evaporates first. A tall column above the flask, cooler at the top, lets the different vapours condense at different heights, so each fraction can be drawn off separately. Ethanol, which boils at 78 degrees Celsius, can be separated from water this way. Oil refineries, such as Natref in Sasolburg, separate crude oil into fractions including petrol, paraffin, diesel and lubricating oil. Air is liquefied by cooling to below minus two hundred degrees, then fractionally distilled to give pure nitrogen, which boils at minus 196 degrees, and pure oxygen, which boils at minus 183 degrees, for hospitals and industry.

Two liquids that do not mix, such as oil and water, form separate layers and can be separated with a separating funnel, a glass vessel with a tap at the bottom: the lower, denser layer is run off through the tap, and the tap is closed when the boundary reaches it.

Paper chromatography separates dissolved substances, such as the dyes in a coloured ink or food colouring. A small spot of ink is placed on a line drawn in pencil near the bottom of a strip of filter paper, and the bottom edge is dipped in a solvent such as water, with the spot above the solvent level. The solvent soaks up the paper, carrying the dyes with it. Dyes that dissolve well and stick weakly to the paper travel far; others travel a shorter distance; so the ink separates into a row of coloured spots. Pencil is used for the line because graphite does not dissolve, while pen ink would run. Chromatography is used to check food colourings, to identify substances in forensic science and to test whether a substance is pure: a pure substance gives only one spot.

The questions for this section are with you now: the distillation set-up and the role of the condenser, the boiling points used in fractional distillation, the separating funnel, and how a chromatogram is made and read.

## Subtopic: Separation in Everyday Life and Industry

Separation methods are used every day on an enormous scale. Drinking water is the clearest example. Water from a river or dam is screened to remove leaves, sticks and litter. A chemical is added that makes fine mud particles clump together into larger flakes, which then settle to the bottom of large tanks and are removed; the clear water above is decanted. The water is then filtered through deep beds of sand, which hold back remaining fine particles. Finally, a small amount of chlorine is added to kill micro-organisms, which filtration alone cannot remove. Every step uses a physical difference, except the last, which kills rather than separates. Rand Water, which supplies millions of people in Gauteng with water mostly drawn from the Vaal River system, runs this process at huge treatment works.

Mining relies on separation. Gold panners swirl crushed rock and water in a pan so that the dense gold settles to the bottom while lighter sand washes over the rim. Diamond mines in South Africa have long used grease tables: crushed, wet ore flows over a surface coated with grease, and diamonds, which repel water, stick to the grease while other minerals wash away. Recycling plants use magnets to pull out steel cans and other methods to separate aluminium, paper and plastics.

In medicine, a centrifuge spins blood samples very fast, so the denser blood cells are flung to the bottom of the tube and the lighter liquid plasma stays on top; this is used to prepare blood products and to run many tests.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying filtration removes salt from salt water; dissolved substances pass through filter paper. Two: confusing residue and filtrate; the residue stays on the paper, the filtrate passes through. Three: drawing the chromatography start line in pen, or placing the spot below the solvent level, which washes it away. Four: thinking chlorine filters water; it kills micro-organisms. Five: using chemical reactions to separate a mixture; mixtures are separated by physical means.

Layout for marks: name the method, name the property it uses, and say which component goes where. "Filtration separates sand from water because sand particles are too large to pass through the filter paper; the sand stays as the residue and the water passes through as the filtrate."

The questions for this section are with you now: the steps of water purification and the purpose of each, panning and grease tables, the centrifuge, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through one simple rule, a kitchen full of separating tools, and the journey from a muddy river to a clean tap — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Use the Difference

Here is the one rule for separating any mixture: find something that is different about the parts, and use it.

In a mixture, the parts are not stuck together chemically. Sand and water in a bucket are still sand and water. Salt dissolved in water is still salt and still water, even though you cannot see the salt any more. So you do not need chemistry to pull them apart. You just need to find the difference.

Different sizes? Use a sieve or a filter. Is one part magnetic? Use a magnet. Is one part heavier? Let it sink and pour off the rest. Does one part dissolve and the other not? Add water, then filter. Does one part boil at a lower temperature? Heat it and catch the vapour.

Some mixtures are easy to see, like gravel in sand. Others look like one thing, like salt water or the air. When something dissolves, like salt in water, we call the mixture a solution. The salt is the solute, the stuff that dissolves. The water is the solvent, the stuff that does the dissolving.

Sometimes you need a few steps in the right order. Sand, salt and iron filings? Magnet first to take the iron. Then add water so the salt dissolves. Then filter out the sand. Then boil away the water to get the salt back.

## Subtopic: Sieve, Filter, Boil

Your kitchen is already a separating lab. A sieve holds back lumps and lets fine flour through. A tea strainer catches the leaves. That is sieving and filtering, separating by size.

In the lab, filter paper is folded into a cone in a funnel. Pour in muddy water: the mud stays on the paper, and that is called the residue; clear water drips through, and that is called the filtrate. But salt water comes through the filter still salty, because dissolved salt is far too small to be caught.

To get the salt back, boil off the water: that is evaporation. On the West Coast, salt farmers let the Sun do it in wide, shallow pans. To get the water back instead, you need distillation: boil the salt water, send the steam through a cold tube so it turns back into drops of water, and collect the pure water in another flask. The salt stays behind.

Oil and water? They do not mix, so let them form layers and drain the bottom one out through a tap. Mixed-up colours in a felt-tip pen? Put a dot on filter paper, dip the bottom edge in water, and watch the water carry each colour a different distance up the paper. That is chromatography, and a pure colour gives only one spot.

## Subtopic: From Muddy River to Clean Tap

Follow the water to your tap. It starts as a muddy river after a storm. First, screens catch the sticks, leaves and plastic. Next, a chemical makes the tiny mud bits clump together into bigger flakes, which sink to the bottom of big tanks, and the clearer water is poured off the top. Then the water trickles through deep beds of sand, which catch the last fine bits. Last, a little chlorine is added to kill germs, because germs are too small to filter out. Then it is piped to millions of homes.

Separation runs the mines too. Gold panners swirl muddy gravel so the heavy gold sinks. Diamond mines run crushed rock over greasy tables, and the diamonds stick to the grease while everything else washes away. At hospitals, a machine spins blood so fast that the heavy cells go to the bottom and the yellow liquid stays on top. And at recycling plants, giant magnets pick the steel cans out of the rubbish.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Mixtures are not chemically joined, so they are separated by physical means: find the difference and use it. Size: sieve and filter. Magnetism: magnet. Density: settle and decant, pan, separating funnel, centrifuge. Solubility: dissolve and filter. Boiling point: evaporation, distillation and fractional distillation. Chromatography separates dyes. Water treatment uses screens, settling, sand filters and chlorine.

Hold the rule, the kitchen and the river, and take the final questions of the lesson: the order of separating sand, salt and iron, the steps of water treatment, and why chlorine is added last.
