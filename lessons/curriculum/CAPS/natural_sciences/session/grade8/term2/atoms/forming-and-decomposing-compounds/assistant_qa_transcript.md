### subtopic_1 — Forming Compounds by Chemical Reactions
**Assistant:** Why does magnesium need a flame to start burning if the reaction then gives out so much energy?

**Tutor:** Because almost every reaction has to get over an energy hill before it can run downhill, and the flame provides the push to the top. Magnesium atoms in the solid metal are bonded to one another, and the oxygen in the air is in the form of O2 molecules, each held together by a strong bond. Before magnesium oxide can form, some of those existing bonds have to be weakened or broken, and breaking bonds takes energy. At room temperature the particles do not collide with enough energy to do this, so a magnesium ribbon can sit in air for years with only a thin dull coating forming on its surface. Heating the ribbon in a flame gives some of the particles enough energy to break their bonds and react. Once a little magnesium oxide forms, the new bonds between magnesium and oxygen are so strong that forming them releases far more energy than was needed to break the old ones. That released energy heats the neighbouring magnesium enough to react as well, so the reaction spreads along the ribbon by itself, giving the brilliant light and heat you see. The energy needed to get a reaction started is called the activation energy. It explains why a match must be struck, why paper does not burst into flame on your desk, and why a braai needs a firelighter even though charcoal releases plenty of energy when it burns. It also explains why storing reactive substances safely is possible: the hill holds them back. Catalysts, which you meet in later grades, work by lowering that hill so reactions can start more easily.

### subtopic_2 — Fixed Ratios in Compounds
**Assistant:** If 3 grams of magnesium always needs 2 grams of oxygen, why is the ratio not one to one when the formula is MgO?

**Tutor:** Because the formula counts atoms, while grams measure mass, and a magnesium atom does not weigh the same as an oxygen atom. The formula MgO says that magnesium oxide contains one magnesium atom for every oxygen atom, so the ratio by number is indeed one to one. But each magnesium atom has a relative mass of 24, and each oxygen atom a relative mass of 16, so in any sample of magnesium oxide, the magnesium contributes 24 parts of the mass for every 16 parts contributed by oxygen. Twenty-four to sixteen simplifies, by dividing both by eight, to three to two. Think of a bag containing equal numbers of tennis balls and golf balls: the numbers are equal, but most of the mass comes from the tennis balls because each one is heavier. Water shows the same thing even more strongly. Its formula, H2O, has two hydrogen atoms for every oxygen atom, so by number hydrogen wins two to one. But each hydrogen atom has a mass of only 1 against oxygen's 16, so the two hydrogens contribute 2 parts of mass to oxygen's 16, a mass ratio of one to eight. Almost all the mass of water is oxygen. Chemists move between number ratios and mass ratios all the time using relative atomic masses, and in Grade 10 and 11 you will learn the mole, a way of counting atoms by weighing them, which makes these conversions routine. For now, the key idea is that a fixed ratio of atoms always gives a fixed ratio of masses, and that is why every sample of a compound has the same composition.

### subtopic_3 — Decomposition by Heating
**Assistant:** Why is making cement from limestone a problem for the climate?

**Tutor:** Because the chemical reaction at the heart of it releases carbon dioxide directly, in addition to the carbon dioxide from the fuel used to heat the kiln. Cement is made by heating limestone, which is mostly calcium carbonate, together with clay to about fourteen hundred degrees Celsius. At these temperatures calcium carbonate decomposes into calcium oxide and carbon dioxide, and the calcium oxide reacts with the clay minerals to form the compounds in cement. The decomposition cannot be avoided, because the calcium oxide is what makes cement set; and by the fixed-ratio rule, every 100 kilograms of pure calcium carbonate decomposed releases 44 kilograms of carbon dioxide into the air. On top of that, reaching such high temperatures takes a great deal of fuel, traditionally coal, which produces more carbon dioxide when it burns. Together these make cement production responsible for roughly eight percent of the world's carbon dioxide emissions, more than the entire aviation industry. Because so much concrete is used in buildings, roads, bridges and dams, especially in fast-growing cities, the industry is searching for ways to reduce this. Options include replacing part of the limestone-based ingredient with other materials such as fly ash from coal power stations or slag from steelworks, using fuels with less carbon, capturing carbon dioxide from kiln exhausts, and designing structures that use less cement. This is a good example of how a simple Grade 8 reaction, calcium carbonate gives calcium oxide plus carbon dioxide, connects to a major global problem. Understanding the chemistry is the first step in finding solutions.

### subtopic_4 — Decomposition by Electrolysis
**Assistant:** Why does hydrogen collect at the negative electrode and oxygen at the positive one?

**Tutor:** Because during electrolysis the particles that carry the current are charged, and opposite charges attract. Water itself contains a very small number of charged particles, and the acid or salt added to it provides many more, which is why it then conducts. Some of these charged particles are positive and some are negative; they are called ions. When the power supply is switched on, the negative electrode, the cathode, has a surplus of electrons, and positive ions drift towards it. Hydrogen ions, which are hydrogen atoms that have lost their electron, are positive. At the cathode each one picks up an electron, becoming a hydrogen atom again; pairs of hydrogen atoms then join to form H2 molecules, which bubble off as hydrogen gas. At the positive electrode, the anode, electrons are being pulled away into the circuit. Negatively charged particles from the water give up electrons there, and the oxygen in them is released as O2 molecules, which bubble off as oxygen gas. So the hydrogen goes to the negative side because it travels there as positive ions, and oxygen is released at the positive side. The volumes follow from the formula. Each water molecule supplies two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom, and both gases form diatomic molecules, so twice as many hydrogen molecules are made as oxygen molecules, giving twice the volume. The same principle, positive particles to the negative electrode, explains why copper metal, from positive copper ions, coats the cathode when copper chloride is electrolysed, and why molten aluminium collects at the cathode in the Richards Bay smelter.
