# Part 1 — Expert

This session shows how compounds are made and how they are taken apart again. The anchor is a ribbon of magnesium held in tongs over a flame. It catches and burns with a blinding white light, and what is left is a white powder that weighs more than the ribbon did. Then the opposite: a green powder, copper carbonate, heated in a test tube turns black, and the gas it gives off turns limewater milky. The governing ideas are that compounds form when elements react chemically, always combining in fixed ratios; and that compounds can be broken down, decomposed, into simpler substances only by chemical means, such as strong heating or passing an electric current, called electrolysis. Word equations for forming and decomposing compounds, the fixed-ratio idea with simple mass calculations, the signs of a chemical reaction, and the tests for hydrogen, oxygen and carbon dioxide are examinable, and they prepare the ground for balanced equations and stoichiometry in Grades 10 and 11.

## Subtopic: Forming Compounds by Chemical Reactions

A chemical reaction is a process in which one or more substances, the reactants, change into one or more new substances, the products. When elements react to form a compound, atoms of the different elements bond together, and a substance with new properties appears.

Magnesium burning in air is the classic example. Magnesium is a shiny grey metal; oxygen is a colourless gas. When the magnesium is heated, it reacts with oxygen in the air with a brilliant white flame, and the product is magnesium oxide, a white powder. The word equation is: magnesium plus oxygen gives magnesium oxide. A word equation names the reactants on the left, joined by plus signs, an arrow meaning gives or forms, and the products on the right.

Iron and sulfur heated together form iron sulfide: iron plus sulfur gives iron sulfide. Carbon burning in oxygen, as charcoal does in a braai, forms carbon dioxide: carbon plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide. Hydrogen burning in oxygen forms water: hydrogen plus oxygen gives water. Sodium reacting with chlorine forms sodium chloride. In each case two elements become one compound, and the compound's properties are different from both elements.

Several observations tell you that a chemical reaction has happened, rather than a physical change such as melting or dissolving. A new substance with different properties forms. There may be a colour change, as when grey magnesium becomes white powder. A gas may be given off, seen as bubbles or fizzing. Energy is usually released or absorbed, felt as heat or seen as light, as in the magnesium flame. A solid may form when two solutions are mixed. And the change is usually difficult to reverse; you cannot simply cool magnesium oxide and get the magnesium back. A physical change, such as ice melting or sugar dissolving, produces no new substance and is easily reversed.

The atoms themselves are neither created nor destroyed in a reaction; they are rearranged into new combinations. That is why the white powder from the magnesium weighs more than the ribbon: it contains the original magnesium atoms plus the oxygen atoms they picked up from the air.

The questions for this section are with you now: writing word equations for forming compounds, the signs of a chemical reaction, the difference between chemical and physical changes, and why magnesium oxide weighs more than the magnesium.

## Subtopic: Fixed Ratios in Compounds

When elements combine to form a particular compound, they always do so in the same ratio of atoms, and therefore in the same ratio by mass. This is called the law of constant composition, and it was one of the key pieces of evidence for Dalton's atomic theory.

Magnesium oxide contains one magnesium atom for every oxygen atom. A magnesium atom is about one and a half times as heavy as an oxygen atom, with relative atomic masses of 24 and 16, so in magnesium oxide the mass ratio of magnesium to oxygen is always 24 to 16, which simplifies to 3 to 2. That means 3 grams of magnesium always combine with exactly 2 grams of oxygen to make 5 grams of magnesium oxide. Scale it up: 12 grams of magnesium combine with 8 grams of oxygen to make 20 grams of magnesium oxide; 6 grams of magnesium need 4 grams of oxygen and make 10 grams of product.

Water contains two hydrogen atoms for every oxygen atom. Hydrogen's relative atomic mass is 1 and oxygen's is 16, so the mass ratio is 2 to 16, or 1 to 8: one gram of hydrogen combines with eight grams of oxygen to give nine grams of water. Iron sulfide, with one iron atom of mass 56 to one sulfur atom of mass 32, has a mass ratio of 56 to 32, which simplifies to 7 to 4.

If the reactants are not supplied in the right ratio, the extra amount of one of them is simply left over, unreacted. Heat 10 grams of iron with 4 grams of sulfur: 4 grams of sulfur need 7 grams of iron, so 7 grams of iron react, 11 grams of iron sulfide form, and 3 grams of iron are left over. This is the difference between a compound and a mixture: a mixture can contain its parts in any proportion, while a compound takes exactly the ratio its formula demands.

The fixed ratio also explains the masses in the magnesium experiment. Every gram of the product is the original magnesium plus the oxygen that joined it; nothing is lost or gained except what crosses the boundary of the container. In a sealed container, the total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products. This is the law of conservation of mass, which you will use constantly in later grades.

The questions for this section are with you now: the law of constant composition, calculating the mass of magnesium oxide from a mass of magnesium, finding the leftover reactant, and the conservation of mass.

## Subtopic: Decomposition by Heating

Decomposition is the opposite of forming a compound: one compound breaks down into two or more simpler substances. When strong heating causes the breakdown, it is called thermal decomposition.

Copper carbonate is a green powder. Heated strongly in a test tube, it turns black, and a gas is given off. The black solid is copper oxide, and the gas is carbon dioxide. The word equation is: copper carbonate gives copper oxide plus carbon dioxide. The test for carbon dioxide is to bubble the gas through limewater, a clear solution of calcium hydroxide; carbon dioxide turns it milky, because an insoluble white solid, calcium carbonate, forms in it.

Limestone, mostly calcium carbonate, decomposes in the same way when heated to about nine hundred degrees Celsius in a kiln: calcium carbonate gives calcium oxide plus carbon dioxide. Calcium oxide, called quicklime, is used to make cement, to treat acidic soils, and in the purification of water and the extraction of metals. The masses follow the fixed-ratio rule: 100 grams of pure calcium carbonate decomposes into 56 grams of calcium oxide and 44 grams of carbon dioxide, and the 44 grams escape into the air, which is why the solid left in the kiln weighs less than the limestone put in. Cement factories in South Africa, near limestone deposits in the North West, Gauteng and the Western Cape, run this reaction on a huge scale, and it is one of the world's large sources of carbon dioxide emissions.

Other compounds decompose when heated. Sugar heated in a dry test tube turns brown, then black, and droplets of water collect on the cool upper part of the tube; the black residue is carbon, showing that sugar contains carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. In 1774 Joseph Priestley heated mercury oxide, a red powder, and collected a gas in which a candle burned brilliantly; it was oxygen, and the mercury metal remained.

Not every compound decomposes easily. Water and table salt do not break down even at the temperature of a Bunsen flame; they need a different method.

The questions for this section are with you now: the decomposition of copper carbonate and its word equation, the limewater test, the mass calculation for limestone, and what heating sugar shows.

## Subtopic: Decomposition by Electrolysis

Electrolysis is the decomposition of a compound by passing an electric current through it, when it is melted or dissolved in water. Two electrodes, usually rods of carbon or platinum, are placed in the liquid and connected to a battery or power supply: the positive electrode is called the anode and the negative electrode the cathode.

The electrolysis of water is the classic example. Pure water conducts electricity very poorly, so a small amount of dilute acid or a salt such as sodium sulfate is added to let the current flow. When the current is switched on, bubbles of gas form at both electrodes. At the negative electrode the gas is hydrogen; at the positive electrode it is oxygen. The word equation is: water gives hydrogen plus oxygen. If the gases are collected in two upturned test tubes, the volume of hydrogen is always twice the volume of oxygen, which matches the formula H2O: twice as many hydrogen atoms as oxygen atoms, and both gases made of diatomic molecules.

The gases are identified by standard tests. Hydrogen: hold a lighted splint at the mouth of the tube and the gas burns with a squeaky pop, as hydrogen combines with oxygen from the air to form water. Oxygen: insert a glowing splint, one that has been lit and blown out so it is just glowing, and it relights, because oxygen supports burning.

Electrolysis is used in industry to break down compounds that heat cannot. Aluminium is extracted from its ore, aluminium oxide, by electrolysis of the molten oxide; the Hillside smelter at Richards Bay in KwaZulu-Natal produces hundreds of thousands of tonnes a year this way, using enormous amounts of electricity. Electrolysis of salt water produces chlorine for purifying water and making plastics, and sodium hydroxide for soap. Copper is purified by electrolysis to the very high purity needed for electrical wires. The electrolysis of copper chloride solution in the classroom deposits pink-brown copper on the negative electrode and releases chlorine, with its swimming-pool smell, at the positive one.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: calling melting or dissolving a chemical reaction; no new substance forms. Two: thinking mass is lost when magnesium burns; it gains oxygen. Three: putting hydrogen at the positive electrode; it forms at the negative. Four: mixing up the tests; a glowing splint relights in oxygen, and a lighted splint pops in hydrogen. Five: thinking any ratio of elements will form a compound; the extra is left over.

Layout for marks: reactants, arrow, products in a word equation; then the observation and the test.

The questions for this section are with you now: the electrolysis of water with its 2 to 1 ratio, the tests for hydrogen and oxygen, one industrial use of electrolysis, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a burning ribbon, a recipe that never changes, and taking a compound apart with heat and electricity — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Making Something New

Hold a strip of magnesium in tongs and put it in a flame. It catches and burns with a light so bright you must not stare at it. When it stops, there is a white powder left. The shiny grey metal is gone, and a new white substance has appeared. That new substance is magnesium oxide, made from magnesium and oxygen from the air. We write it like this: magnesium plus oxygen gives magnesium oxide. That is a word equation.

How do you know something new was made? Look for the clues: a change in colour, bubbles of gas, heat or light given out, and a change you cannot easily undo. You cannot cool the white powder and get your shiny ribbon back. That is a chemical change. Compare melting ice: it is still water, and it freezes again easily. That is a physical change.

And here is a surprise. The white powder is heavier than the ribbon. Did the fire make mass? No. The magnesium grabbed oxygen atoms from the air, and those atoms have mass. Atoms are never made or destroyed in a reaction; they just swap partners.

Keep the burning ribbon in mind and try the questions for this part: writing a word equation, the clues for a chemical change, and why the powder is heavier.

## Subtopic: The Fixed Recipe

Every compound has a fixed recipe, like a recipe for a cake that must never change. Water is always two hydrogens to one oxygen. Magnesium oxide is always one magnesium to one oxygen.

Because the atoms have different weights, the recipe by weight is fixed too. For magnesium oxide it is 3 grams of magnesium for every 2 grams of oxygen, making 5 grams of product. Double it: 6 grams and 4 grams make 10 grams. For water it is 1 gram of hydrogen for every 8 grams of oxygen. For iron sulfide it is 7 grams of iron for every 4 grams of sulfur.

What if you put in too much of one ingredient? The extra is just left over. Use 10 grams of iron with 4 grams of sulfur, and only 7 grams of iron react. You get 11 grams of iron sulfide and 3 grams of iron sitting there unused. A mixture is happy with any amounts, but a compound takes only what its recipe says.

And the total weight never changes if nothing can escape. Weigh everything before and after in a sealed container, and the scale shows the same number.

Hold the fixed recipe in mind and try this part's questions: how much magnesium oxide 6 grams of magnesium makes, and what happens to leftover ingredients.

## Subtopic: Taking It Apart Again

Compounds can be broken back down into simpler substances, but only with chemistry. That is called decomposition, and there are two main ways.

Way one: strong heat. Heat green copper carbonate and it turns black, giving off carbon dioxide gas. Bubble the gas into limewater and the limewater goes milky; that is the test for carbon dioxide. Heat limestone in a kiln and it breaks into quicklime and carbon dioxide; 100 grams of limestone leaves only 56 grams of quicklime, because 44 grams of gas floats away. Heat sugar and it turns black: that black stuff is carbon.

Way two: electricity. Pass an electric current through water with a little acid added and bubbles rise from both rods. Hydrogen comes off at the negative rod and oxygen at the positive rod, and there is always twice as much hydrogen, because water has two hydrogens for every oxygen. Test them: a burning splint makes hydrogen go pop; a glowing splint bursts back into flame in oxygen. This is called electrolysis, and factories use it to get aluminium out of its ore at Richards Bay.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Elements react to form compounds, like magnesium plus oxygen gives magnesium oxide. Signs of a chemical change: new substance, colour change, gas, heat or light, hard to undo. Compounds always form in a fixed ratio, and any extra reactant is left over; mass is conserved. Compounds decompose by heating, like copper carbonate and limestone, or by electrolysis, like water into hydrogen and oxygen in a 2 to 1 ratio. Limewater tests carbon dioxide, pop tests hydrogen, glowing splint tests oxygen.

Hold the burning ribbon, the recipe and the two ways of taking apart, and take the final questions of the lesson: which gas forms at the negative electrode, what limewater shows, and the mass of quicklime from limestone.
