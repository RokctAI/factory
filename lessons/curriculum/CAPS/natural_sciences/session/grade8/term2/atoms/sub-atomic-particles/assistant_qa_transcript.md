### subtopic_1 — Discovering the Inside of the Atom
**Assistant:** How could Rutherford work out that the nucleus was tiny just from particles bouncing off foil?

**Tutor:** From the proportions of what happened, which is a beautiful piece of reasoning from statistics. Think of throwing tennis balls blindfold at a fence that has a few small poles in it and is otherwise open netting. If almost every ball goes through and only a very rare one bounces back, you can conclude that the solid parts are a tiny fraction of the area the balls are passing through. Rutherford's team counted the flashes made when the particles hit a screen placed at different angles around the foil, sitting in a darkened room for hours at a time. The great majority went straight through or were deflected by only a degree or two. Only about one in eight thousand was turned back by more than ninety degrees. For a particle to be turned right round, it must have come very close to a concentrated positive charge with a large mass; a spread-out charge, as in Thomson's pudding, could never produce such a violent push. And since the foil was a few hundred atoms thick, yet only one particle in thousands came close enough to a nucleus to be reversed, the target each nucleus presents must be extremely small compared with the atom. Rutherford worked out the mathematics of how the number of particles should fall off with angle if all the positive charge were at a point, and the counts matched his formula. From the closest approaches he estimated that the nucleus was at most about one ten-thousandth to one hundred-thousandth the size of the atom. Later, more precise experiments confirmed that estimate. No one saw a nucleus; its size was deduced from counting.

### subtopic_2 — Protons, Neutrons and Electrons
**Assistant:** If protons repel each other, why does the nucleus not fly apart?

**Tutor:** Because there is a second, much stronger force acting inside the nucleus that only works over extremely short distances. Two protons, both positive, do repel each other electrically, and at the tiny separations inside a nucleus that repulsion is enormous. If electrical forces were the only ones, every nucleus with more than one proton would burst apart instantly. The force that prevents this is called the strong nuclear force. It acts between all nucleons, proton to proton, proton to neutron and neutron to neutron, pulling them together. Over the distances inside a nucleus it is far stronger than the electrical repulsion, but it fades to almost nothing over a distance only a little larger than a nucleus, which is why we never notice it in everyday life and why it does not pull separate atoms together. Neutrons are crucial to this balance. They add to the strong attraction without adding any electrical repulsion, acting like glue. That is why heavier nuclei, with more protons pushing apart, need proportionally more neutrons: helium has two protons and two neutrons, while lead has eighty-two protons and usually about one hundred and twenty-six neutrons. In the very largest nuclei, such as uranium, the balance is barely held, and they can break apart, releasing energy; that is radioactivity, and splitting such nuclei on purpose is how nuclear power stations, such as Koeberg near Cape Town, generate electricity. The strong force also powers the Sun, where hydrogen nuclei are forced together to make helium, which you will meet in Term 4.

### subtopic_3 — Atoms Are Neutral
**Assistant:** If an atom can lose electrons, why can it not lose protons just as easily in a reaction?

**Tutor:** Because the protons are locked deep inside the tiny nucleus by the strong nuclear force, while the electrons are on the outside, held by much weaker electrical attraction, and only the outer electrons take part in chemical reactions. When two atoms meet in a chemical reaction, it is their outermost electrons that come into contact and interact; the nuclei stay far apart, buried in the middle of their atoms, and never touch. The energies involved in chemical reactions, the energy of a flame, a battery or a reaction in your cells, are enough to move outer electrons from one atom to another or to make atoms share them. They are nowhere near enough to break open a nucleus, which would take roughly a million times more energy. So in chemistry the number of protons in every atom is fixed: carbon stays carbon, oxygen stays oxygen, and only electrons are gained, lost or shared. That is exactly why Dalton's claim that atoms are not changed in chemical reactions holds up so well; it is true of the nucleus. When an atom gains or loses electrons it becomes an ion, a charged particle. Sodium tends to lose its single outer electron, becoming an ion with eleven protons and ten electrons, a charge of plus one; chlorine tends to gain one, becoming an ion with seventeen protons and eighteen electrons, a charge of minus one. Opposite charges attract, which is how table salt holds together. Changing the number of protons requires nuclear processes in reactors, stars or radioactive decay, and when it happens, the element itself changes.

### subtopic_4 — Drawing a Model of the Atom
**Assistant:** If the Bohr model with neat orbits is not quite right, why do we still draw it?

**Tutor:** Because a model does not have to be perfectly true to be useful; it has to explain and predict the things you need it for, and the shell model does that very well at this level. Bohr's model correctly captures several important facts. Electrons are arranged at different energy levels, filling the lowest first. The outermost electrons determine how an atom reacts. A full outer shell makes an atom stable and unreactive, which explains the noble gases. Atoms in the same group have the same number of outer electrons, which explains why groups share properties. The model even explains why heated elements give off light only at particular colours: electrons jump between energy levels and give out light of a specific energy when they fall back. What the model gets wrong is the idea of electrons moving in neat circular paths like planets. Modern physics shows that you cannot know an electron's exact position and motion at the same time; instead, there are regions around the nucleus where an electron is likely to be found, called orbitals, with shapes that are spheres, dumbbells and more complicated forms. That picture is needed to explain the shapes of molecules and the details of bonding, which come in later grades. Science works with a ladder of models. A map of South Africa at the scale of the whole country is useful for planning a long journey even though it leaves out every street; for finding a house, you need a street map. The shell diagram is the country map of the atom: simplified, not to scale, and exactly right for the questions Grade 8 and Grade 9 ask.
