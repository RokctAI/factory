# Part 1 — Expert

This session opens the atom and looks inside. The anchor is an experiment performed in Manchester in 1909, in which a beam of tiny, fast, positively charged particles was fired at a sheet of gold foil only a few hundred atoms thick. Almost all of them passed straight through, as if the foil were empty space. But about one in every eight thousand bounced back towards the source, which Ernest Rutherford later said was as astonishing as firing a large shell at a sheet of tissue paper and having it come back and hit you. The governing idea that came out of that experiment is that an atom is mostly empty space, with a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus at its centre, containing protons and neutrons, and electrons moving in the space around it. The three sub-atomic particles with their charges, masses and positions, the neutrality of the atom, and the use of atomic number and mass number to count the particles are all examinable, and they are the starting point of atomic structure in Grade 10 Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: Discovering the Inside of the Atom

Dalton pictured atoms as solid, indivisible balls. That picture was broken in 1897, when the English physicist J. J. Thomson showed that the rays produced in glass tubes with electric currents were streams of tiny, negatively charged particles, far lighter than any atom, and that the same particles came out of every kind of metal he tested. He had discovered the electron, the first sub-atomic particle, and proved that atoms have parts. Since atoms are electrically neutral, Thomson reasoned that the negative electrons must be balanced by positive charge, and he proposed that an atom was a ball of positive material with electrons scattered through it, like raisins in a pudding; this became known as the plum pudding model.

Rutherford's team tested that model. If positive charge were spread thinly through the whole atom, the fast positive particles fired at the gold foil should all pass through with only tiny deflections. Most did. But a few were deflected through large angles, and some bounced almost straight back. The only explanation was that all the positive charge and nearly all the mass of the atom are concentrated in a very small central region, which strongly repels any positive particle that happens to approach it head-on. Rutherford called this region the nucleus, and in 1911 he proposed the nuclear model: a tiny positive nucleus with electrons in the large space around it. Because most of the particles passed straight through, most of the atom must be empty space.

The positive particles in the nucleus were identified as protons, each carrying one unit of positive charge, equal and opposite to the charge on an electron. But the masses did not add up: a helium nucleus has two protons yet about four times the mass of a hydrogen nucleus. Rutherford predicted a neutral particle in the nucleus, and in 1932 his former student James Chadwick discovered it: the neutron, with almost the same mass as a proton but no charge.

The questions for this section are with you now: what Thomson discovered and his plum pudding model, what the gold foil experiment showed and why, and who discovered the neutron.

## Subtopic: Protons, Neutrons and Electrons

An atom contains three kinds of sub-atomic particle, and each must be described by its charge, its mass and its position.

The proton carries a charge of plus one, has a relative mass of one, and is found in the nucleus. The neutron has no charge, so it is neutral, has a relative mass of one, almost exactly the same as a proton, and is also found in the nucleus. Protons and neutrons together are called nucleons. The electron carries a charge of minus one and has a very small mass, about one eighteen-hundred-and-thirty-sixth of the mass of a proton, so small that it is usually treated as zero in mass calculations. Electrons are found outside the nucleus, moving rapidly in the space around it.

Because protons and neutrons are so much heavier than electrons and are packed into the nucleus, more than 99.9 percent of the mass of an atom is in its nucleus. Yet the nucleus is tiny compared with the whole atom. The diameter of a nucleus is roughly one hundred thousandth of the diameter of the atom. Picture an atom enlarged to the size of a sports stadium about one hundred metres across: the nucleus would be about one millimetre across, a grain of sand on the centre spot, and the electrons would be moving through the whole stadium, out to the back of the stands. Everything else is empty space. The solidity of a table comes from the electrons of its atoms repelling the electrons of your hand's atoms, not from the atoms being packed full of material.

The nucleus holds together even though its protons, all positive, repel one another strongly. A different force, the strong nuclear force, acts between nucleons over very short distances and overwhelms the electrical repulsion; the neutrons add to that attraction without adding repulsion, which is part of why they are needed. The electrons are held around the nucleus by the electrical attraction between their negative charge and the positive nucleus.

The questions for this section are with you now: the charge, relative mass and position of each particle, the stadium picture of the nucleus, and why nearly all of an atom's mass is in its nucleus.

## Subtopic: Atoms Are Neutral

An atom as a whole has no overall charge. It is neutral because the number of electrons equals the number of protons, so the positive and negative charges cancel exactly. A carbon atom has six protons, giving plus six, and six electrons, giving minus six: total zero. Neutrons, having no charge, do not affect the balance.

This gives a quick method for counting particles. The atomic number is the number of protons, and in a neutral atom it is also the number of electrons. The mass number is the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus, which is a whole number close to the relative atomic mass. So the number of neutrons equals the mass number minus the atomic number.

Worked examples. Carbon has atomic number 6 and the common atom has mass number 12: six protons, six electrons, and twelve minus six gives six neutrons. Sodium, atomic number 11, mass number 23: eleven protons, eleven electrons, twenty-three minus eleven gives twelve neutrons. Aluminium, atomic number 13, mass number 27: thirteen protons, thirteen electrons, fourteen neutrons. The common chlorine atom, atomic number 17 and mass number 35: seventeen protons, seventeen electrons, eighteen neutrons. Oxygen, atomic number 8, mass number 16: eight of each. Hydrogen is the special case: its common atom has one proton, one electron and no neutrons at all.

If an atom gains or loses electrons, it is no longer neutral; it becomes a charged particle called an ion. A sodium atom that loses one electron has eleven protons and ten electrons, an overall charge of plus one. That is studied in Grade 9 and Grade 10, but it shows why the neutral atom rule depends on electrons and protons being equal. The number of protons can never change in a chemical reaction; the number of electrons can.

The questions for this section are with you now: why atoms are neutral, how to count protons, neutrons and electrons from the atomic number and mass number, and what happens to the charge if an electron is lost.

## Subtopic: Drawing a Model of the Atom

A model is a simplified representation that helps explain and predict, and the model of the atom has changed as evidence has grown. Dalton's solid ball was replaced by Thomson's plum pudding, which was replaced by Rutherford's nuclear atom. In 1913 the Danish physicist Niels Bohr improved the nuclear model by proposing that electrons move only in particular shells, or energy levels, at fixed distances from the nucleus, much like planets in fixed orbits, though the comparison is only rough. Later twentieth-century physics replaced the neat orbits with regions where electrons are likely to be found, called orbitals, which you will meet in Grade 10. Each model kept what worked from the one before and fixed what failed.

For Grade 8, an atom is drawn as a small circle for the nucleus, labelled with its numbers of protons and neutrons, surrounded by circles for the electron shells, with electrons marked as dots or crosses on them. For the first twenty elements the first shell holds up to two electrons, the second up to eight and the third up to eight, filling from the inside out. Carbon, with six electrons, is drawn with two in the first shell and four in the second. Sodium, with eleven, has two, then eight, then one. Argon, with eighteen, has two, eight and eight: full shells, which matches its place among the unreactive noble gases. Calcium, with twenty, has two, eight, eight and two.

Remember that such a drawing is not to scale. If the nucleus were drawn the size it appears on the page, the shells would have to be hundreds of metres away. The diagram shows the structure, not the proportions.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: putting electrons in the nucleus; they are outside it. Two: giving the neutron a negative charge; it is neutral. Three: saying the electron and proton have the same mass; the electron is about one eighteen-hundred-and-thirty-sixth of the proton's mass. Four: saying an atom is neutral because it has equal numbers of protons and neutrons; it is neutral because protons equal electrons. Five: calculating neutrons by adding the atomic number to the mass number instead of subtracting.

Layout for marks: for any atom, write protons equals atomic number, electrons equals protons, neutrons equals mass number minus atomic number, then draw the nucleus and the shells.

The questions for this section are with you now: the sequence of atomic models, drawing the atoms of carbon, sodium and argon, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a stadium with a grain of sand on the centre spot, three particles with three charges, and a perfect balance — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Stadium With a Grain of Sand

For a long time people thought atoms were solid little balls. Then, in 1909, scientists fired tiny positive particles at a sheet of gold foil so thin that light could almost shine through it. Nearly all the particles zoomed straight through, as if nothing was there. But about one in eight thousand bounced right back. That was shocking, like throwing a cricket ball at a sheet of paper and having it bounce back at you.

The explanation: the atom is almost completely empty, but right in the middle is a tiny, heavy, positive centre called the nucleus. Most particles miss it and fly through the emptiness. The rare one that heads straight at the nucleus gets pushed back, because positive pushes positive away.

How tiny is the nucleus? Imagine one atom blown up to the size of a big stadium. The nucleus would be a grain of sand sitting on the centre spot. The electrons would be zipping around the whole stadium, all the way to the top seats. Everything else is empty. And yet that grain of sand holds almost all the atom's weight.

So why does a table feel solid if its atoms are mostly empty? Because the electrons on the outside of the table's atoms push against the electrons of your hand. You never actually touch the nucleus of anything.

Keep the stadium in mind and try the questions for this part: what the gold foil showed, where most of the mass is, and why the atom is mostly empty.

## Subtopic: Three Particles, Three Charges

Inside every atom there are three kinds of particle.

Protons live in the nucleus. They are positive, plus one each. Neutrons also live in the nucleus. They are neutral, no charge at all, and they weigh about the same as a proton. Electrons live outside the nucleus, whizzing around it. They are negative, minus one each, and they are super light: it takes about eighteen hundred electrons to weigh as much as one proton.

A simple way to remember: protons are positive, neutrons are neutral, and electrons are, well, the other one, negative, and they are the ones outside.

They were found one at a time. The electron came first, in 1897, by Thomson, who thought the atom was like a pudding with raisins in it. Then Rutherford's gold foil found the nucleus in 1911, with its protons. The neutron was found last, in 1932, by Chadwick, because something with no charge is very hard to detect.

What holds it together? The negative electrons are pulled towards the positive nucleus, the way opposite charges attract. And inside the nucleus a super-strong short-range force glues the protons and neutrons together, even though the protons are trying to push each other apart.

Hold the three particles in mind and try this part's questions: the charge of each particle, which ones live in the nucleus, and which one is lightest.

## Subtopic: A Perfect Balance

Every atom has the same number of electrons as protons. So the plus charges and minus charges cancel out, and the atom has no overall charge. It is neutral.

That makes counting easy. Look up the atomic number: that is the number of protons, and also the number of electrons. The mass number tells you protons plus neutrons together. So neutrons are mass number take away atomic number.

Try sodium: atomic number 11, mass number 23. Protons 11, electrons 11, neutrons 23 minus 11, which is 12. Try carbon: 6 and 12. Six protons, six electrons, six neutrons.

To draw an atom, put the protons and neutrons in a little circle in the middle, then draw rings around it for the electrons. The first ring holds two, the next holds eight, the one after that holds eight. Sodium's eleven electrons go two, eight, one. Remember the drawing is not to scale; the real rings would be much, much farther out.

Pocket summary of the lesson. An atom has a tiny positive nucleus with protons and neutrons, and negative electrons moving in mostly empty space around it. Protons are plus one with mass one, neutrons have no charge and mass one, electrons are minus one with almost no mass. Atoms are neutral because protons equal electrons. Neutrons equal mass number minus atomic number. Models of the atom changed from solid balls, to pudding, to a nucleus, to shells, as new evidence arrived.

Hold the stadium, the three particles and the balance, and take the final questions of the lesson: the particles in an aluminium atom, why the atom is neutral, and how electrons fill the rings.
