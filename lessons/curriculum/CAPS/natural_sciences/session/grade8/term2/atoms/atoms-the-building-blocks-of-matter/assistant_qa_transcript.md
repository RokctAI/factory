### subtopic_1 — The Idea of the Atom
**Assistant:** If Democritus had the right idea, why do we say Dalton founded atomic theory?

**Tutor:** Because a scientific theory is judged by the evidence it explains and the predictions it makes, not only by whether it turns out to be right. Democritus reasoned from first principles: he could not accept that matter could be divided for ever, so he concluded there must be smallest particles. That was clever, but it was not tested against anything. Aristotle reasoned just as carefully to the opposite conclusion, and for two thousand years there was no way to decide between them, because neither made a prediction that could be checked by experiment. By Dalton's time, chemists had begun to weigh substances precisely before and after reactions. They found that a compound always contains its elements in the same proportions by mass, and that when two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one element combining with a fixed mass of the other are in simple ratios such as one to two. Dalton saw that these laws followed naturally if each element is made of atoms of a fixed mass and compounds form by atoms joining in small whole-number ratios. His theory explained existing measurements and predicted new ones; it let chemists work out relative atomic masses and write formulas. It also could have been proven wrong by a single compound with variable composition, and it was not. That combination of explaining evidence and making testable predictions is what turned an old philosophical idea into the foundation of modern chemistry. Democritus deserves credit for the idea and the name; Dalton deserves credit for making it science.

### subtopic_2 — How Small Is an Atom?
**Assistant:** How did scientists measure the size of atoms before they could see them?

**Tutor:** By clever indirect methods that all pointed to the same answer, about a tenth of a nanometre. One of the earliest and most elegant was the oil-drop experiment on water. If you place a tiny, measured drop of oil on clean water, it spreads out into a very thin, roughly circular film, and it stops spreading when the film is just one molecule thick. Benjamin Franklin noticed in the 1770s that a teaspoon of oil calmed a large area of a pond. Later scientists measured the volume of the drop and the area of the film, and since volume equals area times thickness, dividing the volume by the area gave the thickness of a single layer of molecules, which came out at a few billionths of a metre. That set an upper limit on molecular size. Other estimates came from the behaviour of gases: how fast they spread, how they conduct heat and how they deviate from simple laws at high pressure all depend on the size of the particles, and calculations gave similar values. In 1905 Albert Einstein used the random jiggling of tiny pollen grains in water, called Brownian motion, to calculate the size and number of the molecules bumping into them, and experiments by Jean Perrin a few years later confirmed his predictions, convincing the last sceptics that atoms were real. In 1912 X-rays, which have wavelengths similar to the spacing of atoms, were shone through crystals, and the patterns they produced revealed the exact distances between atoms. Independent methods agreeing on the same number is one of the strongest kinds of evidence in science.

### subtopic_3 — Elements Consist of One Kind of Atom
**Assistant:** Why is oxygen gas made of pairs of atoms while helium atoms stay single?

**Tutor:** Because atoms join together when doing so makes them more stable, and whether that happens depends on how their outer electrons are arranged. A single oxygen atom has six electrons in its outer shell, two short of the stable arrangement of eight that the noble gases have. Two oxygen atoms can each reach a more stable arrangement by sharing electrons between them, forming a strong bond, so oxygen atoms almost never exist alone; they pair up as O2 molecules. Nitrogen, hydrogen, fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine behave the same way, and these elements are called diatomic, meaning two atoms. Helium is different. Its outer shell is already full, with two electrons in the first shell, and the other noble gases have full outer shells of eight. They gain nothing by sharing, so they stay as separate single atoms, which is also why they are so unreactive. Metals use a third arrangement: their atoms release outer electrons into a shared sea and pack together in a huge lattice. All of these are still elements, because only one kind of atom is present. This is also why the formula of an element is not always just its symbol. Helium is written He, but oxygen gas is written O2, and if you want to describe the oxygen that you breathe correctly, you should say that it consists of oxygen molecules, each made of two oxygen atoms. Ozone, a form of oxygen high in the atmosphere that absorbs ultraviolet light, has three oxygen atoms per molecule, O3, and has different properties from ordinary oxygen even though it is the same element.

### subtopic_4 — The Alphabet of Matter
**Assistant:** If atoms are never destroyed, where do the atoms of a burnt log go?

**Tutor:** Into the air and into the ash, every one of them. A dry log is made mostly of cellulose and similar substances, which are built from carbon, hydrogen and oxygen atoms, with small amounts of other elements such as calcium, potassium and phosphorus. When the log burns, oxygen atoms from the air join with the carbon and hydrogen. The carbon atoms leave as carbon dioxide gas, each carbon atom now attached to two oxygen atoms, and the hydrogen atoms leave as water vapour, attached to oxygen. Both are invisible gases that rise with the hot air from the fire, which is why the log seems to vanish. Smoke contains some unburnt carbon particles and other substances. The mineral atoms, calcium, potassium and the rest, do not form gases, so they stay behind as the small pile of ash. If you could collect every bit of gas and smoke and weigh it with the ash, the total mass would equal the mass of the log plus the oxygen it combined with; no atom appears from nowhere and none disappears. Those carbon atoms then join the carbon dioxide in the atmosphere, where a tree may take them up again through photosynthesis and build them into new wood, which is the carbon cycle from the Term 1 lessons seen at the scale of atoms. The carbon atoms in your body have passed through countless plants, animals and fires before reaching you. This is the deepest meaning of the alphabet picture: the letters are reused over and over, and only the words change.
