# Part 1 — Expert

This session sorts matter into its basic kinds and introduces the two types of pure substance: elements and compounds. The anchor is a salt shaker. Table salt is made of sodium, a soft silver metal that fizzes and bursts into flame on water, and chlorine, a choking green gas that was used as a poison in the First World War. Joined chemically, they make a white, harmless crystal you sprinkle on chips. The governing idea is that a compound is a new substance, made of two or more elements chemically joined in a fixed ratio, with properties completely different from the elements that make it. The classification of matter into pure substances and mixtures, elements with single atoms and diatomic molecules, compounds with their fixed ratios, chemical formulas and particle diagrams are all examinable, and they are the language of every chemistry topic in Grades 9 to 12.

## Subtopic: Pure Substances and Mixtures

All matter can be sorted into two big groups: pure substances and mixtures. A pure substance contains only one kind of particle, so it has a fixed composition and fixed properties. Every sample of pure water, from a laboratory in Cape Town or a glacier in Antarctica, is made of identical water molecules, boils at one hundred degrees Celsius at sea level, and freezes at zero. A mixture contains two or more different substances that are not chemically joined, mixed in any proportion; sea water, air, soil, tea and concrete are mixtures. Their composition varies from sample to sample, and so do their properties: sea water from different places has different saltiness and boils a little above one hundred degrees, at a temperature that depends on how much salt it contains.

Pure substances divide further into elements and compounds. An element is a pure substance made of only one kind of atom, which cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical means. A compound is a pure substance made of two or more different elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio, which can be broken down into its elements, but only by chemical means. Mixtures, by contrast, can be separated by physical means, the subject of the last session in this topic.

A simple test for purity is melting or boiling point. A pure substance melts and boils at one sharp temperature. A mixture usually melts or boils over a range of temperatures, and impurities typically lower the melting point and raise the boiling point. That is why salt is spread on icy roads in cold countries: it lowers the freezing point of water. It is also how chemists check whether a substance they have made is pure.

In everyday language pure means clean or natural, which is not the scientific meaning. Bottled spring water labelled pure is a mixture of water with dissolved minerals; pure fruit juice is a mixture of hundreds of substances. In science, pure means one substance only.

The classification can be laid out as a tree. Matter splits into pure substances and mixtures. Pure substances split into elements and compounds. Every material you meet can be placed somewhere on that tree.

The questions for this section are with you now: the difference between a pure substance and a mixture, the scientific meaning of pure, the melting-point test, and the classification tree.

## Subtopic: Elements: Atoms and Diatomic Molecules

All the particles in an element contain only one kind of atom, but the particles are not always single atoms.

In the noble gases, helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon, the particles are single atoms moving independently; these elements are described as monatomic. In metals, and in carbon as diamond or graphite, the atoms are bonded into huge regular structures called lattices, with no separate small particles at all.

Several non-metal elements exist as molecules. A molecule is a group of two or more atoms chemically bonded together. Seven elements form molecules of exactly two identical atoms, called diatomic molecules: hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine. Their formulas are written with the symbol and a small number below the line, called a subscript, giving the number of atoms: H2, N2, O2, F2, Cl2, Br2 and I2. When we say the air contains oxygen, we mean it contains O2 molecules, each made of two oxygen atoms bonded together. A useful memory aid is that, apart from hydrogen, these seven form a shape like the number seven on the Periodic Table, starting at nitrogen, going across to fluorine, and down Group 17 through chlorine, bromine and iodine.

Some elements form larger molecules. Ozone, a form of oxygen found high in the atmosphere where it absorbs harmful ultraviolet light, has three atoms per molecule, O3. Yellow sulfur is made of rings of eight sulfur atoms, S8, and white phosphorus of groups of four, P4. Different forms of the same element, such as oxygen and ozone, or diamond and graphite, are called allotropes.

The important point for classification is that a molecule made of identical atoms is still an element. O2 is an element; O3 is an element; a lump of iron is an element. Only when different kinds of atom are chemically joined does a compound form.

In a particle diagram, the standard way to show this is to draw each atom as a circle, with one colour or shading for each element. A box containing single circles all of the same colour shows a monatomic element such as neon. A box containing pairs of joined circles, all of the same colour, shows a diatomic element such as oxygen.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of molecule, the seven diatomic elements and their formulas, allotropes, and how to draw an element in a particle diagram.

## Subtopic: Compounds: Elements Joined in a Fixed Ratio

A compound forms when atoms of two or more different elements join by chemical bonds, and it always contains those elements in the same fixed ratio. Water is always two hydrogen atoms to one oxygen atom, formula H2O. Carbon dioxide is always one carbon to two oxygens, CO2. Ammonia is one nitrogen to three hydrogens, NH3. Methane, the main gas in natural gas and biogas, is one carbon to four hydrogens, CH4. Glucose, made in photosynthesis, is six carbons, twelve hydrogens and six oxygens, C6H12O6. Table salt, sodium chloride, is sodium and chlorine in a one-to-one ratio, NaCl, arranged in a lattice of alternating particles rather than as separate molecules.

Because the ratio is fixed, the composition by mass is fixed too, and that is exactly what Dalton used as evidence for atoms. Changing the ratio does not give a different mixture of the same thing; it gives a different compound. Carbon monoxide, CO, with one oxygen per carbon, is a deadly poisonous gas produced by burning fuel without enough air, while carbon dioxide, CO2, is the gas we breathe out. Water, H2O, is essential for life, while hydrogen peroxide, H2O2, is a bleach and disinfectant.

The properties of a compound are different from the properties of the elements in it. Sodium is a reactive metal and chlorine a poisonous gas, yet sodium chloride is a stable white solid we eat. Hydrogen is a flammable gas and oxygen a gas that supports burning, yet water puts fires out. Iron is a grey magnetic metal and sulfur a yellow powder, yet iron sulfide is a dark solid from which the iron can no longer be pulled out with a magnet, as it can from a simple mixture of iron filings and sulfur. Those differences are strong evidence that a new substance has formed, not just a mixture.

Compounds are named with a few simple rules. A compound of a metal and a non-metal is named with the metal first and the non-metal second, with its ending changed to -ide: sodium chloride, magnesium oxide, iron sulfide, calcium fluoride. Prefixes count atoms in many non-metal compounds: mono means one, di means two, tri means three, as in carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide and sulfur trioxide. Some compounds keep traditional names, such as water, ammonia and methane.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of a compound, five compounds with their formulas, why compounds have different properties from their elements, and the -ide and prefix naming rules.

## Subtopic: Reading Formulas and Particle Diagrams

A chemical formula shows which elements are in a substance and how many atoms of each there are in one molecule or formula unit. Each symbol represents one atom of that element; a subscript after a symbol gives the number of atoms of the element just before it; no subscript means one. So H2O has two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom, three atoms in total. CO2 has one carbon and two oxygens, three atoms. NH3 has four atoms. CH4 has five. C6H12O6 has six plus twelve plus six, twenty-four atoms. CaCO3, calcium carbonate, the main substance in limestone, chalk and seashells, has one calcium, one carbon and three oxygens, five atoms.

A large number written in front of a formula, called a coefficient, gives the number of molecules. 2H2O means two water molecules, which contain four hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms, six atoms in total. 3CO2 means three carbon dioxide molecules: three carbon atoms and six oxygen atoms, nine atoms in total. Mixing up the coefficient and the subscript is a common error: H2O2 is one molecule of hydrogen peroxide, a different compound from 2H2O, two molecules of water.

Particle diagrams show the same information as pictures, and examiners often give a set of boxes and ask you to classify each. A box with identical single atoms is an element, monatomic. A box with identical molecules each made of one kind of atom is an element, molecular. A box with identical molecules each made of two or more kinds of atom is a compound. A box with two or more different kinds of particle, not joined to each other, is a mixture; it might be a mixture of elements, such as nitrogen and oxygen molecules in air, a mixture of compounds, or a mixture of both.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying O2 is a compound because it is a molecule; molecules of one element are elements. Two: calling air a compound; it is a mixture of separate gases. Three: saying a compound has the properties of its elements; it has new properties. Four: reading CO as cobalt; two capitals mean two elements. Five: confusing 2H2O with H2O2.

Layout for marks: for any box or substance, ask first how many kinds of particle there are; one kind means pure, more than one means mixture; then ask how many kinds of atom are in each particle; one means element, more than one means compound.

The questions for this section are with you now: counting atoms in formulas with subscripts and coefficients, classifying particle diagrams, and the five exhibits.

## Subtopic: Pure or Mixed?

Think of a bowl of fruit salad. You can see the pieces of apple, banana and orange, you can pick them out again, and every bowl is a little different. That is a mixture: different things mixed together but not joined, in any amounts.

Now think of pure sugar. Every grain is exactly the same stuff, all the way through. That is a pure substance: only one kind of particle. In science, pure does not mean clean or healthy. Pure means one substance only. So bottled spring water is not pure in science; it is water with minerals dissolved in it. Air is not pure; it is a mixture of gases.

There is a neat test. A pure substance melts or boils at one exact temperature. Pure water boils at one hundred degrees at the coast. Add salt and the boiling point goes up and the freezing point goes down. A mixture usually melts or boils over a range.

Pure substances come in two kinds. An element has only one kind of atom, like gold or oxygen. A compound has two or more kinds of atom joined together, like water or salt. So the whole tree is: matter splits into pure substances and mixtures; pure substances split into elements and compounds.

## Subtopic: Couples That Never Split Up

Some elements like to be alone. Helium, neon and argon atoms float around by themselves, single and happy.

Others always come in pairs. Oxygen atoms are never found alone in the air; they are joined in couples, two oxygen atoms bonded together, and we write that as O2, with a small 2 below the line. A group of atoms joined together is called a molecule. Seven elements are always couples: hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine. On the Periodic Table, apart from hydrogen, they make the shape of a number seven.

Some form bigger groups. Ozone, high in the sky, is three oxygen atoms joined, O3, and it shields us from harmful sunlight. Sulfur makes rings of eight.

Here is the important bit: a couple of the same atoms is still an element. O2 is an element, because it has only oxygen in it. It does not matter that the atoms are joined. Only when different kinds of atom join do you get a compound.

In a drawing, use one colour of circle for each element. Single circles all the same colour: an element like neon. Joined pairs all the same colour: an element like oxygen.

Hold the couples in mind and try this part's questions: the meaning of molecule, the seven elements that come in pairs, and why O2 is still an element.

## Subtopic: New Substance, New Personality

Sodium is a metal so wild it bursts into flames on water. Chlorine is a green gas so poisonous it was used as a weapon. Join them together and you get sodium chloride: table salt, which you sprinkle on your chips. That is the magic of a compound. It is a brand-new substance with a brand-new personality.

Water is the same story. Hydrogen explodes, oxygen makes fires burn fiercely, but join two hydrogens to one oxygen and you get H2O, which puts fires out.

And the recipe is always the same. Water is always two hydrogens and one oxygen. Change the recipe and you get a different compound: H2O2 is hydrogen peroxide, a bleach. Carbon dioxide, CO2, is the gas you breathe out, but carbon monoxide, CO, with one oxygen fewer, is a deadly poison.

Reading formulas is counting. The small number after a symbol tells how many of that atom. H2O: two hydrogens, one oxygen, three atoms. A big number in front tells how many molecules: 2H2O is two water molecules, six atoms altogether.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Matter is either a pure substance or a mixture. Pure substances are elements, with one kind of atom, or compounds, with two or more kinds of atom joined in a fixed ratio. Some elements exist as single atoms, some as molecules like the seven diatomic elements, O2, N2 and the rest, and they are still elements. Compounds have new properties, different from their elements. Formulas show the atoms; subscripts count atoms, coefficients count molecules.
