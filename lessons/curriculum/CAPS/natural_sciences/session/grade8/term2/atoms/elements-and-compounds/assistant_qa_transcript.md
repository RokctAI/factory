### subtopic_1 — Pure Substances and Mixtures
**Assistant:** Why does adding salt make ice melt on roads, if salt does not heat anything?

**Tutor:** Because dissolving salt in water changes the temperature at which the water can freeze, and the effect has nothing to do with heat being added. Pure water freezes at zero degrees Celsius because, at that temperature, water molecules are leaving the solid ice and joining the liquid at the same rate as liquid molecules are attaching to the ice. When salt is present, its particles dissolve in the thin layer of liquid water that is always on the surface of ice, and they get in the way: there are fewer water molecules at the surface of the liquid available to attach to the ice, while molecules keep leaving the ice at the same rate. So more ice melts than forms, and the ice keeps melting even below zero. The salty water now needs to be cooled to a lower temperature, several degrees below zero for typical road salt, before it will freeze. This lowering of the freezing point is a property of mixtures in general, which is why it is also a useful test of purity: a pure substance has one sharp melting point, and an impure sample melts lower and over a range. The same effect is used in reverse in an old-fashioned ice-cream maker, where ice and salt packed around the container can reach about minus fifteen degrees or lower, cold enough to freeze the cream. Antifreeze in car engines works the same way, keeping the coolant liquid in cold weather. Dissolved substances also raise the boiling point, which is why salty sea water boils a little above one hundred degrees.

### subtopic_2 — Elements: Atoms and Diatomic Molecules
**Assistant:** Why does it matter whether we write oxygen as O or O2?

**Tutor:** Because the two symbols describe different things, and chemistry depends on counting correctly. O on its own means one oxygen atom. O2 means one molecule of oxygen gas, which contains two oxygen atoms bonded together. Under normal conditions, oxygen atoms do not exist on their own in the air; they are always paired. So when you describe the gas that we breathe and that fires use, the correct formula is O2. The difference becomes essential when you write chemical equations, which you will start doing soon. Consider making water from hydrogen and oxygen. Both gases are diatomic, H2 and O2. Each water molecule, H2O, needs only one oxygen atom, but oxygen arrives in pairs, so one O2 molecule supplies enough oxygen for two water molecules, and those two water molecules need four hydrogen atoms, which is two H2 molecules. The balanced equation is two H2 plus one O2 gives two H2O. If you wrote oxygen as O, you would get the ratio of gases wrong, and the predicted volumes, masses and amounts would all be wrong too. Experiments confirm the diatomic version: two volumes of hydrogen react with exactly one volume of oxygen. The distinction also matters for allotropes. O2 and O3 are both pure oxygen, but ozone, O3, is a pale blue gas with a sharp smell, toxic to breathe at ground level, and it absorbs ultraviolet light in the upper atmosphere. Same element, different molecules, different properties. Writing the right formula is how chemists say exactly which substance they mean.

### subtopic_3 — Compounds: Elements Joined in a Fixed Ratio
**Assistant:** How do we know iron and sulfur heated together make a compound and not just a mixture?

**Tutor:** By comparing what we have before and after, looking for the signs that a new substance has formed. Start with a mixture: stir yellow sulfur powder and grey iron filings together. You can still see yellow and grey specks under a magnifying glass. A magnet pulls the iron out and leaves the sulfur behind. You could mix them in any proportion, a little iron or a lot. Nothing has changed except that the two are side by side. Now heat the mixture in a test tube. Once one part starts glowing, the glow spreads through the whole mixture by itself, which shows that energy is being released by a chemical reaction. When it cools, you have a dark grey-black solid with no yellow or grey specks. A magnet can no longer separate iron filings from it. Add dilute acid and a different gas is given off from the product than from iron alone; it smells of rotten eggs. Each of these observations is evidence that a new substance, iron sulfide, has formed: a change in appearance, energy released, the parts no longer separable by physical means, and different chemical properties. There is one more piece of evidence. If you use more sulfur than needed, the extra sulfur is left over unreacted, because iron and sulfur combine in a fixed ratio by mass, about seven grams of iron to every four grams of sulfur for the simplest iron sulfide. A mixture accepts any ratio; a compound takes exactly what its formula demands and leaves the rest. That fixed ratio, plus new properties, is the signature of a compound.

### subtopic_4 — Reading Formulas and Particle Diagrams
**Assistant:** In a particle diagram, how can I tell a mixture of two elements from a compound?

**Tutor:** By looking at whether the different kinds of atom are joined to each other inside the same particle, or are in separate particles. Ask two questions in order. First, are all the particles in the box identical? If there are two or more different kinds of particle, it is a mixture, whatever they are made of. If they are all identical, it is a pure substance. Second, for a pure substance, look inside one particle. If every atom in it is the same colour, it is an element; if it contains atoms of different colours bonded together, it is a compound. Now apply that to the tricky case. Suppose a box contains some pairs of black circles and some pairs of white circles, with the black pairs never touching the white pairs. There are two different kinds of particle, so it is a mixture, and since each particle contains only one kind of atom, it is a mixture of two elements, like nitrogen and oxygen in air. Now suppose a box contains only particles that each consist of one black circle joined to one white circle, all identical. There is one kind of particle, so it is pure, and each particle contains two kinds of atom, so it is a compound, like carbon monoxide. The atoms of black and white are the same in both boxes; what differs is whether they are bonded together. A third box might contain some black-white particles and some separate black pairs: two kinds of particle, so a mixture, this time of a compound and an element. Marking schemes reward naming both the category and the reason, for example compound, because every particle is identical and contains two different kinds of atom.
