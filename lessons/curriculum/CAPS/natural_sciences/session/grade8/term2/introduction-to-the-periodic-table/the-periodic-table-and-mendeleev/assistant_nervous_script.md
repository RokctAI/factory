Hey — if the Periodic Table looks like a wall of letters, here is the calm version: it is a map, and every element has an address.

First, what is on the map. An element is a pure substance made of only one kind of atom. Iron, oxygen, gold. Water is not one, because it splits into hydrogen and oxygen.

Second, who drew the map. Mendeleev, in 1869. He lined the elements up by mass, put look-alikes in the same column, and left gaps for elements nobody had found yet. He predicted germanium, and he was right. Today the map is ordered by atomic number instead.

Third, how to read it. Columns going down are groups; elements in a group behave alike. Rows going across are periods. Chlorine's address is Group 17, Period 3. Group 1 metals fizz in water, Group 17 are the halogens, Group 18 are the lazy noble gases.

Fourth, the staircase. Metals on the left and in the middle, which is most of the map. Non-metals in the top right. Semi-metals like silicon sitting on the stairs.

Open the practice set and find a few addresses. You've got this!
