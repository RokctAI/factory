Today we meet the most useful chart in chemistry. An element is a pure substance made of only one kind of atom, and 118 of them are now known. In the 1800s chemists had no way to organise them until patterns began to appear: Döbereiner's families of three and Newlands' repeating octaves. Then in 1869 Dmitri Mendeleev arranged sixty-three elements in order of atomic mass, stacked similar ones in columns, and left gaps for elements nobody had found, predicting their properties. When germanium turned up in 1886 with almost exactly the mass and density he had forecast, the world took notice. We learn how the modern table is ordered by atomic number into eighteen groups and seven periods, meet the alkali metals, halogens and noble gases, and draw the staircase that separates the metals on the left from the non-metals on the right, with the semi-metals in between.
