# Part 1 — Expert

This session asks what it actually means to be a metal, a non-metal or a semi-metal. The anchor is a simple test bench: a cell, a torch bulb, two wires with crocodile clips, and a row of samples, a copper wire, an iron nail, a strip of aluminium foil, a lump of sulfur, a pencil lead, a piece of charcoal and a silicon chip from a broken solar cell. Clip each sample into the gap in the circuit and watch the bulb. Then try to bend each sample, scratch it, and look at its surface in the light. The governing idea is that elements are classified by a set of physical properties: lustre, electrical and thermal conductivity, malleability and ductility, and melting point. Metals share one set, non-metals the opposite set, and semi-metals sit in between. The characteristic properties of each class, the important exceptions, the link between properties and uses, and the design of a fair investigation are all examinable, and they lead to the study of bonding and materials in Grade 10 Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: Properties of Metals

Metals share a distinctive set of physical properties. They have lustre: a freshly cut or polished metal surface is shiny and reflects light, which is why mirrors are backed with silver or aluminium. They are good conductors of electricity, so the bulb on the test bench lights brightly for copper, iron and aluminium. They are good conductors of heat, which is why a metal spoon in hot tea quickly becomes hot at the handle. They are malleable, meaning they can be hammered or rolled into thin sheets without breaking; aluminium foil and a gold leaf thinner than a strand of spider silk are examples, and a single gram of gold can be beaten into a sheet about one square metre in area. They are ductile, meaning they can be drawn out into thin wires, as copper is for electrical cables. Most metals are sonorous, ringing when struck, which is why bells are made of metal. Most have high melting points and high densities, and all except one are solid at room temperature.

The exceptions are worth knowing because examiners like them. Mercury is a liquid at room temperature; it melts at minus thirty-nine degrees Celsius, which is why it was used in thermometers. Gallium melts at about thirty degrees, so a piece will melt in your hand. The Group 1 metals, sodium and potassium, are so soft they can be cut with a knife, and their densities are so low that they float on water; sodium's density is 0.97 grams per cubic centimetre, just less than water's 1.00. At the other extreme, tungsten melts at about 3 400 degrees Celsius, which is why it was used for light-bulb filaments, and gold has a density of 19.3 grams per cubic centimetre, more than nineteen times that of water.

Properties decide uses, and the full-marks answer always links the two. Copper is used for electrical wiring because it is an excellent conductor and ductile. Aluminium is used for overhead power cables and aircraft because it conducts well and has a low density of 2.7 grams per cubic centimetre, so it is light. Iron, made into steel, is used for bridges, buildings and car bodies because it is strong and cheap. Gold is used for jewellery and for the contacts inside electronic devices because it is lustrous, conducts well and does not corrode. Stainless steel pots, which contain chromium, conduct heat to food and do not rust.

South Africa's economy has been built on metals. Gold from the Witwatersrand created Johannesburg after 1886. The Bushveld Complex in Limpopo and North West holds most of the world's known platinum, and about seven tenths of the platinum mined each year comes from South Africa; it is used in catalytic converters, which clean car exhaust gases, and in jewellery. South Africa is also a major producer of chromium, manganese and iron ore.

The questions for this section are with you now: the six properties of metals, two exceptions, and matching three metals to uses through their properties.

## Subtopic: Properties of Non-metals

Non-metals have, broadly, the opposite set of properties. They are usually dull rather than lustrous. They are poor conductors of electricity and heat, which makes them insulators; on the test bench the bulb stays dark for sulfur. Solid non-metals are brittle: a lump of sulfur shatters or crumbles when struck rather than flattening, and it cannot be drawn into a wire. Most non-metals have low melting and boiling points, which is why so many are gases at room temperature. Their densities are generally low.

Of the roughly seventeen non-metals, eleven are gases at room temperature: hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine and chlorine, and the six noble gases helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon. One, bromine, is a dark red-brown liquid that gives off a choking vapour. The rest are solids: carbon, phosphorus, sulfur, selenium and iodine.

Carbon is the great exception and must be learned. Carbon exists in more than one form. Graphite, the grey material in pencil leads and in the charcoal on the test bench, conducts electricity, so the bulb lights; that is why graphite is used for the electrodes in some batteries and in electrolysis. Diamond, another form of carbon, does not conduct electricity, but it is the hardest natural substance known and is used on the tips of drill bits and saws for cutting rock; South Africa's diamond industry began at Kimberley in 1871. Graphite is also unusually slippery, used as a dry lubricant, while diamond is the opposite of slippery. Both are pure carbon; the difference lies in how the atoms are arranged.

Uses again follow properties. Oxygen supports respiration and combustion, so it is used in hospitals and for welding. Nitrogen is unreactive enough to fill packets of chips so the food does not go stale, and it is used to make fertilisers. Chlorine kills micro-organisms, so it is used to purify drinking water and swimming pools. Helium is far less dense than air and does not burn, so it fills weather balloons and party balloons. Neon glows red-orange when electricity passes through it at low pressure, which gave neon signs their name. Sulfur is used to make sulfuric acid, the most widely manufactured industrial chemical, and in matches. Plastics, which are made mainly of carbon and hydrogen, are used to insulate electrical wires precisely because non-metal compounds do not conduct.

The questions for this section are with you now: the properties of non-metals, the states of non-metals at room temperature, why graphite is an exception, and uses linked to properties.

## Subtopic: Properties of Semi-metals

Semi-metals, also called metalloids, are the elements along the staircase on the Periodic Table: boron, silicon, germanium, arsenic, antimony and tellurium. They have some properties of metals and some of non-metals.

Silicon is the best example. It has a shiny, grey, metallic lustre, so by appearance it looks like a metal. But it is brittle; strike it and it shatters like a non-metal. On the test bench the bulb glows only faintly or not at all with a cell, because silicon is a semiconductor: it conducts electricity better than a non-metal but far worse than a metal. And its conductivity behaves in the opposite way to a metal's when heated. A metal conducts slightly less well when it gets hotter, while a semiconductor conducts better as its temperature rises, or when light falls on it. That sensitivity, combined with the ability to change its conductivity by adding tiny amounts of other elements, is what makes silicon the basis of computer chips, transistors, and the solar panels now common on South African rooftops.

Other semi-metals have their own uses. Germanium is used in fibre-optic cables and infrared lenses. Boron is added to glass to make heat-resistant laboratory glassware and oven dishes that do not crack when heated. Antimony is added to lead to harden it and is used in flame retardants. Arsenic is poisonous and was once used in rat poisons and pesticides; today tiny amounts are used in electronics.

Semi-metals show that the division of elements into metals and non-metals is a model that works very well for most elements but has a fuzzy border. Different textbooks even disagree slightly about which elements count as semi-metals, which is a useful reminder that classifications are human tools fitted to nature, not the other way round.

The questions for this section are with you now: the six semi-metals, the properties that make silicon in-between, why semiconductors matter, and one use each of boron and germanium.

## Subtopic: Investigating and Comparing the Three Classes

The test bench is a scientific investigation, and it must be designed to be fair. The question might be: which of these elements conduct electricity? The independent variable, the thing you change, is the material in the gap. The dependent variable, the thing you observe, is whether the bulb lights and how brightly. The controlled variables, which must stay the same, are the cell, the bulb, the wires and, as far as possible, the length and thickness of each sample, because a long thin sample conducts less than a short thick one of the same material. A sample should be sanded clean first, because a coating of rust or oxide on a metal can block the current and give a false result. For safety, small cells are used, mercury, arsenic and sodium are never handled, and sulfur is not heated.

Similar tests examine other properties. Hammer each sample gently on a hard surface to test malleability: metals flatten, non-metals and semi-metals break. Look at a freshly scratched or sanded surface in good light for lustre. Hold one end of a rod near a heat source with wax beads stuck along it to compare thermal conductivity; the wax melts first along the best conductor.

The comparison table sums up. Metals: lustrous, good conductors of heat and electricity, malleable and ductile, mostly high melting point and density, solid except mercury. Non-metals: dull, poor conductors except graphite, brittle when solid, low melting points, many are gases. Semi-metals: may be lustrous, brittle, semiconductors whose conductivity rises with temperature.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying all metals are hard and heavy; sodium is soft and floats. Two: saying all metals are solid; mercury is liquid. Three: saying no non-metal conducts electricity; graphite does. Four: confusing malleable, which means hammered into sheets, with ductile, which means drawn into wires. Five: giving a use without the property that explains it.

Layout for marks: property, then use, joined by because. "Copper is used for wiring because it is a good conductor of electricity and is ductile."

The questions for this section are with you now: the variables in the conductivity test, why samples are sanded, the comparison table, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through three personalities: the metal, the non-metal and the in-betweener — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Metal Personality

Think of metals as one kind of personality. They are shiny: polish them and they gleam. They are generous with electricity and heat: they let both flow straight through them, which is why a metal spoon in hot tea gets hot at the handle. They are bendy without breaking: hammer them and they flatten into sheets, like foil; pull them and they stretch into wires, like copper cable. The word for flattening is malleable; the word for stretching into wire is ductile. Most are strong, heavy, and hard to melt, and they ring like a bell when you hit them.

Every personality has a few surprises. Mercury is a metal that is liquid at room temperature. Sodium is so soft you can cut it with a butter knife, and it floats on water. Gallium melts in your hand.

Metals get their jobs because of their personality. Copper makes electrical wires because it conducts and stretches. Aluminium makes power cables and aeroplanes because it conducts and is very light. Steel makes bridges because it is strong. Gold makes jewellery because it stays shiny for ever. And South Africa has built its wealth on metals: gold under Johannesburg, and about seven tenths of the world's new platinum, which goes into the parts of cars that clean their exhaust.

Keep the metal personality in mind and try the questions for this part: what malleable and ductile mean, two surprising metals, and why copper is used for wires.

## Subtopic: The Non-metal Personality

Non-metals are the opposite personality. They are dull, not shiny. They block electricity and heat, so they are insulators; that is why the plastic around a wire keeps your fingers safe. Solid ones are brittle: hit a lump of sulfur and it shatters instead of flattening. And they melt and boil easily, so lots of them are gases. The air you breathe is mostly two non-metals, nitrogen and oxygen.

Eleven non-metals are gases, one, bromine, is a smelly red-brown liquid, and the rest are solids like carbon, sulfur and iodine.

Now the big surprise: carbon. Pencil lead is graphite, a form of carbon, and it conducts electricity. Clip a pencil lead into a circuit and the bulb lights. And diamond is carbon too, the hardest natural thing on Earth, used to cut rock, and South Africa's diamond story started at Kimberley in 1871. Same element, completely different personalities, just because the atoms are arranged differently.

Non-metals have jobs too. Oxygen in hospitals. Chlorine to kill germs in drinking water and pools. Helium to float balloons. Nitrogen in chip packets to keep them fresh. Neon in glowing signs.

Hold the non-metal personality in mind and try this part's questions: why non-metals are good insulators, the non-metal that is a liquid, and the form of carbon that conducts.

## Subtopic: The In-Betweeners

On the staircase of the Periodic Table live the in-betweeners: the semi-metals. Silicon is the famous one. It looks like a metal, shiny and grey. But hit it and it shatters like a non-metal. And with electricity it is half-hearted: it conducts a little. That is why it is called a semiconductor.

Here is the clever bit. Warm silicon up, or shine light on it, and it conducts better. Add a tiny pinch of another element and you can control exactly how well it conducts. That is how computer chips and solar panels work. The panels on roofs around South Africa are silicon turning sunlight into electricity.

To find out which personality an element has, test it fairly. Put each sample into the same simple circuit with the same cell and bulb, sand it clean first, and keep the samples the same size. Bright bulb: metal, or graphite. No light: non-metal. Faint light: maybe a semi-metal. Then tap each one with a hammer: flattens, metal; shatters, non-metal or semi-metal.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Metals are shiny, conduct heat and electricity, are malleable and ductile, and are mostly strong with high melting points; mercury, sodium and gallium are the surprises. Non-metals are dull, insulators, brittle when solid, and often gases; graphite is the surprise because it conducts. Semi-metals like silicon look metallic but are brittle and only partly conduct, which makes them perfect for chips and solar panels. A good answer always says the property and the use together.

Hold the three personalities, and take the final questions of the lesson: which element lights the bulb, which shatters, and why silicon is used in solar panels.
