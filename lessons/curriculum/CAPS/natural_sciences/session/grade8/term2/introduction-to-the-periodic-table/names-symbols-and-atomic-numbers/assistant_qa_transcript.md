### subtopic_1 — Where the Names Come From
**Assistant:** How could scientists discover helium in the Sun before anyone had found it on Earth?

**Tutor:** Because every element gives out its own pattern of colours when it is heated, and that pattern can be read in light from far away. If you heat a substance strongly, for example in a flame, the atoms give out light. Passing that light through a prism splits it, and instead of a full rainbow you see a set of sharp, bright lines of particular colours. Each element produces its own unique set of lines, as recognisable as a fingerprint or a barcode: sodium gives a strong pair of yellow lines, the yellow of old streetlights, and hydrogen gives a red, a blue-green and several violet lines. This technique, called spectroscopy, was developed in the 1850s and 1860s. During a total solar eclipse in 1868, astronomers including the Frenchman Pierre Janssen and the Englishman Norman Lockyer studied light from the glowing gas at the edge of the Sun and found a bright yellow line that did not match sodium or any known element. Lockyer concluded it came from a new element and named it helium after the Greek word for the Sun. Many chemists were sceptical, because naming an element nobody could hold seemed reckless. Then in 1895 the Scottish chemist William Ramsay, investigating gas released from a uranium mineral, found the same yellow line in a laboratory on Earth. The same method is still used: astronomers identify the elements in stars, galaxies and the atmospheres of distant planets entirely from the lines in their light. It is one of the most powerful ideas in science, that we can know what a star is made of without going there.

### subtopic_2 — Chemical Symbols
**Assistant:** Why do chemists bother with symbols at all instead of just writing the names?

**Tutor:** Because symbols are short, international and can be combined into formulas that carry exact information. Consider language first. Iron is iron in English, yster in Afrikaans, insimbi in isiZulu, Eisen in German and fer in French, but in every one of those countries a chemist writes Fe. A paper published in Japan or Brazil can be read by a chemist in Pretoria without translation as far as the chemistry is concerned. Symbols also save space and time. A chemical equation written with names quickly becomes a paragraph; written with symbols, it fits on one line. Most importantly, symbols can be combined into formulas that show exactly which atoms are present and in what numbers. H2O, with a small 2 written below the line after the H, says that a water molecule contains two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. CO2 says carbon dioxide has one carbon and two oxygens. NaCl says table salt has sodium and chlorine in a one-to-one ratio. You cannot do that compactly with words. That is exactly why the capital-letter rule matters so much: in a formula, each capital letter marks the start of a new element. CO is read as C, then O: carbon and oxygen. Co is read as one element, cobalt. A single wrong capital changes the meaning entirely, and chemists, pharmacists and doctors rely on the difference. Before Berzelius introduced letters in the early 1800s, chemists used symbols inherited from alchemy, little circles, crosses and planet signs, which were hard to print and differed from one author to the next. The letter system replaced them within a few decades and has been used ever since.

### subtopic_3 — Atomic Number
**Assistant:** If the atomic number counts protons, what does the relative atomic mass count?

**Tutor:** It compares the average mass of an atom of the element with the mass of other atoms, and it depends on the protons and also on the neutrons, the other particles in the nucleus. Protons and neutrons have almost the same mass, while electrons are about two thousand times lighter, so nearly all the mass of an atom is in its nucleus. A carbon atom has six protons and usually six neutrons, twelve heavy particles altogether, and carbon is given a relative atomic mass of 12. A sodium atom has eleven protons and twelve neutrons, so its relative atomic mass is 23. The two numbers in a box therefore differ by roughly the number of neutrons. Why is chlorine's relative atomic mass 35.5 and not a whole number? Because not every atom of an element has the same number of neutrons. All chlorine atoms have seventeen protons, which is what makes them chlorine, but about three quarters have eighteen neutrons, giving a mass of 35, and about one quarter have twenty neutrons, giving 37. The average over a natural sample works out at about 35.5. Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons are called isotopes, and you will meet them properly in Grade 10. This also explains the argon and potassium puzzle. Argon has eighteen protons, but most argon atoms carry twenty-two neutrons; potassium has nineteen protons but usually only twenty neutrons. So argon ends up heavier even though it has fewer protons. The atomic number is fixed for every atom of an element; the mass can vary, which is why identity and order follow the protons.

### subtopic_4 — Finding an Element's Position
**Assistant:** Why does Period 1 have only two elements when the others have eight or more?

**Tutor:** Because each period corresponds to filling one shell of electrons, and the first shell can hold only two. In a neutral atom the number of electrons equals the number of protons, so as the atomic number goes up by one, the atom gains one electron. Electrons arrange themselves in shells around the nucleus. The first shell, closest to the nucleus, holds at most two electrons. Hydrogen has one electron in it, helium two, and with helium the shell is full, which is why helium is so unreactive and sits at the end of Period 1 in Group 18. Lithium, with three electrons, has to start a new shell, so it begins Period 2. That second shell holds eight electrons, so Period 2 runs for eight elements, from lithium with one outer electron to neon with a full outer shell of eight. Sodium starts the third shell and Period 3, which again holds eight elements, from sodium to argon, before potassium and calcium start the fourth. From Period 4 onward, extra electrons fill an inner set of positions as well, and that is what creates the ten-column block of transition metals in Groups 3 to 12, making Period 4 eighteen elements long. So the shape of the table, the short first row, the gap in the middle of Periods 2 and 3, and the wide central block lower down, is a picture of how electrons are arranged in atoms. Mendeleev built the shape from chemical behaviour without knowing why; electron shells explained it decades later. For now, the counting method for the first twenty elements gives you every position you need.
