# Part 1 — Expert

This session reads the Periodic Table box by box. Every element has four pieces of identity: a name, a chemical symbol, an atomic number and a position. The anchor is a single box, the one for sodium: the number 11 at the top, the symbol Na in the middle, the name sodium, and the relative atomic mass 23 at the bottom, sitting in Group 1 and Period 3. The puzzle in that box, why sodium is written Na when its name begins with S, opens the whole session. The governing idea is that the name and symbol are labels people chose, but the atomic number is a fact of nature: it is the number of protons in every atom of that element, and it alone decides which element an atom is. Reading and writing symbols correctly, knowing the names and symbols of the first twenty elements and a few common metals, using the atomic number, and finding an element's group and period are all examinable, and they are used in every chemistry lesson from here to Grade 12.

## Subtopic: Where the Names Come From

Element names come from many sources, and knowing a few of them makes the table less of a list to memorise.

Some names describe a property. Hydrogen comes from Greek words meaning water-former, because it burns to make water. Oxygen means acid-former, from a mistaken early belief that all acids contain oxygen. Chlorine comes from the Greek word for pale green, the colour of the gas. Bromine comes from a Greek word for stench, because of its choking smell. Iodine comes from the Greek for violet, the colour of its vapour.

Some are named after places. Germanium is named after Germany, where it was discovered; gallium, after Gallia, the Latin name for France; polonium, after Poland, the homeland of Marie Curie, who discovered it; americium, after the Americas; and francium after France. Some are named after scientists: curium after Marie and Pierre Curie, einsteinium after Albert Einstein, and element 101, mendelevium, after Dmitri Mendeleev himself.

Some are named after heavenly bodies and mythology. Helium is from helios, the Greek word for the Sun, because in 1868 it was detected in the light of the Sun during an eclipse, twenty-seven years before it was found on Earth. Uranium, neptunium and plutonium follow the order of the planets and the former planet Pluto. Titanium is named after the Titans of Greek myth, and mercury after the Roman messenger god, because the liquid metal runs so quickly.

And some names are simply very old. Gold, silver, copper, iron, tin, lead and mercury were known in ancient times, long before anyone understood what an element was, and many of them carry names from Latin or older languages. That is the source of the puzzle in the sodium box, which the next section solves.

New elements made in laboratories are named by an international body, the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry, which also confirms each discovery. The most recent names were approved in 2016 for elements 113, 115, 117 and 118: nihonium, moscovium, tennessine and oganesson.

The questions for this section are with you now: three sources of element names with an example of each, and why helium was named after the Sun.

## Subtopic: Chemical Symbols

A chemical symbol is a one- or two-letter abbreviation for an element, used by chemists in every country regardless of language. The system was introduced by the Swedish chemist Jöns Jacob Berzelius in the early 1800s to replace the confusing pictures and signs used by alchemists.

The rules are strict. A symbol is either one capital letter, such as H for hydrogen, C for carbon, O for oxygen, N for nitrogen and S for sulfur, or a capital letter followed by one lowercase letter, such as He for helium, Ca for calcium, Cl for chlorine and Mg for magnesium. The second letter is never a capital. This matters because capitals carry meaning: Co is the element cobalt, while CO is a compound of carbon and oxygen, the poisonous gas carbon monoxide. Writing a symbol with the wrong capitals can turn an element into a compound.

Most symbols come from the first letter or two of the English name, but a second letter is often needed because many names begin with the same letter. Carbon is C, so calcium is Ca, chlorine Cl, chromium Cr and copper Cu. Sometimes the second letter is not the second letter of the name: magnesium is Mg and manganese Mn.

Several symbols come from the Latin names of elements known since ancient times, and these are the ones that cause confusion. Sodium is Na, from natrium. Potassium is K, from kalium. Iron is Fe, from ferrum. Copper is Cu, from cuprum. Silver is Ag, from argentum. Gold is Au, from aurum. Lead is Pb, from plumbum, which is also where the word plumber comes from, because water pipes were once made of lead. Mercury is Hg, from hydrargyrum, meaning liquid silver. Tin is Sn, from stannum. These must simply be learned.

The first twenty elements, in order, with their symbols, are the minimum list to know by heart: hydrogen H, helium He, lithium Li, beryllium Be, boron B, carbon C, nitrogen N, oxygen O, fluorine F, neon Ne, sodium Na, magnesium Mg, aluminium Al, silicon Si, phosphorus P, sulfur S, chlorine Cl, argon Ar, potassium K and calcium Ca. Add iron, copper, zinc Zn, silver, gold, lead and mercury for the common metals.

The questions for this section are with you now: the capital-letter rule and why Co differs from CO, the Latin-based symbols, and the first twenty elements.

## Subtopic: Atomic Number

Every atom has a tiny central nucleus containing particles called protons, which carry a positive charge. The atomic number of an element is the number of protons in the nucleus of each of its atoms. Every hydrogen atom has one proton, so hydrogen's atomic number is 1. Every carbon atom has six protons, so carbon's atomic number is 6. Every sodium atom has eleven protons, every gold atom seventy-nine.

The atomic number is what makes an element that element. If you could add one proton to the nucleus of a carbon atom, it would no longer be carbon; with seven protons it would be nitrogen. This is why no two elements share an atomic number, and why the atomic number is a better way to order the table than atomic mass. Each element's atomic number is one more than the element before it in the table, with no gaps and no repeats, from hydrogen at 1 to oganesson at 118.

A typical box in the Periodic Table shows the atomic number, usually at the top, the symbol in large letters, often the name, and a second number, the relative atomic mass, which compares the mass of an atom of the element with the mass of other atoms. The two numbers are easily confused, and the rule to tell them apart is that the atomic number is always a whole number and always the smaller of the two for every element except hydrogen, where both are 1. In the sodium box, 11 is the atomic number and 23 the relative atomic mass. For carbon they are 6 and 12; for oxygen, 8 and 16; for chlorine, 17 and 35.5; for iron, 26 and 56; for gold, 79 and 197.

The atomic number also explains Mendeleev's swaps. Argon, atomic number 18, has a relative atomic mass of 40, while potassium, atomic number 19, has a mass of 39. Ordered by mass, potassium would come before argon and would land among the noble gases, which makes no sense for a metal that explodes in water. Ordered by atomic number, argon comes first and both fall into their correct families.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of atomic number, why it decides the identity of an element, and how to tell the atomic number from the relative atomic mass in a box.

## Subtopic: Finding an Element's Position

An element's position is given by its group and its period, and it can be found from the atomic number by counting through the table.

Period 1 holds only two elements, hydrogen with atomic number 1 in Group 1, and helium with atomic number 2 in Group 18. Period 2 holds eight elements, atomic numbers 3 to 10: lithium and beryllium in Groups 1 and 2, then a jump across the central block to boron in Group 13, carbon 14, nitrogen 15, oxygen 16, fluorine 17 and neon 18. Period 3 repeats that pattern for atomic numbers 11 to 18: sodium in Group 1, magnesium 2, aluminium 13, silicon 14, phosphorus 15, sulfur 16, chlorine 17 and argon 18. Period 4 begins with potassium at 19 in Group 1 and calcium at 20 in Group 2, and only then do the transition metals of Groups 3 to 12 appear, starting with scandium at 21.

So for the first twenty elements there is a quick method. Find the period by counting: atomic numbers 1 and 2 are Period 1, 3 to 10 are Period 2, 11 to 18 are Period 3, and 19 and 20 are Period 4. Then find the group: the first two elements in each period are in Groups 1 and 2, and the last six in Periods 2 and 3 are in Groups 13 to 18. Example: atomic number 16. It falls in the range 11 to 18, so Period 3; it is the sixth element of that period, so Group 16; the element is sulfur. Example: atomic number 12. Period 3, second element, so Group 2: magnesium.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: writing a symbol with two capitals, such as CL for chlorine; the second letter is always lowercase. Two: writing S for sodium or P for potassium; they are Na and K. Three: reading the relative atomic mass as the atomic number. Four: saying an element's identity depends on its mass; it depends on its number of protons. Five: forgetting that Groups 3 to 12 begin only in Period 4, so magnesium is followed by aluminium in Group 13, not Group 3.

Layout for marks: for any element, write name, symbol, atomic number, group and period in that order.

The questions for this section are with you now: placing atomic numbers 1 to 20 in their periods and groups, identifying an element from its atomic number, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through name tags, short codes and a number that never lies — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Every Element Has a Name Tag

Every element has a name, and the names have stories. Some describe what the element is like: chlorine means pale green, the colour of the gas, and bromine means stink, because it smells awful. Hydrogen means water-maker, because when it burns it makes water.

Some are named after places: germanium after Germany, polonium after Poland. Some are named after famous scientists: curium after Marie and Pierre Curie, einsteinium after Einstein, and mendelevium after Mendeleev, the man who built the table.

And one is named after the Sun. In 1868, during an eclipse, scientists studying sunlight found the fingerprint of an element nobody had ever seen on Earth. They called it helium, from helios, the Greek word for Sun. Twenty-seven years later it was finally found here on Earth too. It is the gas in party balloons.

Some metals are so old that their names come from ancient languages: gold, silver, copper, iron, tin and lead were used thousands of years before anyone knew what an element was.

Brand-new elements are still being named. The last four, in 2016, were elements 113, 115, 117 and 118.

Keep the name tags in mind and try the questions for this part: why helium is named after the Sun, and two different ways elements got their names.

## Subtopic: Short Codes

Writing out names all the time is slow, so every element has a short code called a symbol. It is the same in every language, so a scientist in Johannesburg and one in Tokyo write the same thing.

The rule: one capital letter, or one capital and one small letter. Never two capitals. H is hydrogen, O is oxygen, C is carbon. He is helium, Ca is calcium, Cl is chlorine. The capital matters a lot: Co with a small o is cobalt, a metal, but CO with two capitals is carbon monoxide, a deadly gas. One letter's size changes everything.

Most codes are easy, the first letter or two of the name. But a few seem crazy until you know they come from old Latin names. Sodium is Na, from natrium. Potassium is K, from kalium. Iron is Fe, from ferrum. Copper is Cu, gold is Au, silver is Ag, and lead is Pb, from plumbum, which is why a plumber is called a plumber: old water pipes were made of lead.

Learn the first twenty like a song: H, He, Li, Be, B, C, N, O, F, Ne, then Na, Mg, Al, Si, P, S, Cl, Ar, then K and Ca. Hydrogen, helium, lithium, beryllium, boron, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine, neon, sodium, magnesium, aluminium, silicon, phosphorus, sulfur, chlorine, argon, potassium, calcium.

Hold the short codes in mind and try this part's questions: the capital-letter rule, Co against CO, and the Latin codes.

## Subtopic: The Number That Never Lies

Names and codes were made up by people. But the atomic number is a fact of nature. It is the number of protons in the middle of each atom, and it is the element's true identity card. Every carbon atom has six protons. Give it one more and it is not carbon any more; it is nitrogen. No two elements share a number.

In each box of the table you see two numbers. The atomic number is the small whole number, usually at the top. The bigger one is the relative atomic mass, how heavy the atom is. Sodium's box says 11 and 23: atomic number 11, mass 23.

And the atomic number tells you where the element lives. Numbers 1 and 2 are in the first row. Numbers 3 to 10 fill the second row, and 11 to 18 fill the third, each row starting in Group 1 and 2, then jumping across to Groups 13 to 18. Numbers 19 and 20 start the fourth row. So atomic number 16 is in row three, sixth along: Group 16, Period 3, sulfur.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Every element has a name, a symbol, an atomic number and a position. Names come from properties, places, people and the heavens. Symbols are one capital letter or a capital plus a small letter, and some come from Latin, like Na, K, Fe, Cu, Ag, Au and Pb. The atomic number is the number of protons and decides which element an atom is. From the atomic number you can find the period and group.

Hold the name tags, the short codes and the number that never lies, and take the final questions of the lesson: which element has atomic number 12, the symbol for potassium, and the address of chlorine.
