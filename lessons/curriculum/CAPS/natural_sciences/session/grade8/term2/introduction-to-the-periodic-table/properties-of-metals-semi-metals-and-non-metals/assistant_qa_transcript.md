### subtopic_1 — Properties of Metals
**Assistant:** Why can metals be hammered flat without breaking, when a piece of glass or sulfur would shatter?

**Tutor:** Because of the way the atoms in a metal are held together, which is different from the way atoms are held in brittle solids. In a metal, the atoms are packed closely in regular layers, and each atom gives up one or more of its outer electrons into a shared pool that moves freely through the whole structure. You can picture a lattice of positive metal particles sitting in a sea of mobile electrons, with the attraction between the positive particles and the negative electron sea holding everything together. When you hammer the metal, the layers of atoms slide over one another. Because the electron sea flows along with them, the attraction is maintained wherever the layers end up, so the metal changes shape without the structure coming apart. That is malleability, and pulling layers along into a wire is ductility. In a brittle solid such as sulfur or glass, the particles are held in fixed positions by bonds that point in particular directions, or by attractions that depend on particles lining up in a particular way. A sharp blow forces particles out of position, the bonds break rather than re-form, and the solid cracks along a line. The same electron sea also explains the other metal properties. Free electrons can drift through the metal when a cell pushes them, which is an electric current, so metals conduct electricity. They also carry energy quickly from hot regions to cold, which is why metals conduct heat so well, and they reflect light, giving lustre. One model explains the whole metal personality, and you will study it properly as metallic bonding in Grade 10.

### subtopic_2 — Properties of Non-metals
**Assistant:** How can graphite and diamond both be pure carbon but behave so differently?

**Tutor:** Because properties depend not only on which atoms are present but on how they are arranged and bonded. In diamond, every carbon atom is bonded strongly to four others, arranged in a three-dimensional framework that extends in every direction through the whole crystal. There is no direction in which the structure is weak, which is why diamond is the hardest natural substance, and all of each atom's outer electrons are locked into those four bonds, so there are no free electrons to carry a current, which is why diamond does not conduct. In graphite, each carbon atom is bonded strongly to only three others, forming flat sheets of hexagons, like chicken wire. The fourth outer electron of each atom is not used in those bonds and can move along the sheet, so graphite conducts electricity along its layers, almost like a metal. The sheets themselves are stacked on top of one another with only weak forces between them, so they slide off easily. That is why graphite is soft and slippery, used as a lubricant, and why a pencil works: pressing the lead on paper peels off thin layers of graphite that stick to the paper. Different forms of the same element are called allotropes. Carbon has others, such as the hollow ball-shaped molecules called fullerenes and graphene, a single sheet of graphite one atom thick, which is extraordinarily strong and conducts superbly. Diamond and graphite show a principle you will meet again and again in chemistry: structure determines properties. Same atoms, different arrangement, entirely different material.

### subtopic_3 — Properties of Semi-metals
**Assistant:** Why does a semiconductor conduct better when it gets hotter, when a metal conducts worse?

**Tutor:** Because the two materials are limited by different things. In a metal there is already a huge number of free electrons, so the number of charge carriers is not the problem. What limits the current is how easily those electrons can travel. As a metal heats up, its atoms vibrate more strongly, and the electrons collide with them more often, which slows their drift; the resistance rises, so the metal conducts slightly less well. In a semiconductor such as pure silicon, almost all the outer electrons are held in bonds between neighbouring atoms, so at room temperature there are very few free electrons to carry a current. The limit is the number of carriers. When silicon is heated, some electrons gain enough energy to break free of their bonds and move through the crystal, and each escaping electron also leaves behind a gap, called a hole, which can move as well. More heat means many more free carriers, and that increase far outweighs the extra collisions from vibrating atoms, so the conductivity rises. Light can supply the energy instead of heat, which is the basis of solar cells and light sensors. Engineers also add tiny amounts of other elements, about one atom in a million, to provide extra carriers in a controlled way. Some devices use the temperature effect directly: a thermistor, a component whose resistance falls sharply as it warms, is used in electronic thermometers, car engine sensors and the thermostats of fridges and geysers. So the opposite temperature behaviour is a useful test for telling a semiconductor from a metal, and it is also a feature engineers exploit.

### subtopic_4 — Investigating and Comparing the Three Classes
**Assistant:** In the conductivity test, why must the samples be the same size?

**Tutor:** Because the size and shape of a sample affect how much current flows, independently of what it is made of, and a fair test must change only one thing at a time. Imagine testing a long, very thin iron wire against a short, thick copper rod. The copper might light the bulb more brightly, but you would not know whether that was because copper is a better conductor or because the copper sample was shorter and thicker. Electric current flows more easily through a short path than a long one, and more easily through a wide path than a narrow one, the same way water flows more easily through a short, wide pipe than a long, thin one. If the samples differ in size, the size becomes a second variable mixed up with the material, and the conclusion is unreliable. That is why the controlled variables include the length and thickness of each sample, along with the same cell, bulb and wires. In practice you cannot always get pieces of sulfur, carbon and copper that are exactly identical, so you make them as similar as possible and note the differences when you evaluate the investigation. It also helps to repeat each test and to note the brightness carefully, perhaps on a scale from none to bright, or with a meter that measures current. For a simple classification, the difference between a metal and a non-metal is so large that small size differences do not change the answer; for comparing two metals, though, size control is essential. Noticing which variables could spoil a result, and controlling them, is the habit that separates an experiment from a demonstration.
