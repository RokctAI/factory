# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the Periodic Table of the Elements as a classification system: a way of arranging every known element so that similar elements sit together and the arrangement itself carries information. The anchor is a prediction. In 1869 the Russian chemist Dmitri Mendeleev left a gap in his table below silicon and claimed that an undiscovered element belonged there, with an atomic mass of about 72 and a density of about 5.5 grams per cubic centimetre. Seventeen years later germanium was discovered, with an atomic mass of 72.6 and a density of 5.35. The governing idea is that a good classification does more than tidy things up; it reveals patterns strong enough to predict what has not yet been found. What an element is, how the table developed, how it is organised into groups and periods, and where the metals, semi-metals and non-metals sit are all examinable, and the table is the main reference tool of Grade 10 to 12 Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: Why Scientists Classify the Elements

An element is a pure substance made of only one kind of atom, which cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical means. Iron, oxygen, carbon, gold and chlorine are elements. Water is not, because it can be broken down into hydrogen and oxygen. Today 118 elements are known; about ninety of them occur naturally on Earth, and the rest have been made in laboratories, most of them existing only for fractions of a second.

Classification means sorting things into groups according to shared properties, so that knowing the group tells you something about each member. Biologists classify organisms; librarians classify books; a supermarket classifies its stock so that you find all the dairy products in one aisle. Chemists faced the same problem in the early 1800s. New elements were being discovered every few years, each with its own appearance, density, melting point and way of reacting, and there was no way to organise the growing list or to see whether the properties followed any pattern.

Several chemists noticed partial patterns. In 1829 the German chemist Johann Döbereiner found groups of three elements, which he called triads, with similar chemical behaviour, in which the middle element's atomic mass was roughly the average of the other two. Lithium, sodium and potassium form one triad: all are soft, shiny metals that react violently with water. Chlorine, bromine and iodine form another: all are coloured, poisonous non-metals that react strongly with metals. In 1865 the English chemist John Newlands arranged the elements known to him in order of increasing atomic mass and noticed that similar properties seemed to repeat every eighth element, like the notes of a musical scale; he called it the law of octaves. The pattern worked for the lighter elements but broke down for heavier ones, and other chemists laughed at the musical comparison.

These attempts shared an important insight: when elements are listed in order of the mass of their atoms, properties recur at regular intervals. A property that repeats regularly is called periodic, which is where the Periodic Table gets its name. What was missing was an arrangement flexible enough to cope with elements that did not fit and with elements that had not yet been discovered.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of an element, why chemists needed to classify elements, and what Döbereiner's triads and Newlands' octaves showed.

## Subtopic: Mendeleev's Table and Its Gaps

Dmitri Mendeleev, a chemistry professor in St Petersburg, was writing a textbook in 1869 and wanted a logical order in which to present the elements. He wrote the name, atomic mass and properties of each of the sixty-three elements then known on a separate card and arranged and rearranged the cards, looking for a pattern. He placed the elements in order of increasing atomic mass, and he placed elements with similar properties in the same column.

Mendeleev's genius lay in two decisions that the earlier attempts had not made. First, when the next element in order of mass did not fit the properties of the column it would fall into, he left a gap, and he stated that the gap belonged to an element not yet discovered. Second, he used the properties of the neighbouring elements, above and below and to either side, to predict the properties of each missing element. He named three of them by adding the Sanskrit word eka, meaning one, to the element above the gap: eka-aluminium, eka-boron and eka-silicon.

The predictions were tested within his lifetime. Gallium was discovered in France in 1875 and matched eka-aluminium; Mendeleev had predicted a density of about 5.9 grams per cubic centimetre, and the measured value is 5.9. Scandium, discovered in 1879, matched eka-boron. Germanium, discovered in Germany in 1886, matched eka-silicon: predicted atomic mass about 72, measured 72.6; predicted density about 5.5, measured 5.35; predicted to be a grey solid forming an oxide with two oxygen atoms per atom, as it does. These successes convinced the scientific world that the table described something real about the elements and was not just a convenient list.

Mendeleev also had the confidence to break his own rule when the properties demanded it. Tellurium has a slightly greater atomic mass than iodine, so in order of mass it should come after iodine. But iodine clearly belongs with chlorine and bromine, and tellurium with selenium and sulfur, so Mendeleev swapped them, arguing that the measured masses might be wrong. The masses were in fact correct; the explanation came in 1913, when Henry Moseley showed that each element has a unique atomic number, the number of protons in the nucleus of its atoms, and that ordering by atomic number puts every element, including tellurium and iodine, in its proper place. The modern Periodic Table is therefore arranged in order of increasing atomic number, not atomic mass, and the swaps Mendeleev had to make disappear.

The noble gases, helium, neon, argon and the others, were unknown in 1869 because they react with almost nothing. When they were discovered in the 1890s they did not upset the table; they simply formed a new column at the right-hand side, another sign that the arrangement was sound.

The questions for this section are with you now: how Mendeleev arranged his table, why he left gaps, the germanium prediction, and why the modern table uses atomic number.

## Subtopic: Groups, Periods and the Modern Table

The modern Periodic Table has eighteen vertical columns, called groups, numbered 1 to 18 from left to right, and seven horizontal rows, called periods, numbered 1 to 7 from top to bottom. Every element has one box, and the boxes are arranged in order of increasing atomic number, reading left to right along each period and then continuing at the start of the next.

Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties. Group 1, the alkali metals, lithium, sodium, potassium and below, are soft metals that react vigorously with water to produce hydrogen gas and an alkaline solution; the reaction becomes more violent going down the group. Group 2 metals, the alkaline earth metals, including magnesium and calcium, are less reactive. Group 17, the halogens, fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine, are reactive non-metals that form salts with metals; table salt, sodium chloride, is a compound of a Group 1 metal and a Group 17 non-metal. Group 18, the noble gases, helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon, are unreactive gases. The broad central block, groups 3 to 12, holds the transition metals, which include iron, copper, zinc, silver, gold and platinum, the familiar metals of industry and jewellery.

Along a period the properties change gradually, from reactive metals on the left, through less metallic elements, to non-metals and finally a noble gas on the right; then the pattern starts again in the next period. Period 3 shows it clearly: sodium, magnesium and aluminium are metals; silicon is a semi-metal; phosphorus, sulfur and chlorine are non-metals; argon is a noble gas.

Two rows are usually printed separately below the main table, the lanthanides and actinides, so that the table fits on a page; they belong in periods 6 and 7. Hydrogen is a special case. It sits at the top of Group 1 because of its atomic structure, but it is a non-metal gas and does not behave like the alkali metals.

The examinable skill is to locate an element: give its group and period from the table, or find an element from its group and period. Chlorine is in Group 17, Period 3. Calcium is in Group 2, Period 4. Iron is in Group 8, Period 4. Neon is in Group 18, Period 2.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of group and period, the names and properties of Groups 1, 17 and 18, and locating elements by group and period.

## Subtopic: Metals, Semi-metals and Non-metals on the Map

The Periodic Table can be divided into three broad regions. A zigzag line, often called the staircase, runs from boron in Group 13 down to the region of polonium and astatine in Groups 16 and 17. Elements to the left of the staircase are metals; elements to the right are non-metals; the elements that sit along the staircase are semi-metals, also called metalloids.

Metals make up the large majority of the elements, more than three quarters of them, occupying the left side and the whole central block. Non-metals are few, about seventeen of them, clustered in the top right corner, together with hydrogen at the top left. They include the gases of the air, nitrogen and oxygen, the noble gases, the halogens, and solids such as carbon, sulfur and phosphorus. The semi-metals are a small group: boron, silicon, germanium, arsenic, antimony and tellurium are the ones always listed, and some tables add polonium and astatine.

The position tells you what to expect. A metal is usually shiny, conducts heat and electricity well, and can be hammered into sheets or drawn into wires. A non-metal is usually dull, a poor conductor, and brittle if solid. A semi-metal has some properties of each; silicon is shiny like a metal but brittle like a non-metal and conducts electricity only partly, which is exactly what makes it valuable for computer chips and solar panels. These properties are the subject of the next session.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the modern table is arranged by atomic mass; it is arranged by atomic number. Two: confusing groups and periods; groups are vertical columns, periods are horizontal rows. Three: calling hydrogen a metal because it sits in Group 1; it is a non-metal. Four: thinking Mendeleev discovered the elements in his gaps; he predicted them, and others discovered them. Five: saying semi-metals are a mixture of a metal and a non-metal; each semi-metal is a single element with in-between properties.

Layout for marks: state the rule, then the example. "Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties; for example sodium and potassium both react vigorously with water."

The questions for this section are with you now: the staircase, the proportion of metals, the six common semi-metals, and the five exhibits.

## Subtopic: Sorting the Supermarket

Imagine a supermarket where nothing is sorted. The milk is next to the batteries, the bread is with the shampoo, and the apples are scattered across every aisle. You would never find anything. So shops sort: dairy together, cleaning products together, fruit and veg together. Once things are sorted, the aisle tells you something about everything in it.

Chemists in the 1800s had an unsorted supermarket of elements. An element is a pure substance made of only one kind of atom; it cannot be broken down into anything simpler. Iron, oxygen, gold and carbon are elements. New ones were being found all the time, and nobody could see any order in them.

A few people spotted bits of a pattern. A German chemist noticed little families of three that behaved alike, such as lithium, sodium and potassium, three soft metals that fizz in water. An English chemist lined the elements up by how heavy their atoms were and noticed that similar ones seemed to come round again every eighth place, like the notes in a music scale. People laughed at him, but he was onto something: line the elements up in order, and their properties repeat at regular intervals. Something that repeats regularly is called periodic. That is where the name Periodic Table comes from.

Today we know 118 elements. About ninety are found in nature, and the rest have been made in laboratories.

Keep the supermarket in mind and try the questions for this part: what an element is, why sorting helps, and what periodic means.

## Subtopic: The Man Who Left Gaps

In 1869 a Russian professor called Dmitri Mendeleev was writing a chemistry textbook. He wrote each of the sixty-three known elements on a card, with its atomic mass and how it behaved, and he shuffled the cards around like a game of patience. He put them in order of mass, and he put elements that behaved alike in the same column.

Here is what made him brilliant. When the next card did not fit its column, he did not force it. He left a gap and said: there is an element here that nobody has found yet. And then he went further. He looked at the neighbours around each gap and predicted what the missing element would be like.

Below silicon he predicted a grey element with atoms of mass about 72 and a density of about 5.5. In 1886 germanium was found: mass 72.6, density 5.35. He predicted two more, and they turned up as gallium and scandium. That is when everyone took his table seriously. A list that can predict the future is more than a list.

One more twist. A few elements sat in the wrong order by mass, so Mendeleev swapped them to keep the families together. Years later, in 1913, a young scientist called Moseley found the reason: elements should be ordered by atomic number, the number of protons in each atom, not by mass. Today's table uses atomic number, and Mendeleev's swaps are no longer needed.

Hold the man who left gaps in mind and try this part's questions: how Mendeleev ordered the cards, why he left gaps, and what the modern table is ordered by.

## Subtopic: Reading the Map

Think of the Periodic Table as a map. Columns going down are called groups; there are eighteen of them. Rows going across are called periods; there are seven. Every element has one address: a group and a period. Chlorine lives at Group 17, Period 3. Calcium lives at Group 2, Period 4.

Elements in the same group are like family; they behave alike. Group 1 is the wild family of soft metals that fizz and even explode in water. Group 17 is the halogens, strong-smelling, poisonous non-metals like chlorine, used to clean swimming pools. Group 18 is the noble gases, like neon and helium, which hardly react with anything at all. The wide middle is full of the everyday metals: iron, copper, zinc, silver and gold.

Now draw a zigzag staircase from boron down towards the bottom right. Left of the staircase: metals, and that is most of the map, more than three quarters of all elements. Right of the staircase: the non-metals, a small crowd in the top corner, like oxygen, nitrogen, carbon, sulfur and chlorine. On the staircase itself: the semi-metals, like silicon, the in-between elements that computer chips are made from. One odd one out: hydrogen sits top left with the metals but is actually a non-metal gas.

Pocket summary of the lesson. An element is a pure substance with one kind of atom. Chemists classify elements so that position tells you properties. Mendeleev in 1869 ordered elements by mass, grouped similar ones, left gaps and predicted missing elements like germanium. The modern table is ordered by atomic number, with eighteen groups down and seven periods across. Metals on the left and middle, non-metals top right, semi-metals along the staircase.

Hold the supermarket, the gaps and the map, and take the final questions of the lesson: the address of an element, which side the metals live on, and what makes a semi-metal.
