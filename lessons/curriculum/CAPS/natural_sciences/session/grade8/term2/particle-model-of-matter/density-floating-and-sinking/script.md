# Part 1 — Expert

This session defines density, shows how to measure and calculate it, and uses the particle model to explain why different materials and different states have different densities and why some objects float while others sink. The anchor is an old riddle: which is heavier, a kilogram of bricks or a kilogram of feathers? Neither; each has a mass of one kilogram. Yet the bricks would fit in a shoebox while the feathers would fill a wardrobe. The difference is not in the mass but in how tightly that mass is packed into space. The governing idea is that density is the mass of a substance per unit volume, calculated as mass divided by volume; that it depends on how heavy the particles are and how closely they are packed; and that an object floats in a liquid or gas if its average density is less than that of the fluid, and sinks if it is greater. The definition and formula, the units, the methods for finding the volume of regular and irregular objects, calculations, the particle explanation and the rule for floating and sinking are examinable, and they lead to pressure and buoyancy in later Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: Mass, Volume and Density

Mass is the amount of matter in an object. It is measured in kilograms, symbol kg, or in grams, symbol g, with a balance or a scale. One kilogram is one thousand grams.

Volume is the amount of space an object takes up. It is measured in cubic metres, cubic centimetres or millilitres and litres. One cubic centimetre is exactly the same volume as one millilitre, and one litre is one thousand millilitres, or one thousand cubic centimetres. A cubic metre is a large volume, one thousand litres, about the size of a large washing machine box.

Density is the mass per unit volume of a substance. It tells you how much mass is packed into each cubic centimetre or each cubic metre. The formula is density equals mass divided by volume. In symbols, using the Greek letter rho for density, rho equals m over V. The units of density are grams per cubic centimetre, written g/cm³, or kilograms per cubic metre, written kg/m³. To convert from grams per cubic centimetre to kilograms per cubic metre, multiply by one thousand. Pure water has a density of one gram per cubic centimetre, which is one thousand kilograms per cubic metre. That is no accident: the gram was originally defined as the mass of one cubic centimetre of water.

Density is a property of the substance, not of the size of the object. A small iron nail and a huge iron beam have the same density, about 7.9 grams per cubic centimetre, because although the beam has far more mass, it also has proportionally more volume. Cut a block of wood in half and each half has half the mass and half the volume, so the density is unchanged. That makes density useful for identifying substances. Some typical values in grams per cubic centimetre: cork about 0.24, pine wood about 0.5, ethanol 0.79, cooking oil about 0.92, ice 0.92, water 1.00, seawater about 1.03, aluminium 2.7, iron 7.9, lead 11.3, mercury 13.6 and gold 19.3. Air is about 0.0012 grams per cubic centimetre at sea level, roughly eight hundred times less dense than water.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definitions of mass, volume and density, the formula and units, the conversion between the two density units, and why cutting an object in half does not change its density.

## Subtopic: Measuring and Calculating Density

To find the density of an object you need its mass and its volume.

The mass is measured on a balance. A digital kitchen or laboratory balance is zeroed first, or tared, then the object is placed on it and the reading recorded in grams.

For an object with a regular shape, the volume is calculated from measurements. A rectangular block has volume equal to length times breadth times height. A block of wood 10 centimetres long, 5 centimetres wide and 4 centimetres high has a volume of 10 times 5 times 4, which is 200 cubic centimetres. If its mass is 100 grams, its density is 100 divided by 200, which is 0.5 grams per cubic centimetre.

For an object with an irregular shape, like a stone or a key, the volume is found by displacement of water. Partly fill a measuring cylinder with water and read the volume at the bottom of the curved surface, the meniscus, with your eye level with it. Carefully lower the object in on a thread so that it is fully submerged and no water splashes out. Read the new volume. The difference between the two readings is the volume of the object, because the object has pushed aside its own volume of water. For a larger object, a displacement can, or overflow can, is used: the can is filled to the spout, the object is lowered in, and the water that overflows is collected and measured.

A worked example. A stone has a mass of 54 grams. The water in a measuring cylinder rises from 40 millilitres to 60 millilitres when the stone is lowered in. The volume of the stone is 60 minus 40, which is 20 millilitres, or 20 cubic centimetres. Its density is 54 divided by 20, which is 2.7 grams per cubic centimetre, about the density of granite.

The formula can also be rearranged. If you know the density and the volume, mass equals density times volume: one litre of cooking oil, with a density of 0.92 grams per cubic centimetre, has a mass of 0.92 times 1 000, which is 920 grams, so a one-litre bottle of oil weighs less than a one-litre bottle of water. If you know the density and the mass, volume equals mass divided by density: 386 grams of gold, density 19.3, has a volume of 386 divided by 19.3, which is 20 cubic centimetres, a block smaller than a matchbox.

The density of a liquid is found in the same way: weigh an empty measuring cylinder, add a measured volume of liquid, weigh again, subtract to find the mass of the liquid, and divide by the volume.

Always show the formula, substitute with units, and give the answer with its unit.

## Subtopic: Density and the Particle Model

The particle model explains why different substances and different states have different densities. Two things matter: the mass of each particle, and how closely the particles are packed.

Heavier particles give a higher density if the packing is similar. Gold atoms are about seven times as heavy as aluminium atoms, and they pack slightly more tightly, which is why gold is about seven times as dense. Lead is dense because lead atoms are heavy.

Closer packing gives a higher density for the same particles. That is why the state of a substance affects its density. In a solid, the particles are packed tightly; in a liquid, they are almost as close; in a gas, they are far apart with large empty spaces. So, for nearly every substance, the solid is slightly denser than the liquid, and both are far denser than the gas. Liquid water has a density of one gram per cubic centimetre; steam at one hundred degrees and normal pressure has a density of only about 0.0006 grams per cubic centimetre, around seventeen hundred times less, because the same number of particles now fills about seventeen hundred times the volume.

Water is the important exception between solid and liquid. Because water molecules lock into an open, six-sided arrangement when they freeze, ice takes up about nine percent more volume than the water it came from, and so its density is lower, 0.92 instead of 1.00. That is why ice floats and why icebergs float with about nine tenths of their volume under the water.

Temperature also changes density. When a substance is heated its particles move faster and spread further apart, so its volume increases while its mass stays the same, and its density decreases. Hot air is less dense than cold air, which is why hot air rises, and why a hot-air balloon floats upward. Hot water is less dense than cold water, which is why the water at the top of a geyser or a swimming pool on a sunny day is warmer than the water at the bottom.

Dissolving substances can also change density. Seawater, with salt dissolved in it, is denser than fresh water, because the salt particles add mass to each cubic centimetre. That is why it is easier to float in the sea at Muizenberg than in a swimming pool.

The questions for this section are with you now: the two factors that determine density, why gases are much less dense than solids and liquids, why ice is less dense than water, and the effect of heating and of dissolved salt on density.

## Subtopic: Floating and Sinking

An object placed in a liquid or a gas, both called fluids, experiences an upward push from the fluid, called the upthrust or buoyant force. Whether the object floats or sinks depends on a comparison of densities. If the average density of the object is less than the density of the fluid, it floats. If it is greater, it sinks. If they are equal, it neither rises nor sinks, but stays suspended wherever it is placed.

Average density means the total mass of the object divided by its total volume, including any air spaces inside it. A solid lump of steel sinks in water, because steel is about eight times as dense as water. A steel ship floats, because its hull encloses a huge volume of air, so the total mass of the ship divided by its total volume, including the air inside the hull, is less than the density of water. A ship loaded too heavily has a greater average density and sits lower; overloaded, it sinks. The bulk carriers leaving Saldanha Bay with iron ore are carefully loaded so that they ride low but safe, and lines painted on the hull, the load lines, show how deep a ship may safely sit in fresh water, in seawater and in different seasons.

A submarine controls its average density by pumping water into or out of ballast tanks: taking in water raises its average density so it dives, and blowing the water out with compressed air lowers it so it rises. Bony fish adjust a gas-filled swim bladder in the same way.

Liquids that do not mix form layers in order of density, a density column. Pour honey, water and cooking oil into a glass and they settle with honey at the bottom, water in the middle and oil on top. A cork floats on the oil, a grape may rest between the oil and the water, and a coin sinks to the bottom.

Gases follow the same rule. A helium balloon rises because helium is about one seventh as dense as air. A hot-air balloon rises because the hot air inside is less dense than the cooler air outside, so the average density of the balloon, basket and hot air is less than that of the surrounding air.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying heavy objects sink and light objects float; a heavy ship floats and a light coin sinks, because the comparison is of density, not mass. Two: saying a big piece of a substance is denser than a small piece; density does not depend on size. Three: forgetting to include the air inside a hollow object when finding its average density. Four: saying ice floats because it has air in it; it floats because its molecules are more widely spaced. Five: giving a density without units.

Layout for marks: formula, substitution with units, answer with unit; for floating, compare the density of the object with that of the fluid.

The questions for this section are with you now: the rule for floating and sinking, why a steel ship floats, how a submarine dives, the order of layers in a density column, and the five exhibits.

## Subtopic: Bricks and Feathers

Which is heavier, a kilogram of bricks or a kilogram of feathers? Trick question: they both weigh one kilogram. But the bricks fit in a shoebox, and the feathers fill a whole wardrobe.

That difference is density. Density is how much mass is squeezed into each bit of space. Bricks pack a lot of mass into a small space, so they are dense. Feathers spread a little mass over a lot of space, so they are not dense.

The formula is simple: density equals mass divided by volume. Mass in grams, volume in cubic centimetres, so density is in grams per cubic centimetre. Water is exactly one gram per cubic centimetre, which makes it a handy measuring stick. Gold is about nineteen. Cork is about a quarter.

And here is a key point: density does not change with size. A tiny iron nail and a giant iron beam have the same density. The beam has more mass, but it has more volume too, in the same proportion. Cut a chocolate slab in half and each half is just as dense as the whole slab.

Why are some things denser? Two reasons. Heavier particles, like gold atoms. And tighter packing: solids and liquids are packed close, gases are spread out, so gases have a very low density.

## Subtopic: A Stone in a Measuring Cylinder

How do you find the density of a stone? You need its mass and its volume.

Mass is easy: put it on a scale. Say it is 54 grams.

Volume is trickier, because a stone has no neat shape to measure with a ruler. So use water. Put 40 millilitres of water in a measuring cylinder. Gently lower the stone in on a string. The water rises to 60 millilitres. The stone pushed 20 millilitres of water out of the way, so its volume is 20 cubic centimetres.

Now divide: 54 divided by 20 is 2.7 grams per cubic centimetre. That is about the density of granite.

For a neat box shape, you can just measure: length times breadth times height gives the volume. A block 10 by 5 by 4 centimetres has a volume of 200 cubic centimetres. If it weighs 100 grams, its density is 0.5, about the same as pine wood.

Always write the formula, put in the numbers with units, and give the answer with units.

## Subtopic: A Steel Ship That Floats

Here is the floating rule. Compare densities. If an object is less dense than the water, it floats. If it is more dense, it sinks.

A steel coin sinks, because steel is about eight times denser than water. So how does a giant steel ship float? Because the ship is mostly hollow, full of air. Take the whole mass of the ship and divide by its whole volume, air spaces included, and you get an average density less than water. So it floats. Pack it with too much iron ore, and the average density goes up until it sinks.

Ice floats because ice is a bit less dense than water. Its particles spread out into a roomy pattern when it freezes.

Pour honey, water and oil into a glass and they stack up in layers: honey at the bottom, oil on top. Densest at the bottom.

It works in air too. A helium balloon floats up because helium is much less dense than air, and hot air rises because it is less dense than cold air.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Density is mass divided by volume, in grams per cubic centimetre or kilograms per cubic metre; water is one gram per cubic centimetre. Density does not depend on size. Measure volume with a ruler for neat shapes or by water displacement for odd shapes. Heavier particles and closer packing make a higher density; gases are least dense; heating lowers density. Objects less dense than a fluid float, denser ones sink, and a ship floats because of the air inside its hull.
