### subtopic_1 — Mass, Volume and Density
**Assistant:** Why is density a better way to identify a substance than mass or volume alone?

**Tutor:** Because mass and volume both depend on how much of the substance you happen to have, while density does not. A gold ring and a gold bar have very different masses and very different volumes, but if you divide the mass of each by its volume you get the same number, about 19.3 grams per cubic centimetre, because the bar simply contains more of the same tightly packed gold atoms. That makes density a characteristic property, like melting point or boiling point: a fingerprint of the substance itself rather than of the particular object. Jewellers and assayers have used this for over two thousand years. The famous story of Archimedes tells of a king who suspected that his new crown was not pure gold but had been mixed with cheaper silver. Archimedes could not melt the crown down, but he could find its volume by measuring the water it displaced, and weigh it, and so calculate its density. Silver is only about 10.5 grams per cubic centimetre, so a crown containing silver would have a lower density than pure gold, and would displace more water than a pure gold lump of the same mass. Today the same principle is used to check gold coins such as Krugerrands, to test the purity of milk, whose density drops if water has been added, and to check the charge in a car battery, where the density of the acid changes as the battery runs down. Density alone does not always identify a substance, because some different substances have similar densities, but combined with other properties it is a powerful test.

### subtopic_2 — Measuring and Calculating Density
**Assistant:** How do you find the density of an object that floats, like a cork, using displacement?

**Tutor:** You have to push it completely under the water, because displacement only gives the full volume of an object when the whole object is submerged. A floating cork sits mostly above the water, so if you simply drop it in, the water rises by much less than the cork's volume; in fact it rises by exactly the volume of water whose mass equals the cork's mass, which is the floating rule at work. There are two common ways to deal with it. The first is to push the cork down with a thin pin or a needle until it is just fully under the surface, holding it there while you read the measuring cylinder. The volume of the pin under the water is tiny and can be ignored. The second is the sinker method. Tie a heavy object, such as a small metal weight, to the cork. First lower only the sinker into the water and read the volume. Then lower both the sinker and the cork together, so that both are fully submerged, and read again. The difference between the two readings is the volume of the cork alone. Then measure the mass of the cork separately on a balance, and divide mass by volume. For a cork with a mass of 3 grams and a volume of 12.5 cubic centimetres, the density is 3 divided by 12.5, which is 0.24 grams per cubic centimetre, less than one quarter of the density of water. That low density comes from the many tiny air-filled cells in the bark of the cork oak tree.

### subtopic_3 — Density and the Particle Model
**Assistant:** If hot air rises because it is less dense, why is it colder at the top of a mountain?

**Tutor:** Because rising air does not stay hot; it cools as it rises, and the reason is that air pressure falls with height. Near the ground the air is squeezed by the weight of all the air above it. As a parcel of warm air rises, the pressure around it decreases, so it expands. When a gas expands, its particles do work pushing the surrounding air outward, and that uses up some of their energy of motion, so the particles slow down and the temperature falls. In dry air this cooling is about ten degrees for every kilometre the air rises. That is why the top of a mountain is colder than the valley floor, even though warm air rises: the air that reaches the top has cooled on the way up. It is also why the summit of the Drakensberg, over three thousand metres high, can have snow in winter while Durban, at sea level, is warm. The same cooling produces clouds. When moist air rises and cools enough, its water vapour condenses into tiny droplets. Moist air pushed up the slopes of Table Mountain by the south-easterly wind in summer cools and forms the famous tablecloth of cloud over its top. So two ideas work together. Warm air is less dense than the cooler air around it, so it rises. As it rises into lower pressure it expands and cools. Meanwhile the ground is heated by sunlight and warms the air nearest to it, which is why air near the ground is usually warmer than the air higher up.

### subtopic_4 — Floating and Sinking
**Assistant:** Why does a ship float higher in the sea than in a freshwater river?

**Tutor:** Because seawater is denser than fresh water, so the ship does not need to displace as much of it to be supported. A floating object sinks into the water until the weight of the water it pushes aside equals its own weight. This is Archimedes' principle. A ship with a mass of ten thousand tonnes must displace ten thousand tonnes of water. In fresh water, with a density of 1.000 tonnes per cubic metre, that means displacing ten thousand cubic metres. In seawater, with a density of about 1.025 tonnes per cubic metre, it needs to displace only about 9 760 cubic metres, so the hull sits a little higher. The difference is small, about two and a half percent, but on a big ship it can be tens of centimetres, which matters when a ship moves from an estuary into the open sea, or is loaded in a river port. That is why ships carry load lines, marks on the hull showing the deepest safe level for fresh water, for seawater, for summer, for winter and for tropical waters. The effect is dramatic in very salty water. The Dead Sea between Israel and Jordan has a density of about 1.24 grams per cubic centimetre, so salty that a person floats with much of the body above the surface and can lie back and read a newspaper. In a simple classroom test, an egg sinks in tap water but floats if enough salt is stirred in, because adding salt increases the density of the water until it is greater than the average density of the egg.
