### subtopic_1 — Melting and Freezing
**Assistant:** Why does water expand when it freezes, when most substances shrink?

**Tutor:** Because of the special shape of water molecules and the way they attract each other. A water molecule is bent, with the oxygen atom at the corner and two hydrogen atoms sticking out at an angle. The hydrogen end of one molecule is attracted strongly to the oxygen end of a neighbour, an attraction called a hydrogen bond. In liquid water these attractions keep forming and breaking as the molecules tumble past one another, so the molecules can crowd quite close together in a jumble. When water freezes, every molecule locks into position so that each one is held by four neighbours in a very particular open arrangement, a pattern of six-sided rings with a lot of empty space in the middle of each ring. That open framework takes up about nine percent more room than the same molecules in the liquid. So ice is less dense than water, about 0.92 grams per cubic centimetre compared with 1.00, and it floats. The six-sided rings are also the reason snowflakes have six arms. This unusual behaviour matters enormously. A dam or lake in a cold winter freezes from the top, and the floating ice insulates the water underneath, so fish survive. Water seeping into cracks in rock and freezing overnight pushes the cracks wider, slowly breaking rocks apart in mountains such as the Drakensberg, a process called frost wedging. And water pipes left full on a freezing night can split open as the ice inside expands. Most other substances, like candle wax, really do shrink when they solidify, because their particles simply pack more tightly in the solid.

### subtopic_2 — Evaporation, Boiling and Condensation
**Assistant:** Why does lower air pressure make water boil at a lower temperature?

**Tutor:** Because a bubble of vapour inside the liquid can only survive if the pressure of the vapour pushing outward is as large as the pressure of the air pressing down on the liquid. At any temperature, some water particles escape from the liquid as vapour, and that vapour pushes on its surroundings; this push is called the vapour pressure. As the water gets hotter, more particles have enough energy to escape, so the vapour pressure rises. Boiling begins at the temperature where the vapour pressure equals the pressure of the atmosphere above the liquid. Below that temperature, any bubble that starts to form inside the water is squashed flat by the surrounding pressure; at that temperature, bubbles can grow, rise and burst. At sea level, as in Durban or Cape Town, the atmosphere presses with about 101 kilopascals, and water reaches that vapour pressure at 100 degrees Celsius. In Johannesburg, about 1 750 metres up, there is less air above you, so the pressure is only about 83 kilopascals, and water reaches that vapour pressure at about 94 degrees. On top of Mount Everest the pressure is roughly a third of sea level and water boils at about 70 degrees, too cool to cook rice properly. A pressure cooker uses the opposite idea: it traps the steam, raising the pressure inside to about twice atmospheric, so the water boils at about 120 degrees and food cooks much faster. The boiling point is not a fixed property of water alone; it depends on the pressure around it.

### subtopic_3 — Sublimation and the Heating Curve
**Assistant:** Why does dry ice skip the liquid stage, while ordinary ice melts first?

**Tutor:** Because whether a liquid can exist depends on the pressure as well as the temperature, and each substance has a minimum pressure below which its liquid form cannot exist at all. For carbon dioxide that minimum pressure is about five times normal atmospheric pressure. At ordinary air pressure, there is no temperature at which carbon dioxide can be a liquid, so when solid carbon dioxide warms up to minus 78 degrees Celsius, its particles go straight from being locked in a solid to flying apart as a gas. That is sublimation. If you put dry ice in a strong sealed container and let the pressure build above that minimum, liquid carbon dioxide does form; that is how carbon dioxide is stored in fire extinguishers, as a liquid under high pressure. For water, the minimum pressure for a liquid is tiny, less than one percent of normal air pressure, so at everyday pressures ice melts into liquid water first. But on a very cold, dry Highveld winter night, water vapour in the air can turn directly into ice crystals on grass and car windows without becoming dew first, which is deposition, and on a sunny freezing day frost or snow can slowly sublime away without melting. Freeze-dried food uses the same idea deliberately: the food is frozen, then placed in a low-pressure chamber where the ice sublimes straight into vapour, leaving the food dry but with its shape intact. Mothballs and solid air fresheners sublime slowly at room temperature, which is how their smell spreads.

### subtopic_4 — Changes of State Are Physical Changes
**Assistant:** If a puddle evaporates and the mass seems to disappear, how is mass conserved?

**Tutor:** The mass has not disappeared; it has moved somewhere you are not measuring. When water evaporates, water particles leave the surface of the puddle and spread into the air as water vapour. The particles themselves are unchanged; each one is still a water molecule with exactly the same mass as before. If you could weigh the whole system, the puddle plus all the air the vapour spread into, you would find exactly the same total mass before and after. You can test this in a classroom. Put some water in a flask, seal it with a stopper or a balloon, and weigh it. Heat it gently so that some water evaporates and fills the flask with vapour, then weigh again: the mass is the same, because nothing could escape. Let it cool and the vapour condenses back into droplets, still with the same total mass. Compare that with an open beaker, whose mass falls as the water evaporates, because the vapour leaves the beaker and is no longer on the scale. The same reasoning applies to every change of state. One hundred grams of ice melts into one hundred grams of water; one hundred grams of water boils into one hundred grams of steam, even though the steam takes up about seventeen hundred times the volume. The volume and the density change a great deal, because the spacing of the particles changes, but the number of particles and therefore the mass stay the same. That is one of the clearest signs that a change of state is a physical change.
