Today we explain how a substance spreads through another all by itself. Drop a purple crystal into still water and, given time, the whole beaker turns evenly purple, though nobody stirs it. That is diffusion: the net movement of particles from a region of high concentration to one of low concentration, caused only by their random motion, until they are evenly spread. We see why gas particles moving at about five hundred metres a second still take a minute to carry a smell across a room, and use the ammonia and hydrogen chloride tube to show that lighter particles diffuse faster. We compare gases with liquids and solids, and examine the factors that change the rate: temperature, particle mass, concentration difference and distance, with a fair test to measure one. Finally we find diffusion at work in the lungs, in leaves and in every smell that reaches your nose.
