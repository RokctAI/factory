### subtopic_1 — What Pressure Is
**Assistant:** Why is a dam wall built much thicker at the bottom than at the top?

**Tutor:** Because the pressure of the water increases with depth, so the push on the bottom of the wall is far greater than the push near the top. Think of the water as a stack of layers. Each layer has to support the weight of all the water above it, so the deeper you go, the more water is pressing down, and the greater the pressure. Because a liquid presses in all directions, that pressure pushes sideways on the wall as well as downward. For water, the pressure increases by about ten kilopascals for every metre of depth. So at the surface of a dam the extra pressure is zero, at ten metres down it is about one hundred kilopascals, roughly an extra atmosphere, and at fifty metres down about five hundred kilopascals. The Gariep Dam wall is about eighty-eight metres high, so near its base the water pushes on every square metre of the wall with a force of several hundred tonnes. The wall must be thick and strong at the bottom to resist that, while near the top, where the pressure is small, it can be much thinner. That is why most concrete gravity dams have a triangular cross-section. Arch dams, like the Gariep, also curve upstream, so that the water pressure squeezes the arch into the rock of the valley sides, the way the stones of an arch bridge are pushed together. The same increase of pressure with depth explains why divers must equalise the pressure in their ears as they descend, why deep-sea submarines need very thick hulls, and why water squirts further from a hole near the bottom of a full bucket than from a hole near the top.

### subtopic_2 — Gas Pressure and Particle Collisions
**Assistant:** If gas particles collide with each other all the time, why don't they lose energy and slow down?

**Tutor:** Because the collisions between gas particles are, on average, perfectly bouncy: no energy of motion is lost in them overall. When two billiard balls collide, a little energy is turned into sound and heat, and eventually the balls stop rolling. Gas particles are different. They are single atoms or small molecules, and when two of them collide, the energy of motion is shared between them and passed on, but the total is not converted into anything else. One particle might speed up while the other slows down, but the sum stays the same. Collisions with the walls work the same way, provided the walls are at the same temperature as the gas. Scientists call such collisions elastic. So the particles in a sealed container of gas keep moving forever, at a range of speeds that keeps changing from particle to particle but with an average that stays constant as long as the temperature stays the same. That is why a well-sealed tyre stays hard for weeks: the particles never get tired. If the walls are colder than the gas, the particles that hit them bounce off slightly slower on average, passing some energy to the walls, and the gas cools; that is how a cold window cools the air in a room. If the walls are hotter, the particles bounce off faster, and the gas warms. Temperature is really a measure of the average energy of motion of the particles, so a gas left alone in an insulated container keeps both its temperature and its pressure constant indefinitely. The idea that gas particles are tiny, far apart and colliding elastically is the heart of the kinetic theory of gases.

### subtopic_3 — Atmospheric Pressure
**Assistant:** If the air presses about ten tonnes on every square metre, why doesn't a house collapse?

**Tutor:** Because air pressure acts in all directions, and inside the house there is air pressing outward with the same pressure as the air outside pressing inward. The walls and roof feel equal pushes on both sides, so the forces cancel and there is no overall force trying to crush them. A house only experiences a large overall force if the pressure on one side becomes different from the pressure on the other. That happens in a strong wind. When wind blows fast over a roof, the pressure above the roof drops slightly, while the still air inside the house is at normal pressure. Even a small difference, a couple of kilopascals, acting over a roof area of a hundred square metres, produces an upward force of hundreds of kilonewtons, the weight of more than twenty tonnes. That is why storms in the Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal sometimes lift roofs off houses, and why builders tie roof trusses down firmly to the walls with steel straps. The same balance protects your body. Your blood and the fluids in your tissues are at roughly atmospheric pressure, so they push outward as hard as the air pushes in. The crushed can is the opposite situation: when the steam inside condenses, the inside pressure falls almost to zero while the outside pressure stays at about one hundred kilopascals, and the thin metal cannot withstand the imbalance. A large steel railway tanker car has even been crushed in the same way after it was steam-cleaned and sealed while still hot, then left to cool overnight. Atmospheric pressure is enormous, but because it usually acts equally on both sides of everything, we rarely notice it.

### subtopic_4 — Changing the Pressure of a Gas
**Assistant:** Why does halving the volume of a gas exactly double its pressure, if the temperature stays the same?

**Tutor:** Because halving the volume doubles the number of particles in each cubic centimetre, and pressure depends on how many particles hit each square centimetre of wall every second. Imagine a gas in a cylinder with a movable piston. At constant temperature, the particles keep the same average speed, so each collision with the wall gives the same average push. The only thing that can change the pressure is how often collisions happen. Now push the piston in until the volume is half of what it was. The same number of particles is crowded into half the space, so there are twice as many particles in every cubic centimetre. Near any part of the wall there are now twice as many particles ready to hit it, so twice as many collisions happen on each square centimetre every second. Twice the collisions, each with the same push, means twice the pressure. Halve the volume again, to a quarter of the original, and the pressure becomes four times the original. This is called an inverse proportion: when one quantity is multiplied by a number, the other is divided by the same number, so pressure multiplied by volume stays constant. The relationship was discovered experimentally by Robert Boyle in 1662, long before anyone could explain it with particles, and it is now called Boyle's law. It only holds exactly if the temperature stays constant; if you squeeze a gas quickly, as in a bicycle pump, the gas warms up, which is why the pump barrel gets hot, and the pressure rises a little more than Boyle's law alone predicts. Divers use the law constantly: air in the lungs at ten metres depth is squeezed to half its surface volume.
