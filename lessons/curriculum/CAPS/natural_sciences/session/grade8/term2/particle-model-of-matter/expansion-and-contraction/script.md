# Part 1 — Expert

This session uses the particle model to explain why almost every substance expands when it is heated and contracts when it is cooled, and looks at how engineers allow for it and use it. The anchor is a classic demonstration, the ball and ring. A brass ball on a chain just slips through a brass ring at room temperature. Heat the ball in a flame for a minute and it no longer fits; it sits on top of the ring. Leave it there and in a few minutes it cools and drops through again. Nothing was added to the ball and nothing taken away. The governing idea is that when a substance is heated its particles move faster and push further apart, so it takes up more space, while the particles themselves stay exactly the same size; when it cools, the particles slow down and move closer together, so it contracts. The particle explanation, the comparison between solids, liquids and gases, the problems caused by expansion and the solutions, and the useful applications such as thermometers and bimetallic strips are examinable, and they lead to the gas laws in Grade 11 Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: Why Substances Expand

Expansion is an increase in the size of a substance; contraction is a decrease. When most substances are heated they expand, and when they are cooled they contract.

The particle model explains this. In a solid, the particles vibrate about fixed positions. When the solid is heated, the particles gain energy and vibrate more vigorously, with bigger swings. As they swing further, they push their neighbours further away on average, so the average distance between particles increases. Every particle is now slightly further from its neighbours, and the whole solid becomes slightly larger. In a liquid the particles move about more quickly and spread further apart. In a gas the particles move faster and hit the walls of the container harder and more often; if the container can stretch, like a balloon, the gas pushes it out and the volume increases.

The most important point is what does not change. The particles do not get bigger. A heated iron atom is exactly the same size as a cold one. What increases is the space between the particles. The number of particles also does not change, so the mass does not change. Because the same mass now occupies a larger volume, the density decreases when a substance is heated.

Cooling reverses everything. The particles lose energy, move or vibrate less vigorously, and settle closer together, so the substance contracts and its density increases.

The ball and ring is the classic demonstration for solids. A metal bar fitted between two fixed pegs at room temperature will not fit after heating. A long metal rod with one end clamped and the other resting on a needle with a pointer attached will make the pointer turn as the rod is heated, showing the tiny increase in length.

For liquids, fill a flask with coloured water, fit a stopper with a narrow glass tube through it, and mark the level of the water in the tube. Warm the flask by holding it in your hands or in a bowl of hot water and the level rises up the tube. Cool it in ice water and the level falls.

For gases, stretch a balloon over the neck of an empty glass bottle and stand the bottle in a bowl of hot water: the balloon inflates as the air in the bottle expands. Move the bottle into ice water and the balloon shrinks and may be pulled into the neck.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of expansion and contraction, the particle explanation, why the mass stays the same, why the density decreases, and the three demonstrations.

## Subtopic: Solids, Liquids and Gases Compared

All three states expand when heated, but by very different amounts.

Solids expand the least. A steel rail one hundred metres long grows by only a few centimetres when it warms from a cold winter night to a hot summer afternoon, roughly 3.6 centimetres for a temperature rise of thirty degrees. That is tiny compared with the length, but large enough to matter, because the forces involved are enormous. Different solids expand by different amounts: for the same temperature rise, aluminium expands about twice as much as steel, and ordinary glass a little less than steel, while heat-resistant borosilicate glass, used for oven dishes and laboratory glassware, expands only about a third as much as ordinary glass, which is why it does not crack when heated.

Liquids expand more than solids for the same temperature rise, typically between about five and fifty times more by volume, because the forces between their particles are weaker. That is why a car's radiator has an overflow bottle, and why the fuel tank of a car filled to the brim on a cold morning may overflow when the car stands in the sun.

Gases expand the most, by far. Air heated from zero to one hundred degrees at constant pressure expands by more than one third of its volume. The forces between gas particles are so weak that the expansion depends almost only on the temperature, and all gases expand by nearly the same amount for the same temperature rise.

The order, from least to most expansion, is solid, liquid, gas, and the reason is the strength of the forces between the particles: the stronger the forces holding the particles together, the harder it is for extra energy to push them apart.

Water is an important exception between zero and four degrees Celsius. When water at zero degrees is warmed, it actually contracts until it reaches four degrees, and only then begins to expand like other liquids. So water is densest at four degrees. In winter, water at four degrees sinks to the bottom of a dam or lake, the colder water stays on top and freezes there, and the water at the bottom remains liquid at about four degrees, where fish and plants survive.

The questions for this section are with you now: the order of expansion for solids, liquids and gases with the particle reason, examples of different solids expanding by different amounts, and the unusual behaviour of water between zero and four degrees.

## Subtopic: Problems Caused by Expansion

Because expansion and contraction can produce enormous forces, engineers must allow for them in every structure.

Railway lines were traditionally laid in short lengths with small gaps between them, so that the rails could expand in summer without buckling. The rhythmic click-clack of an old train was the wheels passing over these gaps. Many modern lines use long welded rails that are stretched and clamped firmly while they are laid at a moderate temperature, so that the stress is controlled; even so, on very hot days rails can buckle, which railway engineers call a sun kink.

Bridges have expansion joints, interlocking metal teeth or rubber-filled gaps across the road surface, so that the deck can lengthen and shorten without cracking. If you walk or drive across a long bridge, such as one of the long bridges across the Orange River, you can see and feel the joints. Large bridges also rest one end on rollers or sliding bearings so that the deck can move.

Concrete pavements and roads are laid in slabs with gaps between them, often filled with a flexible material, for the same reason.

Overhead power lines and telephone wires are hung with some sag. In winter they contract and become tighter; if they were hung tight in summer, they would snap in a cold winter.

Glass can crack when heated or cooled suddenly, because one part expands or contracts before the rest. Pouring boiling water into a thick, cold glass may crack it, because the inside surface expands while the outside has not yet warmed.

Metal roofing sheets creak and tick on a hot afternoon and again as they cool at night, as they expand and contract and slide against their fixings. Builders use fixings that allow some movement.

Liquids in sealed containers can burst them if heated. That is why bottles and cans are never completely filled; a small air gap at the top gives the liquid room to expand.

The questions for this section are with you now: why railway lines have gaps and bridges have expansion joints, why power lines sag, why thick glass cracks when hot water is poured in, and why bottles are not filled to the top.

## Subtopic: Using Expansion

Expansion is also put to good use.

A liquid-in-glass thermometer contains a small volume of liquid, usually alcohol coloured red or blue, or in older thermometers mercury, in a bulb connected to a very narrow tube. When the temperature rises, the liquid expands, and because the tube is so narrow, a small expansion in volume makes the liquid rise a noticeable distance up the tube. The scale beside the tube is marked in degrees. Mercury thermometers are now being phased out because mercury is poisonous.

A bimetallic strip is made of two different metals, such as brass and steel, firmly joined along their length. When it is heated, the brass expands more than the steel, so the strip bends, with the brass on the outside of the curve. When it cools, it bends back. Bimetallic strips are used in thermostats, the switches that control the temperature in kettles, irons, geysers and ovens: as the appliance heats up, the strip bends until it breaks an electrical contact and switches the heating off; as it cools, it straightens and switches the heating back on. Older fire alarms and the flashing indicator units in some older cars also used bimetallic strips.

Tight lids on jars can be loosened by running hot water over the metal lid. The metal lid expands more than the glass jar, so it loosens.

Engineers fitting a metal tyre onto a wooden or metal wheel, or a gear onto a shaft, heat the outer part so that it expands, slip it on, and let it cool. As it contracts, it grips tightly. This is called a shrink fit. Rivets used to join steel plates in older ships and bridges were put in red hot and hammered flat; as they cooled and contracted, they pulled the plates tightly together.

Hot-air balloons, rising over the Magaliesberg or the Pilanesberg at dawn, rely on the expansion of air: heating the air in the balloon expands it, so some of it leaves through the opening at the bottom, the air inside becomes less dense than the surrounding air, and the balloon rises.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the particles get bigger when heated; the spaces between them get bigger. Two: saying the mass increases when a substance expands; mass stays the same and density falls. Three: saying solids expand the most; gases expand the most. Four: saying expansion is always a problem; it is used in thermometers and thermostats. Five: forgetting that water contracts between zero and four degrees.

Layout for marks: energy in, particles move faster or vibrate more, average spacing increases, volume increases, mass unchanged, density decreases.

The questions for this section are with you now: how a thermometer works, how a bimetallic strip bends and switches a thermostat, the shrink fit, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a stuck jar lid, a train track on a hot day, and a strip of two metals — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Stuck Jar Lid

You cannot open a jar of jam. The metal lid is stuck tight. So you run hot water over the lid for half a minute, and it twists off easily. Why?

When something is heated, its particles get more energy and jiggle harder. Jiggling harder, they push each other a little further apart. The whole object gets a little bigger. That is expansion. Metal expands more than glass, so the hot lid grew a little more than the jar, and loosened.

Cool something down and the opposite happens. The particles slow down, settle closer together, and the object gets a little smaller. That is contraction.

Here is the part people get wrong. The particles do not grow. A hot particle is the same size as a cold one. Only the gaps between them get bigger. And no particles are added, so the mass stays exactly the same. Same mass in more space means lower density.

You can see it with a balloon on a bottle. Stand the bottle in hot water and the balloon puffs up, because the air inside expands. Put it in ice water and the balloon goes floppy.

Keep the jar lid in mind and try the questions for this part: why the lid loosens, what happens to the particles, and why the mass stays the same.

## Subtopic: A Train Track on a Hot Day

Solids expand a little, liquids more, gases the most. The weaker the pull between the particles, the more they spread when heated.

A little can still matter a lot. A hundred-metre steel rail grows by a few centimetres on a hot day. If the rails were laid touching end to end, they would push against each other and bend. So old railway lines left small gaps between rails, and that is why old trains went click-clack.

Bridges have expansion joints, the metal teeth across the road that you bump over, so the bridge can grow and shrink without cracking. Power lines are hung with a droop, because in a cold winter they shrink and pull tight. Bottles of cooldrink are never filled right to the top, so the drink has room to expand.

And thick glass can crack if you pour boiling water into it, because the inside expands before the outside has warmed up.

Hold the train track in mind and try this part's questions: which state expands most, why rails have gaps, and why power lines droop.

## Subtopic: A Strip of Two Metals

Expansion can be useful too.

A thermometer has a little liquid in a bulb, joined to a very thin tube. Heat it and the liquid expands. The tube is so thin that a tiny expansion pushes the liquid a long way up. Read the number beside it, and that is the temperature.

Now glue a strip of brass to a strip of steel. Heat it. Brass expands more than steel, so the strip bends. Cool it and it straightens again. That bending strip is a little switch inside your kettle, iron or geyser: it bends to switch the heating off when it is hot enough, and straightens to switch it back on when it cools.

One strange fact: water is weird again. From zero to four degrees, water actually shrinks as it warms. So in winter the water at the bottom of a dam stays at about four degrees, and fish survive under the ice.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Heating makes particles move faster and spread further apart, so substances expand; cooling makes them contract. The particles do not change size, and the mass stays the same, so density falls when heated. Gases expand most, then liquids, then solids. Engineers leave gaps in rails, joints in bridges and sag in power lines. Thermometers and bimetallic strips use expansion. Water contracts between zero and four degrees.

Hold the jar lid, the train track and the two-metal strip, and take the final questions of the lesson: how a thermostat works, why a bridge needs joints, and what happens to particle size when heated.
