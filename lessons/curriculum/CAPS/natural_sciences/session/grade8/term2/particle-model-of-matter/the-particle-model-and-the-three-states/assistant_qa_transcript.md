### subtopic_1 — The Particle Model of Matter
**Assistant:** If the space between particles is empty, what stops the particles from collapsing together?

**Tutor:** Their motion, and a repulsion that appears when they get very close. The particle model says there are forces of attraction pulling particles towards each other, and in a solid or liquid those forces do hold them close. But two things prevent a complete collapse. First, the particles are always moving. In a gas they move so fast that the weak attractions cannot hold them together, which is why a gas spreads out. In a liquid and solid the motion is less free, but it still keeps the particles jostling. Second, when two particles get very close, their outer electrons begin to overlap, and since electrons all carry negative charge they repel each other strongly. So at a particular distance, the attraction pulling the particles together is balanced by the repulsion pushing them apart, and that is roughly the spacing you find in solids and liquids. Push them closer and the repulsion rises steeply, which is why solids and liquids are almost impossible to compress. The empty space between particles is real; there is no substance filling it. But particles are not tiny solid balls with sharp edges; each atom is mostly empty space itself, with a tiny nucleus and electrons spread around it, and what defines its size is the region where its electrons are found. When we say particles touch in a solid, we mean their electron clouds are as close as the repulsion allows. The model's simple picture of hard spheres is good enough for explaining states, diffusion and pressure, and later grades refine it.

### subtopic_2 — Particles in a Solid
**Assistant:** If particles in a solid are always vibrating, why does a solid not slowly fall apart?

**Tutor:** Because the forces holding each particle to its neighbours are much stronger than the energy of its vibration, so every particle stays bound in place while it shakes. Picture each particle as connected to its neighbours by invisible springs. It can move back and forth around its position, stretching and squashing the springs, but the springs always pull it back towards the middle. At room temperature, the vibrations in a solid such as iron or salt are tiny compared with the distance it would take to break free. That is why a steel beam can hold a building up for a century. Raise the temperature and the vibrations grow larger, which is why solids expand slightly when heated. Keep heating, and at the melting point the vibrations become so energetic that particles can break out of their fixed positions and slide past one another; the regular pattern collapses and the solid becomes a liquid. So melting is exactly the point at which the energy of vibration overcomes the forces holding the lattice in place. The strength of those forces differs enormously between substances, which is why melting points range from minus two hundred and ten degrees Celsius for nitrogen to over three thousand degrees for tungsten. Even at the surface, some particles of certain solids do escape directly: you can smell mothballs because particles leave the solid and move into the air, a process called sublimation. Dry ice, solid carbon dioxide, sublimes so quickly that it seems to smoke. But for most solids at room temperature, the forces win easily, and the solid holds together indefinitely.

### subtopic_3 — Particles in Liquids and Gases
**Assistant:** Why can a gas be squashed so much more than a liquid when both are made of moving particles?

**Tutor:** Because of how much empty space each one contains, and the difference is enormous. In a liquid, the particles are close together, almost touching, so the empty space between them is small, a small fraction of the total volume. Pushing harder on a liquid can only bring particles a tiny bit closer before the strong repulsion between their electrons stops them. That is why hydraulic systems, such as car brakes and the arms of an excavator, use liquids: when you push on a liquid at one end of a pipe, almost none of the push is wasted on squashing it, and it is passed through to the other end. In a gas at everyday pressure, the particles are about ten times their own diameter apart on average. Volume depends on that distance in all three directions, so the particles themselves occupy only about a thousandth of the gas's volume; the rest is empty. That leaves a great deal of room to push them closer together before they start to touch. Air in a scuba cylinder is compressed to about two hundred times atmospheric pressure, so the cylinder holds two hundred times as much air as its own volume would at the surface. The steam example shows the same thing from the other side: when water boils, the same molecules that filled one millilitre as a liquid spread out to fill about seventeen hundred millilitres as a gas. The particles are identical; only the spacing has changed. So the answer is in the empty space: a liquid has almost none to give up, while a gas is mostly empty space.

### subtopic_4 — Using the Model to Explain
**Assistant:** Why is it wrong to say that particles get bigger when a substance is heated?

**Tutor:** Because the particles themselves stay the same; what changes is how fast they move and how far apart they are, and those changes are enough to explain everything we observe. It is a very natural mistake. A metal rod gets longer when heated, a balloon swells in the sun, and it seems obvious to say the particles have swollen. But consider what that would imply. If copper atoms grew when heated, a copper atom in hot water would be a different object from one in cold water, with different properties, and nothing in chemistry suggests that. The masses of atoms are fixed, their chemical behaviour stays the same, and when the rod cools it returns exactly to its original length. The particle model gives a better explanation. Heating gives particles more energy. In a solid they vibrate more vigorously, each one pushing its neighbours slightly further away during each swing, so the average distance between particles increases a little and the whole solid expands. In a gas, faster particles hit the walls of a balloon harder and more often, pushing them outward. In each case the particles move more and take up more room, but their own size does not change. The same reasoning tells you why particle diagrams should use circles of the same size for ice, water and steam. Examiners look for this point specifically, because it shows whether a learner thinks of particles as tiny pieces of the substance that share its everyday properties, or as the model describes them. A good answer always says: the particles move faster and further apart; the particles do not expand.
