# Part 1 — Expert

This session uses the particle model to explain gas pressure, the push that a gas exerts on every surface it touches. The anchor is a dramatic demonstration. A little water is boiled in an empty cooldrink can until the can is full of steam. The can is then quickly turned upside down into a bowl of cold water. With a loud crack, the can is instantly crushed flat, though nobody touched it. The force that crushed it was the ordinary air of the room, which is pressing on everything around us all the time. The governing idea is that gas pressure is caused by the countless collisions of fast-moving gas particles with the walls of their container, and that the pressure increases when there are more particles, when the particles move faster because the gas is hotter, or when the same particles are squeezed into a smaller volume. The meaning of pressure, the particle explanation, atmospheric pressure, and the effects of the number of particles, temperature and volume on gas pressure are examinable, and they lead directly to the gas laws in Grade 11 Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: What Pressure Is

Pressure is the force acting on each unit of area of a surface. The formula is pressure equals force divided by area. Force is measured in newtons, symbol N, and area in square metres, so pressure is measured in newtons per square metre, which is given the name pascal, symbol Pa. One pascal is a small pressure, about the pressure of a sheet of paper lying flat on a table, so pressures are often given in kilopascals, kPa, where one kilopascal is one thousand pascals.

The same force can produce a large or a small pressure depending on the area it acts on. A sharp knife cuts because the force from your hand acts on the tiny area of its edge, producing a large pressure. A drawing pin goes into a board easily because its point has a tiny area, while its flat head spreads the force from your thumb over a larger area so that it does not hurt. Wide tyres on a tractor and wide feet on a camel or an elephant spread their weight over a large area, producing a small pressure, so they do not sink into soft ground. A learner in stiletto heels can damage a wooden floor that a much heavier person in flat shoes does not, because the same weight acts on a much smaller area.

Solids exert pressure on whatever they rest on, because of their weight. Liquids exert pressure on the bottom and the sides of their containers and on anything submerged in them, and the pressure increases with depth; that is why the wall of a dam such as the Vaal Dam is much thicker at the bottom than at the top, and why a diver's ears hurt at the bottom of a deep pool. Gases also exert pressure, in all directions, and the particle model explains why.

A worked example. A box with a weight of 600 newtons rests on a floor on a face measuring 0.5 metres by 0.4 metres, an area of 0.2 square metres. The pressure on the floor is 600 divided by 0.2, which is 3 000 pascals, or 3 kilopascals. Turn the box onto a smaller face of 0.05 square metres and the same weight now produces 600 divided by 0.05, which is 12 000 pascals, four times as much, because the area is a quarter of the size. Always write the formula, substitute with units, and give the answer in pascals or kilopascals.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition and formula of pressure, the unit pascal, and examples where a small area gives a large pressure and a large area gives a small pressure.

## Subtopic: Gas Pressure and Particle Collisions

According to the particle model, the particles of a gas are far apart and moving rapidly in all directions, in straight lines, until they collide with one another or with the walls of their container. Each time a particle hits a wall, it bounces off, and in doing so it pushes on the wall with a tiny force for a tiny moment. A single collision is far too small to notice. But there are an enormous number of particles: a single cubic centimetre of air contains roughly twenty-five billion billion of them, and each square centimetre of a container wall is struck billions of billions of times every second. All those tiny pushes add up to a steady force on every part of the wall. The force on each unit of area is the gas pressure.

Because the particles move randomly in all directions, they hit every wall equally often, so a gas exerts the same pressure in all directions, up, down and sideways. That is why a balloon, blown up, is round: the gas pushes outward equally everywhere until the stretched rubber pushes back equally.

The pressure inside a balloon or a tyre is greater than the air pressure outside, because more particles have been pushed into it. The extra collisions on the inside push the rubber outward until it is stretched taut. If the balloon is punctured, the particles rush out through the hole, from the region of higher pressure to the region of lower pressure, until the pressures are equal.

Pressure in a gas is measured with a pressure gauge or a manometer. A car tyre gauge at a petrol station shows the pressure above the outside air pressure, typically about two hundred to two hundred and fifty kilopascals for a car tyre.

The questions for this section are with you now: the particle explanation of gas pressure, why a gas pushes equally in all directions, why a blown-up balloon is taut, and what happens when it is punctured.

## Subtopic: Atmospheric Pressure

The Earth is surrounded by a layer of air, the atmosphere, held by gravity. The air particles collide with every surface they touch, including us, producing atmospheric pressure. At sea level, in Cape Town or Durban, the average atmospheric pressure is about 101 kilopascals, about one hundred thousand newtons on every square metre, equivalent to the weight of about ten tonnes on each square metre, or about one kilogram on each square centimetre. We do not feel crushed because the fluids inside our bodies push outward with an equal pressure, and because the air pushes on us from every side, not only from above.

Atmospheric pressure decreases with altitude, because there is less air above pressing down, and the air is less dense, so there are fewer collisions per second. In Johannesburg, about 1 750 metres above sea level, the pressure is about 83 kilopascals. At the top of Mount Everest it is about a third of sea-level pressure, and climbers usually need bottled oxygen. Your ears may pop when you drive from Johannesburg down to Durban, or when an aeroplane descends, as the pressure on the outside of your eardrum changes and the pressure inside adjusts.

The crushing can shows atmospheric pressure. When the can full of steam is upturned into cold water, the steam inside condenses very quickly into a few drops of water. Almost all the gas particles inside are gone, so there are very few collisions on the inside walls. The air outside is still colliding as before, so the outside pressure is now far greater than the inside pressure, and the can is crushed.

Drinking through a straw works the same way. When you suck, you lower the pressure inside the straw and your mouth. The atmospheric pressure acting on the surface of the drink in the glass is now greater, and it pushes the drink up the straw. A suction cup sticks to tiles because pressing it squeezes out the air behind it; the atmosphere then presses it against the wall.

A barometer measures atmospheric pressure. Weather forecasters watch pressure changes: falling pressure often signals approaching cold fronts and rain in the Western Cape winter, while high pressure usually brings clear, dry weather.

The questions for this section are with you now: the cause of atmospheric pressure, its value at sea level, why it is lower in Johannesburg, the crushing can, and how a straw works.

## Subtopic: Changing the Pressure of a Gas

The particle model predicts three ways to increase the pressure of a gas in a container, each by increasing how hard or how often the particles hit the walls.

More particles. Pumping air into a bicycle or car tyre adds more particles to the same volume. With more particles, there are more collisions with the walls every second, so the pressure increases. Letting air out reduces the number of particles and the pressure.

Higher temperature. Heating a gas makes its particles move faster. Faster particles hit the walls more often and with more force, so the pressure increases, as long as the volume stays the same. That is why aerosol cans carry warnings not to leave them in the sun, put them near heat or throw them into a fire: the pressure inside can rise until the can bursts. It is also why tyre pressures should be checked when the tyres are cold, because after a long drive on a hot N1 highway the tyres heat up and the pressure reads higher. Cooling a gas lowers its pressure; a balloon taken from a warm room into a cold fridge shrinks.

Smaller volume. Squeezing the same gas into a smaller space, at the same temperature, means the particles have less distance to travel between collisions with the walls and more particles strike each square centimetre every second, so the pressure increases. Push the plunger of a syringe while blocking its end with your finger and you feel the pressure building against your finger and against the plunger. If the volume of a gas is halved at constant temperature, its pressure doubles. This relationship is studied in detail in Grade 11 as Boyle's law. A bicycle pump works by squeezing air into a smaller volume until its pressure is high enough to push past the valve into the tyre.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying gas pressure is caused by particles pushing each other; it is caused by particles colliding with the walls. Two: saying gas pressure acts only downward; it acts equally in all directions. Three: saying heating a gas makes its particles bigger, raising the pressure; the particles move faster. Four: saying a straw sucks up the drink; atmospheric pressure pushes it up. Five: saying there is no pressure on us because we cannot feel the air; the atmosphere presses about one hundred kilopascals on everything at sea level.

Layout for marks: name the change, then say how it affects the number of collisions per second and the force of each collision, and conclude about the pressure.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three ways to increase gas pressure with their particle explanations, why aerosol cans must not be heated, the syringe, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a bicycle tyre, a crushed can, and a can left in the sun — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Pumping a Bicycle Tyre

Pump up a bicycle tyre and it goes from floppy to hard. What is making it hard?

Billions and billions of air particles inside, zooming about at hundreds of metres a second, crashing into the inside of the tyre and bouncing off. Every crash gives the rubber a tiny push outward. One push is nothing. But there are so many crashes every second that together they push the rubber out hard. That steady push on each bit of the wall is called gas pressure.

Pumping adds more particles. More particles means more crashes every second, so more pressure, and the tyre gets harder. Let some air out and there are fewer crashes, so the tyre goes soft.

Because the particles fly in every direction, they push equally in every direction. That is why a balloon blows up round, not long or flat.

Pressure is force on each bit of area, measured in pascals. A car tyre is usually pumped to around two hundred kilopascals more than the air outside.

Keep the tyre in mind and try the questions for this part: what causes gas pressure, why pumping makes a tyre harder, and why a balloon is round.

## Subtopic: The Crushed Can

We live at the bottom of an ocean of air. The air is pressing on you right now, about one hundred kilopascals at the coast, like a kilogram on every square centimetre of your skin. You do not get squashed because your body pushes back equally, from the inside.

Here is a trick that shows how strong it is. Boil a little water in an empty cooldrink can so it fills with steam. Then flip it quickly into cold water. Crack! It crushes flat. The cold water turned the steam back into a few drops, so almost no particles were left inside to push back. The air outside kept pushing, and won.

Drinking with a straw works the same way. You do not really suck the drink up. You lower the pressure inside the straw, and the air pressing on the drink in the glass pushes it up to you.

Up in Johannesburg there is less air above you, so the pressure is lower, about 83 kilopascals. That is why your ears can pop on the drive down to Durban.

Hold the crushed can in mind and try this part's questions: what crushed the can, how a straw really works, and why pressure is lower high up.

## Subtopic: A Can Left in the Sun

Read the label on a can of spray deodorant: do not leave in the sun, do not put near a fire. Why?

Heat makes the gas particles inside move faster. Faster particles hit the walls harder and more often, so the pressure goes up. Get it hot enough and the can can burst.

There is one more way to raise pressure: squeeze the gas into a smaller space. Block the end of a syringe with your finger and push the plunger. You feel the pressure grow, because the same particles are crammed into less room and hit the walls more often. Halve the space and the pressure doubles.

So three things raise gas pressure: more particles, hotter particles, or less space.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Pressure is force divided by area, measured in pascals. Gas pressure is caused by particles colliding with the walls of the container, and it acts equally in all directions. The atmosphere presses about 101 kilopascals at sea level and less up high. Gas pressure increases with more particles, a higher temperature or a smaller volume.

Hold the tyre, the can and the sunny deodorant, and take the final questions of the lesson: three ways to raise pressure, why aerosols must not be heated, and what happens when you squeeze a syringe.
