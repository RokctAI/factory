### subtopic_1 — What Diffusion Is
**Assistant:** If particles move randomly, why do they always end up spreading out instead of staying clumped?

**Tutor:** Because spreading out is overwhelmingly more likely than staying together, simply by counting the possibilities. Imagine a box divided into two halves, with ten particles all starting in the left half. Each particle moves randomly, so at any moment each is equally likely to be in either half. What is the chance that all ten happen to be on the left at a given moment? For each particle it is one half, so for all ten it is one half multiplied by itself ten times, about one in a thousand. Most of the time you will find roughly five on each side, because there are vastly more ways to arrange ten particles with about half on each side than with all on one side. Now replace ten particles with the number in a drop of food colouring, something like a billion billion. The chance that they all randomly gather back into the original drop is so small that it would not happen once in the entire age of the Universe. So the spreading is not driven by any force or desire. It is the result of random motion plus enormous numbers: there are so many more spread-out arrangements than clumped ones that random motion carries the system towards spread-out arrangements and keeps it there. That is also why diffusion has a direction, from high concentration to low, even though each individual particle has no preferred direction at all. Physicists describe this tendency towards the most probable, most spread-out arrangement using a quantity called entropy, which you may meet in later grades. For now, the key idea is that the net movement comes from the numbers, not from the particles knowing where to go.

### subtopic_2 — Diffusion in Gases
**Assistant:** Why do lighter gas particles move faster than heavier ones at the same temperature?

**Tutor:** Because at the same temperature all gas particles have, on average, the same energy of motion, and a lighter particle needs a higher speed to carry the same energy. Temperature is a measure of the average energy of motion of the particles in a substance. In a mixture of gases at one temperature, collisions constantly share energy among the particles, so the average energy of motion is the same for every kind of particle, light or heavy. The energy of a moving object depends on both its mass and its speed: a heavy truck moving slowly can carry as much energy as a light bicycle moving fast. So if an ammonia molecule and a hydrogen chloride molecule have the same average energy, the lighter ammonia molecule must be moving faster. The relationship is not a simple proportion; speed depends on the square root of the mass ratio. Hydrogen chloride is about 2.1 times as heavy as ammonia, so ammonia moves about 1.46 times as fast, the square root of 2.1. That is why the white ring in the tube experiment forms about three fifths of the way along from the ammonia end. The same rule explains why helium escapes from a party balloon faster than air would, why the lightest gas, hydrogen, diffuses fastest of all, and why scientists in the 1940s could separate two forms of uranium, which differ very slightly in mass, by letting a uranium gas diffuse through thousands of porous barriers one after another, each stage enriching the lighter form a tiny amount. Lighter particles are faster, and faster particles diffuse faster.

### subtopic_3 — Diffusion in Liquids and the Factors That Affect It
**Assistant:** Why does diffusing twice as far take about four times as long?

**Tutor:** Because a random walk does not make steady progress in one direction; it wanders back and forth, and the distance it covers grows much more slowly than the time it spends. Picture a single particle taking steps of equal length, each in a random direction. If it walked in a straight line, ten steps would take it ten step-lengths away. But because each step is random, many steps cancel out: a step to the left is often undone by a step to the right. Working out the average distance after many random steps shows that it grows with the square root of the number of steps. After a hundred steps the particle is typically about ten step-lengths from the start, not a hundred. After four hundred steps, about twenty. So to double the distance you need four times as many steps, and therefore four times as long. This has big consequences. Over the width of a single cell, about a hundredth of a millimetre, oxygen diffuses in a few thousandths of a second, which is why cells can rely on diffusion. Over one centimetre it would take minutes to hours, and over a metre years. That is why animals larger than a few millimetres need hearts and blood vessels to carry substances over long distances, with diffusion doing only the final short step across thin walls. It is also why lungs and leaves are built with very thin surfaces: halving the thickness of a membrane makes diffusion across it about four times faster. The square-root rule turns a simple observation about spreading colour into the reason living things are shaped the way they are.

### subtopic_4 — Diffusion in Living Things
**Assistant:** How do the lungs keep oxygen diffusing into the blood, instead of the concentrations evening out and stopping?

**Tutor:** By constantly refreshing both sides, so that a concentration difference is always maintained. Diffusion only produces a net movement while there is a difference in concentration. If the air in the alveoli and the blood next to it were left alone, oxygen would diffuse into the blood until the concentrations were equal, and then the net movement would stop. The body prevents that in two ways. First, breathing replaces the air in the lungs about twelve to sixteen times a minute at rest, bringing in fresh air rich in oxygen and removing air that has lost some of its oxygen and gained carbon dioxide. That keeps the oxygen concentration in the alveoli high and the carbon dioxide concentration low. Second, the heart pumps blood through the lung capillaries continuously. Blood arriving from the body is low in oxygen, because the cells have used it up in respiration, and high in carbon dioxide, which the cells have produced. As soon as that blood has picked up oxygen, it is carried away to the body and replaced by more oxygen-poor blood. Inside the red blood cells, a substance called haemoglobin binds the oxygen as it arrives, which keeps the concentration of free oxygen in the blood low and so keeps oxygen diffusing in. The same principle works in reverse for carbon dioxide. During exercise, both breathing and heart rate increase, refreshing the two sides faster and allowing more gas exchange per minute. Plants use the same trick: photosynthesis uses carbon dioxide inside the leaf as fast as it arrives, so it keeps diffusing in through the stomata. Living things are experts at keeping diffusion going.
