# Part 1 — Expert

This session uses the particle model to explain what happens when matter changes from one state to another. The anchor is a beaker of crushed ice with a thermometer in it, heated steadily over a burner while someone reads the temperature every minute. The reading climbs from below zero to zero, then stays at zero for several minutes while the ice melts, even though the burner is still heating it. Then it climbs again to one hundred, and stays at one hundred while the water boils away. The governing idea is that a change of state happens when energy changes how strongly the particles are held together, not how big they are, and that while a pure substance is changing state its temperature stays constant, because the energy supplied goes into overcoming the forces between particles rather than making them move faster. Melting, freezing, evaporation, boiling, condensation, sublimation and deposition, the particle explanation of each, the heating and cooling curve, and the conservation of mass in a change of state are examinable, and they lead to the kinetic theory and latent heat in Grade 10 and 11 Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: Melting and Freezing

Melting is the change from a solid to a liquid. Freezing, also called solidifying, is the reverse, the change from a liquid to a solid. For a pure substance, both happen at the same temperature: the melting point, which is equal to the freezing point. Pure ice melts at zero degrees Celsius, and pure water freezes at zero degrees Celsius. Other substances have their own melting points: candle wax melts at around fifty to sixty degrees, chocolate at about thirty-four, which is why it melts in your mouth, iron at about fifteen hundred, and tungsten at over three thousand.

The particle model explains melting. In a solid, the particles vibrate about fixed positions, held by strong forces. When the solid is heated, the particles gain energy and vibrate more and more strongly. At the melting point, they have enough energy to break free of their fixed positions and slide past one another, although they stay close together. The regular arrangement collapses into the jumble of a liquid. Melting therefore requires energy: the substance absorbs energy from its surroundings. That is why ice in a cooler box keeps drinks cold, by taking in energy as it melts.

Freezing is the reverse. As a liquid cools, its particles lose energy and move more slowly. At the freezing point, the forces of attraction are able to pull them into fixed positions in a regular arrangement, and the liquid becomes a solid. Freezing releases energy to the surroundings. Farmers in frost-prone areas sometimes spray water onto fruit trees on cold nights, because as the water freezes it releases energy that keeps the buds at about zero degrees, protecting them from colder temperatures.

The striking fact is that during melting, the temperature does not rise, even though energy is being supplied. All the energy goes into breaking the particles free from their fixed positions, not into making them move faster. Only when all the solid has melted does the temperature rise again. The same is true in reverse during freezing: the temperature stays at the freezing point until all the liquid has become solid.

Water has an unusual property: it expands when it freezes, so ice is less dense than liquid water and floats. That is why water pipes can burst in winter and why lakes freeze from the top down, with liquid water below where fish can survive.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definitions of melting and freezing, the particle explanation, why energy is absorbed in melting and released in freezing, and why the temperature stays constant during melting.

## Subtopic: Evaporation, Boiling and Condensation

Changing from a liquid to a gas can happen in two ways, evaporation and boiling, and the difference matters.

Evaporation happens at the surface of a liquid, at any temperature below the boiling point. Particles in a liquid move at a range of speeds. Some fast-moving particles near the surface have enough energy to escape the attraction of their neighbours and leave as gas. Wet washing dries on the line, puddles disappear after rain, and a saucer of water slowly empties, all by evaporation. Evaporation is faster when the temperature is higher, when there is a larger surface area, when there is wind to carry the escaped particles away, and when the air is dry. Because the fastest particles leave, the average energy of those left behind falls, so evaporation cools a liquid. That is why sweating cools you, why a wet cloth wrapped around a clay pot keeps water cool, and why you feel cold after climbing out of a swimming pool on a windy day.

Boiling happens throughout the liquid, at one particular temperature, the boiling point. At the boiling point, bubbles of gas form inside the liquid, rise and burst at the surface. Pure water boils at one hundred degrees Celsius at sea level. The boiling point depends on air pressure: at high altitude, where air pressure is lower, water boils at a lower temperature. In Durban, at sea level, water boils at about one hundred degrees; in Johannesburg, at an altitude of about 1 750 metres, it boils at about ninety-four degrees, which is why food takes a little longer to cook there. As with melting, the temperature stays constant during boiling: the energy supplied goes into separating the particles completely, not into making them faster.

Condensation is the change from a gas to a liquid. When gas particles lose energy, for example by touching a cold surface, they slow down enough for the forces of attraction to pull them close together again. Droplets form on the outside of a glass of cold cooldrink, on a bathroom mirror after a shower, and on grass as dew on a cool morning. Clouds form when water vapour in rising air cools and condenses onto tiny particles of dust. Condensation releases energy, the reverse of evaporation, which is why steam burns are worse than burns from boiling water: steam releases a large amount of energy as it condenses on the skin.

The questions for this section are with you now: the differences between evaporation and boiling, the factors that speed up evaporation, why evaporation cools, the effect of altitude on boiling point, and three examples of condensation.

## Subtopic: Sublimation and the Heating Curve

A few substances change directly from a solid to a gas without becoming liquid first; this is called sublimation. Dry ice, which is solid carbon dioxide, sublimes at minus seventy-eight degrees Celsius at normal pressure, producing the white fog used on stages; the fog is actually tiny droplets of water condensed from the air by the cold gas. Iodine crystals, gently warmed, give off a purple vapour. Mothballs slowly shrink as they sublime. The reverse change, from a gas directly to a solid, is called deposition; frost on a cold winter morning in the Highveld forms when water vapour in the air turns directly into ice crystals on cold surfaces.

The full set of changes can be drawn as a triangle with solid, liquid and gas at the corners. Solid to liquid is melting; liquid to solid is freezing; liquid to gas is evaporation or boiling; gas to liquid is condensation; solid to gas is sublimation; gas to solid is deposition. Melting, evaporation, boiling and sublimation all absorb energy; freezing, condensation and deposition all release it.

A heating curve shows what happens to the temperature of a substance as it is heated steadily. For water starting as ice at minus twenty degrees, the curve has five parts. First, the temperature rises as the ice warms to zero. Second, a flat section at zero degrees while the ice melts; solid and liquid are both present. Third, the temperature rises again as the water warms from zero to one hundred. Fourth, a flat section at one hundred degrees while the water boils; liquid and gas are both present. Fifth, the temperature rises again as the steam is heated further. The flat sections are the key to the graph: they appear at the melting and boiling points, and the substance is changing state throughout each. The boiling plateau is much longer than the melting plateau, because separating the particles completely into a gas takes far more energy than loosening them into a liquid; for water, about seven times as much.

A cooling curve is the same graph in reverse, with flat sections at the boiling point, where steam condenses, and at the freezing point, where water freezes. A substance with no flat section, melting gradually over a range of temperatures, is not pure.

The questions for this section are with you now: sublimation and deposition with examples, the names of all six changes, and the five parts of the heating curve of water with the reason for the flat sections.

## Subtopic: Changes of State Are Physical Changes

Every change of state is a physical change. No new substance is formed: ice, liquid water and steam are all made of the same water molecules. The particles do not change; only their arrangement, movement and the distances between them change. Changes of state are reversible: melted ice can be refrozen, condensed steam is water again.

Mass is conserved in a change of state. One hundred grams of ice melts into exactly one hundred grams of water. If water evaporates from an open beaker the mass of the beaker decreases, but the water has not been destroyed; the particles have moved into the air. In a sealed container, the mass stays the same whatever the state of the substance inside.

The water cycle is a natural sequence of changes of state powered by the Sun: water evaporates from the sea, dams and leaves, the vapour rises and cools, condenses into clouds, and falls as rain, hail or snow; in the Drakensberg winter it may freeze; then it melts, runs off in rivers or soaks into the ground, and the cycle continues.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the temperature rises while ice melts; it stays at zero until all the ice has melted. Two: saying the bubbles in boiling water are air; they are water vapour. Three: saying the droplets on a cold glass come through the glass; they condense from water vapour in the air. Four: saying evaporation happens only at the boiling point; it happens at any temperature, from the surface. Five: saying the particles melt or expand; the particles stay the same and their arrangement changes.

Layout for marks: name the change, state the energy direction, then explain with arrangement, movement and forces of the particles.

The questions for this section are with you now: why changes of state are physical, conservation of mass, the water cycle as changes of state, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through an ice lolly on a hot day, a kettle and a cold glass, and the flat bits on a graph — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: An Ice Lolly on a Hot Day

Hold an ice lolly in the summer sun. It starts dripping. The solid is turning into a liquid: that is melting. Put the drips back in the freezer and they turn solid again: that is freezing.

Here is what the particles are doing. In the solid lolly, they are lined up, vibrating on the spot, held tightly by strong forces. The sun gives them energy, so they vibrate harder and harder until they break out of their places and start sliding past each other. Now it is a liquid. In the freezer the opposite happens: the particles lose energy, slow down, and the forces pull them back into neat rows.

Pure ice always melts at zero degrees, and pure water always freezes at zero degrees. Same temperature, opposite directions.

Now a strange fact. If you heat a bowl of ice, the thermometer climbs to zero and then stops, sitting at zero the whole time the ice is melting, even though you are still heating it. All the energy is busy breaking the particles out of their rows, so none is left to make them faster. Only when the last bit of ice has melted does the temperature start rising again.

And water is odd: it gets bigger when it freezes, so ice floats, and pipes can burst on a freezing night.

Keep the ice lolly in mind and try the questions for this part: what melting and freezing are, the temperature for water, and why the thermometer stops at zero while ice melts.

## Subtopic: A Kettle and a Cold Glass

Wet washing on the line dries even on a cool day. Some water particles at the surface are moving fast enough to escape into the air. That is evaporation, and it happens at any temperature, just from the surface. Warmth, wind, dry air and spreading the washing out all make it faster. And because the fastest particles escape, the water left behind gets cooler. That is why sweat cools you down.

Switch on a kettle and the water gets hotter and hotter until big bubbles form all through it. That is boiling, and it happens at one temperature: one hundred degrees at the coast. The bubbles are not air; they are water turned into gas. Up in Johannesburg, where the air is thinner, water boils at about ninety-four degrees.

Now take a cold cooldrink out of the fridge. Drops of water appear on the outside of the glass. They did not leak through. Water vapour in the air touched the cold glass, lost energy, and turned back into liquid. That is condensation. It is how dew forms on the grass and how clouds form in the sky.

Hold the kettle and the cold glass in mind and try this part's questions: evaporation against boiling, what is inside the bubbles, and where the drops on a cold glass come from.

## Subtopic: The Flat Bits on the Graph

A few things skip the liquid stage completely. Dry ice turns straight from solid to gas, making spooky stage fog: that is sublimation. Frost on a cold Highveld morning forms when water vapour turns straight into ice: that is deposition.

Now the graph. Heat ice from minus twenty degrees and plot the temperature. It goes up to zero. Then a flat bit, while the ice melts. Then up again to one hundred. Then a long flat bit, while the water boils. Then up again as the steam heats. The flat bits are where the state is changing, and the temperature stays still because the energy is busy pulling particles apart. The boiling flat bit is the longest, because pulling particles all the way apart into a gas takes the most energy.

And remember, changing state never makes a new substance. Ice, water and steam are all water. Melt 100 grams of ice and you get exactly 100 grams of water. It can always be changed back.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Melting and freezing happen at the same temperature, zero for water. Evaporation happens from the surface at any temperature and cools; boiling happens all through the liquid at the boiling point, one hundred degrees at sea level and lower up high. Condensation turns gas back into liquid on cold surfaces. Sublimation goes solid to gas, deposition gas to solid. During any change of state the temperature stays flat. Changes of state are physical, reversible, and mass is conserved.

Hold the ice lolly, the kettle and the flat bits, and take the final questions of the lesson: drawing the heating curve, naming the change for frost, and why the temperature stays flat.
