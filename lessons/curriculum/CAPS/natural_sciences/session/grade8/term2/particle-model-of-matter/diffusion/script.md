# Part 1 — Expert

This session uses the particle model to explain diffusion, the spreading of one substance through another without any stirring or wind. The anchor is a single purple crystal of potassium permanganate dropped carefully into a beaker of still water. At first it sits on the bottom surrounded by a dark purple cloud. Over an hour the cloud grows, and by the next day the whole beaker is evenly pale purple, although nobody touched it. The governing idea is that diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region where they are more concentrated to a region where they are less concentrated, caused entirely by the random motion of particles, and that it continues until the particles are evenly spread. The definition, the particle explanation, the difference in speed between gases and liquids, the factors that affect the rate, and the role of diffusion in living things are examinable, and they lead to the study of gas exchange and cell transport in Grade 10 Life Sciences.

## Subtopic: What Diffusion Is

Diffusion is the spreading out of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, as a result of their random motion, until they are evenly distributed. Concentration means how many particles of a substance are present in a given volume. Where the permanganate crystal dissolves, there are many purple particles in a small volume, a high concentration; in the rest of the beaker there are none, a concentration of zero.

The particle model explains why the particles spread. Every particle, of both the water and the dissolved permanganate, is moving randomly, colliding with its neighbours and changing direction constantly. No particle knows where the others are, and none is trying to go anywhere. But consider the boundary of the purple cloud. Because there are many purple particles inside it and few outside, many more purple particles happen to move outward across the boundary than happen to move inward. The overall result, called the net movement, is outward, from high concentration to low. The same random motion moves water particles into the purple region. The process continues until the concentration is the same everywhere; after that, particles still move randomly in all directions, but as many cross any imaginary line one way as the other, so there is no further net change. The mixture is then uniform.

Diffusion needs no stirring, no wind, no pump and no outside energy. It happens because particles are always moving. That makes diffusion one of the strongest pieces of evidence for the particle model: a substance spreading by itself through a still liquid or gas is very hard to explain unless both are made of moving particles with spaces between them.

Everyday examples are everywhere. The smell of vetkoek frying in a kitchen reaches the next room. Perfume sprayed in one corner can soon be smelt across the classroom. A tea bag in a cup of hot water colours the whole cup. A drop of food colouring spreads through a glass of still water. A teaspoon of sugar left unstirred at the bottom of a cup of tea eventually sweetens the whole cup, though slowly.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of diffusion, the meaning of concentration and net movement, why diffusion stops changing once particles are evenly spread, and three everyday examples.

## Subtopic: Diffusion in Gases

Diffusion is fastest in gases, because gas particles move quickly and there are large spaces between them. At room temperature, the nitrogen and oxygen molecules in air move at about five hundred metres per second on average, faster than a passenger jet. Yet a smell takes seconds or minutes to cross a room, not a fraction of a second. The reason is collisions. Each air molecule collides with others several billion times every second, so it moves only a tiny distance before being knocked in a new direction. Its path is a zigzag, a random walk, and the overall progress in any one direction is slow even though the particle itself is very fast.

A classic experiment shows diffusion in gases and the effect of particle mass. A long glass tube is clamped horizontally. A cotton-wool plug soaked in concentrated ammonia solution is placed in one end, and a plug soaked in concentrated hydrochloric acid in the other, both at the same time, and the ends are sealed with stoppers. Ammonia gas and hydrogen chloride gas diffuse along the tube towards each other. Where they meet, they react to form a white ring of solid ammonium chloride on the inside of the glass. The ring forms well past the middle of the tube, closer to the hydrochloric acid end, roughly three fifths of the way along from the ammonia. That shows that ammonia particles have travelled further in the same time, so they diffuse faster. Ammonia molecules are lighter, with a relative mass of 17, than hydrogen chloride molecules, with 36.5, and at the same temperature lighter particles move faster on average. The experiment is done by a teacher in a fume cupboard, because both gases are harmful.

Another demonstration uses a jar of a coloured gas, such as brown bromine vapour, placed under an upturned jar of air with a cover between them. When the cover is removed, the brown colour spreads upward into the top jar against the pull of gravity, even though bromine is much denser than air, and after a while both jars are the same pale brown. Diffusion beats the tendency of the dense gas to stay at the bottom, because the particles are moving randomly.

The questions for this section are with you now: why gas particles move fast yet diffuse slowly, the ammonia and hydrochloric acid experiment and why the ring is nearer one end, and the bromine jar.

## Subtopic: Diffusion in Liquids and the Factors That Affect It

Diffusion also happens in liquids, but much more slowly than in gases. In a liquid the particles are close together, so a moving particle collides with its neighbours almost immediately, after travelling a distance smaller than its own size. Liquid particles also move more slowly than gas particles. The permanganate crystal takes many hours to colour a beaker of water evenly; a smell crosses a room in minutes. In solids, where particles only vibrate in fixed positions, diffusion is so slow that it is usually negligible, although blocks of gold and lead clamped tightly together for years do show a thin region where the two metals have mixed.

The rate of diffusion depends on several factors, each explained by the particle model.

Temperature: at a higher temperature particles move faster, so diffusion is faster. A tea bag in hot water colours the water within a minute; in cold water it takes much longer. A permanganate crystal in warm water spreads noticeably faster than in cold.

Mass of the particles: at the same temperature, lighter particles move faster and diffuse faster, as the ammonia experiment shows.

State: diffusion is fastest in gases, slower in liquids and slowest in solids, because of the spacing and freedom of movement of the particles.

Concentration difference: the bigger the difference in concentration between two regions, the faster the net movement; diffusion slows down as the concentrations even out.

Distance: diffusion is effective over short distances and very slow over long ones. Particles spreading by random collisions cover small distances quickly but large distances very slowly; to spread twice as far takes about four times as long.

A fair investigation of the effect of temperature might place identical crystals or identical drops of food colouring into equal volumes of water at different measured temperatures, in identical beakers on a still surface, and time how long it takes for the colour to reach a mark or to spread evenly. The independent variable is the temperature, the dependent variable the time taken, and the controlled variables include the size of the crystal or drop, the volume of water and the shape of the beaker.

The questions for this section are with you now: why diffusion is slower in liquids than gases, the five factors and their particle explanations, and a fair test of the effect of temperature.

## Subtopic: Diffusion in Living Things

Living organisms depend on diffusion every second. In the lungs, air reaches millions of tiny air sacs, the alveoli, whose walls are only one cell thick and surrounded by blood capillaries. The air in the alveoli has a higher concentration of oxygen than the blood arriving from the body, so oxygen diffuses from the air into the blood; the blood has a higher concentration of carbon dioxide, so carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood into the air to be breathed out. The very thin walls and the huge combined surface area of the alveoli, roughly the size of a tennis court in an adult, make the short distances and large areas that fast diffusion needs.

In plants, carbon dioxide diffuses into leaves through the stomata, the tiny pores in the leaf surface, because it is used up inside the leaf during photosynthesis, keeping its concentration low there; oxygen diffuses out. Water vapour also diffuses out of the leaf, which is the main way plants lose water. In single-celled organisms such as amoeba, oxygen diffuses in and carbon dioxide out across the cell membrane, with no lungs needed because the distances are tiny. Larger animals need circulatory systems precisely because diffusion alone is far too slow over large distances.

Smell itself works by diffusion: molecules released from food, flowers or smoke diffuse through the air into the nose, where receptor cells detect them. Many animals use diffusing smells to find food or mates, and sharks can detect blood in water from a considerable distance.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying particles diffuse because they want to spread out; their motion is random, and the spreading is a result of numbers, not intention. Two: saying diffusion stops when concentrations are equal; the particles keep moving, but there is no net movement. Three: saying diffusion needs stirring or wind; those speed up mixing but are not diffusion. Four: saying particles move from low to high concentration; the net movement is from high to low. Five: saying diffusion is fastest in liquids; it is fastest in gases.

Layout for marks: state that particles move randomly, that more move from high to low concentration than the reverse, and that the net movement continues until evenly spread; then add the factor or example.

The questions for this section are with you now: diffusion of gases in the alveoli, diffusion into leaves, why large animals need blood circulation, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through the smell of frying vetkoek, a race between gases and liquids, and the diffusion keeping you alive right now — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Smell of Frying Vetkoek

Someone is frying vetkoek in the kitchen, and a minute later you can smell it in the lounge. Nobody carried the smell to you, and there was no wind. How did it get there?

Tiny particles from the hot oil and dough escape into the air. Like all particles, they are always moving, in random directions, bumping into air particles and bouncing off again. At first they are all crowded together near the pan, where the concentration is high. Out in the lounge there are none, a concentration of zero. Because there are so many near the pan, more of them happen to wander outward than wander back in. Bit by bit they spread out, until some reach your nose.

That spreading is called diffusion: particles moving from where there are lots of them to where there are fewer, just because they are always moving randomly, until they are evenly spread. No stirring, no fan, no pushing needed.

And when they are evenly spread, they do not stop moving. They keep zooming about. It is just that as many go one way as the other, so nothing changes any more.

You see it everywhere: perfume spreading across a room, a tea bag colouring a cup, a drop of food colouring slowly filling a glass of still water.

Keep the vetkoek in mind and try the questions for this part: what diffusion means, why it happens without wind, and what happens when the particles are evenly spread.

## Subtopic: Racing Gases and Slow Liquids

Gas particles are fast, really fast: air particles move at about five hundred metres every second. So why does a smell take a whole minute to cross a room? Because each particle crashes into others billions of times a second. It zigzags about like someone trying to cross a packed dance floor. Fast feet, slow progress.

Liquids are even slower. Drop a purple crystal into still water and the colour creeps outward over hours. In a liquid the particles are packed close together, so each one bumps into a neighbour almost straight away, and they move more slowly too.

What speeds diffusion up? Heat: hot particles move faster, so a tea bag colours hot water in a minute but cold water takes ages. Light particles: they move faster than heavy ones. In one famous experiment, ammonia gas from one end of a tube and a heavier gas from the other end meet and make a white ring, and the ring is much closer to the heavy gas's end, because the lighter ammonia travelled further. A big difference in concentration: the bigger the crowd at one end, the faster the spreading. And a short distance: diffusion is quick over tiny gaps and very slow over long ones.

Hold the race in mind and try this part's questions: why gases diffuse faster than liquids, what heat does, and why the white ring forms nearer one end.

## Subtopic: Diffusion Keeps You Alive

Right now, diffusion is keeping you alive. In your lungs there are millions of tiny air bags, with walls only one cell thick, wrapped in blood vessels. There is more oxygen in the air bags than in the blood, so oxygen diffuses into your blood. There is more carbon dioxide in your blood than in the air bags, so carbon dioxide diffuses out, and you breathe it away. Spread flat, those air bags would cover about a tennis court, which gives diffusion plenty of room to work.

Plants do it too. Carbon dioxide diffuses into leaves through tiny holes, because the leaf uses it up for photosynthesis, and oxygen diffuses out. A tiny amoeba needs no lungs at all; it is so small that gases diffuse straight in and out through its skin. But big animals like us need blood to carry things around, because diffusion is far too slow over long distances.

And every smell you have ever noticed is diffusion: particles drifting from the food, the flower or the smoke into your nose.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Diffusion is the spreading of particles from high concentration to low concentration because of their random motion, until they are evenly spread; then the particles keep moving but there is no net change. It is fastest in gases, slower in liquids, almost nothing in solids. It speeds up with higher temperature, lighter particles, bigger concentration differences and shorter distances. Living things rely on it for oxygen and carbon dioxide exchange in lungs and leaves.

Hold the vetkoek, the race and your lungs, and take the final questions of the lesson: which way oxygen diffuses in the lungs, why hot tea colours faster, and why big animals need blood.
