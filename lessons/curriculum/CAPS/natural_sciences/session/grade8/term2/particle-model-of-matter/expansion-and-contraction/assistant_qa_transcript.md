### subtopic_1 — Why Substances Expand
**Assistant:** If particles only vibrate harder in a hot solid, why does that make the average spacing bigger?

**Tutor:** Because the forces between particles are lopsided: it is much harder to push two particles closer together than to pull them further apart. Imagine two neighbouring atoms in a solid. At their normal spacing, the attractive and repulsive forces between them balance. If they are pushed closer, the repulsion grows extremely quickly, like squashing two very stiff springs; even a small push closer meets a huge resistance. If they are pulled further apart, the attraction holding them together grows only gradually and then weakens. So when an atom vibrates, it cannot swing very far towards its neighbour before being violently pushed back, but it can swing further away before being pulled back. The vibration is not symmetrical. On average, the atom spends a little more of its time further away than closer. At low temperature the swings are small and this lopsidedness hardly matters. As the temperature rises and the swings get bigger, the difference grows, and the average distance between the atoms increases. Multiply that tiny increase across the billions of atoms in a row along a steel rail and you get a measurable increase in length. It also explains why different materials expand by different amounts: in a material where the forces between particles are strong and stiff, like the bonds in diamond or in borosilicate glass, the lopsidedness is small and expansion is small; in a material with weaker forces, like aluminium or plastic, it is larger. In liquids and gases, with even weaker forces, the expansion is larger still.

### subtopic_2 — Solids, Liquids and Gases Compared
**Assistant:** Why does water shrink when it warms from zero to four degrees Celsius?

**Tutor:** Because two effects are competing, and below four degrees the first one wins. In ice, water molecules are held in an open six-sided framework by hydrogen bonds, with a lot of empty space inside each ring. When ice melts at zero degrees, most of that framework collapses and the molecules crowd closer, which is why liquid water is denser than ice. But not all of the framework disappears at once. Just above zero, the liquid still contains many small, short-lived clusters of molecules arranged in the open ice-like pattern. As the water is warmed from zero towards four degrees, more and more of these open clusters break up, and the molecules pack more closely, so the volume decreases. At the same time, the ordinary effect of heating is working the other way: the molecules move faster and push slightly further apart, which increases the volume. Between zero and four degrees, the collapse of the open clusters has the bigger effect, so water contracts. Above four degrees most of the clusters are gone, the ordinary expansion wins, and water behaves like other liquids. So pure water has its maximum density at about four degrees, close to 1.000 grams per cubic centimetre. This matters in nature. In a cold winter, surface water cooled to four degrees becomes the densest water and sinks, while colder water stays on top. Eventually the top layer freezes, and the ice, being least dense, floats and insulates the water beneath, which stays at about four degrees. That is how fish survive in frozen lakes in cold countries, and in high mountain dams in Lesotho in winter.

### subtopic_3 — Problems Caused by Expansion
**Assistant:** How much force can expansion produce if a steel rail is not allowed to lengthen?

**Tutor:** An enormous force, far more than most people would guess, because solids are very stiff and resist being squashed. If a steel rail is clamped so that it cannot grow, heating it does not change its length; instead it becomes compressed, as if it had expanded freely and then been pushed back to its original length. The force needed to push a steel bar back by even a tiny fraction of its length is huge. For steel, a temperature rise of thirty degrees in a fully restrained rail produces a compressive stress of roughly seventy million newtons on every square metre of its cross-section. A typical heavy rail has a cross-section of about seventy-five square centimetres, so the push along it is in the region of half a million newtons, roughly the weight of fifty tonnes. With such forces, a rail that is not properly anchored will suddenly buckle sideways to relieve the stress, the sun kink that railway engineers check for on hot days. The same forces explain why concrete roads without gaps can crack and lift in a hot summer, and why bridges must have expansion joints and sliding bearings. Engineers have two choices: leave room for the expansion, as with gaps and joints, or anchor the structure strongly enough to contain the stress, as with modern continuous welded rail. That rail is heavy, clamped every half metre or so to concrete sleepers on a deep bed of stone ballast, and laid at a chosen neutral temperature, so that in summer it is under compression and in winter under tension, but never more than it can safely hold.

### subtopic_4 — Using Expansion
**Assistant:** Why does a bimetallic strip bend instead of just getting longer?

**Tutor:** Because its two layers try to grow by different amounts, but they are joined along their whole length and cannot slide past each other. Picture a strip of brass bonded firmly to a strip of steel of the same length. When the strip is heated, the brass would like to lengthen by about twice as much as the steel. If they were separate, the brass would simply end up a little longer. But they are joined, so at every point along the strip the two layers must stay together. The only way for one layer to be longer than the other while they remain stuck together is for the strip to curve, so that the longer brass is on the outside of the curve and the shorter steel is on the inside. Think of the inside and outside lanes of an athletics track: the outside lane is longer, yet the lanes stay side by side, because the track bends. The greater the temperature change, the bigger the difference in length, and the more the strip bends. Cool the strip below the temperature at which it was made, and the brass shrinks more than the steel, so the strip bends the other way. That predictable bending is what makes the strip useful. In an electric iron, the strip carries a contact that touches another contact to complete the circuit to the heating element. As the iron heats, the strip bends away and breaks the circuit; as it cools, it bends back and reconnects. A control knob moves the fixed contact, changing how far the strip must bend, and so sets the temperature. Coiled bimetallic strips are used in dial thermometers on ovens and braais.
