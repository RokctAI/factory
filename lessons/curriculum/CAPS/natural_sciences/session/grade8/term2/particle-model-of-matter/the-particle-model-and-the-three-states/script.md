# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the particle model of matter, the single most useful idea in Grade 8 physical science, and uses it to explain why solids, liquids and gases behave so differently. The anchor is a surprising measurement. Pour 50 millilitres of water into a measuring cylinder and add 50 millilitres of ethanol, mix, and read the volume: it is about 96 millilitres, not 100. Nothing has escaped. The only sensible explanation is that both liquids are made of particles with spaces between them, and some of the smaller particles have slipped into the gaps between the larger ones. The governing idea is the particle model: all matter is made of tiny particles; there is empty space between them; the particles are always moving; and there are forces of attraction between them. The postulates of the model, the evidence for it, the arrangement, movement and forces of the particles in each state, and the use of particle diagrams to explain properties are examinable, and the model is the basis of the kinetic theory taught in Grade 10 and 11 Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: The Particle Model of Matter

A scientific model is a simplified description that explains observations and makes predictions. The particle model, also called the kinetic molecular theory, describes matter in four statements.

First, all matter is made of very small particles. Depending on the substance, the particles are atoms, as in a metal or a noble gas, or molecules, as in water, oxygen or sugar. They are far too small to see.

Second, there is empty space between the particles. Not air, not some other substance, but nothing at all. The amount of space is small in solids and liquids and very large in gases.

Third, the particles are in constant motion. In a solid they vibrate about fixed positions; in a liquid they move about and slide past one another; in a gas they move rapidly in all directions. The higher the temperature, the faster they move, on average.

Fourth, there are forces of attraction between the particles, pulling them towards one another. The forces are strongest when the particles are close together, as in a solid, and become very weak when the particles are far apart, as in a gas.

Several observations support the model. The volume puzzle with water and ethanol shows that liquids contain spaces into which other particles can fit. A spoon of sugar stirred into a full glass of water dissolves and seems to disappear, and the water level hardly rises, because the sugar molecules spread out into the spaces between the water molecules. A drop of food colouring spreads through still water without stirring, because the moving water particles jostle the dye particles around. In 1827 the botanist Robert Brown, looking through a microscope at tiny particles released from pollen grains in water, saw them jiggling constantly and randomly, though nothing was pushing them; we now know the jiggling is caused by invisible water molecules bumping into them from all sides, and the effect is called Brownian motion. Smoke particles in air, viewed under a microscope, jiggle in the same way. And gases can be compressed easily into a much smaller volume, as in a bicycle pump with a finger over the outlet, because most of a gas is empty space.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four statements of the particle model, what is between the particles, and three pieces of evidence for the model.

## Subtopic: Particles in a Solid

In a solid, the particles are packed very closely together, touching their neighbours, and in most solids they are arranged in a regular, repeating pattern called a lattice. The forces of attraction between the particles are strong, strong enough to hold each particle in a fixed position. The particles are not still; they vibrate continuously about those fixed positions, but they cannot move past one another or change places.

These features explain the properties of solids. A solid has a fixed shape, because the particles are held in fixed positions and cannot flow. A solid has a fixed volume, because the particles are already as close together as they can get. A solid cannot be compressed, for the same reason: there is almost no space to squeeze into. Solids do not flow and cannot be poured; a pile of sand or sugar seems to pour, but each grain is itself a small solid with a fixed shape. Solids are usually denser than the liquid form of the same substance, because their particles are packed most tightly, though water is a famous exception, as you will see later in this topic.

The regular arrangement in many solids shows itself in crystals. Salt crystals are cubes, because the sodium and chloride particles are stacked in a cubic pattern; snowflakes have six-sided symmetry because of the way water molecules arrange themselves in ice; quartz crystals are six-sided columns. When you heat a solid, the particles vibrate more strongly and push slightly further apart, which is why solids expand when heated, the subject of a later session.

In a particle diagram, a solid is drawn as circles of equal size touching one another in neat rows, with no gaps between neighbours and a note or small marks showing that each vibrates in place.

The questions for this section are with you now: the arrangement, movement and forces of particles in a solid, and how the model explains the fixed shape and volume of a solid and why it cannot be compressed.

## Subtopic: Particles in Liquids and Gases

In a liquid, the particles are still close together, almost touching, but they are not arranged in a regular pattern and they are not held in fixed positions. The forces of attraction are weaker than in the solid, strong enough to keep the particles close together but not strong enough to stop them moving about. The particles slide past one another and change places constantly, in a disorderly jumble.

That explains the properties of liquids. A liquid has a fixed volume, because the particles stay close together. It has no fixed shape; it takes the shape of the part of the container it fills, because the particles can move past one another. It flows and can be poured. It is very hard to compress, because there is little space between the particles. The surface of a liquid, where the particles are pulled inward by the ones below, behaves like a stretched skin, which is why some insects can walk on water.

In a gas, the particles are far apart, on average about ten times their own diameter, with large empty spaces between them. They move quickly and randomly in straight lines in all directions until they collide with another particle or with the walls of the container, then bounce off in a new direction. The forces of attraction between them are very weak, because they are so far apart, so they do not stay together.

That explains the properties of gases. A gas has no fixed shape and no fixed volume; it spreads out to fill whatever container it is in, completely. It can be compressed easily, because most of its volume is empty space; that is how a scuba tank holds enough air for an hour under water, and how a bicycle pump pushes air into a tyre. Gases flow, and they have very low densities compared with solids and liquids. The difference in spacing is dramatic: when one millilitre of liquid water boils, it becomes about seventeen hundred millilitres of steam at the same pressure, with the same number of molecules, because the particles are now so far apart.

In particle diagrams, a liquid is drawn as circles mostly touching but randomly arranged, filling the bottom of the container with a flat surface, and a gas as a few circles spread widely through the whole container with arrows showing random motion.

The questions for this section are with you now: the arrangement, movement and forces in liquids and gases, how the model explains why liquids flow and gases can be compressed, and drawing the particle diagrams.

## Subtopic: Using the Model to Explain

The power of the model is that one set of ideas explains many observations. Each explanation should name the arrangement, movement and forces of the particles.

Why can you smell food cooking in another room? Gas particles from the food move rapidly and randomly, and spread out through the spaces between the air particles. Why does a liquid take the shape of its container while a solid does not? The forces in a liquid are weaker and allow particles to slide past each other, while in a solid strong forces hold them in fixed positions. Why is it so hard to squash water in a syringe with the end blocked, but easy to squash air? The water particles are already close together with very little space between them, while the air particles are far apart. Why does a gas fill a whole room? The forces between gas particles are very weak, so nothing holds them together, and they move in all directions until they meet the walls.

The model also has limits, and the error museum collects the misunderstandings that come from treating particles like tiny pieces of the material itself. One: saying the particles of a solid are hard and the particles of a gas are soft; the particles are the same in every state; only their arrangement, movement and spacing change. Two: saying there is air between the particles of water; there is nothing between them, empty space. Three: saying particles in a solid do not move; they vibrate. Four: saying particles expand when heated; the particles stay the same size and the spaces between them increase. Five: drawing gas particles bigger than solid particles; the circles should be the same size, only further apart.

A useful summary table. Solid: particles closely packed in a regular pattern, vibrate about fixed positions, strong forces; fixed shape and volume, not compressible. Liquid: particles close together but irregular, slide past each other, weaker forces; fixed volume, no fixed shape, flows, hardly compressible. Gas: particles far apart, random fast motion, very weak forces; no fixed shape or volume, fills container, compressible.

Layout for marks: arrangement, movement, forces, and then the property they explain, in that order.

The questions for this section are with you now: explaining three everyday observations with the model, completing the summary table, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a mystery in a measuring cylinder, a school day from assembly to the home bell, and three quick drawings — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Tiny Particles, Empty Space

Here is a magic trick you can do in a science lab. Measure 50 millilitres of water. Measure 50 millilitres of alcohol. Pour them together. You should have 100 millilitres, right? You get about 96. Where did the rest go?

Nowhere. Nothing was lost. Here is the secret: everything is made of tiny particles, far too small to see, and between the particles there are gaps. Not air, just empty space. When you mix the two liquids, some of the particles slip into the gaps between the others, like pouring marbles into a bucket of tennis balls. The total takes up less space.

That is the particle model, and it has four rules. Everything is made of tiny particles. There is empty space between them. The particles never stop moving. And the particles pull on each other, more strongly when they are close.

More clues: stir a spoon of sugar into a full glass of water and it hardly overflows, because the sugar hides in the gaps. Drop food colouring into still water and it spreads all by itself, because the water particles are always moving and bumping the colour around.

Keep the vanishing four millilitres in mind and try the questions for this part: the four rules of the particle model, and what is between the particles.

## Subtopic: Assembly, Break and Home Time

Think of a school day as the three states of matter.

Morning assembly is a solid. Everyone stands in neat rows, close together, in fixed places. Nobody can walk around, but they do shuffle and fidget on the spot. That is a solid: particles packed tightly in a pattern, held firmly by strong forces, only vibrating in place. So a solid keeps its shape and size, and you cannot squash it.

Break time in a crowded corridor is a liquid. Everyone is still close together, but there are no rows any more, and people slide past each other and swap places. That is a liquid: particles close, but jumbled and moving past each other, with weaker forces. So a liquid keeps the same amount, but it flows and takes the shape of its container.

Home time on the big field is a gas. Everyone runs off in every direction, fast, far apart, bumping into each other now and then and bouncing away, until the whole field is filled. That is a gas: particles far apart, moving fast and randomly, with very weak forces. So a gas spreads to fill any space, and you can squash it, because it is mostly empty.

The learners never change size, only how they are arranged and how fast they move. Particles are the same: same particles, different arrangement.

Hold the school day in mind and try this part's questions: which state is like assembly, why liquids flow, and why gases can be squashed.

## Subtopic: Three Quick Drawings

Exams love particle drawings, so here are the three you need.

Solid: draw circles all the same size, touching, in neat rows. Little wiggle marks show they vibrate.

Liquid: draw circles the same size, mostly touching, but jumbled with no rows, filling the bottom of the container with a flat top.

Gas: draw just a few circles, the same size again, spread far apart all over the container, with arrows pointing in different directions.

Same-sized circles every time. The particles do not grow or shrink. Only the gaps and the movement change. And between the circles there is nothing, not air, just empty space.

One more amazing fact. Boil one millilitre of water and you get about seventeen hundred millilitres of steam. Same particles, just much, much further apart.

Pocket summary of the lesson. All matter is made of tiny particles with empty space between them, always moving, and attracting each other. Solids: packed tight in rows, vibrate in place, strong forces, fixed shape and volume. Liquids: close but jumbled, slide past each other, weaker forces, fixed volume, flow. Gases: far apart, fast and random, very weak forces, fill any container, can be compressed. Particles never change size; only their spacing and movement change.

Hold the vanishing millilitres, the school day and the three drawings, and take the final questions of the lesson: drawing a gas, explaining why a solid keeps its shape, and why steam takes up so much more room.
