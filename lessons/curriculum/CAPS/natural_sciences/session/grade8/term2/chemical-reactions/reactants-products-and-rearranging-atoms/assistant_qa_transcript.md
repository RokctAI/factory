### subtopic_1 — Reactants and Products
**Assistant:** How can the product of a reaction have completely different properties from the elements that formed it?

**Tutor:** Because the properties of a substance come from the way its atoms are bonded and arranged, not from the atoms on their own. Table salt is the best example. Sodium is a soft, silvery metal that reacts so violently with water that it can catch fire, and it must be stored under oil. Chlorine is a poisonous yellow-green gas that was used as a weapon in the First World War. Yet when they react, they form sodium chloride, the white crystals you sprinkle on your food. In the metal, sodium atoms each have one loosely held outer electron, which is why the metal reacts so readily. In chlorine gas, the atoms are paired in molecules, and each atom strongly attracts an extra electron. When they react, each sodium atom passes its loose electron to a chlorine atom. The sodium becomes a stable positive particle and the chlorine a stable negative particle, and these attract each other strongly into a regular crystal lattice. In that new arrangement, the particles no longer have the urge to react, so the salt is stable, safe to eat and dissolves in water. Water shows the same thing: hydrogen and oxygen are gases that together explode, but bonded into water molecules they make a liquid that puts out fires. Even with exactly the same atoms, different arrangements give different substances. Carbon atoms bonded in a rigid three-dimensional network make diamond, the hardest natural substance, while carbon atoms bonded in flat sheets that slide over one another make graphite, soft enough to use in pencils. Arrangement is everything.

### subtopic_2 — Rearranging Atoms
**Assistant:** Why does one methane molecule need exactly two oxygen molecules to burn completely?

**Tutor:** Because the atoms in the products must exactly match the atoms in the reactants, and the products of complete burning are carbon dioxide and water. Start with one methane molecule, one carbon atom and four hydrogen atoms. All of the carbon ends up in carbon dioxide, and each carbon dioxide molecule has one carbon atom and two oxygen atoms, so one carbon atom needs two oxygen atoms. All of the hydrogen ends up in water, and each water molecule has two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom, so four hydrogen atoms make two water molecules, which need two more oxygen atoms. Altogether, four oxygen atoms are needed. Oxygen in the air comes as molecules of two atoms each, so four oxygen atoms means two oxygen molecules. That is the reason for the ratio of one methane to two oxygen. Chemists write it as a balanced equation: CH4 plus 2 O2 produces CO2 plus 2 H2O, which you will learn to write in Grade 9. The ratio matters in practice. If a gas heater or a paraffin stove burns in a small room without enough ventilation, there is not enough oxygen for every carbon atom to get two oxygen atoms. Some carbon atoms get only one, forming carbon monoxide, an invisible, odourless and very poisonous gas that stops blood from carrying oxygen. Every winter in South Africa, people die from carbon monoxide poisoning after using braziers or heaters indoors with closed windows. Incomplete burning also produces soot, which is pure carbon that got no oxygen at all, and makes a yellow, smoky flame instead of a clean blue one.

### subtopic_3 — Breaking and Forming Bonds
**Assistant:** If burning releases energy, why doesn't a gas stove or a match light itself?

**Tutor:** Because before any new bonds can form, some of the old bonds have to be broken, and that first step needs energy that the cold reactants do not have. At room temperature, methane and oxygen molecules collide constantly, but they bounce off each other unchanged, because the collisions are not energetic enough to break the strong bonds inside the molecules. The reaction needs an initial input of energy, the activation energy, to break some of those bonds and get the rearrangement started. A spark from the igniter or the flame of a match supplies that energy to a small region of the gas. There, some molecules break apart and react, forming carbon dioxide and water and releasing more energy than was needed to start them. That released energy heats the neighbouring gas, giving its molecules enough energy to react too, and so the reaction spreads and sustains itself as a flame, as long as fuel and oxygen keep arriving. A match works in stages. Rubbing the head on the rough strip on the box creates heat by friction, enough to start a reaction between chemicals in the strip and the head. That reaction is hot enough to set the head burning, which in turn lights the wooden stick. Safety matches only light on the box because one of the needed chemicals, red phosphorus, is on the strip, not on the head. The activation energy is also why wood, paper and petrol in air are safe at room temperature even though they would release a huge amount of energy if they burned. It is a barrier that keeps fuels stable until we choose to light them.

### subtopic_4 — Signs of a Reaction and Conservation of Mass
**Assistant:** If mass is always conserved, why does a burning candle get lighter?

**Tutor:** Because the products of the burning escape into the air, and the scale only weighs what is still on it. A candle is made of wax, a substance whose molecules are long chains of carbon and hydrogen atoms. When it burns, the wax melts, travels up the wick, evaporates and reacts with oxygen from the air, producing carbon dioxide and water vapour. Both are gases, and they rise away from the flame with the hot air. So the candle loses the mass of the wax that has burned. But the total mass of all the substances involved has not changed. The mass of wax burned plus the mass of oxygen it used equals the mass of the carbon dioxide and water vapour produced. In fact the products are heavier than the wax alone, because they include the oxygen atoms taken from the air. You can show this with a clever experiment: burn a candle on a balance under a tube packed with substances that absorb carbon dioxide and water, so that the gaseous products are trapped and stay on the balance. As the candle burns, the reading on the balance goes up, not down, because oxygen from the air is being added to the products and captured. That is exactly the kind of evidence Lavoisier used to overturn the old idea that burning released a substance from the fuel. He showed that burning combines the fuel with something from the air, which he named oxygen, and that if every product is collected, mass is perfectly conserved. Whenever mass seems to change in a reaction, look for a gas that has escaped or been taken in.
