### subtopic_1 — Fermentation in Brewing Umqombothi
**Assistant:** Why does yeast make alcohol only when there is no oxygen, and what does it do when oxygen is present?

**Tutor:** Because yeast can release energy from sugar in two different ways, and it chooses the one that gives the most energy when it can. When plenty of oxygen is available, yeast carries out aerobic respiration, the same process you learned about in Term 1: glucose plus oxygen produces carbon dioxide plus water, releasing a large amount of energy. With that much energy, the yeast cells grow and divide rapidly, but no alcohol is made. When oxygen runs out, as it soon does inside a thick porridge or a covered pot, yeast switches to fermentation. Without oxygen it can only partly break down the glucose, into ethanol and carbon dioxide, and releases far less energy from each sugar molecule, roughly a sixteenth as much. The ethanol still contains a lot of chemical energy, which is why alcohol can be burned as a fuel. Brewers make use of both stages. At the start, when the porridge has been stirred and exposed to air, the yeast grows quickly and multiplies. As the oxygen is used up, the many yeast cells switch to fermentation and produce alcohol and gas. The same switch happens in your own muscles during a sprint, except that muscle cells without enough oxygen make lactic acid instead of alcohol, which is why tired muscles can ache. Fermentation eventually stops when the sugar runs out or when the alcohol and acid build up enough to harm the yeast itself. Traditional brewers learned by experience how long to leave the pot, judging readiness by smell, taste and the amount of froth, long before anyone knew yeasts were living cells, which was only shown by Louis Pasteur in the 1850s.

### subtopic_2 — Amasi, Mageu and Lactic Acid Fermentation
**Assistant:** How does the acid in amasi make the milk thick instead of just sour?

**Tutor:** Because milk proteins, mainly a group called casein, normally float about in tiny separate clusters, and acid makes those clusters stick together into a soft network. In fresh milk, the casein proteins are gathered into very small balls, far too small to see, spread evenly through the watery part of the milk. Each ball carries a small negative electric charge on its surface. Because like charges repel, the balls push each other away and stay separate, so milk stays runny and smooth. As lactic acid bacteria turn the milk sugar into lactic acid, the milk becomes more and more acidic. The acid adds positive charges that cancel out the negative charges on the casein balls. Once the charges are neutralised, the balls no longer repel each other, so when they bump together they stick, linking into a loose three-dimensional mesh that traps the liquid inside it. That mesh is the curd, and the milk sets thick, a little like jelly. If the curd is cut or disturbed, some of the trapped liquid, the yellowish whey, seeps out, which is why traditional amasi makers drained the whey through a hole at the base of the calabash to get a thicker product. The same chemistry, with different bacteria and conditions, makes yoghurt, and if the curd is pressed and the whey squeezed out further, it is the first step towards making cheese. You can see the effect quickly in a kitchen by stirring a little lemon juice or vinegar into warm milk: it curdles within minutes, because the acid is added directly instead of being made slowly by bacteria.

### subtopic_3 — Smelting Iron in Early Southern Africa
**Assistant:** Why couldn't early smelters simply melt iron out of the rock in an ordinary fire?

**Tutor:** For two reasons: the iron in ore is not present as metal at all, and pure iron melts at about 1 540 degrees Celsius, far hotter than an ordinary wood fire can reach. In iron ore, iron atoms are chemically bonded to oxygen atoms in iron oxide, a reddish or blackish rock. Heating iron oxide alone does not release the iron; the bonds between iron and oxygen are strong, and the oxygen must be pulled away by something that bonds to it even more strongly. Carbon, in the form of carbon monoxide, does that. An open wood fire also only reaches about six hundred to nine hundred degrees, and much of its heat escapes. Smelters solved both problems at once. Charcoal supplied pure carbon. Packing it with ore inside a clay furnace trapped the heat. Forcing air in with bellows through clay pipes made the charcoal burn much more fiercely, raising the temperature to around twelve hundred degrees, and creating carbon monoxide in the hot zone. At that temperature, carbon monoxide steals oxygen from the iron oxide, but the iron itself does not melt. Instead it forms a spongy solid lump mixed with slag, the bloom. The slag does melt, and runs off. That is why traditional iron was worked by hammering the hot bloom rather than by pouring molten metal into moulds. The smith reheated the bloom many times in a forge and hammered it to drive out the slag and weld the iron into a solid piece, a skilled and exhausting task. Melting iron completely in a furnace became common much later, when blast furnaces could reach higher temperatures and the iron absorbed enough carbon to lower its melting point.

### subtopic_4 — Soap, Tanning and Other Traditional Reactions
**Assistant:** How does soap actually remove grease, when water alone just runs off a greasy plate?

**Tutor:** Because a soap particle is a long molecule with two very different ends, one that mixes with water and one that mixes with grease, so it can act as a bridge between them. Water molecules are attracted strongly to each other and to anything with electric charges, but grease and oil molecules have no charges and are not attracted to water, so water slides over grease without picking it up. That is why oil and water separate. A soap molecule has a charged head, which is strongly attracted to water, and a long tail of carbon and hydrogen atoms, which behaves like grease and dissolves in it. When you wash a greasy plate, the tails of millions of soap molecules bury themselves in the grease while their heads stay in the water. When you scrub, the grease breaks into tiny droplets, and each droplet becomes completely surrounded by soap molecules, tails pointing in and charged heads pointing out. Now the droplet's outer surface is covered in charged heads, so it is attracted to the water and the droplets also repel one another, because their surfaces carry the same charge. The droplets stay spread through the water instead of joining up again, and they are rinsed away. These little coated droplets are called micelles. Traditional soap makers did not know about molecules, but they knew that fat boiled with wood-ash lye, in the right proportions and for long enough, produced a substance that lathered and cleaned, and that too much lye made a soap that burned the skin. Modern detergents work in the same way, with molecules designed to work even in hard water.
