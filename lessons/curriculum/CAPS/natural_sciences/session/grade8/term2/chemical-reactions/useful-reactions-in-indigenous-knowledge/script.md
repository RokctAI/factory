# Part 1 — Expert

This session shows that chemical reactions were understood and used skilfully in southern Africa long before they were written as equations, and explains several of those traditional processes in terms of reactants, products and rearranged atoms. The anchor is a clay pot of umqombothi, the traditional sorghum and maize beer brewed in many Xhosa and Zulu homes for ceremonies and celebrations. Over a few days, a thick porridge of maize meal and sprouted sorghum, left in a warm place, begins to bubble, develops a sour smell and a thin layer of froth, and becomes a mildly alcoholic, creamy drink. Nothing was added during those days. The change was a chemical reaction carried out by living micro-organisms. The governing idea is that indigenous knowledge systems developed reliable methods for carrying out useful chemical reactions, such as fermentation, smelting and soap making, by careful observation and practice passed down through generations, and that the particle model and the idea of rearranged atoms explain why those methods work. Fermentation in brewing and food preservation, and at least one other traditional chemical process, explained with word equations and the signs of a reaction, are examinable, and they connect to microbiology and industrial chemistry in later grades.

## Subtopic: Fermentation in Brewing Umqombothi

Fermentation is a chemical reaction in which micro-organisms such as yeast or bacteria break down sugars, without using oxygen, to release energy for themselves, producing other substances as waste products. In alcoholic fermentation, carried out by yeast, the word equation is: glucose produces ethanol plus carbon dioxide. Ethanol is the alcohol in beer and wine, and carbon dioxide is the gas that makes the brew bubble and froth.

Brewing umqombothi involves two stages. First, the grain must be malted. Sorghum grains are soaked in water and left in a warm, damp place for a few days so that they begin to sprout. As they sprout, the grains produce substances called enzymes, which break down the starch stored in the grain into simple sugars. The sprouted grain is then dried and ground into sorghum malt. Starch on its own cannot be fermented by yeast; the malting step turns it into sugars that can.

Second, the malt is mixed with cooked maize meal porridge and water and left to stand. Natural yeasts and bacteria, carried on the grain, in the air and in the pot from earlier brews, begin to ferment the sugars. Many families now also add a little commercial brewer's yeast. Over two to three days, the signs of a chemical reaction appear: bubbles of carbon dioxide rise, the mixture becomes warm, the smell changes, and the taste becomes sour and slightly alcoholic. When the brewer judges by smell and taste that it is ready, it is strained through a woven grass strainer and served in a shared clay pot.

The sour taste comes from a second, simultaneous fermentation by lactic acid bacteria, which turn some of the sugars into lactic acid. The acidity helps to stop harmful bacteria from growing. Umqombothi is low in alcohol, usually only a few percent, and rich in carbohydrates and some vitamins from the grain, which is why it was historically regarded as food as much as drink.

The same yeast fermentation makes bread rise. Yeast mixed into dough ferments the sugars in the flour, and the carbon dioxide produced forms bubbles trapped in the dough, which expand in the oven; the small amount of ethanol evaporates during baking.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of fermentation, the word equation for alcoholic fermentation, why sorghum is malted first, the signs of a reaction during brewing, and how yeast makes bread rise.

## Subtopic: Amasi, Mageu and Lactic Acid Fermentation

Lactic acid fermentation is carried out by lactic acid bacteria. They break down sugars into lactic acid, without producing alcohol. The simplified word equation is: glucose produces lactic acid.

Amasi, also called maas, is soured, thickened milk, a traditional food across southern Africa. Traditionally, fresh milk was poured into a calabash or a clay pot, or a leather bag, and left for a few days. Lactic acid bacteria already present in the milk and in the container, kept from previous batches, ferment the milk sugar, lactose, into lactic acid. As the acid builds up, it causes the proteins in the milk to clump together and set, which thickens the milk into a soft curd with a pleasantly sour taste. The liquid whey was drained off through a small hole at the bottom of the calabash, leaving the thick amasi behind. Amasi is often eaten with crumbly maize-meal porridge, umphokoqo.

Mageu, also called amahewu, is a sour, non-alcoholic drink made from thin maize porridge. A small amount of wheat flour, or some mageu from a previous batch, is stirred into the cooled porridge and it is left to stand in a warm place for a day or two. Lactic acid bacteria ferment the sugars, the porridge becomes sour, and it can be drunk as a nourishing, filling drink. It is now also sold commercially in cartons in supermarkets.

These fermentations are also a method of preservation. The lactic acid lowers the pH, making the food acidic, and most of the bacteria that cause food poisoning and spoilage cannot grow in acidic conditions. In a hot climate without refrigerators, turning fresh milk into amasi kept its nutrients available for many more days. The bacteria also make some nutrients easier to digest, and people who struggle to digest lactose in fresh milk often tolerate amasi better, because much of the lactose has been converted.

The yoghurt you studied in Term 1 is made in exactly the same way, and so are sauerkraut and many kinds of pickles around the world.

The questions for this section are with you now: the word equation for lactic acid fermentation, how amasi thickens, how mageu is made, and why fermentation preserves food.

## Subtopic: Smelting Iron in Early Southern Africa

Farming communities in southern Africa have been smelting iron for over fifteen hundred years. Archaeologists have found remains of furnaces, slag and iron tools at many sites, including the Phalaborwa area in Limpopo, where copper and iron were mined and worked for well over a thousand years, and across KwaZulu-Natal, Mpumalanga and the Highveld. Smelting is a chemical reaction that extracts a metal from its ore, a rock containing a compound of the metal.

Iron ore contains iron oxide, a compound of iron and oxygen. To get iron, the oxygen must be removed. Traditional smelters built a furnace of clay, often about one to two metres high, and filled it with alternating layers of charcoal and crushed ore. Charcoal is almost pure carbon, made by heating wood with very little air. Clay pipes called tuyères led into the base of the furnace, and air was pumped in through them by bellows made from goatskin or animal hide, worked by hand for many hours. The forced air made the charcoal burn fiercely, reaching temperatures of well over one thousand degrees Celsius.

Two reactions happen in the furnace. First, the burning charcoal, with a limited supply of oxygen, forms carbon monoxide gas. Second, the hot carbon monoxide takes oxygen atoms away from the iron oxide, leaving iron behind. In words: iron oxide plus carbon monoxide produces iron plus carbon dioxide. The atoms are rearranged: oxygen atoms leave the iron and join the carbon. Unwanted rock in the ore melts and runs out as a glassy waste called slag. The iron collects at the bottom as a spongy lump, the bloom, which the smith reheated and hammered repeatedly to squeeze out the remaining slag and shape it into hoes, axes, spears and ornaments.

Iron smelting was specialist knowledge, often surrounded by ritual and restricted to certain families, and it depended on detailed skill: choosing the right ore, judging the right mixture of charcoal and ore, and controlling the air supply. Too much air and the charcoal burns to carbon dioxide too quickly; too little and the furnace is not hot enough. The same reaction is used today, on an enormous scale, in the blast furnaces of South African steelworks such as Vanderbijlpark, where coke replaces charcoal.

The questions for this section are with you now: what an ore is, the role of charcoal and bellows, the word equation for removing oxygen from iron oxide, and what slag and the bloom are.

## Subtopic: Soap, Tanning and Other Traditional Reactions

Soap making is another reaction long known in many parts of the world, including Africa. Animal fat or plant oil is boiled with lye, a strongly alkaline liquid traditionally made by soaking wood ash in water and draining the liquid off. The fat reacts with the alkali to form soap and glycerol: fat plus alkali produces soap plus glycerol. The soap particles have one end that is attracted to water and one end that is attracted to grease, so they can lift grease off skin and cloth into the washing water. In West Africa, black soap is still made this way from plantain skins, cocoa pods and palm oil. In southern Africa, people also used plants rich in natural soap-like substances, whose crushed roots or leaves contain natural soap-like substances that lather in water.

Tanning turns animal skins into leather that does not rot. Hides were treated with fat, worked and stretched to soften them, and often soaked with the crushed bark of trees rich in tannins, such as some acacias. The tannins react with the proteins in the skin, binding them together, so that bacteria can no longer break them down. The product, leather, is a new material with different properties from raw skin: it is soft, flexible, waterproof and long-lasting, used for clothing, shields, bags and the traditional milk bags used for amasi.

Other traditional processes are also chemical. Clay pots fired in an open bonfire are permanently hardened, because heating changes the minerals in the clay into a new, hard material that does not soften in water again, which is why fired pottery is so durable. Ochre is an iron oxide pigment; yellow ochre heated in a fire turns red, as its iron compound changes, and ochre has been used as paint for at least a hundred thousand years, as shown by the ochre workshop found at Blombos Cave on the southern Cape coast. Rooibos tea, from the Cederberg region, is cut, bruised and left in heaps in the sun, where reactions between natural compounds in the leaves and oxygen turn the green leaves red-brown and develop the flavour; the farmers call it fermentation, although it is mainly oxidation rather than the work of micro-organisms.

The error museum, four exhibits. One: saying fermentation needs oxygen; it happens without oxygen. Two: saying amasi is spoiled milk; it is milk deliberately soured by useful bacteria and preserved by the acid. Three: saying smelting simply melts iron out of rock; it is a chemical reaction that removes oxygen from iron oxide. Four: saying traditional methods are not science; they are based on careful observation, testing and improvement over generations.

Layout for marks: name the process, write the word equation, name the reactants and products, and give at least one sign that a reaction has occurred.

The questions for this section are with you now: the word equation for soap making, how tannins turn hide into leather, why firing clay is a chemical change, and the four exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again through a pot of umqombothi, a calabash of amasi, and a clay furnace roaring with bellows — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Pot of Umqombothi

At a family ceremony, a big clay pot of umqombothi is passed around. It is creamy, sour and fizzy. How is it made?

First, sorghum grains are soaked until they start to sprout. Sprouting turns the starch in the grain into sugar. The sprouted grain is dried and ground into malt.

Then the malt is mixed with maize porridge and water and left in a warm place for a couple of days. Tiny living things called yeasts get to work. They eat the sugar and turn it into alcohol and carbon dioxide gas. That is fermentation: sugar turning into alcohol and gas, made by yeast, with no oxygen needed.

You can see the clues of a chemical reaction: bubbles of gas, froth on top, a new smell, a sour taste. Some helpful bacteria also make a little acid, which gives the sour taste and keeps germs away.

Bread uses the same trick. Yeast in the dough makes carbon dioxide bubbles, and the dough puffs up.

Keep the pot of umqombothi in mind and try the questions for this part: what fermentation is, what yeast turns sugar into, and why the grain is sprouted first.

## Subtopic: A Calabash of Amasi

Pour fresh milk into a calabash and leave it for a few days. It turns thick and sour. That is amasi, eaten with crumbly porridge.

What happened? Helpful bacteria in the milk and the calabash ate the milk sugar and turned it into lactic acid. The acid made the milk proteins clump together, so the milk set thick. The watery whey was let out through a little hole at the bottom.

Mageu works the same way, with maize porridge instead of milk. The bacteria make it sour, and it becomes a filling drink.

Here is the clever part. The acid also stops harmful germs growing. So long before fridges, turning milk into amasi made it last much longer. It is not spoiled milk; it is milk changed on purpose by good bacteria.

Hold the calabash in mind and try this part's questions: what makes amasi thick and sour, and why it keeps longer than fresh milk.

## Subtopic: A Furnace, Fat and Ash

Over fifteen hundred years ago, people in southern Africa were already making iron. They built clay furnaces, filled them with charcoal and crushed iron ore, and pumped air in with goatskin bellows for hours, until the fire was hotter than a thousand degrees.

Iron ore is iron joined to oxygen. In the furnace, gas from the burning charcoal pulls the oxygen away from the iron. Iron is left behind as a spongy lump, which the smith hammered into hoes and spears. That is a chemical reaction: atoms rearranged, oxygen moving from the iron to the carbon.

Soap is another one. Boil fat with a liquid made from wood ash, and they react to make soap. Soak animal skin with tree bark, and the bark chemicals change the skin into leather that never rots.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Indigenous knowledge used chemical reactions skilfully. Yeast fermentation turns sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide, in umqombothi and bread. Lactic acid fermentation turns sugar into lactic acid, in amasi and mageu, and preserves food. Smelting removes oxygen from iron oxide using charcoal. Fat and wood-ash lye make soap, and bark tannins turn hide into leather.

Hold the pot, the calabash and the furnace, and take the final questions of the lesson: the fermentation word equation, how smelting works, and why traditional methods are real science.
