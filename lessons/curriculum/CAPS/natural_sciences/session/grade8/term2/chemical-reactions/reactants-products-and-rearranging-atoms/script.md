# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains what a chemical reaction is in terms of atoms and molecules: the starting substances are rearranged into new substances, with bonds broken and new bonds formed, and no atoms created or destroyed. The anchor is a gas stove. Methane gas flows from the burner, meets the oxygen in the air, and, once lit, burns with a steady blue flame. The gas disappears and the oxygen is used up, yet nothing vanishes: every carbon, hydrogen and oxygen atom that entered the flame leaves it again, now joined in different partners, as carbon dioxide and water vapour. The governing idea is that in a chemical reaction the atoms of the reactants are rearranged to form the products; the bonds holding the reactant particles together are broken and new bonds are formed in the products; and because the atoms are only rearranged, the number of each kind of atom and the total mass are the same before and after. Reactants and products, word equations, particle diagrams of reactions, bond breaking and bond forming, the signs of a chemical reaction and the conservation of atoms and mass are examinable, and they lead to balanced chemical equations in Grade 9 and stoichiometry in Grade 10 and 11 Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: Reactants and Products

A chemical reaction is a process in which one or more substances are changed into one or more new substances with different properties. The substances that are present at the start and are used up are called the reactants. The new substances that are formed are called the products.

A reaction is described by a word equation. The reactants are written on the left, joined by plus signs, then an arrow, which is read as produces or forms, then the products on the right, also joined by plus signs. For the gas stove: methane plus oxygen produces carbon dioxide plus water. For magnesium burning in air with a dazzling white light: magnesium plus oxygen produces magnesium oxide. For iron filings heated with sulfur powder: iron plus sulfur produces iron sulfide. For baking soda added to vinegar, which fizzes vigorously: sodium hydrogen carbonate plus acetic acid produces sodium acetate plus water plus carbon dioxide. The arrow is not an equals sign; it shows the direction of the change, from reactants to products.

The products of a reaction have different properties from the reactants. Magnesium is a shiny grey metal and oxygen a colourless gas; magnesium oxide is a white powder. Iron is grey and magnetic, sulfur is yellow and not magnetic; iron sulfide is a dark grey-black solid with properties different from both. Hydrogen is an explosive gas and oxygen supports burning, yet the water they form puts fires out.

A chemical reaction is different from a physical change. In a physical change, such as melting ice, dissolving sugar or crushing a rock, no new substance is formed; the particles are the same before and after, only their arrangement or spacing has changed. In a chemical change, new substances with new particles are formed. Chemical changes are often hard to reverse: you cannot easily turn ash and smoke back into wood, or a cooked egg back into a raw one.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of reactant and product, writing word equations, how products differ from reactants, and the difference between a chemical and a physical change.

## Subtopic: Rearranging Atoms

The particle model, together with the idea of atoms from earlier in this term, explains what happens during a reaction. Every substance is made of particles: single atoms, molecules of two or more atoms bonded together, or large lattices of atoms. In a chemical reaction, the atoms in the reactants separate from their old partners and join new partners, forming the particles of the products.

Take the burning of methane. A methane molecule is one carbon atom bonded to four hydrogen atoms. An oxygen molecule is two oxygen atoms bonded together. In the flame, one methane molecule reacts with two oxygen molecules. The atoms rearrange: the carbon atom joins with two oxygen atoms to make one carbon dioxide molecule, and the four hydrogen atoms join with the other two oxygen atoms to make two water molecules, each with two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom.

Count the atoms. Before the reaction: one carbon, four hydrogen and four oxygen atoms, in one methane molecule and two oxygen molecules. After the reaction: one carbon, four hydrogen and four oxygen atoms, in one carbon dioxide molecule and two water molecules. Every atom is still there. No atom has been created, none destroyed, and no atom of one element has turned into another element. Only the partnerships have changed.

The same counting works for any reaction. When hydrogen burns in oxygen, two hydrogen molecules and one oxygen molecule rearrange into two water molecules: four hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms before, four hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms after. When carbon burns in oxygen, one carbon atom and one oxygen molecule rearrange into one carbon dioxide molecule.

Reactions are often drawn as particle diagrams, with circles of different colours or sizes for different kinds of atoms, joined together to show molecules. A correct particle diagram of a reaction shows the same number of each kind of circle on both sides of the arrow, arranged into different groupings. Building the molecules with modelling clay balls and toothpicks or with a molecular model kit, and then pulling them apart and reassembling them into the products, is the clearest way to see that a reaction only rearranges atoms.

The questions for this section are with you now: what happens to atoms in a reaction, the rearrangement in the burning of methane and of hydrogen, counting atoms on both sides, and drawing a particle diagram of a reaction.

## Subtopic: Breaking and Forming Bonds

The atoms in a molecule or a lattice are held together by chemical bonds, strong forces of attraction between atoms. For atoms to rearrange in a reaction, some of the bonds in the reactants must first be broken, and then new bonds must form to make the products.

Breaking a bond requires energy, because the atoms are attracted to each other and must be pulled apart. Forming a bond releases energy, because the atoms move together under their attraction. Every chemical reaction therefore involves an energy change. Whether a reaction gives out or takes in energy overall depends on the balance between the energy needed to break the old bonds and the energy released when the new bonds form.

When the energy released in forming new bonds is greater than the energy needed to break the old ones, the reaction releases energy to the surroundings, usually as heat and often as light. Such reactions are called exothermic. Burning methane on a stove, burning wood in a braai, burning magnesium and the respiration of glucose in your cells are all exothermic: the bonds in carbon dioxide and water are stronger than the bonds in the fuel and oxygen, and the difference is released.

When more energy is needed to break the old bonds than is released in forming the new ones, the reaction takes in energy from its surroundings and the surroundings become colder. Such reactions are called endothermic. Photosynthesis, which takes in energy from sunlight, is endothermic, and so is the decomposition of limestone into lime and carbon dioxide in a kiln, which continues only while the limestone is strongly heated. Mixing citric acid with baking soda in water is an endothermic reaction you can feel: the fizzing mixture becomes noticeably colder.

Many reactions need a push to get started, even if they release energy overall. A gas stove does not light by itself: a spark or a match is needed to break the first bonds. Once the reaction starts, the energy released keeps breaking more bonds, and the flame keeps itself going. This starting energy is called the activation energy.

The questions for this section are with you now: why bonds must break and form, which of these needs energy and which releases it, the meaning of exothermic and endothermic with examples, and why a match is needed to light a stove.

## Subtopic: Signs of a Reaction and Conservation of Mass

Because new substances are formed, a chemical reaction usually produces observable changes. The common signs are: a gas is given off, seen as bubbles or fizzing, as when baking soda meets vinegar; a change in colour, as when shiny copper turns green over many years or a cut apple turns brown; a change in temperature, the mixture becoming hot or cold; light or a flame being given off; a precipitate, an insoluble solid that suddenly appears when two clear solutions are mixed; or a new smell. None of these signs alone proves a reaction, because a temperature change also happens when a substance dissolves and bubbles appear when water boils, so scientists look for evidence that a new substance with new properties has formed.

Because atoms are only rearranged, the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants. This is the law of conservation of mass, established by the French chemist Antoine Lavoisier in the 1780s through careful weighing of reactions in sealed vessels. Before Lavoisier, many people believed that burning destroyed matter, because a log seems to vanish into a small pile of ash. It does not: most of the mass of the wood, combined with oxygen from the air, leaves as invisible carbon dioxide gas and water vapour. If the gases could be captured and weighed together with the ash, the total would equal the mass of the wood plus the oxygen it used.

Conservation of mass can be tested in class. Place a small test tube of vinegar inside a plastic bottle containing baking soda, seal the bottle with its cap, and weigh it. Tip the bottle so that the vinegar and baking soda mix and fizz, then weigh again: the mass is unchanged, because the carbon dioxide produced is trapped inside. Repeat with an open bottle and the mass decreases, because the gas escapes into the air. The mass has not been destroyed; it has left the scale.

Steel wool heated in air gets heavier, not lighter, because iron atoms combine with oxygen atoms from the air to form iron oxide; the extra mass is the mass of the oxygen.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying atoms are destroyed when something burns; they are rearranged into gases. Two: saying the arrow in an equation means equals; it means produces. Three: saying breaking bonds releases energy; breaking bonds needs energy. Four: saying mass is lost when a gas is produced; in a sealed container it stays the same. Five: drawing particle diagrams with different numbers of atoms on each side.

Layout for marks: identify reactants and products, write the word equation, count atoms on both sides, and mention bonds broken and formed.

The questions for this section are with you now: the signs of a chemical reaction, the law of conservation of mass, the sealed and open bottle experiment, why heated steel wool gains mass, and the five exhibits.

## Subtopic: Building Blocks Taken Apart

Imagine a set of plastic building blocks. You build a little car. Then you take the car apart and use exactly the same blocks to build a little house. You did not buy new blocks or throw any away. You just rearranged them.

A chemical reaction is like that, with atoms instead of blocks. The substances you start with are called the reactants. The atoms in them come apart and join up in new ways, making new substances called the products. Same atoms, new arrangement, new substance.

We write it as a word equation, reactants on the left, an arrow, products on the right. Magnesium plus oxygen produces magnesium oxide. The arrow means produces, not equals.

The products can be totally different from the reactants. Grey shiny magnesium and invisible oxygen gas make a white powder. Explosive hydrogen and oxygen make water, which puts out fires.

That is the difference from a physical change. Melting ice is still water. But a reaction makes something new, and it is often hard to undo: you cannot unburn a match.

Keep the building blocks in mind and try the questions for this part: what reactants and products are, how to write a word equation, and how a reaction differs from melting.

## Subtopic: The Gas Stove

Turn on a gas stove and light it. The gas is methane. Each methane molecule is one carbon atom holding four hydrogen atoms. It meets oxygen from the air, each oxygen molecule a pair of oxygen atoms.

In the flame, the old bonds snap and the atoms swap partners. The carbon grabs two oxygens and becomes carbon dioxide. The four hydrogens grab the other two oxygens and become two water molecules. Count them: one carbon, four hydrogens, four oxygens before, and exactly the same after.

Breaking bonds takes energy. That is why the stove needs a spark or a match to start. Making new bonds gives energy out. In burning, the new bonds give out more than the old ones took, so you get heat and a flame. Reactions that give out heat are called exothermic. Reactions that take in heat and feel cold are called endothermic.

Hold the gas stove in mind and try this part's questions: counting the atoms in burning methane, why a match is needed, and what exothermic means.

## Subtopic: The Sealed Bottle

How can you tell a reaction has happened? Look for clues: bubbles of gas, a colour change, the mix getting hot or cold, light or flame, a new smell, or a solid suddenly appearing in a clear liquid.

Now a test. Put baking soda in a bottle, stand a little tube of vinegar inside it, screw the lid on tight and weigh it. Tip it so they mix. Fizz! Weigh it again. Exactly the same mass. The carbon dioxide gas is trapped inside, and no atoms were lost.

Do the same with the lid off and the mass goes down, because the gas escapes into the air. Not destroyed, just gone off the scale.

That is the law of conservation of mass: in a reaction, the mass of the products equals the mass of the reactants, because atoms are only rearranged.

Pocket summary of the lesson. In a chemical reaction, reactants change into new products with different properties. The atoms are rearranged: old bonds break, which needs energy, and new bonds form, which releases energy. No atoms are created or destroyed, so the number of each kind of atom and the total mass stay the same. Signs of a reaction include gas, colour change, temperature change, light and a precipitate.

Hold the blocks, the stove and the sealed bottle, and take the final questions of the lesson: writing a word equation, counting atoms, and explaining why the sealed bottle keeps its mass.
