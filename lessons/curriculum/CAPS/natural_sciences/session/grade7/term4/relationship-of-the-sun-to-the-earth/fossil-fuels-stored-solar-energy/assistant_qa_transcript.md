### subtopic_1 — How Coal Formed
**Assistant:** How do we know coal really came from plants that lived millions of years ago?

**Tutor:** Because coal is full of evidence: fossils of plant parts, the chemical make-up of plant material, and the layers of rock around it, all of which tell the same story. First, the fossils. Split a piece of coal or the shale above a coal seam, and you often find perfect imprints of leaves, stems, bark and even seeds and spores. In South African coalfields, the most famous fossil is Glossopteris, a tree with tongue-shaped leaves. The same Glossopteris fossils are found in coal in India, South America, Australia and Antarctica, which helped scientists show that these continents were once joined together as Gondwana. Under a microscope, thin slices of coal show plant cells, woody tissues and spores. Second, the chemistry: coal is made mostly of carbon, with hydrogen, oxygen and some nitrogen and sulfur, the same elements as plant material, in proportions that change gradually from peat through lignite to anthracite, matching what heat and pressure do. Third, the rock layers: coal seams are sandwiched between sandstones and mudstones that show signs of river channels and swamps, such as ripple marks and roots preserved in their original growing position. Scientists can date the layers using radioactive minerals in volcanic ash beds nearby, which show the coal is roughly 250 to 300 million years old. Peat bogs forming today show the first stage of the process happening before our eyes.

### subtopic_2 — How Oil and Natural Gas Formed
**Assistant:** Why are oil and gas found under the sea and in deserts if they formed from sea creatures?

**Tutor:** Because oil and gas formed in ancient seas that existed millions of years ago, and since then the Earth's surface has changed dramatically, with seas becoming land and land becoming sea. The Earth's crust is made of plates that move slowly, a few centimetres a year. Over tens and hundreds of millions of years, this moves continents thousands of kilometres, opens and closes oceans, and lifts sea floors up to become land. Sea levels also rise and fall as the climate changes. So a shallow sea full of plankton a hundred million years ago might be desert today, as in the Middle East, where the Arabian Peninsula was once covered by a warm, shallow sea teeming with life. The remains buried there became some of the largest oil fields on Earth. Other ancient sea floors are now under the continental shelves around today's coasts, like the gas fields off Mossel Bay or the oil fields under the North Sea and off the coast of Angola, so oil companies drill from platforms anchored in the ocean. Oil and gas are found in particular places only where the right conditions all came together: plenty of buried organisms, the right amount of heat to form oil, porous rock for it to move through, and a cap of solid rock to trap it. If any one is missing, there is no oil field.

### subtopic_3 — Stored Solar Energy and Non-Renewable Resources
**Assistant:** If fossil fuels are still forming today, why are they called non-renewable?

**Tutor:** Because they form millions of times more slowly than we use them, so for all practical purposes, once they are burned, they are gone. It is true that the processes that made coal, oil and gas have not stopped. Peat is building up in wetlands in South Africa and around the world, and plankton remains are settling into sea-floor mud right now. Given enough burial, heat, pressure and time, some of that material may become coal or oil. But the timescale is enormous. A one-metre seam of coal may represent thousands of years of peat accumulation, followed by millions of years of burial and slow change. Oil fields being drilled today took tens to hundreds of millions of years to form. Humans, by contrast, have burned a large share of the world's easily reached oil in a little over a century and a half. South Africa's power stations together burn on the order of a hundred million tonnes of coal or more each year. Nature might replace a tiny fraction of that over a million years. A resource is called renewable only if it is replaced at about the rate we use it, within a human lifetime: sunlight arrives fresh every day, wind blows every year, and replanted trees regrow in decades. Measured against human lifetimes, fossil fuels are a one-time gift of ancient sunlight that we are spending very quickly.
