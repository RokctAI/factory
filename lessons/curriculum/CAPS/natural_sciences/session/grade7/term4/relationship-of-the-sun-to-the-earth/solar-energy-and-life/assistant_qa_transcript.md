### subtopic_1 — The Sun as Earth's Energy Source
**Assistant:** Are there any living things on Earth that do not depend on the Sun at all?

**Tutor:** Yes, a few remarkable communities live without sunlight, but they are rare exceptions, and almost every other living thing depends on the Sun. In 1977, scientists exploring the deep ocean floor in a submarine discovered hydrothermal vents: cracks in the seabed, more than two kilometres down, where water heated by volcanic rock gushes out, full of dissolved minerals and a gas called hydrogen sulfide. No sunlight reaches these depths; it is pitch dark. Yet around the vents they found dense communities of giant tube worms over two metres long, clams, crabs, shrimp and fish. The food producers here are not plants but bacteria that get energy from chemical reactions with the hydrogen sulfide, a process called chemosynthesis. The tube worms have no mouth or gut; they house billions of these bacteria inside their bodies and absorb food made by them. Similar bacteria live deep underground, including in rock kilometres below the surface in South African gold mines, where scientists have found microbes powered by chemicals produced by radioactive rock. These discoveries are exciting because they suggest life might exist on moons such as Europa or Enceladus, which have hidden oceans beneath ice. But for everything we see around us, from mealies to hawks to people, the Sun is the source of energy, captured by plants and passed along.

### subtopic_2 — Plants Capture Solar Energy
**Assistant:** Why are plants green if they need to absorb as much sunlight as possible?

**Tutor:** Because chlorophyll absorbs mainly the red and blue parts of sunlight and reflects much of the green, and the reflected green light is what reaches our eyes. Sunlight looks white, but it is a mixture of all the colours of the rainbow, as you see when light passes through a prism or raindrops. Different substances absorb different colours. Chlorophyll is very good at absorbing violet-blue light and red light, using their energy to power photosynthesis. It absorbs much less green light, reflecting or transmitting it, so leaves look green. Scientists still debate why plants evolved this way, since green light is plentiful in sunlight. One idea is that the earliest photosynthesising organisms in the ocean were purple microbes that absorbed green light, so later organisms evolved chlorophyll to use the leftover red and blue. Another is that absorbing all the light would overheat or damage the leaf, so reflecting some is safer. Leaves also contain other pigments, yellow and orange carotenoids, that absorb extra light and protect the chlorophyll. In autumn, when deciduous trees stop making chlorophyll and break it down, these yellow and orange pigments show through, which is why leaves change colour. Some plants and seaweeds that look red or brown have extra pigments that help them catch the light that reaches them in shade or deep water.

### subtopic_3 — Solar Energy Through Food Chains and Webs
**Assistant:** Why are big fierce predators like lions so rare compared with antelope and grass?

**Tutor:** Because so much energy is lost at each step in a food chain that there is only enough left at the top to support a small number of large predators. Start with the grass on a savanna. Of all the sunlight falling on the grass, plants capture only around one percent in photosynthesis. When an antelope eats grass, it cannot digest all of it, much passes out as dung, and most of the energy it does absorb is used for its own life processes: walking, breathing, keeping warm, and running from danger. That energy ends up as heat. Only about a tenth of the energy in the grass eaten is built into the antelope's body. When a lion eats the antelope, the same thing happens again: only about a tenth is built into the lion. So each lion needs a huge number of antelope over its lifetime, and those antelope need a vast area of grass. In the Kruger National Park, there are hundreds of thousands of impala, but only around a couple of thousand lions. Drawn as a pyramid, with producers at the bottom and top predators at the tip, the numbers and total mass get smaller at each level. This is also why eating plant foods directly can feed more people from the same farmland than feeding the plants to animals first.
