# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains how coal, oil and natural gas formed, why they are called stored solar energy, and why they are non-renewable resources. The anchor is the coalfields of Mpumalanga, near eMalahleni, where coal is mined and burned in power stations that supply most of South Africa's electricity, and the gas fields off the coast near Mossel Bay. The governing idea is that fossil fuels formed over millions of years from the remains of plants and tiny organisms that had stored solar energy, buried and changed by heat and pressure, and because they take so long to form, they cannot be replaced once used. ## Subtopic: How Coal Formed

Coal is a black or brown rock that burns, formed from the remains of plants that lived in swamps hundreds of millions of years ago.

In South Africa, most coal formed about 250 to 300 million years ago, when the land was part of the supercontinent Gondwana, and vast swampy forests of ferns, horsetails and seed ferns such as Glossopteris grew in a cool, wet climate. These plants had captured solar energy in photosynthesis and stored it as chemical energy in their stems and leaves.

Step one: plants died and fell into the waterlogged swamp. In the swamp water, there was little oxygen, so the plant material did not rot away completely. It built up in thick layers called peat, a spongy brown material that is still forming in some wetlands today.

Step two: over time, rivers and seas covered the peat with layers of sand and mud. As more layers piled up, the peat was buried deeper.

Step three: the weight of the overlying layers squeezed the peat with enormous pressure, and the heat deep underground slowly drove out water and other substances. Over millions of years, the peat changed into lignite, brown coal, then into bituminous coal, the black coal mined in Mpumalanga, and with even more heat and pressure, into anthracite, hard shiny coal.

The coal formed seams, layers between other rocks, which are mined today in open-cast pits or underground. Fossils of Glossopteris leaves are often found in South African coal mines.

It takes a layer of plant material many metres thick to make a seam of coal one metre thick.

South Africa's coal is mined mainly in Mpumalanga, with smaller fields in Limpopo, KwaZulu-Natal and the Free State. Open-cast mines strip away the soil and rock above shallow seams and dig the coal out with huge machines, while underground mines reach deeper seams through shafts and tunnels. Coal mining employs many thousands of workers, but it is dangerous work, with risks of rock falls, explosions of methane gas and lung disease from coal dust.

## Subtopic: How Oil and Natural Gas Formed

Crude oil and natural gas formed mostly from the remains of tiny organisms that lived in ancient seas, especially plankton: microscopic algae, which photosynthesised, and tiny animals that ate them.

Step one: when these organisms died, they sank to the sea floor and were buried in mud, where there was little oxygen, so they did not rot completely.

Step two: layers of sediment piled on top, burying the remains deeper and deeper over millions of years.

Step three: heat and pressure deep underground changed the remains into crude oil, a thick black liquid, and natural gas, mostly methane. Deeper and hotter conditions produced more gas.

Step four: oil and gas are lighter than water, so they slowly moved upward through porous rocks, like sandstone, until trapped beneath a layer of non-porous rock, such as shale, that they could not pass through. These underground traps are where oil and gas collect, and they are found by geologists and drilled into.

South Africa has little crude oil, but natural gas is found offshore near Mossel Bay, where it was used to make liquid fuels, and more has been discovered off the south and west coasts. South Africa also makes liquid fuels from coal at Secunda, using a process developed in the country.

Crude oil is separated at refineries into petrol, diesel, paraffin, jet fuel, lubricating oil, and bitumen for roads; it is also the raw material for most plastics.

## Subtopic: Stored Solar Energy and Non-Renewable Resources

Fossil fuels are called stored solar energy because the energy in them came from the Sun. Ancient plants and algae captured sunlight in photosynthesis and stored it as chemical energy. When they were buried and changed into coal, oil and gas, that chemical energy was preserved. When we burn fossil fuels today, we release sunlight that was captured millions of years ago.

They are called fossil fuels because they formed from the remains of ancient living things, like fossils.

Fossil fuels are non-renewable resources. A non-renewable resource cannot be replaced in a human lifetime once it is used. Coal took hundreds of millions of years to form, but a power station burns thousands of tonnes every day. At present rates, South Africa's coal reserves may last for some decades to around a century or so, and world oil reserves are also limited.

Renewable resources, by contrast, are replaced naturally and quickly: sunlight, wind, flowing water, and plants and wood, if forests are replanted.

Burning fossil fuels also causes problems: it releases carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas that traps heat and is causing climate change; sulfur dioxide, which causes acid rain; and smoke and ash, which harm people's lungs. Mining scars the land and can pollute water.

That is why South Africa is building more solar and wind farms, such as the solar plants in the Northern Cape and wind farms in the Eastern Cape, and why saving energy matters.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying coal formed from dinosaurs. Two: thinking fossil fuels will form again soon. Three: forgetting that their energy first came from the Sun. Four: saying oil formed from swamp trees; coal did, oil formed mostly from plankton. Five: thinking renewable means unlimited in every way; wood is renewable only if trees are replanted.

Many countries have agreed to reduce their use of fossil fuels to slow climate change. South Africa plans to close its oldest coal power stations over the coming decades, replacing them with solar, wind and other cleaner sources, while helping coal-mining communities find new kinds of work. This change, sometimes called a just energy transition, is one of the biggest challenges the country faces, because so many jobs and so much electricity still depend on coal.

## Subtopic: Coal From Old Swamps

Long, long ago, about 300 million years ago, South Africa was covered in swampy forests.

The plants caught sunlight and stored its energy. When they died, they fell into the swamp water, where they did not rot away. They piled up as soft brown peat.

Sand and mud buried the peat. More and more layers pressed down. Deep underground, heat and squeezing slowly turned the peat into coal over millions of years.

That is the coal mined in Mpumalanga today.

## Subtopic: Oil and Gas From Old Seas

Oil and gas came from tiny living things in old seas, too small to see.

When they died, they sank into the mud on the sea floor. Mud piled on top. Heat and squeezing deep underground turned them into oil and gas.

Oil and gas are lighter than water, so they crept upward through rocks until they got stuck under a rock they could not pass. That is where we drill for them.

From oil we make petrol, diesel, paraffin and plastics.

## Subtopic: Old Sunshine That Runs Out

The energy in coal, oil and gas first came from the Sun, caught by ancient plants. When we burn them, we let out sunshine from millions of years ago.

But they took millions of years to make, and we burn them very fast. Once used, they are gone. That is why they are called non-renewable.

Burning them also makes smoke and carbon dioxide, which is warming the planet.

Sun, wind and water are renewable: they keep coming.

They are non-renewable because they take millions of years to form, and burning them releases carbon dioxide and pollution.
