# Part 1 — Expert

This session follows the Sun's energy as it powers life on Earth. The anchor is a mealie field in the Free State on a summer morning: green leaves soaking up sunlight, cattle grazing at the field's edge, a farmer eating pap for breakfast, and a hawk circling above. The governing idea is that solar energy sustains almost all life on Earth because green plants capture it in photosynthesis and store it as chemical energy in food, which then passes along food chains to animals, including people. Explaining how plants capture solar energy, tracing it through food chains and webs, and describing why the Sun is essential for life are examinable.

## Subtopic: The Sun as Earth's Energy Source

The Sun is a star, a huge ball of extremely hot gases about 150 million kilometres from Earth. Deep in its core, nuclear reactions release vast amounts of energy, which travels out into space as radiation, including visible light, infrared radiation, which we feel as heat, and ultraviolet radiation.

Only a tiny fraction of the Sun's output reaches Earth, but it is enormous: the sunlight falling on the Earth in about one hour carries more energy than all the people on Earth use in a whole year.

Solar energy does many jobs that make life possible. It warms the land, seas and air, keeping most of the Earth's surface within a temperature range where liquid water can exist. It drives the water cycle, evaporating water from oceans and lakes, which later falls as rain on land. It causes winds, by heating the Earth unevenly, which spread heat and moisture around the planet. And, most importantly for living things, it provides the light that plants need to make food.

Without the Sun, the Earth would be a frozen, dark rock with a surface far below minus two hundred degrees Celsius, and almost no life could exist.

Some solar energy is reflected straight back into space by clouds, ice and light-coloured surfaces; some is absorbed by the atmosphere; and about half reaches and warms the surface.

People have used the Sun's energy directly for thousands of years: drying crops, meat such as biltong, fish and clothes in the sun, and harvesting salt by letting seawater evaporate in shallow pans, as is done along the West Coast. Today, solar panels change sunlight directly into electricity, and solar water heaters use it to heat water. Solar farms in the Northern Cape, one of the sunniest regions on Earth, now feed electricity into the national grid.

The questions for this section are with you now: what the Sun is, the forms of solar energy, and the jobs solar energy does on Earth.

## Subtopic: Plants Capture Solar Energy

Green plants are producers: they make their own food using energy from sunlight, in a process called photosynthesis.

Photosynthesis happens mainly in the leaves, in tiny structures called chloroplasts, which contain the green pigment chlorophyll. Chlorophyll absorbs light energy from the Sun.

The plant takes in carbon dioxide from the air through tiny pores in its leaves, called stomata, and water from the soil through its roots. Using the captured light energy, it combines carbon dioxide and water to make glucose, a sugar, and releases oxygen as a by-product.

The word equation is: carbon dioxide plus water, with light energy and chlorophyll, gives glucose plus oxygen.

The plant uses some glucose for its own life processes, releasing energy through respiration, and changes the rest into starch for storage, into cellulose to build cell walls, and into proteins and oils. In this way, the Sun's light energy is stored as chemical potential energy in the plant's body: in mealie grains, sugar-cane stems, potato tubers, fruit, seeds and leaves.

Photosynthesis also supplies almost all the oxygen in the air that animals and people breathe, and removes carbon dioxide.

Algae in the oceans and lakes, tiny floating organisms called phytoplankton, also photosynthesise, and they produce about half of the world's oxygen.

A simple investigation shows that plants need light to make starch. Cover part of a leaf on a potted plant with foil, leave the plant in sunlight for a day or two, then remove the leaf, soften it in boiling water, remove the green colour by warming it in alcohol in a water bath, and add iodine solution. The parts that received light turn blue-black, showing that starch is present, while the covered part stays brown, showing that no starch was made without light.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of producer, the raw materials and products of photosynthesis, and how plants store solar energy.

## Subtopic: Solar Energy Through Food Chains and Webs

Animals cannot make their own food. They are consumers, and they get their energy by eating plants or other animals.

A food chain shows how energy passes from one organism to the next. The arrows point in the direction energy flows, from the food to the eater. In the mealie field: sun, then mealie plant, then grasshopper, then frog, then snake, then hawk. Or: grass, then cow, then person.

Herbivores, such as cattle, grasshoppers and springbok, eat plants. Carnivores, such as hawks, lions and frogs, eat animals. Omnivores, such as people, baboons and pigs, eat both. Decomposers, such as bacteria and fungi, break down dead plants and animals and waste, releasing nutrients back into the soil.

Most animals eat more than one kind of food, so food chains link together to form a food web.

At each step of a food chain, only about a tenth of the energy is passed on to the next level. The rest is used by the organism for its life processes and lost as heat, or remains in parts that are not eaten. That is why food chains rarely have more than four or five links, and why there are far fewer hawks than grasshoppers.

Every food chain begins with a producer, and almost every producer depends on the Sun. So when you eat pap, you are eating solar energy captured by a mealie plant; when you eat beef, you are eating solar energy that passed from grass through a cow.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying plants get their food from the soil; they make it from air and water using light. Two: drawing food chain arrows from eater to food. Three: forgetting the Sun at the start of the chain. Four: thinking all the energy passes along the chain. Five: saying plants do not respire.

Decomposers complete the cycle. When plants and animals die, or drop leaves and dung, bacteria and fungi break them down, using the remaining stored energy for their own life processes and releasing nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus back into the soil. Plants absorb these nutrients through their roots to grow again. Energy flows through an ecosystem in one direction, from the Sun and eventually out as heat, but the nutrients are recycled again and again.

The questions for this section are with you now: food chains and webs, types of consumers, and why energy decreases along a food chain.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a mealie field and a hungry hawk — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Sun Powers Everything

The Sun is a giant star, very far away, that sends out light and heat.

It warms the Earth so water can stay liquid. It lifts water from the sea to make rain. It makes winds blow.

And it gives plants the light they need to make food.

Without the Sun, the Earth would be frozen and dark, and almost nothing could live.

Keep the warm Sun in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: Plants Catch Sunlight

Green leaves are like solar panels. The green stuff inside, chlorophyll, catches sunlight.

The plant takes in carbon dioxide from the air and water from the soil. Using the sunlight, it turns them into sugar, its food, and gives out oxygen, which we breathe.

The plant stores the food in its grains, stems, roots and fruit: mealies, sugar cane, potatoes. That stored food holds the Sun's energy.

Hold the mealie leaf in mind and try this part's questions.

## Subtopic: Passing the Energy On

Animals cannot make their own food. They eat plants, or eat animals that ate plants.

Sun, mealie, grasshopper, frog, snake, hawk: that is a food chain. The arrows show which way the energy goes.

At each step, most energy is used up or lost as heat. Only about a tenth passes on. That is why there are lots of grasshoppers but only a few hawks.

When you eat pap, you are eating sunshine that a mealie caught!

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Sun sends out light and heat that warm the Earth, drive rain and winds, and let plants make food. In photosynthesis, chlorophyll captures sunlight to turn carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen, storing solar energy as chemical energy. That energy passes along food chains and webs to herbivores, carnivores and omnivores, with only about a tenth passed on at each step.
