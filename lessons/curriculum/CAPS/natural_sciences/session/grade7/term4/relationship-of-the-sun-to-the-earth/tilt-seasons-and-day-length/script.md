# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains why South Africa has hot summers and cold winters, and why winter days are short and summer days long. The anchor is a learner in Pretoria who notices that in December it is still light at seven in the evening, while in June it is dark before half past five, and that the midday Sun is almost overhead in summer but low in the north in winter. The governing idea is that the Earth spins on an axis tilted at 23,5 degrees, and as it orbits the Sun once a year, the tilt changes the angle and intensity of sunlight reaching each hemisphere and the number of hours of daylight, producing the four seasons. Describing the tilt, explaining how it causes seasons through the intensity of solar energy, and explaining changing day length are examinable.

## Subtopic: The Earth's Tilted Axis and Orbit

The Earth makes two important movements. It rotates, or spins, on its axis, an imaginary line through the North and South Poles, once every 24 hours, which gives us day and night. It also revolves, or orbits, around the Sun once every 365 and a quarter days, which is one year.

The Earth's axis is not upright compared with its path around the Sun. It is tilted at an angle of 23,5 degrees. Importantly, the axis always points in the same direction in space, towards the North Star, all year round.

This means that during part of the year, the Southern Hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun, and during the opposite part of the year, it is tilted away from the Sun. When the Southern Hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun, around December, the Northern Hemisphere is tilted away, and the reverse is true around June.

The tilt also creates special lines of latitude. The Tropic of Capricorn, at 23,5 degrees south, passes just north of Pretoria through Limpopo; on about 21 December, the midday Sun is directly overhead there. The Tropic of Cancer, at 23,5 degrees north, has the Sun overhead around 21 June. The equator lies between them.

A model helps: shine a torch or lamp in the middle of a darkened room as the Sun, and carry a globe tilted at 23,5 degrees in a circle around it, keeping the axis pointing at the same wall. You will see the southern half lit more directly on one side of the circle and the northern half on the other.

Rotation also explains why the Sun appears to rise in the east and set in the west. The Earth spins from west to east, so as your part of the Earth turns towards the Sun, the Sun seems to appear over the eastern horizon; as it turns away, the Sun seems to sink in the west. The Sun is not really moving across the sky; we are.

The questions for this section are with you now: rotation and revolution, the 23,5-degree tilt, and why the hemispheres take turns facing the Sun.

## Subtopic: Tilt, Solar Intensity and the Four Seasons

Seasons are caused by the tilt, not by the Earth being closer to or further from the Sun. In fact, the Earth is closest to the Sun in early January, during South Africa's summer, but during the northern winter.

When the Southern Hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun, the midday Sun is high in our sky, and its rays strike the ground nearly straight down. The energy in each beam is concentrated on a small area, so the intensity of solar energy is high, and the ground heats strongly. This is summer.

When the Southern Hemisphere is tilted away, the Sun is low in our sky, and its rays strike the ground at a slanting angle. The same beam of energy is spread over a larger area, so the intensity is lower, and the ground heats less. Slanting rays also pass through more atmosphere, which absorbs and scatters some energy. This is winter.

You can show this with a torch: shine it straight down on a sheet of paper and you see a small, bright circle. Tilt it, and the patch becomes a larger, dimmer oval: the same energy spread more thinly.

In between, in autumn and spring, neither hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun; the Sun is overhead at the equator around 21 March and 23 September, the equinoxes.

The four seasons in South Africa: summer, December to February; autumn, March to May; winter, June to August; spring, September to November. When it is summer in South Africa, it is winter in Europe, which is why people there celebrate Christmas in the snow.

The questions for this section are with you now: how tilt changes the angle and intensity of sunlight, the torch demonstration, and the order and dates of the seasons.

## Subtopic: Day Length Varies with the Seasons

The tilt also changes how many hours of daylight we get.

When the Southern Hemisphere is tilted towards the Sun, a place like Pretoria spends more than half of each rotation in the lit half of the Earth, so days are longer than nights. On about 21 December, the summer solstice, we have the longest day of the year: about 13 hours 40 minutes of daylight in Pretoria and over 14 hours in Cape Town.

When the Southern Hemisphere is tilted away, a place spends less than half of each rotation in sunlight, so days are shorter than nights. On about 21 June, the winter solstice, we have the shortest day: about 10 hours 30 minutes in Pretoria and under 10 hours in Cape Town.

At the equinoxes in March and September, day and night are roughly equal everywhere, about 12 hours each.

Day length changes more the further a place is from the equator. At the equator, days are about 12 hours all year. Cape Town's day length changes more than Pretoria's. Near the poles, the effect is extreme: in summer the Sun does not set at all for weeks or months, the midnight sun, and in winter it does not rise at all.

Longer days add to summer heating: the Sun shines for more hours and the ground has less night time to cool. Short winter days with long, clear nights let the ground lose heat by radiation, which is why Highveld winter mornings can be frosty.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying seasons happen because the Earth is closer to the Sun in summer. Two: thinking the axis tilts back and forth during the year. Three: forgetting that the hemispheres have opposite seasons. Four: thinking day and night are always 12 hours each. Five: saying the Sun is directly overhead everywhere at noon.

The questions for this section are with you now: why day length changes, the solstices and equinoxes, and how latitude affects day length.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a globe, a torch and a Pretoria sunset — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Leaning Planet

The Earth spins like a top, once every 24 hours: that gives us day and night. It also travels around the Sun, once a year.

But the Earth leans. Its spinning line, from the North Pole to the South Pole, is tipped over by 23,5 degrees. And it always leans the same way, all year.

So for part of the year, our southern half leans towards the Sun. Half a year later, when the Earth is on the other side of the Sun, our half leans away.

Keep the leaning globe in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: Summer and Winter

When our half leans towards the Sun, the midday Sun is high. Its light hits the ground straight on, packed into a small area, so it heats strongly. That is summer.

When our half leans away, the Sun is low. Its light hits the ground at a slant and is spread out, so it heats less. That is winter.

Try it with a torch: straight down makes a small bright spot; tilted makes a big dim patch.

It is not about being closer to the Sun! When we have summer, Europe has winter.

Hold the torch in mind and try this part's questions.

## Subtopic: Long Days, Short Days

In summer, our half leans towards the Sun, so we spend more of each spin in sunlight. Days are long. In Pretoria, it is still light at seven in the evening in December.

In winter, days are short. It gets dark before half past five in June.

In March and September, day and night are about equal: 12 hours each.

The further from the equator, the bigger the change. Cape Town has longer summer days and shorter winter days than Pretoria.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Earth spins every 24 hours and orbits the Sun every year, with its axis tilted at 23,5 degrees, always pointing the same way. When a hemisphere tilts towards the Sun, the Sun is high, its energy is concentrated and days are long: summer. When it tilts away, the Sun is low, its energy is spread out and days are short: winter. Seasons are opposite in the two hemispheres.

Hold the globe, the torch and the Pretoria sunset, and take the final questions of the lesson.
