### subtopic_1 — The Earth's Tilted Axis and Orbit
**Assistant:** If the axis is tilted, why does it keep pointing the same way instead of wobbling around?

**Tutor:** Because the spinning Earth behaves like a giant spinning top or gyroscope, and a spinning object resists any change to the direction of its axis. Think of a bicycle wheel spinning fast: if you hold it by its axle and try to tilt it, you feel it pushing back, and a spinning top stays upright while a still one falls over. The Earth spins once a day, and because it is so massive, its spin carries an enormous amount of rotational energy and momentum. Nothing in space pushes on it hard enough to swing its axis around quickly, so as the Earth travels around the Sun, its axis stays pointing towards the same spot in the sky, near the star Polaris in the northern sky. That is why the hemispheres take turns leaning towards the Sun: the axis does not turn to face the Sun; the Earth simply moves to the other side of its orbit. The axis does wobble very slowly. The pull of the Sun and Moon on the slightly bulging equator makes the axis trace a slow circle in the sky, like the wobble of a slowing top, but one wobble takes about 26 000 years. Over thousands of years, the North Star will change. The angle of tilt itself also varies slightly, between about 22 and 24,5 degrees, over about 41 000 years, which scientists think helped cause ice ages in the distant past.

### subtopic_2 — Tilt, Solar Intensity and the Four Seasons
**Assistant:** If the Earth is closest to the Sun in January, why is it winter in Europe then?

**Tutor:** Because the distance between the Earth and the Sun changes only slightly during the year, and that small change matters far less than the tilt, which decides how directly sunlight hits each hemisphere. The Earth's orbit is very nearly a circle. At its closest point, in early January, the Earth is about 147 million kilometres from the Sun; at its furthest, in early July, about 152 million kilometres. That is a difference of only about three percent, which changes the total sunlight reaching the Earth by about seven percent. In January, the Northern Hemisphere is tilted away from the Sun. In London, the midday Sun climbs only about fifteen degrees above the horizon, so its rays arrive at a very shallow, slanting angle, spreading their energy thinly over the ground, and daylight lasts only about eight hours. Those effects together reduce the solar energy reaching each square metre in London by far more than seven percent, so it is cold, despite the closer Sun. Meanwhile, in Pretoria, tilted towards the Sun, the midday Sun is nearly overhead and days are long, so it is summer. If distance caused the seasons, both hemispheres would have summer at the same time, which they do not. The opposite seasons in the two hemispheres are the clearest evidence that tilt, not distance, is the cause.

### subtopic_3 — Day Length Varies with the Seasons
**Assistant:** Why does Cape Town have longer summer days than Pretoria if both are in South Africa?

**Tutor:** Because Cape Town is further from the equator than Pretoria, and the further a place is from the equator, the more the tilt of the Earth's axis changes its day length during the year. Imagine the Earth tilted with the Southern Hemisphere towards the Sun in December. The boundary between day and night, a line circling the Earth, is slanted compared with the lines of latitude. Near the equator, each place still spends about half of its daily rotation on the lit side. But as you move south, towards the South Pole, the slant means that a larger and larger part of each latitude circle lies in sunlight. Pretoria, at about 26 degrees south, spends roughly 13 hours 40 minutes in daylight on the longest day. Cape Town, at about 34 degrees south, spends over 14 hours 20 minutes, and it stays light until well after eight in the evening. Further south, in Antarctica, beyond 66,5 degrees south, the Antarctic Circle, the whole circle lies in sunlight, and the Sun never sets. In June, everything reverses: Cape Town's shortest day is only about 9 hours 50 minutes, shorter than Pretoria's 10 hours 30 minutes. So people in Cape Town enjoy long summer evenings but very short winter days, while people living near the equator, in Kenya or Uganda, have nearly twelve-hour days all year and hardly notice any change.
