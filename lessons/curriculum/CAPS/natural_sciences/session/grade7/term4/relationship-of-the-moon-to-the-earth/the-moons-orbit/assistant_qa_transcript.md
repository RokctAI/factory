### subtopic_1 — The Moon, Earth's Natural Satellite
**Assistant:** Why does the Moon have so many craters when the Earth has very few?

**Tutor:** Both have been struck by many meteorites over billions of years, but the Earth has ways of hiding and erasing craters that the Moon lacks. First, the Earth has an atmosphere. Most small rocks from space burn up from friction with the air before reaching the ground, which we see as shooting stars. The Moon has no air, so every rock, however small, hits the surface at full speed and makes a crater. Second, the Earth's surface is constantly being changed. Wind and rain wear down crater rims; rivers wash sediment into them; plants cover them; oceans hide craters on the sea floor; and the moving plates of the crust slowly recycle the surface, carrying old sea floor down into the mantle. Volcanoes cover land with new lava. Over millions of years, most craters on Earth disappear. The Moon has no wind, no rain, no water, no plants and no moving plates, and its volcanoes stopped erupting billions of years ago. A crater made there three billion years ago looks almost as fresh today as when it formed. So the Moon is like a museum of the solar system's history. Earth does keep a few old craters. One of the largest and oldest known is the Vredefort Dome, near Parys in the Free State, made by an asteroid about two billion years ago. The original crater was perhaps 250 to 300 kilometres wide, but erosion has worn most of it away.

### subtopic_2 — The Moon's Orbit and Rotation
**Assistant:** Why do we always see the same face of the Moon?

**Tutor:** Because the Moon spins on its axis exactly once for every trip it makes around the Earth, so the same side always stays turned towards us, an arrangement called tidal locking. It may seem like an amazing coincidence, but it is actually a result of gravity. Long ago, soon after it formed, the Moon spun much faster. But the Earth's gravity pulls more strongly on the side of the Moon nearer to it than on the far side. This stretched the Moon very slightly into an egg shape, with a bulge pointing towards the Earth. As the Moon spun, the Earth's gravity kept tugging on this bulge, trying to hold it pointed at the Earth, which acted like a brake. Over millions of years, the braking slowed the Moon's spin until the bulge stayed pointing at the Earth all the time, and the spin matched the orbit exactly. Now there is no more braking, and the Moon will keep the same face towards us. The same thing has happened to many moons in the solar system: most of the large moons of Jupiter and Saturn always show the same face to their planets. The Moon's gravity is also slowly braking the Earth's spin through the tides, lengthening our day by about two thousandths of a second each century. In the far future, the Earth might show only one face to the Moon too.

### subtopic_3 — Moonlight and the Phases of the Moon
**Assistant:** If the Earth's shadow does not cause the phases, what is a lunar eclipse then?

**Tutor:** A lunar eclipse is a special event when the Earth passes directly between the Sun and the Moon, so that the Earth's shadow really does fall on the Moon; it can only happen at full Moon, and it is quite different from the ordinary monthly phases. The phases happen because we see different parts of the Moon's sunlit half as it moves around us. At full Moon, the Earth is roughly between the Sun and Moon, but the Moon's orbit is tilted by about five degrees compared with the Earth's path around the Sun, so most months the full Moon passes a little above or below the Earth's shadow and stays fully lit. A few times a year, the full Moon crosses the Sun's path exactly, and then it passes into the Earth's shadow. Over an hour or more, a dark curve creeps across the Moon. During a total lunar eclipse, the Moon does not vanish but turns a deep coppery red, because sunlight passing through the Earth's atmosphere is bent around the edge of the Earth, and its red light reaches the Moon; it is as if all the world's sunrises and sunsets are lighting it. Lunar eclipses are safe to watch without eye protection and can be seen from the whole night half of the Earth at once. A solar eclipse is the opposite: the new Moon passes between the Sun and the Earth and blocks the Sun.
