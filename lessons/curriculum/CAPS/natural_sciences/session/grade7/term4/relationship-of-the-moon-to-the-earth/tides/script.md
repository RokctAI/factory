# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the tides, the regular rise and fall of the sea, and how they shape life on the shore. The anchor is a family exploring rock pools at a beach near Port Alfred in the Eastern Cape: in the morning, the sea is far out, exposing rocks covered with mussels, limpets and anemones; by late afternoon, the same rocks are under water. The governing idea is that tides are caused mainly by the Moon's gravity pulling on the Earth's oceans, producing two high tides and two low tides roughly every day; that when the Sun and Moon line up, they produce extra-large spring tides; and that the organisms of the shoreline are adapted to survive in the zone between high and low tide. Explaining tides, spring and neap tides, and describing adaptations in shoreline ecosystems are examinable.

## Subtopic: What Causes the Tides

The tide is the regular rise and fall of the level of the sea. At high tide, the water comes far up the beach; at low tide, it goes out, exposing rocks, sand and mudflats.

Most coasts, including South Africa's, have two high tides and two low tides in about 24 hours and 50 minutes, so each high tide comes about 12 hours 25 minutes after the one before. The tides arrive about 50 minutes later each day, the same as the Moon rises about 50 minutes later each day, a strong clue that the Moon is responsible.

Tides are caused mainly by the Moon's gravity. The Moon pulls on the whole Earth, but it pulls most strongly on the ocean on the side nearest to it, because that water is closest. The water piles up into a bulge facing the Moon: a high tide.

On the opposite side of the Earth, there is a second bulge. The Moon pulls the Earth's centre more strongly than it pulls the water on the far side, which is further away, so the Earth is pulled slightly away from that water, leaving it behind as a second bulge. So there are two bulges, one facing the Moon and one on the far side.

Between the bulges, the water is lower: low tides.

As the Earth spins once a day, any place on the coast passes through both bulges and both low areas, giving two high tides and two low tides each day.

The difference in water height between high and low tide is called the tidal range. Along most of the South African coast, it is about one and a half to two metres.

## Subtopic: Spring Tides and Neap Tides

The Sun's gravity also affects the tides, but less than the Moon's, because the Sun, though far more massive, is much further away. The Sun's tidal effect is just under half that of the Moon.

Spring tides happen when the Sun, the Earth and the Moon are in a straight line, at new Moon and full Moon. The Sun's and Moon's tidal pulls work together, producing very high high tides and very low low tides: the biggest tidal range. They happen about every two weeks. The name has nothing to do with the season of spring; it comes from an old word meaning to leap or rise up.

Neap tides happen when the Sun and the Moon are at right angles to each other as seen from the Earth, at first quarter and last quarter. The Sun's pull partly cancels out the Moon's, so high tides are lower and low tides are higher than usual: the smallest tidal range. They also happen about every two weeks, in between spring tides.

During spring low tides, the sea goes out furthest, exposing rock pools and reefs that are normally underwater. This is the best time for exploring the shore, and when people in some coastal communities harvest mussels and other seafood, which must be done within legal limits. Spring high tides, especially during storms, can flood low-lying coastal areas.

Tide tables, published for harbours such as Durban, Port Elizabeth and Cape Town, predict the times and heights of tides for every day of the year. Fishermen, surfers, ship captains, lifeguards and harbour pilots use them.

## Subtopic: Shoreline Ecosystems

The zone between the highest high tide and the lowest low tide is called the intertidal zone. Organisms that live here face a tough environment: twice a day, they are covered by sea water and then exposed to air, sun, wind and sometimes rain. They must cope with drying out, changes in temperature and saltiness, crashing waves, and predators from both sea and land.

Organisms are adapted to their zone. Near the top, in the splash zone, tiny periwinkle snails and lichens survive long hours out of water. In the middle zone, limpets clamp tightly to rocks with a muscular foot and seal water inside their shells at low tide; barnacles close their plates; mussels shut their shells tightly and anchor themselves with strong threads. Sea anemones pull in their tentacles and become blobs of jelly to avoid drying out. Lower down, where rocks are exposed only at spring low tides, live sea urchins, starfish, red bait and kelp, which cannot survive long in air.

Rock pools trap sea water at low tide and are home to small fish, such as klipfish, crabs, shrimp, octopus and sea anemones, though they can become warm and salty on a hot day.

Shore birds, such as the African black oystercatcher, an endangered species found along South Africa's coast, feed at low tide on mussels and limpets, timing their feeding to the tides.

People affect shoreline ecosystems by over-harvesting, trampling, pollution and building too close to the shore. South Africa protects many sections of coast as marine protected areas.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying wind causes the tides. Two: thinking spring tides only happen in spring. Three: thinking there is only one high tide a day. Four: forgetting the bulge on the far side. Five: thinking intertidal organisms live underwater all the time.

## Subtopic: The Moon Pulls the Sea

Go to the beach in the morning, and the sea is far out. Come back in the afternoon, and the water has come right up the beach. That rise and fall is called the tide.

The Moon pulls on the sea with its gravity. The water closest to the Moon bulges out towards it: high tide. There is another bulge on the far side of the Earth too.

As the Earth spins, your beach passes through both bulges every day. So you get two high tides and two low tides each day.

## Subtopic: Extra-Big and Extra-Small Tides

The Sun pulls on the sea too, but less than the Moon.

When the Sun, Earth and Moon line up, at new Moon and full Moon, their pulls add together. High tides are extra high and low tides are extra low. These are spring tides, nothing to do with the season.

When the Sun and Moon pull at right angles, at half Moon, the tides are small. These are neap tides.

Tide tables tell fishermen and surfers when the tides will be.

## Subtopic: Life Between the Tides

The rocks between high and low tide are a hard place to live. Twice a day they are under water, then out in the sun and wind.

Limpets clamp onto the rock and keep a little water inside. Mussels shut tight. Sea anemones pull in their arms and become little jelly blobs.

Rock pools keep water at low tide, and small fish, crabs and octopus hide there.

Black oystercatchers wait for low tide to eat mussels and limpets.

Look, but do not take or trample: these places need protecting.

Spring tides, with the biggest range, happen when the Sun, Earth and Moon line up at new and full Moon; neap tides, with the smallest range, happen at the quarter Moons. Shoreline organisms such as limpets, mussels and anemones are adapted to being covered and exposed every day.
