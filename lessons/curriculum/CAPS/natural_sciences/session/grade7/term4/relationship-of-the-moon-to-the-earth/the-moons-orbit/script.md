# Part 1 — Expert

This session studies the Moon and its journey around the Earth. The anchor is a family in a village near Mthatha who watch the Moon over a month: a thin crescent in the west after sunset, a half Moon, a bright full Moon rising in the east at dusk and lighting up the paths, then a waning Moon seen only in the early morning. The governing idea is that the Moon is a natural satellite that revolves around the Earth in its orbit about once a month, that it shines only by reflecting sunlight, and that its changing position in orbit causes the phases we see. Describing the Moon and its orbit, explaining why it shines, and explaining the phases of the Moon are examinable.

## Subtopic: The Moon, Earth's Natural Satellite

A satellite is an object that orbits, or revolves around, a larger object in space. The Moon is the Earth's only natural satellite. Artificial satellites, such as those for weather forecasting, GPS and television, are made by people.

The Moon is a rocky, round body about a quarter of the Earth's diameter, roughly 3 470 kilometres across. It is about 384 000 kilometres away on average, so light from the Moon reaches us in just over one second. Its mass is only about one eightieth of the Earth's, so its gravity is about one sixth of the Earth's; an astronaut who weighs 60 kilograms worth on Earth would feel as light as 10 kilograms on the Moon.

The Moon has no atmosphere and no liquid water on its surface. Without air to trap heat or spread it around, its surface is extremely hot in sunlight, over 100 degrees Celsius, and extremely cold in darkness, below minus 150 degrees. With no wind or rain, footprints left by astronauts in 1969 to 1972 are still there.

Its surface is covered in grey dust and rock and pitted with craters, made by meteorites crashing into it over billions of years. The dark patches we see from Earth are flat plains of ancient solidified lava, called maria, meaning seas, though they contain no water. The lighter areas are highlands.

Many cultures see shapes in the Moon's dark patches: a rabbit, a face, or a person carrying a bundle of sticks.

Scientists think the Moon formed about 4,5 billion years ago, when a planet-sized body collided with the young Earth, and the debris thrown into orbit gathered together.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of satellite, the Moon's size, distance and gravity, and its surface features.

## Subtopic: The Moon's Orbit and Rotation

The Moon revolves around the Earth in a nearly circular path called its orbit. It takes about 27,3 days to complete one orbit compared with the stars. Because the Earth is also moving around the Sun at the same time, it takes about 29,5 days for the Moon to go from one full Moon to the next. This is the origin of the word month, from Moon.

The Moon also rotates on its own axis, and it takes exactly the same time, about 27,3 days, to rotate once as it does to orbit once. As a result, the same side of the Moon always faces the Earth. We never see the far side from Earth; it was first photographed by a spacecraft in 1959. The far side is not always dark: it gets just as much sunlight as the near side, only at different times.

Gravity holds the Moon in its orbit: the Earth's gravity pulls the Moon towards it, while the Moon's motion carries it sideways; together, these make it curve around the Earth instead of flying away or falling in.

The Moon moves in its orbit from west to east, so each night, it rises about 50 minutes later than the night before, and it appears in a slightly different place among the stars.

At the same time, the Earth and Moon together orbit the Sun once a year. So the Moon's path through space is a wavy line around the Sun.

A model shows this: one learner stands still as the Earth while another, holding a ball as the Moon, walks in a circle around them, always keeping their face towards the Earth. Watchers see that the Moon-walker also turns once while going around once.

The Moon has been visited by people. Between 1969 and 1972, twelve astronauts walked on its surface during the American Apollo missions, bringing back about 380 kilograms of Moon rock for study. Since then, robotic spacecraft from several countries, including China and India, have landed there, and new missions plan to return people to the Moon, possibly to build a base near its south pole, where ice may be hidden in permanently shadowed craters.

The questions for this section are with you now: the Moon's orbit time, why we always see the same side, and how gravity keeps the Moon in orbit.

## Subtopic: Moonlight and the Phases of the Moon

The Moon does not produce its own light. It is lit by the Sun, and we see it because it reflects some of the sunlight back towards the Earth. Moonlight is reflected sunlight.

At every moment, exactly half of the Moon is lit by the Sun, the half facing the Sun. As the Moon orbits the Earth, we see different amounts of that lit half. These changing shapes are called the phases of the Moon.

New Moon: the Moon is between the Earth and the Sun; its lit side faces away from us, so we see almost nothing.

Waxing crescent: a few days later, a thin sliver appears in the west after sunset. Waxing means growing.

First quarter: about a week after new Moon, we see half of the Moon's face lit.

Waxing gibbous: more than half lit.

Full Moon: about two weeks after new Moon, the Earth is between the Sun and the Moon, and we see the whole lit side. A full Moon rises in the east at sunset and sets at sunrise.

Then waning gibbous, last quarter and waning crescent, as the lit part shrinks, before the next new Moon. Waning means shrinking.

A model shows the phases: in a dark room with a single lamp as the Sun, hold a white ball at arm's length and turn slowly round. The lit part of the ball changes through all the phases.

In the Southern Hemisphere, a waxing Moon is lit on the left side; in the Northern Hemisphere, on the right.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying the Moon makes its own light. Two: thinking the phases are caused by the Earth's shadow; that is a lunar eclipse. Three: saying the Moon does not rotate. Four: thinking the far side is always dark. Five: thinking the Moon is only seen at night; it is often visible in daytime.

The questions for this section are with you now: why the Moon shines, the eight phases in order, and the lamp-and-ball model.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a month of moonlit nights near Mthatha — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Our Moon

The Moon is a big ball of rock that goes round the Earth. Something that goes round a bigger thing in space is called a satellite. The Moon is Earth's natural satellite.

It is about a quarter as wide as the Earth and very far away. It has no air and no water. It is covered in grey dust and holes called craters, made by space rocks crashing into it.

Its pull of gravity is weak, so astronauts on the Moon bounce around.

## Subtopic: Round and Round

The Moon goes round the Earth once in about a month. In fact, the word month comes from Moon.

The Moon spins too, but very slowly, once each trip around us. So it always shows us the same face. We never see its back from Earth.

Earth's gravity holds the Moon so it keeps going round instead of flying off.

Hold the Moon's trip in mind and try this part's questions.

## Subtopic: The Changing Moon

The Moon makes no light of its own. It shines because sunlight bounces off it.

Half of the Moon is always lit by the Sun. As it goes round us, we see different amounts of that lit half. That is why it seems to change shape: new Moon, then a thin crescent, then a half, then a full round Moon, then back again.

Growing is called waxing. Shrinking is called waning.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Moon is Earth's natural satellite, a rocky body with no air or water, covered in craters. It orbits the Earth about once a month, held by gravity, and spins once per orbit, so we always see the same face. It shines by reflecting sunlight, and as it orbits, we see different amounts of its lit half: the phases, from new Moon through crescent, quarter and gibbous to full Moon and back.
