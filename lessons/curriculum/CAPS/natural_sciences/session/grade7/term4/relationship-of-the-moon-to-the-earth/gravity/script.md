# Part 1 — Expert

This session studies gravity, the force that holds the Earth and Moon in their orbits and keeps our feet on the ground. The anchor is a learner at a school in Bloemfontein who drops a stone and a feather, watches a soccer ball arc back to the ground, and then sees a video of astronauts bouncing on the Moon. The governing idea is that gravity is a force of attraction between all objects that have mass, that its strength depends on how much mass the objects have and how far apart they are, and that gravity holds the Moon in orbit around the Earth and the Earth in orbit around the Sun. Describing gravity, explaining how mass and distance affect it, distinguishing mass from weight, and explaining how gravity holds orbits are examinable.

## Subtopic: What Gravity Is

Gravity is a force of attraction, a pull, that acts between any two objects that have mass. Mass is the amount of matter, or stuff, in an object, measured in kilograms.

Every object pulls on every other object: you pull on the Earth, the Earth pulls on you; your pencil pulls on your desk. But gravity is a very weak force, so we only notice it when at least one of the objects is enormous, like a planet, a moon or a star.

The Earth's gravity pulls everything towards the centre of the Earth. That is why objects fall down when dropped, why water flows downhill, why rain falls and why a ball thrown upwards always comes back. People on the other side of the world, in Brazil or Japan, are pulled towards the centre too, so they do not fall off.

Gravity is a non-contact force: it acts without the objects touching. It works across empty space, which is how the Sun's gravity reaches the Earth 150 million kilometres away.

Sir Isaac Newton, an English scientist, described gravity in 1687. He realised that the same force that makes an apple fall from a tree also keeps the Moon in orbit around the Earth, an idea that united events on Earth with the motions of the heavens.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of gravity and mass, gravity as a non-contact force, and Newton's insight.

## Subtopic: Gravity Depends on Mass and Distance

Two things decide how strong the pull of gravity is between two objects.

Mass: the greater the mass of the objects, the stronger the force of gravity between them. The Sun has about 330 000 times the mass of the Earth, so its gravity is very strong. The Moon has much less mass than the Earth, about one eightieth, so its surface gravity is only about one sixth of the Earth's. Jupiter, the largest planet, has more than 300 times the Earth's mass, and its surface gravity is about two and a half times the Earth's.

Distance: the further apart the objects are, the weaker the force of gravity between them. The pull drops quickly as distance increases. If the distance between two objects doubles, the force becomes four times weaker. Astronauts on the International Space Station, about 400 kilometres up, still feel about nine tenths of the Earth's gravity; far out in space between the stars, the pull of any planet is almost nothing.

Mass and weight are different. Mass is the amount of matter in an object and stays the same everywhere. Weight is the force of gravity pulling on that mass, measured in newtons with a spring balance, and it changes depending on where you are. A learner with a mass of 45 kilograms has a mass of 45 kilograms on Earth, on the Moon and on Jupiter, but on the Moon they weigh only one sixth as much, and on Jupiter, about two and a half times as much.

On Earth, all objects fall with the same acceleration if air resistance is ignored. A stone falls faster than a feather only because air resistance slows the feather; on the airless Moon, astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a feather in 1971, and they hit the ground together.

Gravity also affects the shape of things on Earth. It pulls water downhill into rivers and oceans, holds the atmosphere close to the planet so that we can breathe, and pulls rain and hail to the ground. It is why plants' roots grow downwards and why builders use a plumb line, a weight on a string, to check that walls are vertical. Without gravity, nothing would stay on the ground, and the air would drift away into space.

The questions for this section are with you now: how mass and distance change gravity, the difference between mass and weight, and falling objects.

## Subtopic: Gravity Holds the Earth and Moon in Their Orbits

An orbit is the curved path of one object around another, caused by gravity.

The Moon is moving sideways very fast, about 3 700 kilometres per hour. Without gravity, it would travel in a straight line and fly off into space. Without its sideways motion, the Earth's gravity would pull it straight down into the Earth. The two together, sideways motion and the pull of gravity towards the Earth, bend the Moon's path into a near circle around the Earth. The Moon is constantly falling towards the Earth, but always missing.

A model shows this: tie a rubber stopper to a string and swing it in a horizontal circle above your head. The string pulls the stopper towards your hand, like gravity; the stopper's motion carries it sideways. If you let go of the string, the stopper flies off in a straight line, just as the Moon would if gravity disappeared.

In the same way, the Sun's gravity holds the Earth and all the other planets in their orbits. The Earth moves around the Sun at about 107 000 kilometres per hour, and the Sun's pull keeps bending its path into an orbit that takes one year. Closer planets, like Mercury, feel a stronger pull and move faster; distant planets, like Neptune, feel a weaker pull and move more slowly, taking 165 years to complete one orbit.

Gravity also holds artificial satellites in orbit, and it holds the Earth's atmosphere and oceans in place. The Moon's gravity pulls on the Earth too, causing the tides, the topic of the next lesson.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying there is no gravity in space. Two: confusing mass with weight. Three: thinking heavy objects fall faster when air resistance is not involved. Four: thinking the Moon is not falling. Five: saying only the Earth pulls on the Moon; the Moon pulls on the Earth too, equally.

The questions for this section are with you now: how gravity and sideways motion produce an orbit, the string-and-stopper model, and how the Sun holds the planets.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a dropped stone and a bouncing astronaut — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Invisible Pull

Drop a stone, and it falls. Throw a ball up, and it comes back. Something is pulling them down: gravity.

Gravity is a pull between all things that have mass, which means stuff. Everything pulls on everything else, but the pull is tiny unless something is huge, like the Earth.

Gravity pulls without touching. It even works across empty space.

Keep the falling stone in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: Bigger Is Stronger, Further Is Weaker

The more mass something has, the stronger its pull. The Sun has a huge pull. The Moon has a small pull, only one sixth of Earth's. That is why astronauts bounce on the Moon.

The further away you are, the weaker the pull.

Your mass, the amount of you, stays the same everywhere. Your weight, how hard gravity pulls you, changes. On the Moon, you would weigh much less.

Without air, a hammer and a feather fall together.

Hold the bouncing astronaut in mind and try this part's questions.

## Subtopic: Falling Around

The Moon is zooming sideways very fast. Earth's gravity keeps pulling it in. Together, these make the Moon go round and round instead of flying away.

Swing a stopper on a string around your head. The string pulls it in, like gravity. Let go, and it flies off in a straight line.

The Sun's gravity holds the Earth in its yearly trip around the Sun in the same way.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Gravity is a pull between all objects with mass, acting without contact, even across space. More mass means a stronger pull; more distance means a weaker pull. Mass stays the same everywhere, but weight, the pull of gravity on a mass, changes. Gravity, together with sideways motion, holds the Moon in orbit around the Earth and the Earth in orbit around the Sun.

Hold the stone, the astronaut and the swinging stopper, and take the final questions of the lesson.
