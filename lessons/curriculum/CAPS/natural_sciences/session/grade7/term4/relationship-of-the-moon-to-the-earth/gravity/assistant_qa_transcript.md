### subtopic_1 — What Gravity Is
**Assistant:** If all objects pull on each other, why don't two people standing close together get pulled towards each other?

**Tutor:** They do pull on each other, but the force is so tiny that friction between their feet and the ground easily overwhelms it, and they never notice. Gravity between two objects depends on their masses. The Earth has a mass of about six million billion billion kilograms, so its pull on a person is strong enough to give them weight. Two people of 50 kilograms each, standing one metre apart, attract each other with a force less than one millionth of the weight of a grain of sugar. Friction between their shoes and the floor is billions of times stronger, so nothing moves. Even if they floated in space, far from any planet, it would take hours for them to drift together. Yet this tiny attraction has actually been measured. In 1798, an English scientist named Henry Cavendish hung a rod with small lead balls on a thin wire, inside a closed room to block draughts, and brought large lead balls near them. The rod twisted very slightly as the small balls were pulled towards the large ones, and by measuring the tiny twist, he calculated the strength of gravity and even estimated the mass of the whole Earth. Modern versions of his experiment are still being done today. So yes, you are pulling on your desk, your friends and the mountains, but only something as massive as a planet makes the pull strong enough to feel.

### subtopic_2 — Gravity Depends on Mass and Distance
**Assistant:** Why do astronauts float on the space station if gravity there is still nine tenths as strong?

**Tutor:** Because the space station and everything inside it are falling around the Earth together, all the time, so the astronauts never press against the floor and feel weightless. This sounds strange, so imagine Newton's famous thought experiment. Stand on a very high mountain and throw a ball sideways. It curves down and lands some distance away. Throw it faster, and it lands further. Throw it fast enough, about 28 000 kilometres per hour at the height of the space station, and as it falls, the Earth's curved surface drops away beneath it at the same rate, so the ball keeps falling but never reaches the ground. It is in orbit. The space station is doing exactly this: it is falling towards the Earth under gravity, while moving sideways so fast that it keeps missing, circling the planet about every 90 minutes. The astronauts are falling at exactly the same rate as the station, so they float relative to its walls, just as you would briefly float off the seat in a lift if its cable snapped, or as you feel lighter at the top of a drop on a roller coaster. The proper name for this is free fall. If gravity really vanished at that height, the station would fly off in a straight line into space. So the floating astronauts are actually strong evidence that gravity is working on them all the time.

### subtopic_3 — Gravity Holds the Earth and Moon in Their Orbits
**Assistant:** Is the Moon slowly falling towards the Earth or moving away?

**Tutor:** It is actually moving slowly away from the Earth, by about 3,8 centimetres each year, roughly as fast as your fingernails grow, and the reason is the tides. Scientists measure the Moon's distance extremely precisely by firing laser beams from Earth at special mirrors left on the Moon by the Apollo astronauts and timing how long the light takes to bounce back. The measurements show the distance growing by a few centimetres each year. The Moon's gravity raises tidal bulges in the Earth's oceans. Because the Earth spins faster than the Moon orbits, the Earth's rotation drags these bulges slightly ahead of the Moon. The bulge's gravity then tugs the Moon forward along its orbit, giving it a little extra energy, which lifts it into a slightly larger orbit. At the same time, friction from the tides acts as a brake on the Earth's spin, making our days very slowly longer. Fossil corals that lived about 400 million years ago show growth rings indicating that a year then had about 400 days, each only about 22 hours long. Long ago, the Moon was much closer, and it would have looked enormous in the sky. Billions of years in the future, it will be further away and look smaller; total eclipses of the Sun will then no longer be possible, because the Moon will appear too small to cover the Sun completely.
