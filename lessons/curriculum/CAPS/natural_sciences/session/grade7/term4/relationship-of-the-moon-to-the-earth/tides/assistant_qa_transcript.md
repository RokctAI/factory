### subtopic_1 — What Causes the Tides
**Assistant:** Why does the second bulge form on the side of the Earth facing away from the Moon?

**Tutor:** Because gravity weakens with distance, so the Moon pulls hardest on the nearest water, less hard on the Earth's centre, and least hard on the water on the far side; this difference stretches the oceans out in both directions along the line to the Moon. Imagine three runners tied loosely together, all being pulled forward, but the front runner is pulled hardest, the middle runner a bit less, and the back runner least. The front runner pulls ahead, and the back runner falls behind, so the group stretches out. In the same way, the water facing the Moon is pulled towards the Moon more than the Earth as a whole, so it bulges out towards the Moon. The solid Earth is pulled towards the Moon more than the water on the far side, so the Earth moves slightly away from that water, which is left behind as a bulge pointing away from the Moon. The Earth and Moon are also both moving around their shared centre of balance, which lies inside the Earth about three quarters of the way from the centre to the surface, and this motion helps explain the far bulge too. The result is that the Earth's oceans are stretched into a slight oval, with one bulge towards the Moon and one away. The same stretching force, called a tidal force, acts on the solid Earth, raising the ground by up to about 30 centimetres twice a day, though we cannot feel it.

### subtopic_2 — Spring Tides and Neap Tides
**Assistant:** Do lakes, dams and swimming pools have tides too?

**Tutor:** In theory, the Moon pulls on every body of water, but in a lake, a dam or a swimming pool, the tide is so tiny that it cannot be noticed, because the body of water is far too small for the difference in the Moon's pull from one side to the other to matter. Tides happen because the Moon pulls a little harder on water closer to it than on water further away. Across the whole Earth, about 12 700 kilometres wide, that difference is enough to raise ocean bulges. Across a swimming pool 25 metres long, the difference in distance to the Moon is almost nothing, so the pull is practically the same at both ends, and no bulge can form. Even large lakes have only very small tides. Lake Victoria, the biggest lake in Africa, has tides of only a centimetre or two, hidden among the waves and changes caused by wind and rain. The oceans have large tides partly because they are huge and connected, so water can flow over great distances to form bulges. In fact, the shape of the coast and the sea floor greatly affects the size of the tides. In some bays and narrow channels, the tidal water is funnelled and amplified: in the Bay of Fundy in Canada, the tidal range can reach about 16 metres, among the largest on Earth. The tidal range along the South African coast is moderate, about two metres at spring tides.

### subtopic_3 — Shoreline Ecosystems
**Assistant:** How do limpets find their way back to the same spot on the rock after feeding?

**Tutor:** Many limpets do return to the very same spot, called a home scar, after each feeding trip, and scientists think they follow a chemical trail of their own mucus, the slime they leave behind as they crawl. A limpet is a snail with a cone-shaped shell. At high tide, when it is covered with water, or on damp nights, it slowly crawls across the rock, scraping off thin films of algae with a rough, ribbon-like tongue covered in tiny teeth, called a radula. As it moves, it lays down a trail of mucus. Before the tide goes out, it follows a trail back to its home scar, a shallow dip in the rock that exactly fits the edge of its shell. Over years, the limpet grinds its shell edge and the rock to match each other perfectly. When it clamps down there, with a powerful muscular foot, the seal is so tight that it traps a little sea water inside, so the limpet does not dry out during low tide, and predators like birds and crabs find it very hard to pry off. Limpet teeth are made of one of the strongest natural materials ever tested, stronger than spider silk. South Africa has an extraordinary variety of limpets, more species than almost anywhere else, and some, like the giant limpet, guard their own garden of algae on the rock and chase other grazers away.
