### subtopic_1 — Evaporation and Crystallisation
**Assistant:** Why do some salt pans in South Africa turn pink?

**Tutor:** Because of tiny living things that thrive in very salty water and contain red and orange pigments. As seawater or salty groundwater evaporates in a salt pan, it becomes several times saltier than the sea. Very few organisms can survive in such brine, because the salt draws water out of most cells. But a few specialists can. One is a single-celled green alga called Dunaliella, which protects itself from the strong sunlight and salt by making large amounts of orange-red pigments called carotenoids, the same family of pigments that colour carrots. Another group are salt-loving microorganisms called haloarchaea, single-celled organisms that look like bacteria but belong to a separate group of life, which contain a pink-purple pigment. As the brine gets saltier, these organisms multiply and can colour the whole pan pink, red or even purple. Tiny brine shrimps feed on the algae, and flamingos, which feed on the shrimps and algae in pans and lagoons in places such as the Kamfers Dam near Kimberley and the West Coast, get their pink feathers from the same pigments in their food. Salt-makers sometimes value the microbes because their colour helps the brine absorb more of the Sun's heat, which speeds up evaporation. The pink colour therefore links three ideas from this year: evaporation concentrates the salt, adaptations allow special organisms to live there, and pigments pass along a food chain.

### subtopic_2 — Distillation
**Assistant:** Why is it so hard to turn seawater into drinking water for a whole city?

**Tutor:** Because removing salt from water takes a great deal of energy, and a city needs enormous volumes of water every day. The salt in seawater is dissolved, so it cannot be filtered out with ordinary filters. One option is distillation: boiling seawater and condensing the steam. Boiling water requires a lot of heat energy, more than two thousand joules for every gram turned to steam, so distilling the hundreds of millions of litres a large city uses each day would need huge amounts of fuel or electricity. Most modern plants instead use reverse osmosis, in which seawater is pumped at very high pressure against special membranes with pores so fine that water particles pass through but salt particles do not. This uses less energy than distillation, but the pumps still need a lot of electricity, the membranes must be protected from clogging by sand, algae and shells, and they need regular cleaning and replacement. There is also waste: for every litre of fresh water produced, roughly one to one and a half litres of very salty brine are left over, which must be returned to the sea in a way that does not harm marine life near the outlet. During the severe drought of 2015 to 2018, Cape Town built several temporary desalination plants, and desalination is planned as part of its long-term supply, but it remains more expensive than water from dams and groundwater. That is why saving water, fixing leaks and recycling wastewater are usually the first steps, with desalination as an important addition, especially for coastal towns.

### subtopic_3 — Chromatography and Choosing a Method
**Assistant:** How can chromatography help catch athletes who use banned drugs?

**Tutor:** Because it can separate the many substances in a sample of urine or blood and show whether any of them match known banned drugs. A urine sample contains hundreds of different dissolved substances produced naturally by the body, plus anything the person has eaten, drunk or taken as medicine. If an athlete has taken a banned performance-enhancing drug, such as an anabolic steroid or a stimulant, small amounts of the drug or of substances the body makes from it appear in the urine. In a testing laboratory, the sample is first treated to concentrate the substances of interest. It is then passed through a chromatography system. In gas chromatography, for example, the sample is turned into vapour and carried by a gas through a very long, thin coiled tube lined with a special material; each substance moves through at its own speed, depending on how strongly it clings to the lining, so the substances come out of the end of the tube one after another, at different times. The time each one takes is compared with the time known drugs take under identical conditions. Usually the substances coming out are then sent into a second instrument, a mass spectrometer, which identifies them by the masses of their particles, like a fingerprint. Together, the two methods can detect tiny amounts with great certainty. The principle is exactly the same as the felt-tip pen on paper: different substances travel different distances or at different speeds, so they separate and can be identified.
