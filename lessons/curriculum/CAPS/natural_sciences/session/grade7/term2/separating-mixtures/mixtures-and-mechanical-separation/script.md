# Part 1 — Expert

This session defines a mixture and introduces the simplest ways of separating one. The anchor is a gold panner on a river in the old Pilgrim's Rest goldfields of Mpumalanga in the 1870s, swirling a pan of gravel, water and sand so that the heavy grains of gold are left behind, together with a modern school experiment in which a mixture of sand, salt, iron filings and dried beans must be separated into its parts. The governing idea is that a mixture contains two or more substances that are not chemically joined and keep their own properties, and that mixtures can be separated by using the differences in those properties: size, magnetism, density and whether a substance dissolves. The meaning of mixture, and the methods of hand sorting, sieving, filtration and magnetic separation with their uses, are examinable.

## Subtopic: What a Mixture Is

A mixture is made of two or more substances that are mixed together but not chemically joined. Each substance in a mixture keeps its own properties, and the substances can be present in any proportion. This is what makes separation possible: the parts of a mixture still behave as themselves.

Mixtures are everywhere. Air is a mixture of nitrogen, oxygen, argon, carbon dioxide and water vapour. Sea water is a mixture of water and dissolved salts. Soil is a mixture of rock particles, humus, water, air and living things. Muesli, fruit salad and trail mix are obvious mixtures; you can see each part. Tea, cooldrinks, blood, concrete and many rocks such as granite are mixtures too.

Mixtures differ from pure substances. A pure substance, such as pure water, gold or sugar, contains only one substance, either an element or a compound. A compound, such as water or table salt, has its elements chemically joined in a fixed ratio and has new properties quite different from those of its elements; sodium is a dangerously reactive metal and chlorine a poisonous gas, but joined together they form the table salt we eat. A compound can only be broken up by a chemical reaction. A mixture of iron filings and sulphur powder, by contrast, can be separated by a magnet, because the iron is still iron.

Some mixtures are uniform throughout and look like one substance; these are called solutions when one substance has dissolved in another, such as salt or sugar in water. The substance that dissolves is the solute and the liquid it dissolves in is the solvent. A substance that dissolves is soluble; one that does not, like sand in water, is insoluble. Other mixtures, such as sand in water or a fruit salad, are not uniform, and their parts can be seen.

Separating mixtures is important in daily life and industry: cleaning water, purifying salt from the sea, extracting metals from ores, refining crude oil into petrol and diesel, recovering recyclable materials from waste, and testing blood in medical laboratories all depend on it.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of a mixture, how a mixture differs from a compound, and the terms solution, solute, solvent, soluble and insoluble.

## Subtopic: Hand Sorting, Sieving and Magnets

The simplest separation methods use differences in the size, shape, colour or magnetic properties of the parts.

Hand sorting is picking out the parts of a mixture by hand, or with tweezers, when the pieces are large and look different. Sorting beans or stones out of dried maize or sorghum before cooking, separating recyclable materials from household waste, and picking out stones from a mixture of gravel are examples. It is slow but needs no equipment.

Sieving separates solid particles of different sizes using a mesh. Small particles fall through the holes and large ones stay on top. Builders sieve sand to remove stones before mixing plaster; cooks sift flour to remove lumps; farmers use screens to separate grain from chaff and stones; and in diamond and gold mining, huge vibrating screens sort crushed rock by size. The size of the holes in the sieve decides which particles pass through. Sieving only works for solids and only when the particle sizes are different.

Magnetic separation uses a magnet to pull out magnetic materials from a mixture. Only a few substances are attracted to magnets: iron, steel, nickel, cobalt and some of their compounds. Running a magnet over a mixture of sand and iron filings collects the iron; covering the magnet with plastic wrap makes it easy to remove the filings afterwards. At recycling plants, large electromagnets lift steel cans out of mixed waste, leaving aluminium cans, which are not magnetic, behind. Magnetic separation is also used to remove iron fragments from food in factories and to concentrate magnetic iron ore at mines.

The density method used by the gold panner relies on the fact that gold is very dense, about nineteen times as dense as water and much denser than sand. When gravel, sand and water are swirled in a shallow pan, the light sand is washed over the edge while the heavy gold sinks to the bottom. Winnowing is a related method: tossing threshed grain in the wind lets the light chaff blow away while the heavier grain falls back.

The questions for this section are with you now: which property each method uses, everyday and industrial examples, and why a magnet separates steel cans but not aluminium cans.

## Subtopic: Filtration and Planning a Separation

Filtration separates an insoluble solid from a liquid by passing the mixture through a filter, a material with tiny holes that let the liquid through but trap the solid particles. In the laboratory, filter paper is folded into a cone and placed in a funnel over a beaker. The mixture is poured in; the liquid that passes through is called the filtrate, and the solid left on the paper is called the residue. Filtering muddy water through filter paper gives clear filtrate and a residue of mud.

Filtration is used everywhere. A tea strainer or a coffee filter separates the leaves or grounds from the drink. Water treatment works pass water through thick beds of sand and gravel to remove fine particles. Vacuum cleaners and car air filters trap dust while letting air through. Our kidneys filter blood. Masks filter particles from the air we breathe.

Filtration cannot separate a dissolved substance from a liquid. If salty water is filtered, the salt particles pass straight through the paper with the water, and the filtrate is still salty. Filtration also does not make water safe to drink by itself, because bacteria and viruses are small enough to pass through ordinary filters; water must also be disinfected, for example by boiling or by adding chlorine.

To separate a complex mixture, plan the order of methods, using one property at a time. For the anchor mixture of beans, iron filings, sand and salt: first, pick out the beans by hand or with a sieve, because they are large. Second, use a magnet to remove the iron filings. Third, add water to the sand and salt and stir: the salt dissolves and the sand does not. Fourth, filter: the sand stays on the paper as residue, and the salt water passes through as filtrate. Fifth, evaporate the water from the filtrate to recover the salt, a method covered in the next lesson.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying filtration removes dissolved salt; it does not. Two: thinking all metals are magnetic; only iron, steel, nickel and cobalt are among common ones. Three: calling a solution a compound because it looks like one substance. Four: mixing up filtrate and residue; the filtrate passes through. Five: assuming filtered water is safe to drink; it may still contain germs.

The questions for this section are with you now: how filtration works, the terms filtrate and residue, what filtration cannot do, and planning the order of methods for a complex mixture.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a messy bowl, a kitchen sieve and a gold pan — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Mixed, Not Joined

Tip some beans, sand, salt and iron filings into one bowl and stir. That is a mixture: different things mixed together, but not joined. Each one is still itself. The beans are still beans; the iron is still iron.

Air is a mixture of gases. Sea water is water with salt mixed in. Soil is a mixture of bits of rock, rotting leaves, water and air.

When something dissolves, like sugar in tea, it seems to disappear. But it is still there; taste the tea and it is sweet. That kind of mixture is called a solution. The sugar is soluble. Sand is insoluble: it never dissolves.

Because each part of a mixture keeps its own properties, we can use those properties to pull the mixture apart.

Keep the messy bowl in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: Big, Small and Magnetic

Now pull the bowl apart, one difference at a time.

Big things that look different? Pick them out by hand. That is hand sorting, like picking stones out of beans before cooking.

Different sizes? Use a sieve. Small bits fall through the holes; big bits stay on top. Builders sieve sand to get rid of stones.

Iron in the mix? Use a magnet. It grabs iron and steel, but not aluminium, sand or salt. Recycling plants use giant magnets to pull steel cans out of rubbish.

Something very heavy? Long ago, gold panners at Pilgrim's Rest swirled river gravel in a pan of water. The light sand washed away and the heavy gold stayed at the bottom.

Hold the sieve and the magnet in mind and try this part's questions.

## Subtopic: Through the Filter

What about sand mixed with water? A sieve's holes are too big. You need a filter, like a coffee filter or filter paper in a funnel.

The water squeezes through the tiny holes. The sand cannot, so it stays behind on the paper. The water that comes through is called the filtrate. The sand left on the paper is the residue.

But a filter cannot catch something that has dissolved. Filter salty water and the water that comes out is still salty, because the salt bits are far too tiny. Germs can sneak through too, so filtered water still needs boiling before you drink it.

So for our bowl: beans out by hand, iron out with a magnet, add water so the salt dissolves, filter to catch the sand, and then get the salt back by drying out the water, which comes next lesson.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A mixture is two or more substances mixed but not joined, each keeping its own properties. We separate mixtures using differences: hand sorting for big, different-looking pieces; sieving for different sizes; magnets for iron and steel; panning for very dense things; and filtering to separate an insoluble solid from a liquid. Filtering cannot remove dissolved substances or all germs.
