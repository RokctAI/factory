# Part 1 — Expert

This session applies separation to the biggest mixture most households produce: their rubbish. The anchor is a single black bin bag from a family home in Soshanguve on collection day, holding food scraps, a cooldrink bottle, a tin can, newspapers, a broken glass jar, a used battery and a light bulb, all mixed together. At a landfill it is buried as one mixture; at a recycling centre each part could become something new. The governing idea is that household waste is a mixture of materials with different properties, that sorting it, ideally at the source, allows materials to be recycled or composted, and that poor waste management harms health, land, water and wildlife. The waste hierarchy, sorting and recycling methods, responsible disposal of hazardous waste, and the consequences of poor waste management are examinable.

## Subtopic: Waste as a Mixture and the Waste Hierarchy

Waste is any material that is thrown away because it is no longer wanted. Household waste is a mixture: in a typical South African household bin, roughly a third or more is organic waste, such as food scraps and garden cuttings; much of the rest is paper and cardboard, plastics, glass and metals; and there are small amounts of textiles, nappies and hazardous items such as batteries, paint, medicines and light bulbs.

South Africa produces tens of millions of tonnes of general waste a year, and most of it is still sent to landfill sites, where it is buried. Only a minority is recycled, although recycling rates for some materials, such as paper, cans and PET bottles, are quite high. Many landfill sites are filling up, especially around big cities, and finding new sites is difficult and expensive.

The waste hierarchy ranks ways of dealing with waste from most to least preferred. First, avoid and reduce: do not create the waste in the first place, for example by buying products with less packaging, using both sides of paper and avoiding single-use items. Second, reuse: use items again for the same or a different purpose, such as refilling bottles, using jars for storage and repairing instead of replacing. Third, recycle: process used materials into new products. Fourth, recover: obtain energy or other value from waste that cannot be recycled, for example by capturing methane gas from landfills to generate electricity, or by composting organic waste. Last, dispose: send what is left to a properly managed landfill. The higher up the hierarchy, the less energy and raw material are used.

Separation is the key to recycling. A mixed bag of waste contaminated with food is difficult and expensive to separate, and paper soaked with food often cannot be recycled at all. When households and businesses separate waste at the source, into recyclables, organic waste and general waste, the materials stay clean and valuable. Many municipalities provide separate bags or bins, and many schools collect recyclables.

## Subtopic: Sorting and Recycling Materials

At a materials recovery facility, mixed recyclables are separated using many of the methods learned in this topic, often combined. Large items and contaminants are removed by hand sorting on a moving belt. Rotating screens, which are giant sieves, separate small items from large ones and flat cardboard from bottles and cans. Powerful magnets lift out steel cans. Aluminium cans, which are not magnetic, are removed by an eddy current separator, a spinning magnetic device that makes a pulse of electric current in the aluminium and flicks it off the belt. Air blowers separate light paper and film from heavier items, and optical sensors that recognise different plastics by the light they reflect direct jets of air to push each type into the right bin. Glass may be sorted by colour.

Each material is then recycled in its own way. Paper and cardboard are mixed with water and pulped, cleaned and pressed into new paper and board; each time, the fibres get shorter, so paper can be recycled only five to seven times. Steel and aluminium cans are melted and cast into new metal, and they can be recycled indefinitely without loss of quality; recycling aluminium saves about ninety-five per cent of the energy needed to make it from ore. Glass is crushed into cullet and melted to make new bottles and jars, saving raw materials and energy, and it too can be recycled endlessly. PET plastic bottles are washed, shredded into flakes and melted to make new bottles, polyester fibre for clothing, pillow filling or strapping; South Africa recycles a large proportion of its PET bottles. Other plastics are more difficult and are often made into lower-value products, such as plastic poles and garden furniture.

Organic waste can be composted, at home or at municipal sites. In a compost heap, bacteria, fungi and earthworms break down food scraps, leaves and grass clippings into compost, a dark, crumbly material that improves soil. A good heap needs a mixture of green material, rich in nitrogen, and brown material, rich in carbon, such as dry leaves, as well as air and moisture. Earthworm farms, or wormeries, do the same in a small space.

## Subtopic: Hazardous Waste and Poor Waste Management

Some waste is hazardous, meaning that it can harm people or the environment if it is not handled correctly. Household hazardous waste includes batteries, which contain metals such as lead, mercury, cadmium or lithium; energy-saving light bulbs, which contain a small amount of mercury; paint, solvents and pesticides; old medicines; motor oil; and electronic waste such as old cellphones and computers. These should never go into the ordinary bin, be burned or be poured down drains. Many shops and municipal drop-off sites accept batteries, bulbs, electronic waste and used oil; pharmacies can take back old medicines.

Poor waste management has serious consequences. Illegal dumping, where waste is dumped on open land, along roads or in rivers, is a major problem in many South African towns and cities, partly where municipal collection services are unreliable. Dumped waste attracts rats, flies and mosquitoes that spread diseases; it blocks storm-water drains, causing flooding; and it is ugly and lowers property values. Burning waste in open fires releases toxic smoke, including harmful chemicals from burning plastic, which damages people's lungs. Waste in rivers flows to the sea, where plastic harms marine animals; after floods in KwaZulu-Natal in 2022, enormous amounts of waste washed into Durban's harbour and onto its beaches.

Badly managed landfills also cause harm. Rainwater seeping through waste dissolves substances and forms a polluted liquid called leachate, which can contaminate groundwater and rivers if the landfill is not lined; landfills can catch fire; and landfill gas adds to climate change. Well-managed landfills are lined with clay and plastic sheeting, have pipes to collect leachate and gas, and cover waste with soil every day.

Everyone can help: reduce what we buy and throw away, reuse items, separate recyclables and compost organics, take hazardous waste to proper drop-off points, never litter or dump, and support community clean-ups and waste pickers.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: thinking recycling is the best option; reducing and reusing come first. Two: putting batteries or bulbs in the ordinary bin. Three: thinking all plastics are equally recyclable. Four: putting food-soiled paper in the recycling. Five: thinking burning rubbish makes it disappear safely; it releases toxic smoke.

## Subtopic: One Bag, Many Things

Look inside a black bin bag on collection day. Food scraps, a cooldrink bottle, a tin can, newspapers, a glass jar, maybe a used battery. All mixed together. Rubbish is a mixture.

If the bag goes to a landfill, it is all buried in the ground. Landfills are filling up, and they can leak and smell.

Better is to follow the ladder. Top step: reduce. Do not make the rubbish in the first place; skip extra packaging. Next step: reuse. Refill bottles and use jars again. Next: recycle. Turn old things into new things. Next: recover, like making compost or getting energy from rubbish. Bottom step, only if nothing else works: throw it away.

And the secret to recycling: sort at home. If the food stays separate, the paper, plastic and cans stay clean and valuable.

## Subtopic: The Sorting Line

At a recycling plant, rubbish rolls along a moving belt, and the plant uses everything we learned about separating mixtures.

Workers pick out big things by hand. Spinning screens, like giant sieves, sort by size. Big magnets grab the steel cans. A spinning magnetic machine flicks the aluminium cans off the belt. Fans blow light paper away. Special cameras spot different plastics and puff them into the right bins.

Then each material gets a new life. Cans are melted into new metal, again and again. Glass is crushed and melted into new bottles, forever. Paper is soaked into mush and pressed into new paper, but its fibres get shorter, so it can only do this a few times. Cooldrink bottles are shredded and turned into new bottles or even the stuffing in pillows.

And food scraps? Put them on a compost heap with dry leaves. Worms, fungi and bacteria turn them into rich soil food.

## Subtopic: Danger in the Bin

Some rubbish is dangerous. Batteries, energy-saving light bulbs, paint, old medicines and old cellphones contain poisons that can leak into soil and water. Never put them in the ordinary bin, burn them, or pour them down the drain. Take them to drop-off points at shops or municipal sites, and old medicines back to a pharmacy.

When rubbish is not managed well, everyone suffers. Rubbish dumped on open land brings rats and flies that spread disease. It blocks drains so streets flood. It washes down rivers into the sea, where it harms turtles and seabirds. Burning rubbish makes poisonous smoke.

Each of us can help: buy less, reuse, sort our rubbish, take dangerous items to the right place, and never litter. Waste does not have to be waste; sorted properly, it becomes a resource.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Household waste is a mixture. The waste hierarchy says reduce first, then reuse, recycle, recover, and dispose only as a last resort. Sorting at home keeps materials clean for recycling. Recycling plants use hand sorting, screens, magnets and air to separate materials. Hazardous waste needs special disposal, and poor waste management spreads disease, causes flooding and pollutes land, water and air.
