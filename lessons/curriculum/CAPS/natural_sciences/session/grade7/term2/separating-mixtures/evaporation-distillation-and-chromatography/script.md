# Part 1 — Expert

This session adds three methods that separate substances which filtration cannot: evaporation, distillation and chromatography. The anchor is a salt works on the West Coast near Velddrif, where seawater is let into shallow pans and the Sun and wind slowly evaporate the water, leaving glittering crusts of salt, together with a black felt-tip pen line on a strip of paper that, dipped in water, spreads into blue, purple and yellow bands. The governing idea is that evaporation separates a dissolved solid from its solvent by removing the liquid, distillation separates a liquid from a solution or from another liquid by boiling and condensing it, and chromatography separates a mixture of dissolved substances, such as dyes, by how far each travels through paper. How each method works, the apparatus, the property used and practical uses are examinable.

## Subtopic: Evaporation and Crystallisation

Evaporation is the change of a liquid into a gas at the surface of the liquid, below its boiling point. A puddle drying in the Sun and washing drying on a line are examples. Evaporation can be used to separate a dissolved solid, the solute, from the liquid it is dissolved in, the solvent: the liquid evaporates and leaves the solid behind.

In the laboratory, a solution such as salt water is poured into an evaporating dish and heated gently over a Bunsen burner or spirit burner, or simply left on a sunny windowsill. As the water evaporates, the solution becomes more concentrated, and eventually crystals of salt appear. Heating should be gentle near the end, because the last of the solution can spit, and goggles should be worn.

Evaporation recovers the solid but loses the liquid, which escapes into the air as water vapour. It works because the solute does not evaporate at the temperature used, while the solvent does.

Salt production is the classic use. In South Africa salt is produced from seawater along the coast and from salt pans inland, where underground salty water rises and evaporates; a large share of the country's salt comes from inland pans in the Northern Cape. In the pans the brine becomes more and more concentrated as water evaporates in the hot, dry wind, until salt crystallises and can be harvested. Some salt pans turn pink because tiny salt-loving algae and other microbes that live in the brine contain a red pigment.

Crystallisation is a gentler version: instead of evaporating all the water quickly, a hot, concentrated solution is left to cool and evaporate slowly. Larger, purer crystals form, and any small amounts of other dissolved substances tend to stay behind in the remaining liquid. Sugar is purified this way in sugar mills in KwaZulu-Natal: juice from crushed sugar cane is cleaned, concentrated by boiling off water, and allowed to crystallise.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of evaporation, how to recover salt from salt water, what is lost in evaporation, and the uses of evaporation and crystallisation.

## Subtopic: Distillation

Distillation separates a liquid from a solution, or one liquid from another, by boiling the liquid and then cooling the vapour so that it condenses back into a liquid, which is collected. Unlike evaporation, distillation keeps the liquid.

Simple distillation is used to obtain pure water from salt water. The salt water is heated in a flask. When the water boils, water vapour rises, leaving the salt behind, because salt has a far higher boiling point. The vapour passes into a condenser, a tube surrounded by a jacket of cold running water, where it cools and condenses into liquid water. The pure water, called the distillate, drips into a collecting beaker. A thermometer at the top of the flask shows that the vapour is at 100 degrees Celsius, the boiling point of water. Boiling chips prevent the liquid from boiling violently. At home, a simple version can be made by boiling water in a pot with a lid: drops of pure water condense on the cool lid.

Distillation can also separate two liquids with different boiling points. When a mixture of alcohol and water is heated, the alcohol, which boils at about 78 degrees Celsius, evaporates more readily than the water, which boils at 100 degrees, so the first distillate is rich in alcohol. Repeating the process, or using a tall column packed with glass beads, called fractional distillation, gives a better separation.

Distillation has many important uses. Distilled water, free of dissolved minerals, is used in car batteries, steam irons and laboratories. Desalination plants turn seawater into drinking water, either by distillation, which uses a lot of energy, or more commonly today by forcing water through very fine membranes. Crude oil is separated by fractional distillation in refineries into fractions with different boiling points: gases such as LPG, petrol, paraffin, diesel, lubricating oil and bitumen for roads. Perfumes and essential oils, such as those from buchu and other fynbos plants, are extracted by steam distillation. Liquid air can be distilled to obtain pure oxygen for hospitals and nitrogen.

The questions for this section are with you now: the steps and apparatus of simple distillation, how distillation differs from evaporation, separating liquids with different boiling points, and the uses of distillation.

## Subtopic: Chromatography and Choosing a Method

Chromatography separates a mixture of substances that are dissolved in the same solvent, such as the dyes in an ink or food colouring. The name means colour writing.

In paper chromatography, a pencil line is drawn about two centimetres from the bottom of a strip of filter paper or chromatography paper. A small, concentrated spot of the ink is placed on the line. The bottom edge of the paper is dipped in a solvent, such as water or alcohol, with the spot above the level of the solvent so that the ink does not simply dissolve away into the beaker. The solvent soaks up the paper. As it passes the spot, it dissolves the dyes and carries them up the paper. Each dye travels at a different speed: dyes that dissolve well in the solvent and cling only weakly to the paper move far, while dyes that cling strongly to the paper move only a short distance. The dyes therefore separate into distinct spots or bands, giving a chromatogram. The line is drawn in pencil because pencil graphite does not dissolve and run, whereas pen ink would.

A black felt-tip pen often separates into several colours, such as blue, purple and yellow, showing that black ink is a mixture. A dye that gives only one spot may be a pure substance. Comparing chromatograms allows identification: if the dye in an unknown sample travels the same distance as a known dye under the same conditions, they are probably the same substance.

Chromatography is used to check whether food colourings are permitted and safe, to test athletes' urine for banned drugs, to detect pesticides in food and pollutants in water, in forensic science to compare inks on documents, and in medicine to analyse blood. Modern laboratories use advanced forms such as gas chromatography, but the principle is the same.

Choosing a separation method means asking which property differs. Different sizes: sieve. Magnetic and non-magnetic: magnet. Insoluble solid and liquid: filtration. Dissolved solid, want the solid: evaporation or crystallisation. Want the liquid from a solution, or liquids with different boiling points: distillation. Several dissolved dyes: chromatography.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying evaporation collects the water; it is lost to the air. Two: saying distillation and evaporation are the same. Three: drawing the chromatography start line in pen. Four: placing the ink spot below the solvent level. Five: concluding that black ink is a pure substance because it looks like one colour.

The questions for this section are with you now: how paper chromatography works, why the line is drawn in pencil, uses of chromatography, and choosing the right separation method for a mixture.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a salt pan, a pot lid and a felt-tip pen — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Let the Sun Do It

How do you get salt out of seawater? You cannot filter it; the salt is dissolved. But you can get rid of the water.

On the West Coast, seawater is let into big shallow pans. The Sun and the wind dry the water away, little by little. That is evaporation. The salt cannot evaporate, so it is left behind as white crystals that workers rake up.

You can do it at home: put some salty water in a saucer on a sunny windowsill. After a few days, the water is gone and salt crystals are left.

The catch: the water is gone too. It floated off into the air. Evaporation gives you the solid but throws away the liquid.

If you let a solution dry slowly, you get bigger, prettier crystals. That is how sugar mills get sugar crystals from sugar-cane juice.

## Subtopic: Catch the Steam

What if you want to keep the water and leave the salt behind? Then you catch the steam.

Boil salty water in a pot with a lid. The water turns to steam and rises. The salt stays in the pot. When the steam touches the cool lid, it turns back into drops of water. Those drops are pure, with no salt. That is distillation: boil it, then cool the steam so it turns back into liquid, and collect it.

In a laboratory, the steam goes through a cold glass tube called a condenser, and the pure water drips out the end into a beaker.

Distillation can also separate liquids that boil at different temperatures. Oil refineries use it to split crude oil into petrol, diesel and other fuels. Perfume makers use it to get sweet-smelling oils out of plants like buchu.

## Subtopic: The Hidden Colours

Draw a dot with a black felt-tip pen on a strip of paper, just above a pencil line. Stand the strip in a little water so the water is below the dot. Watch.

The water creeps up the paper. When it reaches the dot, it carries the ink upward. But the different colours in the ink travel at different speeds. Soon the black dot splits into blue, purple and maybe yellow. Black ink was a mixture all along. This is chromatography, colour writing.

We use pencil for the line because pencil does not run in the water. Pen would.

Scientists use chromatography to check food colourings, to catch athletes using banned drugs, and to solve crimes by comparing inks.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Evaporation removes a liquid to leave a dissolved solid, like salt from seawater, but the liquid is lost. Distillation boils a liquid and cools the vapour to collect the pure liquid, like water from salt water or fuels from crude oil. Chromatography separates mixtures of dissolved colours on paper. Choose the method by asking what is different about the parts.
