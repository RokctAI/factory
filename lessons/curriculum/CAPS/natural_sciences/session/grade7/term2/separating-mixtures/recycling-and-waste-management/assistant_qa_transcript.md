### subtopic_1 — Waste as a Mixture and the Waste Hierarchy
**Assistant:** Why do landfill sites produce gas, and is it dangerous?

**Tutor:** Landfill sites produce gas because the organic waste buried in them, such as food scraps, garden waste and paper, is broken down by bacteria. Once waste is buried and covered, oxygen is quickly used up, and the decomposition continues by bacteria that do not need oxygen. These bacteria produce landfill gas, roughly half methane and half carbon dioxide, with small amounts of other gases, some of which cause the bad smell. This gas can be dangerous in several ways. Methane is flammable, and if it collects in enclosed spaces, such as buildings or drains near a landfill, it can explode; landfill fires can also burn underground for long periods and release toxic smoke. Methane is also a powerful greenhouse gas, trapping much more heat than carbon dioxide over a period of decades, so uncontrolled landfill gas contributes to climate change. Properly managed landfills therefore have systems of pipes buried in the waste to collect the gas. The gas can be burned in a flare, which turns the methane into less harmful carbon dioxide, or, better still, used as fuel to generate electricity. Several South African landfill sites, including some in eThekwini and Johannesburg, have had landfill gas-to-electricity projects that supply power to the grid. The best solution, though, is higher up the waste hierarchy: keeping organic waste out of landfills altogether by composting it, which turns it into useful fertiliser instead of methane.

### subtopic_2 — Sorting and Recycling Materials
**Assistant:** Why can metals and glass be recycled forever but paper only a few times?

**Tutor:** Because recycling metals and glass melts them down to their basic material, which is unchanged by melting, while recycling paper physically damages the fibres that paper is made of. A steel can is mostly iron atoms; an aluminium can is aluminium atoms. When they are melted in a furnace, the metal becomes a liquid that can be purified and cast into any new shape, and the atoms themselves are not damaged, so the new metal is just as good as the old. The same is true of glass: crushed and melted at high temperature, it becomes molten glass again with the same properties, which is why a glass bottle can be recycled into a new bottle again and again. Paper is different. It is a mat of tiny cellulose fibres from wood or other plants, tangled and pressed together. To recycle it, paper is soaked and stirred into a pulp so the fibres separate, then reformed into a new sheet. Each time this happens, the fibres are cut, broken and shortened, and they lose some of their ability to bond to each other. After about five to seven cycles, the fibres are too short to hold together as strong paper, and they wash out of the process. That is why recycled paper often has new fibres added, why the lowest grades of recycled fibre end up in egg boxes and tissue, and why reducing paper use matters, even though paper recycling is still very worthwhile.

### subtopic_3 — Hazardous Waste and Poor Waste Management
**Assistant:** Why is electronic waste called both a problem and a resource?

**Tutor:** Because old cellphones, computers, televisions and chargers contain both dangerous substances and valuable materials, and what happens to them decides which side wins. Electronic waste is the fastest-growing type of waste in the world, because people replace devices often. Inside these devices are small amounts of hazardous substances: lead in old screens and solder, mercury in some displays and switches, cadmium and lithium in batteries, and flame-retardant chemicals in plastics. If e-waste is dumped, these can leach into soil and water; if it is burned in the open to recover copper from wires, as happens on some informal dump sites around the world, the smoke poisons the people doing the burning and the surrounding community. But the same devices also contain valuable materials: copper, aluminium, steel, plastics, and tiny amounts of precious metals such as gold, silver, palladium and platinum. A tonne of old cellphones can contain far more gold than a tonne of gold ore from a typical mine. Recovering these materials safely, in proper facilities, reduces the need for new mining, saves energy and creates jobs; this is sometimes called urban mining. South Africa has laws that make producers responsible for collecting and recycling some types of electronic waste, and there are registered e-waste recyclers and drop-off points in many cities. The best steps for individuals are to use devices for longer, repair them where possible, pass on working devices to others, and take dead ones to a registered e-waste collection point rather than the bin.
