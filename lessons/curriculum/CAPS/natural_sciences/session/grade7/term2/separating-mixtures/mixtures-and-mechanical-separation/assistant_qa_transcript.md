### subtopic_1 — What a Mixture Is
**Assistant:** Is salt water a mixture or a compound, since you cannot see the salt?

**Tutor:** Salt water is a mixture, even though it looks like one clear liquid. The test is not whether you can see the parts, but whether they are chemically joined and whether they keep their own properties. When salt dissolves in water, the salt breaks up into particles so tiny that they are spread evenly among the water particles and cannot be seen, even with a microscope. But the salt has not combined with the water to form a new substance. The salt water tastes salty, showing that the salt keeps its properties. The amount of salt can vary: you can dissolve a pinch or a spoonful in the same glass of water, whereas a compound always has its elements in a fixed ratio. And the salt can be recovered without any chemical reaction, simply by evaporating the water, which leaves the salt crystals behind; this is exactly how salt has been produced along the coasts of South Africa and in inland salt pans, such as those near Soutpansberg and the Kalahari, for centuries. Salt water also boils at a slightly higher temperature than pure water and freezes at a lower one, which shows it is not a single pure substance. Compare that with water itself, which is a compound of hydrogen and oxygen: you cannot separate it by evaporation or filtering, only by a chemical process such as passing electricity through it. So the rule is: if the parts keep their own properties and can be separated by physical means, it is a mixture.

### subtopic_2 — Hand Sorting, Sieving and Magnets
**Assistant:** How did gold panning start the gold rushes in South Africa, and why did it stop being enough?

**Tutor:** Gold panning was the first way that prospectors found and collected gold, because it needs only a pan, water and patience. In the 1870s alluvial gold, meaning loose gold grains and nuggets washed out of rocks and deposited in river gravels, was found around Pilgrim's Rest and Lydenburg in what is now Mpumalanga, and later at Barberton. Prospectors swirled river gravel in pans and in larger wooden troughs called sluices, through which water ran over ridges that trapped the heavy gold while the lighter sand washed away. These methods work because gold is so much denser than the rock and sand around it. The great change came in 1886, when gold was found in the rocks of the Witwatersrand, where Johannesburg now stands. This gold was not in loose grains in rivers; it was in tiny, mostly invisible particles spread through hard, ancient layers of rock called reefs, which ran deep underground. Panning was useless there. The rock had to be mined from ever deeper shafts, crushed into fine powder, and the gold extracted by chemical methods, first by mixing the crushed rock with mercury and later, from 1890, by dissolving the gold with a cyanide solution and recovering it, which is far more effective but involves poisonous chemicals. This required huge investment, large companies and an enormous migrant labour force, which shaped South Africa's economy, cities and history, including deep social injustices.

### subtopic_3 — Filtration and Planning a Separation
**Assistant:** How can you make dirty water safer to drink if there is no tap water available?

**Tutor:** By combining several separation and cleaning steps, because no single step removes everything. First, let the water stand in a clean container so that heavier particles settle to the bottom, a process called sedimentation, and then carefully pour off the clearer water from the top, which is called decanting. Second, filter it. A clean cloth folded several times removes larger particles; a homemade filter, such as a cut plastic bottle filled with layers of gravel, coarse sand and fine sand, with a cloth at the neck, removes finer cloudiness. Water treatment works use the same idea on a giant scale with deep sand beds. But filtering mainly makes the water clearer; it does not reliably remove the bacteria, viruses and parasites that cause diseases such as cholera, typhoid and diarrhoea, which kill many young children where water is unsafe. So the third and most important step is disinfection. Boiling water vigorously for at least one minute kills germs. Adding a small measured amount of household bleach, as recommended by health authorities, also works; the usual guideline is about one teaspoon of unscented bleach in twenty-five litres of water, left to stand for at least thirty minutes before drinking. Leaving clear water in a clear plastic bottle in direct sunlight for six hours or more, called solar disinfection, is another tested method. Finally, store the clean water in a covered, clean container and pour it out rather than dipping in cups, so it does not become contaminated again.
