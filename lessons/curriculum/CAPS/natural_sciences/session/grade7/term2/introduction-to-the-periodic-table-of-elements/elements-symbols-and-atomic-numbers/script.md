# Part 1 — Expert

This session teaches how to read a single square of the Periodic Table. The anchor is a card game invented by a Grade 7 class in Bloemfontein, in which each card shows one square of the table, and players must name the element, its symbol, its atomic number and where it sits. The governing idea is that every element has a name, a one- or two-letter chemical symbol, an atomic number equal to the number of protons in its atoms, and a fixed position on the table given by its period and group, and that these four facts are linked: the atomic number fixes the position. Reading an element square, writing symbols correctly, the meaning of atomic number, and the names and symbols of the first twenty elements and common metals are examinable, and they are used in every chemistry lesson that follows.

## Subtopic: Names and Symbols

Every element has a name. Some names are ancient, such as gold, iron, copper and tin, which were known in prehistoric times. Others come from Greek or Latin words describing a property: hydrogen means water-maker, oxygen acid-maker, chlorine pale green, and bromine stench. Some honour places, such as germanium for Germany, polonium for Poland and americium for America; some honour scientists, such as einsteinium and mendelevium; and some honour planets and mythology, such as uranium, neptunium and titanium.

Every element also has a chemical symbol, a shorthand used by scientists all over the world, whatever language they speak. The system was introduced by the Swedish chemist Jöns Jacob Berzelius in 1813. The rules are strict. A symbol has one or two letters. The first letter is always a capital letter; the second, if there is one, is always a small letter. So cobalt is Co, while CO, with two capitals, means carbon monoxide, a compound of carbon and oxygen.

Many symbols come from the English name: H for hydrogen, He for helium, C for carbon, N for nitrogen, O for oxygen, Ne for neon, Mg for magnesium, Al for aluminium, Si for silicon, S for sulphur, Cl for chlorine, Ca for calcium and Zn for zinc. When two elements start with the same letter, a second letter distinguishes them: carbon C, calcium Ca, chlorine Cl, chromium Cr, cobalt Co, copper Cu.

Some symbols come from the Latin name of the element, which can be confusing at first. Sodium is Na from natrium. Potassium is K from kalium. Iron is Fe from ferrum. Copper is Cu from cuprum. Silver is Ag from argentum. Gold is Au from aurum. Tin is Sn from stannum. Lead is Pb from plumbum, which is also why a person who fixes pipes, once made of lead, is called a plumber. Mercury is Hg from hydrargyrum, meaning liquid silver.

The questions for this section are with you now: the rules for writing chemical symbols, the symbols of common elements, and the elements whose symbols come from Latin.

## Subtopic: Atomic Number and the Element Square

Every atom has a tiny, dense centre called the nucleus, which contains particles called protons, each with a positive electric charge, and usually particles called neutrons, with no charge. Around the nucleus move much lighter particles called electrons, each with a negative charge. In a neutral atom the number of electrons equals the number of protons.

The atomic number of an element is the number of protons in the nucleus of each of its atoms. It is the element's identity card: every atom of hydrogen has one proton, every atom of helium two, every atom of carbon six, every atom of oxygen eight, every atom of iron twenty-six, and every atom of gold seventy-nine. If the number of protons changed, the atom would be a different element. No two elements share an atomic number.

The modern Periodic Table is arranged in order of increasing atomic number, starting with hydrogen at 1 and ending, at present, with oganesson at 118. Reading across each row and then on to the next row, the atomic numbers increase one at a time with no gaps, because every element from 1 to 118 has now been discovered or made.

Each square of the Periodic Table usually shows the element's chemical symbol in large letters, its atomic number, often at the top, its name, and often a decimal number for its relative atomic mass, a measure of how heavy its atoms are compared with others. For carbon, a typical square shows 6, C, carbon, 12. The layout of squares differs slightly between printed tables, so a key is usually given; the whole number that increases by one from square to square is always the atomic number.

The first twenty elements in order are: hydrogen H 1, helium He 2, lithium Li 3, beryllium Be 4, boron B 5, carbon C 6, nitrogen N 7, oxygen O 8, fluorine F 9, neon Ne 10, sodium Na 11, magnesium Mg 12, aluminium Al 13, silicon Si 14, phosphorus P 15, sulphur S 16, chlorine Cl 17, argon Ar 18, potassium K 19, calcium Ca 20.

The questions for this section are with you now: the particles of an atom, the meaning of atomic number, how to read an element square, and the first twenty elements.

## Subtopic: Position on the Table

An element's position on the Periodic Table is given by its period and its group. The period is the row, numbered 1 to 7 from top to bottom. The group is the column, numbered 1 to 18 from left to right. Because the table is arranged by atomic number, the atomic number fixes the position, and the position tells us a great deal about the element.

The first period contains only hydrogen and helium. The second and third periods each contain eight elements, lithium to neon and sodium to argon. From period four, the table widens to include the transition metals in the middle block, groups 3 to 12, such as iron, copper, zinc, silver and gold. Two rows are usually printed separately at the bottom of the table, to keep it from becoming very wide; they belong in periods six and seven.

Some groups have family names. Group 1, apart from hydrogen, is the alkali metals: lithium, sodium and potassium, soft metals that react strongly with water. Group 2 is the alkaline earth metals, including magnesium and calcium. Group 17 is the halogens, including fluorine, chlorine and iodine, reactive non-metals. Group 18 is the noble gases, helium, neon and argon, which hardly react at all, which is why helium is safe in party balloons and neon and argon are used in lighting.

Using position: sodium, atomic number 11, is in period 3, group 1, so it is a metal on the far left, an alkali metal. Chlorine, atomic number 17, is in period 3, group 17, so it is a non-metal on the right, a halogen. Silicon, atomic number 14, is in period 3, group 14, on the staircase, so it is a semi-metal. Argon, atomic number 18, is in period 3, group 18, a noble gas.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: writing symbols with two capitals, such as CA or NA. Two: guessing that S is sodium; S is sulphur and sodium is Na. Three: confusing atomic number with relative atomic mass; the atomic number is the whole number that goes up by one. Four: thinking a chemical reaction can change one element into another; only nuclear reactions can change the number of protons. Five: mixing up periods and groups; periods are rows, groups are columns.

The questions for this section are with you now: identifying periods and groups, the families of groups 1, 17 and 18, and using atomic number and position to describe an element.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with an ID card, a nickname and a seat in a classroom — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Short Names

Every element has a name and a short name, called a symbol. Scientists all over the world use the same symbols, whatever language they speak.

The rules: one or two letters. The first letter is always a capital. The second letter, if there is one, is always small. So calcium is Ca, not CA.

Lots of symbols are easy: H for hydrogen, O for oxygen, C for carbon, He for helium, Mg for magnesium.

Some are sneaky, because they come from old Latin names. Sodium is Na. Potassium is K. Iron is Fe. Copper is Cu. Silver is Ag. Gold is Au. Lead is Pb, from plumbum, which is where the word plumber comes from, because pipes used to be made of lead.

Keep the short names in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: The ID Number

Every element also has an ID number, its atomic number.

Atoms have a tiny centre called the nucleus, and in it are particles called protons. The atomic number is the number of protons. Hydrogen has one proton, so its atomic number is 1. Carbon has 6. Oxygen has 8. Gold has 79.

The protons decide what the element is. Change the number of protons and you have a different element. That is why no chemistry trick could ever turn lead into gold.

Each square on the Periodic Table is like an ID card. It shows the symbol, big in the middle, the atomic number, the name, and usually the mass. The squares are in order of atomic number: 1, 2, 3, 4, all the way to 118, with nothing missing.

Hold the ID number in mind and try this part's questions.

## Subtopic: Where Do You Sit?

Each element has its own seat on the Periodic Table, like a seat in a classroom. Its row is called a period, and its column is called a group.

Some columns are famous families. Column 1 is the alkali metals: soft metals like sodium and potassium that fizz in water. Column 17 is the halogens, like chlorine, used to clean swimming pools. Column 18 is the noble gases, like helium and neon, which are so stable they hardly ever react, which is why helium is safe in party balloons.

Sodium sits in period 3, group 1: a metal on the far left. Chlorine sits in period 3, group 17: a non-metal on the right.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Every element has a name and a symbol with a capital first letter and a small second letter; some symbols come from Latin, like Na for sodium and Fe for iron. The atomic number is the number of protons and is the element's ID. The table is in order of atomic number. Rows are periods and columns are groups, and groups 1, 17 and 18 are the alkali metals, halogens and noble gases.

Hold the short name, the ID number and the seat, and take the final questions of the lesson.
