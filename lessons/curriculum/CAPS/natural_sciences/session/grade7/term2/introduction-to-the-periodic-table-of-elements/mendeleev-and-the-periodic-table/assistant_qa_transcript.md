### subtopic_1 — Elements and the Need for a Table
**Assistant:** Why is platinum so important to South Africa and to the world?

**Tutor:** Platinum is important because of a combination of unusual properties, and South Africa matters because it has by far the largest deposits on Earth. Platinum is a dense, silvery metal that does not rust or tarnish, resists attack by almost all acids, has a very high melting point and, most importantly, is an excellent catalyst, a substance that speeds up chemical reactions without being used up. Its biggest use is in catalytic converters in car exhausts, where a thin coating of platinum and the related metals palladium and rhodium changes poisonous gases such as carbon monoxide into less harmful ones. Platinum is also used in jewellery, in laboratory equipment, in some cancer medicines, in electronics, in the petroleum industry and in fuel cells, which produce electricity from hydrogen without burning it and could become important for clean energy. The Bushveld Igneous Complex, a huge body of ancient rock stretching across Limpopo, North West, Mpumalanga and Gauteng, formed about two thousand million years ago when molten rock rose from deep in the Earth and cooled slowly, allowing platinum-group metals to collect in thin layers. The most famous layer, the Merensky Reef, was found in 1924. Mining these metals provides many jobs and export earnings, but it also raises questions about safe working conditions, the sharing of benefits with local communities, water use and environmental damage, which South Africans continue to debate.

### subtopic_2 — Mendeleev's Table
**Assistant:** Why was making correct predictions so important for Mendeleev's table to be accepted?

**Tutor:** Because a prediction that comes true is one of the strongest kinds of evidence in science. Anyone can arrange known facts into a pattern after the event; the real test is whether the pattern tells you something you did not already know. When Mendeleev published his table, several other chemists had also noticed repeating properties and had proposed arrangements. What made Mendeleev different was that he used his table to make specific, risky claims about elements that nobody had ever seen: that an element would be found below aluminium with an atomic mass of about sixty-eight, a low melting point and a density of about six grams per cubic centimetre. If the predicted elements had never been found, or had turned out very different, his table would have been shown to be wrong. When gallium was discovered with an atomic mass of about seventy, a melting point so low that it melts in the hand, and a density close to his prediction, it was a striking success. There is even a famous story that Mendeleev wrote to the discoverer suggesting that his first density measurement was wrong, and a careful new measurement agreed with Mendeleev's value. Scandium and germanium followed. This is how science works more generally: a good theory not only explains what is already known, it makes testable predictions, and the more surprising the predictions that come true, the more confidence scientists have in it.

### subtopic_3 — Metals, Non-metals and Semi-metals on the Table
**Assistant:** Why is hydrogen placed above the metals if it is a non-metal?

**Tutor:** Because the Periodic Table is arranged by atomic number and by the arrangement of the tiny particles called electrons in atoms, and on that basis hydrogen fits at the start of the first row. Hydrogen has atomic number one, the smallest of all, so it must come first. Its atom has just one electron in its outer region, which is also true of lithium, sodium and potassium in the first group, so it is usually placed at the top of that group. Like those metals, hydrogen can lose that electron in chemical reactions. But in most of its physical properties hydrogen behaves like a non-metal: it is a colourless gas at room temperature, made of pairs of atoms, it does not conduct electricity, and it does not look or behave like a metal. In some ways it also resembles the elements in group 17, such as fluorine and chlorine, because it can gain one electron to fill its outer region, so some tables place it above that group or float it on its own above the table. Its position is therefore a compromise, and different tables handle it differently. Scientists predict that under the enormous pressures inside giant planets like Jupiter, hydrogen is squeezed into a liquid that does conduct electricity like a metal, which makes the puzzle even more interesting. For Grade 7, remember that hydrogen sits at the top left but is a non-metal, the one exception to the rule that the left side of the table is metals.
