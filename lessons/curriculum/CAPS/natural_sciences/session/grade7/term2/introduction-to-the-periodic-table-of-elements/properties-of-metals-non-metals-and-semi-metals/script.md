# Part 1 — Expert

This session describes how metals, non-metals and semi-metals behave, and why their properties make them useful. The anchor is the inside of an ordinary electrical plug and cable from a South African home: copper wires carry the current, a plastic coating made mostly from non-metal elements keeps it in, and the device it powers may contain a silicon chip, a semi-metal, that controls it. The governing idea is that metals are generally shiny, good conductors of heat and electricity, malleable and ductile, and mostly solid with high melting points; non-metals are generally dull, poor conductors and brittle when solid, and many are gases; and semi-metals have properties between the two. The properties of each group, simple tests that show them, and uses that depend on them are examinable, and they link directly to the next topic, properties of materials.

## Subtopic: Properties of Metals

Metals make up about three quarters of the elements, and they share a set of physical properties, which are properties that can be observed or measured without changing the substance into something new.

Lustre: metals are shiny when freshly cut or polished. Many metals become dull over time because their surfaces react with air or water, for example iron rusting or silver tarnishing, but a scratch or a polish reveals the shine underneath.

Electrical conductivity: metals are good conductors of electricity; electric current flows through them easily. Copper and aluminium are used for electrical wiring and power lines for this reason. Silver is the best conductor of all but is too expensive for most wiring.

Thermal conductivity: metals are good conductors of heat. A metal spoon in a hot cup of tea quickly becomes hot at the handle. Pots and pans are made of metals such as aluminium, stainless steel and copper so that heat flows quickly from the stove to the food.

Malleability: metals can be hammered or pressed into thin sheets without breaking. Aluminium foil, corrugated iron roofing and the bodies of cars are made from metal sheets. Gold is so malleable that it can be beaten into leaf thinner than a hundred-thousandth of a millimetre.

Ductility: metals can be drawn out into thin wires. Copper wire in cables and the steel wire in fences show ductility.

Sonority: metals ring when struck, which is why bells, gongs and the triangle in an orchestra are made of metal.

Other common properties: most metals are hard, strong and dense, and most have high melting and boiling points, so they are solid at room temperature. Iron melts at about 1 538 degrees Celsius and tungsten, used in old light bulb filaments, at about 3 422 degrees. The exception is mercury, which is a liquid at room temperature, melting at about minus 39 degrees, which is why it was once used in thermometers. Some metals, including iron, nickel and cobalt, are magnetic, but most metals, such as copper, aluminium and gold, are not, so magnetism is not a general property of metals.

The questions for this section are with you now: the six main properties of metals, an everyday use that depends on each, and the exceptions mercury and magnetism.

## Subtopic: Properties of Non-metals

Non-metals are found on the right of the Periodic Table, together with hydrogen. There are far fewer of them than metals, but they include the elements that make up most of the air and most of living things: oxygen, nitrogen, carbon and hydrogen.

At room temperature, many non-metals are gases: hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine, chlorine and all the noble gases. One, bromine, is a dark red-brown liquid. The rest are solids, such as carbon, sulphur, phosphorus and iodine.

Solid non-metals usually have the opposite properties to metals. They are dull, with no metallic lustre; sulphur is a dull yellow powder, carbon as charcoal or coal is dull black. They are brittle: when hit with a hammer, a lump of sulphur shatters into pieces instead of flattening, so they are neither malleable nor ductile. They are poor conductors of heat and electricity; they are insulators. Most non-metals have low melting and boiling points compared with metals; sulphur melts at only about 115 degrees Celsius. They are not sonorous, and they usually have lower densities than metals.

Carbon is an important exception to some of these rules, because it exists in different forms. Graphite, the form in pencil lead, is a non-metal that conducts electricity, which is why it is used in batteries and electric motors; it is also slippery and soft. Diamond, another form of pure carbon, is the hardest natural substance and an excellent insulator. Both are carbon atoms, arranged differently.

Non-metals are vital. Oxygen is needed for respiration and burning. Nitrogen is used to make fertilisers and to keep packaged food fresh. Carbon is the basis of all living things and of fuels. Chlorine is used to purify drinking water and swimming pools. Sulphur is used to make sulphuric acid, a key industrial chemical, much of it made in South Africa for mining and fertilisers. Silicon is a semi-metal, but it is joined with the non-metal oxygen in sand and glass.

The questions for this section are with you now: the states of non-metals at room temperature, the properties of solid non-metals, and the special case of carbon.

## Subtopic: Semi-metals and Comparing the Three Groups

Semi-metals, also called metalloids, are the elements along the staircase line on the Periodic Table: mainly boron, silicon, germanium, arsenic, antimony and tellurium. Their properties are in between those of metals and non-metals.

Silicon is the best example. It looks shiny and grey, like a metal, but it is brittle and shatters when hit, like a non-metal. Its most important property is that it is a semiconductor: it conducts electricity better than an insulator but much less well than a metal, and its conductivity can be changed by adding tiny amounts of other elements, a process called doping, or by changing its temperature or shining light on it. This makes it possible to build switches that control electric current without moving parts. Billions of these switches, called transistors, are built onto a single silicon chip in every cellphone, computer and modern appliance. Solar panels use silicon to turn light into electricity; South Africa has built large solar power plants in the Northern Cape that use silicon panels. Germanium was used in early electronics before silicon. Boron is used in heat-resistant glass and in some cleaning products.

A comparison of the three groups. Metals: shiny; good conductors of heat and electricity; malleable and ductile; sonorous; mostly high melting points and solid; on the left and centre of the table. Non-metals: dull; poor conductors, insulators, except graphite; brittle when solid; low melting points; many gases; on the right. Semi-metals: may look shiny; brittle; semiconductors; on the staircase.

Simple tests can classify an unknown element. Check the appearance, scratching or polishing a small area. Test conductivity by placing the sample in a simple circuit with a cell and a bulb: if the bulb lights, the sample conducts. Tap it gently with a hammer on a hard surface, wearing eye protection: a metal flattens, a non-metal shatters.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying all metals are magnetic. Two: saying all metals are solid; mercury is a liquid. Three: saying all non-metals are gases; carbon, sulphur and iodine are solids, bromine a liquid. Four: saying no non-metal conducts; graphite does. Five: calling silicon a metal because it is shiny; it is brittle and a semiconductor, a semi-metal.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a plug, a hammer and a silicon chip — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Metal Superpowers

Look inside an electric cable. In the middle are copper wires. Why copper? Because metals have superpowers.

They shine when polished, like a new coin.

They let electricity flow through them, which is why wires are made of copper. They also let heat flow, which is why a metal spoon in hot tea gets hot all the way to the handle.

You can hammer them flat without breaking them, like aluminium foil or a tin roof. That is called malleable. You can stretch them into thin wires. That is called ductile.

They ring when you hit them, like a school bell.

And almost all are solid, with very high melting points. One odd one out is mercury, which is a liquid. And careful: only a few metals, like iron and nickel, stick to a magnet. A copper coin or an aluminium can will not.

Keep the superpowers in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: The Opposite Team

Non-metals are mostly the opposite.

Many are gases you cannot even see, like the oxygen and nitrogen in the air you breathe.

The solid ones, like yellow sulphur or black charcoal, are dull, not shiny. Hit them with a hammer and they crumble or shatter. They do not let electricity or heat through easily, so we call them insulators. That is why the plastic around a cable, made mostly from non-metal elements, stops you from getting a shock.

There is one surprise: carbon. In pencil lead it is called graphite, and it does let electricity through. In a diamond, the very same carbon is the hardest natural thing on Earth. Same atoms, arranged differently.

Hold the opposite team in mind and try this part's questions.

## Subtopic: Somewhere in Between

On the staircase of the Periodic Table sit the semi-metals. They are a bit of both.

Silicon is the star. It looks shiny like a metal, but it shatters like a non-metal. And it lets electricity through only a little, an amount that scientists can control. That makes it perfect for computer chips, which are full of billions of tiny switches. There is a silicon chip in every cellphone. Silicon in solar panels turns sunlight into electricity, like the big solar farms in the Northern Cape. And silicon comes from ordinary sand.

How can you tell which team an element is on? Look at it: shiny or dull? Put it in a circuit with a bulb: does the bulb light? Tap it gently: does it flatten or shatter?

Pocket summary of the lesson. Metals are shiny, conduct heat and electricity, can be hammered and stretched, ring when struck and are mostly solid with high melting points. Non-metals are mostly the opposite: dull, brittle, insulators and many are gases, with graphite as a surprise conductor. Semi-metals, like silicon, are in between and are used in chips and solar panels.
