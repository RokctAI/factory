### subtopic_1 — Names and Symbols
**Assistant:** Why do chemists use symbols from Latin, when most people do not speak Latin?

**Tutor:** Because when the symbol system was created, Latin was the shared language of science across Europe, and the symbols were designed to be international, not tied to any modern language. In the early 1800s, scholars in Sweden, France, Germany, Britain and Russia often wrote their scientific books in Latin or knew it well, and many elements had been known since ancient times by Latin names. Berzelius, who proposed the symbols, chose letters from the Latin names partly so that no single modern language would be favoured. The choice has a lasting benefit: a chemist in Johannesburg, Tokyo, São Paulo or Cairo reads Fe as iron, Na as sodium and Au as gold, even though the words for those elements are completely different in isiZulu, Japanese, Portuguese and Arabic. In isiZulu, for example, gold is igolide and iron is insimbi, and in Afrikaans they are goud and yster, but in every country the symbols are the same. This makes chemical equations, formulae and the Periodic Table a truly universal language. The Latin symbols must simply be memorised; there are only about a dozen common ones, and learning the Latin names, such as aurum for gold and ferrum for iron, makes them easier to remember. They also leave traces in everyday English: ferrous metals contain iron, Argentina was named after silver, and plumbing comes from lead.

### subtopic_2 — Atomic Number and the Element Square
**Assistant:** If gold has seventy-nine protons and lead has eighty-two, can lead be turned into gold?

**Tutor:** In principle yes, but not by any chemical method, and in practice it is not worth doing. For centuries alchemists tried to turn cheap metals such as lead into gold by heating, mixing and dissolving them with all kinds of substances. They always failed, and modern science explains why: chemical reactions only rearrange the electrons around atoms and join atoms together in new ways; they never change the number of protons in the nucleus. Since the number of protons decides which element an atom is, no chemical reaction can change one element into another. Changing the nucleus requires nuclear reactions, which involve enormous amounts of energy, such as those in nuclear reactors, particle accelerators or stars. In 1980 scientists did produce a few atoms of gold from bismuth, the element next to lead, by smashing particles into it in a particle accelerator, which removed protons from the nucleus. But the amount was tiny, a few thousand atoms, and the cost was vastly greater than the value of the gold. Natural processes in stars do make gold: scientists now think most gold in the universe was made in violent collisions between neutron stars, which scattered heavy elements into space, where they became part of new stars and planets, including Earth. So the gold mined in the Witwatersrand was made in a cosmic explosion before the Sun was born. The idea that the atomic number defines an element is what explains why the alchemists' dream was impossible by chemistry.

### subtopic_3 — Position on the Table
**Assistant:** Why do the noble gases hardly ever react with anything?

**Tutor:** Because their atoms are already in an especially stable arrangement, so they have very little tendency to gain, lose or share electrons, which is what chemical reactions involve. Electrons around an atom are arranged in layers, sometimes called shells, and each layer can hold a certain number of electrons. Atoms of most elements have outer layers that are only partly filled, and they react with other atoms in ways that let them reach a filled outer layer, by giving away, taking or sharing electrons. Sodium easily gives away its single outer electron; chlorine easily takes one more to fill its outer layer; that is why sodium and chlorine react violently to form table salt. The noble gases, helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon and radon, in group 18, already have full outer layers. They have nothing to gain by reacting, so they stay as single, separate atoms and are very unreactive. This was once thought to be absolute, and they were called inert gases, but in 1962 the chemist Neil Bartlett made a compound of xenon, and a few compounds of krypton and xenon are now known, made under special conditions. Their unreactivity makes them useful. Helium fills balloons and airships safely, unlike flammable hydrogen; argon is used inside light bulbs and in welding to stop hot metal reacting with air; and neon glows red-orange when electricity passes through it in signs.
