# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the Periodic Table of the Elements, the single most important chart in chemistry. The anchor is a large Periodic Table on a classroom wall in Gqeberha, with its colourful blocks of squares, set beside a story: in 1869 the Russian chemist Dmitri Mendeleev arranged the sixty-three elements then known into a table, left gaps for elements nobody had discovered yet, and predicted their properties, and within twenty years three of those predicted elements were found. The governing idea is that the Periodic Table is a classification system for the elements, arranging them in order and grouping those with similar properties, with metals on the left, non-metals on the far right and semi-metals on a staircase between. What an element is, the history of the table, and the positions of metals, non-metals and semi-metals are examinable, and the table is used throughout Grade 8 and 9 and in Physical Sciences.

## Subtopic: Elements and the Need for a Table

All matter is made of tiny particles. An element is a pure substance made of only one kind of atom, which cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical means. Iron, copper, gold, oxygen, carbon and helium are elements. Water is not an element: it can be broken down into hydrogen and oxygen, so it is a compound, a substance made of two or more elements chemically joined.

There are one hundred and eighteen known elements. About ninety occur naturally on Earth; the rest have been made in laboratories, usually existing for only a fraction of a second. Everything around us, rocks, air, water, living things and every manufactured object, is made of elements, alone or in combination. The human body is mostly oxygen, carbon, hydrogen and nitrogen, with calcium and phosphorus in bones and small amounts of many others. South Africa's wealth has been built on elements in its rocks: gold from the Witwatersrand, platinum from the Bushveld Complex, which holds most of the world's known platinum reserves, manganese and chromium, iron, coal, which is mostly carbon, and diamonds, which are pure carbon.

By the middle of the 1800s chemists had discovered more than sixty elements and had learned a great deal about how each one behaved. They noticed that some elements were very alike. Lithium, sodium and potassium are all soft, shiny metals that react violently with water. Chlorine, bromine and iodine are all coloured, poisonous non-metals that react strongly with metals. Chemists wanted a way to organise the elements that would show these family resemblances and make it easier to learn, remember and predict their behaviour, in the same way that biologists classify living things into groups.

Several scientists tried. Some arranged the elements in order of the mass of their atoms and noticed that similar properties seemed to repeat at regular intervals, like the notes of a musical scale. But their arrangements broke down for heavier elements.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of an element, the difference between an element and a compound, and why chemists wanted to classify the elements.

## Subtopic: Mendeleev's Table

In 1869 Dmitri Mendeleev, a chemistry professor in St Petersburg in Russia, published the table that became the basis of the modern Periodic Table. He wrote the name, the atomic mass and the properties of each of the sixty-three known elements on separate cards, and he arranged and rearranged them, looking for a pattern.

He placed the elements in order of increasing atomic mass, in rows, and started a new row whenever the properties began to repeat, so that elements with similar properties fell into the same column. Lithium, sodium and potassium lined up in one column; fluorine, chlorine and bromine in another.

Mendeleev's genius lay in two bold decisions. First, where an element did not fit the pattern, he trusted the pattern more than the measurement. In a few places he swapped the order of two elements so that each sat in the column with its chemical relatives, arguing that their measured masses were wrong or that properties mattered more. Second, he left gaps. Where the next known element did not have the right properties for the next position, he left a blank space and claimed that an undiscovered element belonged there. He went further and predicted the properties of these missing elements, such as their atomic masses, densities and the compounds they would form.

For one gap, below aluminium, he predicted an element he called eka-aluminium. In 1875 the French chemist Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran discovered gallium, and its properties matched Mendeleev's predictions closely. Scandium was discovered in 1879 and germanium in 1886, filling two more gaps and again matching his predictions. These successful predictions convinced scientists that the table revealed something real about nature, not just a convenient filing system.

The table is called periodic because properties repeat periodically, at regular intervals, as one moves through the elements in order. Later scientists discovered that the correct order is by atomic number, the number of protons in an atom, rather than by mass, which explained why Mendeleev had needed to swap some pairs. Other chemists, such as Lothar Meyer in Germany, developed similar tables at about the same time, but Mendeleev is usually given most credit because of his predictions.

The questions for this section are with you now: how Mendeleev arranged the elements, why he left gaps, and how the discovery of gallium supported his table.

## Subtopic: Metals, Non-metals and Semi-metals on the Table

The modern Periodic Table arranges all one hundred and eighteen elements in order of atomic number, in horizontal rows called periods and vertical columns called groups. There are seven periods and eighteen groups. Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties, just as in Mendeleev's columns.

One of the most useful patterns on the table is the position of the metals and non-metals. Metals are on the left and in the centre of the table, and they make up the large majority of elements, about three quarters of them. Sodium, magnesium, iron, copper, zinc, silver, gold and platinum are metals. Non-metals are on the far right of the table, together with hydrogen, which is placed at the top left but is a non-metal. Oxygen, nitrogen, carbon, sulphur, chlorine, helium and neon are non-metals.

Between them, a staircase or zigzag line runs diagonally down from near boron to near astatine. The elements along this line are the semi-metals, also called metalloids, which have some properties of metals and some of non-metals. Boron, silicon, germanium, arsenic, antimony and tellurium are usually counted as semi-metals. Silicon, the most important, is used to make computer chips and solar cells.

Many Periodic Tables are printed with metals, non-metals and semi-metals in different colours, which makes the pattern easy to see. A simple rule summarises it: to the left of the staircase are metals, to the right are non-metals, and on the staircase are semi-metals.

The arrangement allows predictions. If an unfamiliar element is on the far left of the table, it is a metal and is likely to be shiny and to conduct electricity; if it is on the far right, it is a non-metal and is likely to be a gas or a brittle solid that does not conduct.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying Mendeleev arranged elements in order of atomic number; he used atomic mass, and atomic number was understood later. Two: saying non-metals are on the left; they are on the right, except hydrogen. Three: calling hydrogen a metal because of its position; it is a non-metal. Four: thinking there are about as many non-metals as metals; most elements are metals. Five: calling compounds such as water or salt elements.

The questions for this section are with you now: periods and groups, where metals, non-metals and semi-metals are found, and how to use position to predict properties.

## Subtopic: Building Blocks

Everything around you, your desk, the air, your body, is made of building blocks called elements. An element is a substance made of only one kind of atom, and you cannot break it down into anything simpler. Gold, iron, oxygen and carbon are elements.

When elements join together, they make compounds. Water is a compound: it is made of hydrogen and oxygen joined together.

There are one hundred and eighteen elements. South Africa digs many of them out of the ground: gold, platinum, iron, chromium and carbon in the form of coal and diamonds.

Long ago, chemists noticed that some elements behave like family members. Lithium, sodium and potassium are all soft metals that fizz wildly in water. So they wanted to organise the elements into families, like sorting living things into groups.

## Subtopic: The Puzzle With Missing Pieces

In 1869, a Russian teacher called Dmitri Mendeleev played a kind of card game. He wrote each of the sixty-three known elements on a card, with its mass and how it behaved. Then he laid them out in order, lightest to heaviest, starting a new row whenever the behaviour started repeating. Family members ended up in the same column.

But some places did not fit. So Mendeleev did something brave. He left empty spaces and said: there are elements we have not found yet, and they belong here. He even described what they would be like.

A few years later, scientists found gallium, and it matched his description almost perfectly. Then scandium and germanium filled two more gaps. It was like predicting the missing pieces of a jigsaw puzzle before anyone had found them.

Today the table is ordered by atomic number, but Mendeleev's idea still works.

## Subtopic: Reading the Map

Think of the Periodic Table as a map. The rows going across are called periods. The columns going down are called groups, and each group is a family that behaves alike.

The map has a staircase drawn on it, a zigzag line on the right-hand side. Everything to the left of the staircase is a metal: iron, copper, gold, sodium. That is most of the map. Everything to the right is a non-metal: oxygen, nitrogen, carbon, helium. The elements sitting right on the staircase, like silicon, are semi-metals, a bit of both.

One trick: hydrogen sits at the top left, but it is a non-metal, a gas.

Pocket summary of the lesson. An element is made of one kind of atom; compounds are elements joined together. Mendeleev arranged the elements in 1869, left gaps for undiscovered elements and predicted their properties, which came true. On the Periodic Table, rows are periods and columns are groups. Metals are on the left and centre, non-metals on the right, and semi-metals on the staircase between.
