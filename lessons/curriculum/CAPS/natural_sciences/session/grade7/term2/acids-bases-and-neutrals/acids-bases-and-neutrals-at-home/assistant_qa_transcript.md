### subtopic_1 — Properties of Acids
**Assistant:** Why does fizzy cooldrink damage teeth, if the acids in it are weak?

**Tutor:** Because teeth are exposed to those acids repeatedly and for long periods, and tooth enamel is attacked by acid even when the acid is weak. Tooth enamel is the hardest substance in the human body, but it is made mainly of a calcium phosphate mineral, which slowly dissolves in acidic conditions. Fizzy cooldrinks are quite acidic, because they contain carbonic acid from the dissolved carbon dioxide and often phosphoric or citric acid for flavour; many are as acidic as orange juice or more so. Each sip bathes the teeth in acid, and the enamel begins to soften and lose minerals. Saliva gradually neutralises the acid and helps repair the surface, but this takes about half an hour or more. Sipping a cooldrink slowly throughout the day keeps the mouth acidic most of the time, so the enamel never gets a chance to recover; this is called dental erosion. On top of that, the sugar in sweet cooldrinks feeds bacteria in the plaque on the teeth, and those bacteria produce more acid, which causes tooth decay, or cavities. Diet cooldrinks avoid the sugar but are still acidic. Ways to protect teeth include drinking water instead, drinking cooldrinks with meals rather than sipping all day, using a straw, rinsing the mouth with water afterwards, waiting a while before brushing so that softened enamel is not scrubbed away, and using toothpaste containing fluoride, which strengthens enamel.

### subtopic_2 — Properties of Bases
**Assistant:** Why is a strong base in the eye often more dangerous than an acid?

**Tutor:** Because of the way strong bases react with the tissues of the body. When an acid touches the eye or skin, it damages the proteins at the surface, and these damaged proteins clump together into a kind of barrier that tends to slow the acid from penetrating deeper, so the injury, though serious, is often limited to the surface layers. A strong base such as sodium hydroxide behaves differently. It reacts with the fats in cell membranes, turning them into a soap-like substance, and it breaks down proteins into a softened, liquefied mass rather than a barrier. This allows the base to keep penetrating deeper into the tissue, so the damage continues for some time after contact, sometimes reaching the inside of the eye and causing permanent scarring of the clear front layer, the cornea, or blindness. This is called liquefactive damage. It is why drain cleaners and oven cleaners carry strong warnings, why they must never be used without goggles and gloves, and why the first aid for any chemical splash in the eye is so urgent: rinse immediately with large amounts of clean running water for at least fifteen to twenty minutes, keeping the eyelids open, and then get medical help without delay, taking the container along. It is also why people must never try to neutralise a chemical in the eye with another chemical; the heat and reaction can make the injury worse. Plain water is the right first response.

### subtopic_3 — Neutrals and Neutralisation
**Assistant:** Why do farmers in some parts of South Africa add lime to their soil?

**Tutor:** Because many soils, especially in high-rainfall areas, become too acidic for good crop growth, and lime neutralises the acid. Several processes make soil acidic. Heavy rainfall over many years washes basic minerals such as calcium and magnesium out of the soil, leaving it more acidic, which is why acid soils are common in parts of Mpumalanga, KwaZulu-Natal and the Eastern Cape. Some fertilisers that supply nitrogen, especially those based on ammonium, make the soil more acidic as they are used by plants and soil bacteria. Decaying plant matter also produces weak acids. When soil becomes too acidic, several problems arise: important nutrients such as phosphorus become locked up and unavailable to plants, while metals such as aluminium and manganese dissolve and reach levels that poison plant roots, so roots stay short and stunted. Most crops, including maize, wheat and many vegetables, grow best in soils that are only slightly acidic to neutral. Farmers test their soil, sending samples to laboratories that measure acidity, and then spread agricultural lime, which is ground limestone, calcium carbonate, or dolomitic lime, which also contains magnesium. The lime reacts with the acids in the soil and neutralises them, releasing the locked-up nutrients and making the aluminium harmless. Because liming must be repeated every few years, it is a major cost for farmers, and getting the amount right is important. This is a large-scale, real-world example of neutralisation feeding the country.
