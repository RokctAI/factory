# Part 1 — Expert

This session describes the properties of acids, bases and neutral substances and finds them in the home. The anchor is a tour of a South African kitchen and bathroom: vinegar and lemon juice beside the stove, baking soda in the cupboard, a bottle of drain cleaner under the sink, toothpaste and soap at the basin, and a jug of tap water and a bowl of sugar on the table. The governing idea is that acids and bases are two opposite groups of substances with characteristic properties, that neutral substances are neither acidic nor basic, that both acids and bases range from weak and safe to strong and corrosive, and that acids and bases can cancel each other in neutralisation. The properties of acids and bases, household examples of each, the meaning of neutral, and simple examples of neutralisation are examinable.

## Subtopic: Properties of Acids

An acid is a substance that, when dissolved in water, produces a solution with characteristic properties. Acids taste sour, although tasting must never be used as a test. Acids turn blue litmus paper red, which is the main test used in Grade 7. Many acids react with certain metals, such as zinc and magnesium, producing bubbles of hydrogen gas, and they react with substances called carbonates, such as baking soda, chalk and limestone, producing bubbles of carbon dioxide. Acids conduct electricity when dissolved in water. Strong acids are corrosive, damaging skin, clothing, metals and stone.

Acids range from weak to strong. Weak acids are found in many foods and are safe to eat in normal amounts. Citric acid is in lemons, oranges and other citrus fruit; acetic acid is in vinegar; lactic acid is in amasi, yoghurt and sour porridge; malic acid is in apples; tartaric acid is in grapes; carbonic acid forms when carbon dioxide dissolves in fizzy cooldrinks, which also often contain phosphoric acid; and ascorbic acid is vitamin C. Our own stomachs make hydrochloric acid, which helps digest food and kills bacteria swallowed with it.

Strong acids are dangerous. Hydrochloric acid, sold in hardware stores as spirits of salts for cleaning, sulphuric acid in car batteries, and nitric acid are corrosive and must be handled with gloves and goggles. Sulphuric acid is one of the most widely produced industrial chemicals and is used in South Africa to make fertilisers and to extract metals from ores.

Acids are also found in nature. Rainwater is naturally slightly acidic because carbon dioxide from the air dissolves in it, and where coal is burned, sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides dissolve in rain to form acid rain, which damages plants, buildings and lakes. Ant stings and nettle stings contain methanoic acid, also called formic acid.

The questions for this section are with you now: the properties of acids, examples of weak acids in food, examples of strong acids and their dangers, and acids in nature.

## Subtopic: Properties of Bases

A base is a substance that has the opposite properties to an acid and can neutralise an acid. Bases that dissolve in water are called alkalis, and their solutions are described as alkaline or basic. Bases often taste bitter and feel slippery or soapy to the touch, although tasting or touching must not be used as a test. Bases turn red litmus paper blue. Strong bases are corrosive: they attack skin, eyes and many materials, and can be even more damaging to the eyes than acids.

Weak bases are common in the home. Baking soda, sodium hydrogen carbonate, is used in baking and as a mild cleaner, and in medicine to relieve indigestion. Antacid tablets and milk of magnesia, magnesium hydroxide, neutralise excess stomach acid. Soap and many detergents are basic, which helps them lift grease and dirt. Toothpaste is usually slightly basic. Household ammonia, used in some glass cleaners, is a weak base. Wood ash, mixed with water, gives a basic solution, and people in many cultures, including in Southern Africa, have used ash to make soap and as a mild cleaner. Lime, calcium hydroxide, is used to treat acidic soils on farms, to make whitewash for walls and in building mortar.

Strong bases are dangerous. Caustic soda, sodium hydroxide, is the main ingredient in drain cleaners and oven cleaners; it works by breaking down fats, hair and grease that block pipes, but it can cause severe burns and blindness. Bleach is also basic and is an irritant. Strong bases must be handled with gloves and goggles and kept away from children.

The questions for this section are with you now: the properties of bases, the meaning of alkali, household examples of weak bases and their uses, and the dangers of strong bases.

## Subtopic: Neutrals and Neutralisation

A neutral substance is neither acidic nor basic. Pure water is neutral; so are solutions of sugar and of table salt in pure water, and cooking oil. Neutral solutions do not change the colour of either red or blue litmus paper: red stays red and blue stays blue. Tap water is usually close to neutral, sometimes very slightly acidic or basic depending on the minerals dissolved in it.

Acids and bases react with each other. When the right amounts of an acid and a base are mixed, they cancel each other's properties, and the result is a neutral solution containing a salt and water. This reaction is called neutralisation. For example, hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide neutralise each other to form sodium chloride, ordinary table salt, and water. The word salt in chemistry means any compound formed when an acid reacts with a base, so there are many different salts.

Neutralisation is used every day. Antacid tablets, which contain weak bases, neutralise excess stomach acid that causes heartburn and indigestion. A bee sting is acidic, so it is sometimes treated with baking soda paste, while a wasp sting is often said to be basic and treated with vinegar; doctors note that the evidence for these home remedies is limited, but they show the idea. Toothpaste, being slightly basic, helps neutralise acids produced by bacteria in the mouth. Farmers spread lime on acidic soils to neutralise them, because most crops grow best in soil that is close to neutral; acid soils are common in the high-rainfall areas of Mpumalanga and KwaZulu-Natal. Factories neutralise acidic or basic waste before it is released, and acid mine drainage is treated with lime.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: thinking all acids are dangerous; many are in food. Two: thinking bases are safe because they are not acids; strong bases are corrosive. Three: calling all salts table salt; chemists use salt for any compound formed from an acid and a base. Four: saying water turns litmus red; neutral substances leave both litmus colours unchanged. Five: neutralising a chemical in the eye with another chemical instead of rinsing with water.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of neutral, the products of neutralisation, and everyday uses of neutralisation.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a tour of the kitchen, a sour team, a soapy team and a peace treaty — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Sour Team

Acids are the sour team. Walk into the kitchen and you will find lots of gentle ones. Lemon juice has citric acid. Vinegar has acetic acid. Amasi and yoghurt have lactic acid. Cooldrinks are acidic too, which is why sipping them all day is bad for your teeth. Even your stomach makes acid to help digest food.

Acids turn blue litmus paper red. They fizz when they meet baking soda or chalk.

Some acids are fierce, not gentle. The acid in a car battery or in strong cleaning products can burn skin and eat through things. Those need gloves, goggles and great care.

Keep the sour team in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: The Soapy Team

Bases are the opposite team. They often taste bitter and feel slippery, like soap.

In the house you will find gentle bases: baking soda, antacid tablets for a sore tummy, soap and toothpaste. Ash from a fire mixed with water is a base too, and people used it long ago to make soap.

Bases turn red litmus paper blue.

Some bases are fierce. Drain cleaner contains caustic soda, a very strong base that can burn skin and blind eyes. That is why it has warning labels and must be locked away.

Hold the soapy team in mind and try this part's questions.

## Subtopic: In the Middle, and Making Peace

Some substances are on neither team. They are neutral. Pure water, sugar water and salt water are neutral. They do not change the colour of litmus at all.

And when the sour team meets the soapy team, they cancel each other out. An acid plus a base makes a salt and water. That is called neutralisation, a kind of peace treaty.

We use it all the time. Heartburn is too much stomach acid; an antacid tablet, which is a base, calms it down. Toothpaste helps cancel the acid that bacteria make on your teeth. Farmers spread lime, a base, on sour, acidic soil so their crops grow better.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Acids taste sour, turn blue litmus red and include lemon juice, vinegar and battery acid. Bases taste bitter, feel soapy, turn red litmus blue and include baking soda, soap and drain cleaner. Neutral substances, like pure water, change neither litmus colour. Acids and bases neutralise each other to make a salt and water.

Hold the sour team, the soapy team and the peace treaty, and take the final questions of the lesson.
