# Part 1 — Expert

This session teaches the safe, scientific way to classify substances as acids, bases or neutrals: indicators. The anchor is a laboratory tray holding strips of red and blue litmus paper and a row of test tubes containing vinegar, soapy water, lemon juice, sugar water, baking soda solution and tap water, together with a jar of purple juice made by boiling red cabbage, which turns pink, purple or green depending on what is added to it. The governing idea is that an acid-base indicator is a substance that has one colour in acids and a different colour in bases, that litmus is used with both colours because a single strip cannot show a neutral on its own, and that results must be recorded carefully and fairly. How to use red and blue litmus, how to interpret the results, natural indicators, and a fair testing procedure are examinable.

## Subtopic: What an Indicator Is

An indicator is a substance that changes colour depending on whether it is in an acidic, basic or neutral solution. Indicators let us classify substances safely, without tasting or touching them, and they work for colourless liquids that look identical, such as water, vinegar and dilute drain cleaner.

Litmus is the most widely used indicator in schools. It is a dye obtained from lichens, which are partnerships of fungi and algae that grow on rocks and tree bark. Litmus has been used by chemists for centuries. It is usually supplied as small strips of paper soaked in litmus and dried, in two forms: red litmus paper and blue litmus paper. Litmus is red in acidic solutions and blue in basic solutions.

The rules are simple. An acid turns blue litmus paper red; red litmus paper stays red in an acid. A base turns red litmus paper blue; blue litmus paper stays blue in a base. A neutral solution does not change either colour: red stays red and blue stays blue.

This explains why two colours of litmus paper are needed. Suppose only blue litmus is used and it stays blue: the substance could be a base or a neutral, and there is no way to tell. Suppose only red litmus is used and it stays red: the substance could be an acid or a neutral. Testing with both colours gives a full answer. If blue turns red, it is an acid. If red turns blue, it is a base. If neither changes, it is neutral.

A useful memory aid is: Blue to Red, Acid instead; Red to Blue, Base for you. Another is that acid and red both contain short words with the letter r, while base and blue both begin with b.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of an indicator, the source of litmus, the colour rules for acids, bases and neutrals, and why both red and blue litmus are needed.

## Subtopic: Testing Household Substances

A fair, safe litmus test follows a careful procedure. First, put on safety goggles, and use only substances provided or approved by the teacher; never test strong drain cleaners or battery acid in a school classroom without proper supervision. Second, label clean test tubes, cups or spots on a white tile with the name of each substance, and dissolve solids such as baking soda, soap and sugar in a little water. Third, use a clean glass rod, dropper or straw for each substance, so that one substance does not contaminate the next; a rod must be rinsed and dried between tests. Fourth, touch a drop of each substance onto a small piece of red litmus paper and a small piece of blue litmus paper, placed on a white tile or paper towel so that colours are easy to see. Fifth, observe the colours immediately and record them in a table.

A suitable results table has columns for the substance, the colour of red litmus after testing, the colour of blue litmus after testing, and the conclusion: acid, base or neutral. Typical results are: lemon juice, red stays red, blue turns red, acid; vinegar, red stays red, blue turns red, acid; soapy water, red turns blue, blue stays blue, base; baking soda solution, red turns blue, blue stays blue, base; toothpaste mixed with water, red turns blue, base; sugar water, no change to either, neutral; salt water, no change, neutral; distilled water, no change, neutral. Tap water may give a faint change in some areas because of dissolved minerals, which is a reminder that real results need careful thought.

Fair testing means changing only the substance being tested, while keeping everything else the same: the same kind of litmus paper, similar amounts of each liquid, and the same way of observing. To be confident, each test can be repeated, and results compared with other groups. If one group's result differs, possible reasons include contamination, a dirty rod, or a solution that was too weak to show a clear change.

Litmus has limits. It shows whether a substance is acidic, basic or neutral, but it does not show how strong an acid or base is: weak vinegar and strong hydrochloric acid both turn blue litmus red. Weakly coloured or very dark liquids, such as cooldrinks with strong colouring or coffee, can also make results hard to see.

The questions for this section are with you now: the safe steps of a litmus test, how to record results in a table, typical results for household substances, and the limits of litmus.

## Subtopic: Natural Indicators

Many plants contain natural pigments that act as acid-base indicators. The most famous is red cabbage. To make red cabbage indicator, chop some red cabbage leaves, cover them with hot water and leave them to soak, or boil them gently, until the water turns deep purple; then strain off the leaves. The purple liquid is the indicator. Added to an acid such as vinegar or lemon juice, it turns pink or red. Added to a neutral substance such as pure water or sugar water, it stays purple. Added to a base such as baking soda solution, it turns blue or green, and in a strong base it can turn yellow.

Red cabbage indicator has an advantage over litmus: it shows several colours, so it gives a rough idea of how strongly acidic or basic a substance is, not just which group it belongs to. Its colour comes from anthocyanins, the same family of pigments found in beetroot, purple grapes, blueberries, plums, the skins of red onions, and many flowers, such as hibiscus and purple petunias, all of which can be used as indicators. Some flowers change colour depending on the acidity of the soil they grow in; hydrangeas are blue in acidic soils and pink in neutral to slightly basic soils, although this happens through aluminium in the soil and not by the flower acting directly as an indicator. Turmeric, the yellow spice used in curries, turns red-brown in bases; a curry stain on a white shirt can turn red when washed with soap, which is basic.

Making and testing natural indicators is a good investigation: the question might be which plant gives the clearest colour changes. Variables to keep constant include the amount of plant material, the volume and temperature of water, and the amounts of test substances.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: concluding that a substance is neutral from one litmus colour only. Two: mixing up the colour rules: blue to red means acid, red to blue means base. Three: thinking litmus shows strength. Four: using one rod for all substances. Five: tasting a substance to check the indicator result.

The questions for this section are with you now: how to make red cabbage indicator, its colours in acids, neutrals and bases, other natural indicators, and planning a fair indicator investigation.

## Subtopic: Two Colour Spies

Many liquids look the same. Water, vinegar and some cleaning liquids are all clear. So how do you tell them apart safely? Not by tasting! You use an indicator, a colour spy that changes colour.

The most famous spy is litmus, a dye made from lichens, the crusty patches that grow on rocks. It comes as little strips of red paper and blue paper.

Here are the rules. An acid turns blue paper red. A base turns red paper blue. A neutral does nothing to either.

Memory trick: Blue to Red, Acid instead. Red to Blue, Base for you.

Why do you need both colours? If you only had blue paper and it stayed blue, was that a base or a neutral? You could not tell. With both, you always know.

Keep the two spies in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: The Testing Table

Let's test some things from home, safely. Goggles on. Label a row of cups: lemon juice, vinegar, soapy water, baking soda water, sugar water and salt water.

Use a clean stirring stick for each cup, so nothing gets mixed up. Dab a drop onto a bit of red litmus and a bit of blue litmus on a white tile. Write what you see in a table.

Lemon juice and vinegar: the blue paper turns red. Acids. Soapy water and baking soda water: the red paper turns blue. Bases. Sugar water and salt water: nothing changes. Neutral.

One thing litmus cannot tell you is how strong something is. Gentle vinegar and fierce battery acid both turn blue paper red.

Hold the testing table in mind and try this part's questions.

## Subtopic: Cabbage Water Magic

You can make your own colour spy in the kitchen. Chop some red cabbage, cover it with hot water and let it soak until the water turns deep purple. Pour off the purple water.

Now add a little to different cups. Vinegar or lemon juice: it turns pink. Plain water or sugar water: it stays purple. Baking soda water or soapy water: it turns blue or green.

The cabbage spy is even better than litmus in one way: its many colours give a hint of how strong an acid or base is.

Beetroot, purple grapes and some flowers work too. And turmeric, the yellow curry spice, turns red when it meets soap, which is why a curry stain can go red in the wash.

Pocket summary of the lesson. An indicator changes colour in acids and bases. Litmus turns blue to red in acids and red to blue in bases, and neither colour changes in neutrals, so we use both. Test safely: goggles, labels, a clean stick for each substance and a results table. Red cabbage water turns pink in acids, stays purple in neutrals and turns blue or green in bases.
