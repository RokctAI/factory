### subtopic_1 — What an Indicator Is
**Assistant:** Why does litmus change colour at all?

**Tutor:** Because the litmus dye is a substance whose particles can gain or lose a tiny charged particle, and the two forms absorb light differently, so they look different colours. Acidic solutions contain many hydrogen ions, which are hydrogen atoms that have lost their electron and carry a positive charge; basic solutions contain many hydroxide ions, made of an oxygen and a hydrogen with a negative charge, which remove hydrogen ions. Litmus particles behave like a switch. In an acidic solution, the dye particles take up hydrogen ions, and in that form they absorb more blue and green light and reflect red, so the dye looks red. In a basic solution, the hydroxide ions pull hydrogen ions off the dye particles, changing their structure so that they absorb more red and yellow light and reflect blue, so the dye looks blue. The change is reversible: dip a blue strip in acid and it turns red, then dip it in a base and it turns back to blue. Many other natural pigments behave as indicators for the same reason, including the anthocyanins that colour red cabbage, beetroot, blueberries, purple grapes and many flowers. Chemists have made many indicators that change colour at different levels of acidity, and by mixing several together they make universal indicator, which shows a whole rainbow of colours across the range from strongly acidic to strongly basic. That scale, called pH, is met in Grade 8 and Grade 9.

### subtopic_2 — Testing Household Substances
**Assistant:** Why must a fresh glass rod or a rinsed rod be used for each substance?

**Tutor:** Because even a tiny leftover drop of one substance can change the result for the next, and the whole point of a test is that the colour change is caused only by the substance being tested. Suppose a learner tests vinegar with a glass rod and then, without rinsing, uses the same rod to test sugar water. A small film of vinegar clings to the rod and is carried into the sugar water and onto the litmus paper. Sugar water is neutral and should leave blue litmus blue, but the trace of acid may turn the paper red or pinkish, and the learner wrongly concludes that sugar water is an acid. The same problem can happen with droppers, cups, spoons or fingers. In science this is called contamination, and it is one of the most common sources of error in experiments. It leads to results that are not valid, because they do not measure what they were meant to measure, and not reliable, because repeating the test properly gives a different answer. Using a clean rod for each substance, or rinsing the rod in clean water and drying it between tests, and labelling everything clearly, removes this source of error. Scientists in real laboratories take contamination so seriously that they use fresh disposable tips for every sample, clean surfaces repeatedly, and run blank tests with pure water to check that nothing unexpected is causing a colour change. Careful habits in a Grade 7 litmus test are exactly the same habits.

### subtopic_3 — Natural Indicators
**Assistant:** Can flowers and spices really be used to test for acids and bases, and why do they work?

**Tutor:** Yes, many can, because the substances that give plants their colours often change structure, and therefore colour, as the acidity around them changes. The best natural indicators contain pigments called anthocyanins, which give red, purple and blue colours to red cabbage, beetroot, purple grapes, blackberries, plums, red onion skins, and petals of flowers such as hibiscus, roses and purple petunias. In an acidic solution, anthocyanin particles hold on to extra hydrogen ions and appear red or pink; near neutral they lose some and appear purple; in basic solutions they lose more and their structure changes again, so they appear blue, green and finally yellow. Red cabbage is especially good because it contains a mixture of anthocyanins that together show a wide range of colours. Turmeric works differently: its yellow pigment, curcumin, changes to red-brown in basic conditions, so a yellow turmeric paper or stain turns red when touched by soap, baking soda or a basic cleaner. People have noticed such changes for a very long time; scientists in the 1600s, such as Robert Boyle, used plant extracts like violet syrup to sort substances into acids and alkalis, and litmus itself is a natural dye from lichens. The colours from natural indicators can be less sharp and less predictable than from laboratory indicators, and they fade or go off after a few days, but they work well enough to classify common household substances and make a safe and interesting home experiment.
