# Part 1 — Expert

This session begins the study of acids and bases with the sense of taste, and with the rule that makes it safe. The anchor is a kitchen table set with familiar foods: lemon juice, sugar, table salt, and strong rooibos tea or coffee without sugar, alongside a bottle of bleach and a box of drain cleaner that must never go near the mouth. The governing idea is that the tongue can detect basic tastes, including sweet, salty, sour and bitter, that sour tastes are typical of acids and bitter tastes are often found in bases, and that tasting is never a safe way to identify an unknown substance, because many substances are poisonous or corrosive. ## Subtopic: The Basic Tastes

Taste is one of the five senses. It is detected mainly by taste buds, tiny clusters of sensory cells found on the tongue, especially in the small bumps called papillae, and also on the roof of the mouth and the throat. An adult has several thousand taste buds. When food dissolves in saliva, substances in it reach the taste cells and trigger nerve messages to the brain, which interprets them as tastes. A substance must dissolve to be tasted, which is why a completely dry tongue tastes very little.

Four basic tastes are usually described in school science. Sweet is the taste of sugars, as in honey, ripe fruit, sugar cane and cooldrinks. Salty is the taste of table salt and salty foods such as biltong, chips and olives. Sour is the taste of acids, as in lemons, vinegar, sour milk such as amasi, unripe fruit, sour sweets and tamarind. Bitter is the taste of substances such as strong coffee, tea without milk, dark chocolate, the bitter leaves of some wild greens and certain medicines; many bitter substances are bases or contain bases. Scientists now also recognise a fifth basic taste, umami, a savoury taste found in meat broths, cheese, tomatoes and mushrooms.

A common myth says that each taste is detected only on one area of the tongue, sweet at the tip, bitter at the back and so on, shown on a tongue map. Research has shown this is wrong: all the basic tastes can be detected on all parts of the tongue that have taste buds, though some areas may be slightly more sensitive to some tastes.

What we call the flavour of food is a combination of taste with smell, texture and temperature. When the nose is blocked by a cold, food seems to lose its flavour, because most of what we think of as taste is actually smell. Holding the nose while eating an apple and a raw potato with eyes closed can make them hard to tell apart.

## Subtopic: Sour, Acids and Bitter, Bases

The sour taste is closely linked to acids. Lemons contain citric acid; vinegar contains acetic acid; sour milk and amasi contain lactic acid, made by bacteria that ferment the milk; yoghurt contains lactic acid too; and sour sweets are often coated with citric or malic acid. The word acid comes from the Latin acidus, meaning sour. The stronger or more concentrated the acid, the more sour the taste.

Many bases taste bitter and feel slippery or soapy. Baking soda, a weak base, has a slightly bitter, soapy taste, which is why using too much in baking spoils a cake. Soap, which is basic, tastes bitter, as anyone who has accidentally got soap in their mouth knows. However, the strongest bases, such as drain cleaner, oven cleaner and caustic soda, are far too dangerous to taste at all.

Salty tastes are usually caused by salts, compounds such as table salt, sodium chloride, which form when acids and bases react together. Sweet tastes are usually caused by sugars, which are neutral, neither acidic nor basic.

Tasting has a long history in science and medicine. Early chemists tasted substances to describe them, and doctors once tasted patients' urine to diagnose diabetes, because it tasted sweet. Some chemists were harmed or even killed by tasting poisonous substances, which is one reason why tasting was abandoned as a scientific test.

Taste is useful, but it cannot measure accurately. It cannot tell how strong an acid is in numbers, it cannot be used for substances that are poisonous or corrosive, and different people perceive taste differently. That is why scientists use indicators, substances that change colour depending on whether a solution is acidic or basic, which are the subject of the following lessons.

## Subtopic: Safety: Never Taste to Test

The most important rule of this topic is that tasting is never used to identify an unknown substance, in the laboratory, the home or anywhere else. Many substances that look harmless, such as clear liquids or white powders, are dangerous.

Some acids and bases are corrosive: they damage skin, eyes and the lining of the mouth and throat on contact. Battery acid, which contains sulphuric acid, hydrochloric acid used for cleaning, caustic soda in drain cleaners and oven cleaners, and bleach are all corrosive or irritant. Swallowing even a small amount can cause severe burns inside the mouth, throat and stomach. Other substances, such as paraffin, pesticides, some medicines and many plants, are poisonous even though they are not corrosive.

In South Africa, children are often poisoned by paraffin and household chemicals stored in cooldrink bottles, because they mistake them for drinks. Safety rules include: keep chemicals in their original, labelled containers; never store chemicals in food or drink bottles; store them out of reach of children, in a locked cupboard if possible; read and follow warning labels and hazard symbols, such as the corrosive symbol showing liquid eating into a hand and a surface; and never mix cleaning products, because some combinations, such as bleach with acids or with ammonia, release toxic gases.

In the science laboratory, the rules are strict: never taste or eat anything; never smell a chemical directly but waft a little of the vapour towards the nose with the hand; wear safety goggles; use small amounts; clean up spills; and wash hands after experiments. If a chemical gets on the skin or in the eyes, rinse with plenty of clean running water for at least fifteen minutes and tell an adult. If a chemical is swallowed, do not make the person vomit; contact a doctor, clinic or the poison information helpline immediately and take the container along.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: tasting an unknown substance to find out what it is. Two: believing each taste is found only on one part of the tongue. Three: thinking all clear liquids are water. Four: storing paraffin or bleach in a cooldrink bottle. Five: thinking a substance is safe because it is natural; many plants and berries are poisonous.

## Subtopic: Sweet, Salty, Sour, Bitter

Your tongue is covered with thousands of tiny taste buds. When food mixes with your spit, the taste buds send messages to your brain.

There are four main tastes. Sweet, like honey and ripe mangoes. Salty, like chips and biltong. Sour, like lemons and vinegar. Bitter, like strong coffee or dark chocolate. Scientists add a fifth: umami, the yummy savoury taste of gravy and cheese.

You might have seen a tongue map that puts sweet at the tip and bitter at the back. It is wrong. Every part of your tongue with taste buds can taste every taste.

And here is a trick: hold your nose, close your eyes, and eat a piece of apple. It is hard to tell from raw potato. Most of what we call flavour is really smell.

## Subtopic: Sour Means Acid

Here is a useful clue. Things that taste sour usually contain acids. Lemons have citric acid. Vinegar has acetic acid. Amasi and yoghurt have lactic acid, made by friendly bacteria. In fact, the word acid comes from an old word meaning sour.

Things on the other side, called bases, often taste bitter and feel slippery and soapy. Get soap in your mouth by accident and you know that bitter taste. Baking soda is a weak base; too much in a cake makes it taste bitter.

Salty tastes come from salts, and sweet tastes from sugars, which are neither acid nor base. They are neutral.

But taste can only give a rough hint. It cannot measure how strong something is, and many things are far too dangerous to taste.

## Subtopic: Never Taste to Test

Some clear liquids and white powders look harmless but can burn your mouth, throat and stomach, or poison you. Drain cleaner, bleach, battery acid and paraffin are all dangerous.

So the golden rule is: never taste anything to find out what it is. In the science lab, never taste or eat anything, never sniff a chemical directly, wear goggles, and wash your hands.

At home, keep chemicals in their own labelled bottles, never in cooldrink bottles, where a small child might drink them. Lock them away. And never mix cleaning products: bleach plus some other cleaners can make a poisonous gas.

If something splashes in your eyes, rinse with lots of clean running water and tell an adult. If someone swallows a chemical, get medical help straight away.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Taste buds detect sweet, salty, sour and bitter, plus umami. Sour tastes come from acids; bases often taste bitter and feel soapy; sugars are neutral. But taste is not a safe or accurate test. Never taste unknown substances, store chemicals safely in labelled containers, and use indicators to test for acids and bases.
