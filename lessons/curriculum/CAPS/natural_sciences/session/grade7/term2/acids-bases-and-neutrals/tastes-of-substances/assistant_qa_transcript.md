### subtopic_1 — The Basic Tastes
**Assistant:** Why do bitter tastes seem unpleasant to most people, especially children?

**Tutor:** Scientists think the dislike of bitterness evolved as a warning system. Many poisonous substances made by plants to protect themselves from being eaten, such as certain alkaloids, taste bitter, so animals, including our ancestors, that spat out bitter food were less likely to be poisoned. Humans have about twenty-five different types of bitter-taste receptors, far more than for sweet, which suggests that detecting a wide range of bitter substances was important for survival. Sweetness, on the other hand, signals sugars, a rich source of energy, so a liking for sweet food helped our ancestors find ripe fruit and honey. Children are especially sensitive to bitterness and especially fond of sweetness, which makes sense for young people who are growing quickly and who are more vulnerable to poisons. This is why many children dislike vegetables such as spinach or bitter wild greens, and why medicines for children are often flavoured. As people grow older and have repeated experiences of bitter foods that are safe, they often learn to enjoy them, which is why many adults like coffee, tea, dark chocolate or bitter herbs such as those used in some traditional dishes. People also differ genetically: some people inherit a version of a bitter receptor that makes them very sensitive to certain bitter substances, while others can hardly taste them, which is another example of inherited variation. The lesson for safety is that bitterness is a hint of danger, but not a reliable test, because some poisons do not taste bitter.

### subtopic_2 — Sour, Acids and Bitter, Bases
**Assistant:** Why does amasi taste sour when fresh milk does not?

**Tutor:** Because bacteria have turned some of the sugar in the milk into an acid. Fresh milk contains a natural sugar called lactose, which gives it a very slightly sweet taste, and it is close to neutral. Amasi, the traditional fermented milk popular across South Africa, is made by leaving milk to stand, traditionally in a calabash or a clay pot, so that lactic acid bacteria, either naturally present or left over from a previous batch, can grow in it. These bacteria feed on the lactose and, as a waste product of their respiration without oxygen, they produce lactic acid. As the acid builds up, the milk becomes sour, and the acid also makes the milk proteins clump together, which is why amasi is thick and why it can separate into curds and a watery whey. Yoghurt is made in a similar way using particular bacteria at a warm temperature. The acid has another benefit: most harmful bacteria cannot grow well in acidic conditions, so fermentation helps preserve milk without a fridge, which is why fermented milk products were so valuable before refrigeration and remain important in rural areas. Fermentation of this kind is used for many foods: sour porridge such as ting, made from fermented sorghum, sauerkraut from cabbage, and traditional sorghum beer. Each sour taste is a sign that microorganisms have produced acid, linking this lesson to the useful micro-organisms studied in later grades.

### subtopic_3 — Safety: Never Taste to Test
**Assistant:** Why is it dangerous to mix bleach with other cleaning products?

**Tutor:** Because household bleach contains a chemical called sodium hypochlorite, which reacts with certain other cleaning products to release poisonous gases. When bleach is mixed with an acid, such as some toilet cleaners, descaling products for kettles, or even vinegar, the reaction releases chlorine gas. Chlorine is a yellow-green gas with a sharp smell that irritates and damages the eyes, nose, throat and lungs; it was used as a weapon in the First World War, and even in a small bathroom it can cause coughing, difficulty breathing and, in high amounts, serious lung injury. When bleach is mixed with ammonia, found in some glass and surface cleaners and also produced from urine, it releases gases called chloramines, which are also toxic to breathe. Mixing bleach with rubbing alcohol can produce other harmful substances. People sometimes mix products thinking that two cleaners together will clean better, but the result is less cleaning power and a dangerous gas. The safe rules are: use one product at a time, rinse surfaces with water before switching products, use cleaning products in a well-ventilated area with windows open, read the label for warnings, and keep products in their original containers. If someone accidentally breathes in fumes from mixed cleaners, they should go into fresh air immediately, and medical help should be sought if they have trouble breathing. This is another example of the main lesson: chemicals must be identified and used safely, never tested by tasting, smelling deeply or mixing at random.
