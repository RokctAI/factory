# Part 1 — Expert

This session adds the human factors to choosing materials: cost, colour, texture and availability. The anchor is a family in Mthatha deciding what to use for the roof of a new rondavel: traditional thatch grass, corrugated iron sheets, or clay tiles. Each would keep the rain out, but they differ in price, in how hot or cool they keep the room, in how they look and feel, in how long they last and in how easily they can be obtained and repaired. The governing idea is that the physical properties of a material decide whether it can do a job, but the final choice also depends on cost, appearance, texture, availability, durability and the needs and values of the people using it. These factors, how to weigh them in a decision, and the reasoning in a design choice are examinable, and they connect to Technology lessons on design.

## Subtopic: Cost and Availability

A material is only useful if people can obtain and afford it. The cost of a material depends on several things: how rare the raw material is, how difficult and energy-hungry it is to extract and process, how far it must be transported, and how much demand there is for it.

Precious metals show the effect of rarity. Gold and platinum are excellent conductors that never corrode, but they are far too expensive to use for ordinary electrical wiring, so copper and aluminium are used instead; gold is used only in tiny amounts on the contacts of electronic connectors and in some components where reliability matters most, such as in spacecraft. Copper itself has become valuable enough that cable theft is a serious problem in South Africa, disrupting trains, telephone lines and electricity supply; some cables are now made of aluminium, which is cheaper and less attractive to thieves.

Processing costs matter too. Aluminium is the most common metal in the Earth's crust, but extracting it from its ore uses enormous amounts of electricity, so it is more expensive than iron. Recycling aluminium uses only about five per cent of the energy needed to make new aluminium, which is why aluminium cans are worth collecting.

Availability and transport also matter. A material that is found locally is often cheaper, faster to obtain and easier to repair. Thatching grass grows in many rural areas and can be cut and dried by the community; clay for bricks is found in river banks; stone is available in rocky areas. In remote areas, transporting cement, steel or roof sheeting can add greatly to cost.

Cost must be judged over the whole life of the product, not only at purchase. A cheap material that wears out quickly or needs constant repair may cost more in the end than an expensive, durable one. Long-life energy-saving light bulbs cost more to buy than old incandescent bulbs but use far less electricity and last many times longer, so they save money over time.

The questions for this section are with you now: the factors that affect the cost of a material, why copper rather than gold is used in wiring, and the idea of cost over the whole life of a product.

## Subtopic: Colour, Texture and Appearance

People do not choose materials only for their usefulness; they also care how things look and feel. Colour, texture, pattern and finish affect whether a product is attractive, comfortable and suitable for its purpose.

Colour can be decorative, but it can also have a function. Light-coloured roofs and walls reflect more sunlight and keep buildings cooler, while dark surfaces absorb more heat; this is why many houses in hot regions are painted white. Bright colours, such as the orange and yellow of safety vests, make people visible on roads and mines. Colour can show information: the coloured insulation on electrical wires shows which wire is live, neutral or earth, and red is used for danger and fire equipment.

Texture is how a surface feels: smooth, rough, soft, hard, slippery or grippy. Smooth surfaces such as glazed tiles and stainless steel are easy to clean, so they are used in kitchens, bathrooms and hospitals. Rough or ridged surfaces give grip, so they are used for steps, tyre treads, the soles of shoes and the handles of tools. Soft, flexible textures such as cotton, wool and fleece are comfortable against the skin in clothing and bedding.

Appearance also involves cultural meaning and personal taste. Traditional Ndebele house painting uses bold geometric patterns in bright colours; Zulu beadwork uses colour and pattern to carry messages; shweshwe, the printed cotton fabric popular across South Africa, is valued for its distinctive patterns and its stiff, crisp feel when new. A material may be chosen because it fits a style, expresses identity or feels right, as well as because it works.

Sometimes appearance and properties pull in different directions. A glass dining table looks elegant but scratches and breaks more easily than wood; a white school shirt looks neat but shows stains; a shiny metal finish looks modern but shows fingerprints. Designers often treat or coat materials to get both: steel can be painted or galvanised with zinc to look good and resist rust; wood can be varnished to look polished and resist water.

The questions for this section are with you now: ways that colour can have a function, how texture affects the choice of material, and examples where appearance and properties must be balanced.

## Subtopic: Weighing Up a Choice

Real decisions about materials involve weighing several factors at once. A useful method is to list the requirements, compare the options against each requirement, and decide which factors matter most for this particular situation.

The rondavel roof is a good example. Thatch grass is cheap or free where it grows locally, can be collected and laid by the community with traditional skills, is a good thermal insulator, so it keeps the house cool in summer and warm in winter, is quiet in rain and looks attractive. But it must be replaced every fifteen to twenty-five years or so, can harbour insects, and burns easily, which makes fire a serious risk. Corrugated iron sheets are fairly cheap, quick to install, long-lasting if galvanised and fireproof, and they allow rainwater to be collected in tanks; but thin metal is a good conductor of heat, so the room becomes very hot in summer and cold in winter unless a ceiling or insulation is added, rain on it is noisy, and it can rust. Clay or concrete tiles are fireproof, durable, attractive and moderately good insulators, but they are heavier, so they need a stronger roof frame, and they cost more to buy, transport and install.

There is no single right answer: a family with access to thatching grass and skilled thatchers might choose thatch and take precautions against fire; a family wanting to collect rainwater might choose iron sheets and add an insulating ceiling; a family with more money might choose tiles for long life and low maintenance. The best answers explain the trade-offs and justify the choice.

The same reasoning applies to a school bag, a soccer ball, a water container or a chair. A good answer names the properties needed, considers cost, availability, appearance, texture and durability, and then justifies a choice.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: choosing the material with the best physical properties without considering cost. Two: thinking the cheapest material is always the best value; consider how long it lasts. Three: treating appearance as unimportant; colour and texture can have functions. Four: thinking there is always one correct answer; good decisions explain trade-offs. Five: ignoring safety factors such as fire risk.

The questions for this section are with you now: listing requirements, comparing options, explaining trade-offs and justifying a choice of material.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a rondavel roof, a shopping trip and a shweshwe dress — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Can We Afford It?

Imagine building a roof. Many materials would work, but can you afford them, and can you get them?

Gold conducts electricity brilliantly and never rusts, but it costs a fortune, so wires are made of copper. Copper is now so valuable that thieves steal cables, which is why some cables are now aluminium.

Some materials cost a lot because making them uses lots of energy. Aluminium needs huge amounts of electricity to make. But recycling an old can uses only a tiny fraction of that energy, so recycling saves money.

Local materials are often cheapest. Thatching grass grows in the veld, and clay comes from the river bank.

And think long-term. A cheap thing that breaks quickly can cost more in the end than a good thing that lasts.

Keep the shopping trip in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: Looks and Feel

We also care how things look and feel, and sometimes looks do a job.

A white roof bounces sunlight away and keeps the house cooler. A bright orange vest makes a road worker easy to see. Red on a fire extinguisher shouts danger and help.

Feel matters too. Smooth tiles are easy to wipe clean in a kitchen. Rough, bumpy soles stop your shoes slipping. Soft cotton feels good on your skin.

And people choose materials because they love how they look: bold Ndebele painted patterns, colourful beadwork, or a shweshwe dress with its crisp printed cotton.

Sometimes looks and properties need balancing. A glass table looks lovely but scratches easily. Painting steel makes it look good and stops it rusting at the same time.

## Subtopic: The Roof Decision

Back to the rondavel roof. Three choices.

Thatch: cheap if the grass grows nearby, keeps the room cool in summer and warm in winter, quiet in the rain, and beautiful. But it must be replaced after some years, and it can catch fire.

Iron sheets: cheap, quick to put up, cannot burn, and you can catch rainwater off them. But the room gets boiling hot in summer and cold in winter unless you add a ceiling, and rain sounds like drums.

Clay tiles: last a long time, cannot burn, look good. But they are heavy and cost more.

There is no single right answer. The best answer says what matters most to this family and why.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Physical properties decide if a material can do the job. Cost, availability, colour, texture, durability and safety then help decide which material is best. Good choices compare options, explain the trade-offs and give reasons.
