### subtopic_1 — Melting and Boiling Points
**Assistant:** Why does water boil at a lower temperature in Johannesburg than in Durban?

**Tutor:** Because the boiling point of a liquid depends on the air pressure above it, and air pressure is lower at high altitude. A liquid boils when its particles have enough energy to form bubbles of vapour inside the liquid, and for those bubbles to form, the pressure of the vapour must match the pressure of the air pushing down on the surface. At sea level, in Durban, the weight of the whole atmosphere presses down, and water must be heated to 100 degrees Celsius before its vapour pressure is high enough. Johannesburg is about 1 750 metres above sea level, so there is less air above it and the air pressure is roughly four fifths of the pressure at the coast. Water therefore boils at a lower temperature, about 94 degrees Celsius. This has practical effects. Food cooks more slowly in boiling water on the Highveld, because the water never gets as hot, so pasta and potatoes take longer, and recipes for baking may need adjusting. A pressure cooker solves the problem: it seals in the steam, which raises the pressure inside and so raises the boiling point to about 120 degrees, cooking food much faster. On top of Mount Everest, water boils at only about 70 degrees, too cool to make a proper cup of tea. This is why scientists always state that melting and boiling points are measured at standard atmospheric pressure.

### subtopic_2 — Conductors and Insulators
**Assistant:** Why is it dangerous to touch electrical appliances with wet hands if water is not a good conductor?

**Tutor:** Because the water on your hands and in your home is not pure water. Pure water, which contains only water particles, conducts electricity very poorly. But tap water, rainwater, sweat and bath water all contain dissolved substances, such as salts and minerals, and these break up into electrically charged particles in the water. These charged particles can move and carry an electric current, so ordinary water conducts electricity well enough to be dangerous. Sweat is particularly salty. There is a second reason. Dry skin has quite a high resistance, which limits how much current can flow through the body if a person touches a live wire. Wet skin has a much lower resistance, so the same voltage can push a much larger current through the body, and it is the size of the current that causes harm, disturbing the heart's rhythm and burning tissues. Household electricity in South Africa is supplied at about 230 volts, more than enough to kill if the current flows across the chest. Water also lets current reach places it should not, for example by seeping into a plug or an appliance and connecting the live wire to its outer surface. That is why bathrooms have special rules, why hairdryers should never be used near a filled bath, and why homes should have an earth leakage unit in the distribution board, which switches off the power in a fraction of a second if current starts leaking to earth.

### subtopic_3 — Strength, Hardness, Flexibility and Density
**Assistant:** Why are aeroplanes not made of steel, if steel is so strong?

**Tutor:** Because for an aeroplane, what matters most is not strength alone but strength compared with weight, and steel is too heavy for the strength it provides in most parts of an aircraft. Every kilogram of an aeroplane's structure must be lifted by its wings and pushed by its engines, so a heavier aircraft burns much more fuel and can carry fewer passengers. Steel has a density of about eight grams per cubic centimetre, while aluminium alloys, which are aluminium mixed with small amounts of copper, zinc or magnesium, have a density of about 2,8. Although steel is stronger for a given thickness, an aluminium part can be made thicker and still be lighter and strong enough, so for the same weight, the aluminium structure is stiffer and stronger. Since the 1930s most aircraft bodies and wings have been made from aluminium alloys. Newer aircraft go further and use composite materials, such as carbon fibre set in plastic resin, which are even lighter for their strength and do not corrode; in some modern airliners, around half the structure by weight is composite. Steel is still used where its particular properties are needed, such as in landing gear, which must take enormous forces when the plane lands, and titanium is used in hot parts of engines, because it keeps its strength at high temperatures. This is a perfect example of the main idea of this lesson: engineers balance several properties at once to choose the best material for each job.
