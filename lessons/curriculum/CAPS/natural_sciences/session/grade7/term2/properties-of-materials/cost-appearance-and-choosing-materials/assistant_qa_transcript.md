### subtopic_1 — Cost and Availability
**Assistant:** Why does cable theft cause so much damage when the copper itself is only worth a little?

**Tutor:** Because the value of the copper to a thief is tiny compared with the cost of the damage done and the services that are interrupted. A thief who cuts out a length of railway signalling cable might sell the copper as scrap for a few hundred rand. But that cable controls the signals that keep trains safely apart, so its removal can stop trains on a whole line for hours or days, delaying thousands of commuters, who may lose wages or even their jobs, and costing the railway operator far more in repairs, replacement cable, security and lost income. Theft of electricity cables can leave whole suburbs or hospitals without power; theft of telephone and data cables cuts off businesses and emergency services. Studies have estimated that cable theft costs the South African economy several billion rand each year. It is also extremely dangerous for the thieves, many of whom are electrocuted. Responses have included laws that make cable theft a serious crime with heavy penalties, rules requiring scrap dealers to record where their metal comes from, security patrols, alarms that detect when a cable is cut, burying cables deeper or in concrete, and replacing copper with aluminium or with fibre-optic cables made of glass, which have little scrap value. The problem shows that the choice of a material is affected not only by its physical properties and cost, but also by social factors such as how likely it is to be stolen.

### subtopic_2 — Colour, Texture and Appearance
**Assistant:** Why are many roofs in hot countries painted white, and does it really make a difference?

**Tutor:** Yes, it makes a measurable difference, because of the way surfaces of different colours absorb and reflect sunlight. Sunlight carries energy. When it strikes a surface, some of it is reflected and the rest is absorbed and turned into heat. Dark surfaces absorb most of the light that falls on them; a black roof may absorb more than ninety per cent of the sunlight and become far too hot to touch on a summer afternoon, well above sixty degrees. White and light-coloured surfaces reflect most of the sunlight, so they stay much cooler. A cooler roof passes less heat into the rooms below, so the house stays more comfortable and needs less electricity for fans or air-conditioning. Measurements have shown that painting dark roofs white can lower the roof surface temperature by twenty or thirty degrees and reduce indoor temperatures by a few degrees, which matters a great deal in a small house with no ceiling or insulation. In South Africa, projects have painted the iron-sheet roofs of homes and schools in townships with reflective white paint for this reason. White roofs across a whole city can also reduce what is called the urban heat island effect, where cities become hotter than the surrounding countryside because of dark roofs and roads. The same physics explains why people wear light-coloured clothes in summer and why cars parked in the sun are hotter inside if they are dark.

### subtopic_3 — Weighing Up a Choice
**Assistant:** How can thatched roofs be made safer from fire without giving up their advantages?

**Tutor:** By reducing the chances of the thatch catching fire and slowing down any fire that does start. Thatch burns easily because dry grass is full of air spaces and contains material that ignites at quite low temperatures, so a spark from a cooking fire, a candle, a lightning strike or a nearby veld fire can set it alight, and once burning, a thatched roof can be engulfed within minutes. Several measures help. First, keep sources of fire away: build chimneys well above the thatch with spark arrestors, never leave open flames or paraffin lamps unattended, and keep cooking fires outside or in a separate structure. Second, the thatch itself can be treated: fire-retardant chemicals can be sprayed onto or soaked into the grass, which make it much harder to ignite and slow the spread of flames; these treatments must be renewed every few years because rain gradually washes them out. Third, a fire-resistant barrier, such as a special fire-resistant board, can be placed under the thatch to stop a fire spreading into the room below. Fourth, lightning conductors can be installed on tall thatched buildings, as is required for many thatched lodges. Fifth, clearing dry grass and vegetation around the building creates a firebreak against veld fires. Finally, having water or fire extinguishers ready and a clear escape plan saves lives. These measures show that a material's weaknesses can often be reduced, so a choice need not be all or nothing.
