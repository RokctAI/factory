### subtopic_1 — Metals: Mining and Processing
**Assistant:** If mining causes so much damage, should South Africa simply stop mining?

**Tutor:** It is not that simple, and thoughtful people disagree about the right balance, so it is worth seeing the different perspectives. Mining has been central to South Africa's economy for more than a century. It employs hundreds of thousands of people directly and supports many more jobs in transport, manufacturing and services, and it earns a large share of the country's export income. Metals from mines are also essential for things the world needs, including the platinum used in clean-air technology and in some fuel cells, and the copper, manganese and other metals needed for electricity grids, wind turbines, solar panels and batteries; a switch away from fossil fuels actually requires more mining of some metals. On the other hand, mining has caused lasting damage to land, rivers and the health of workers and nearby communities, including lung diseases such as silicosis among gold miners, and the history of migrant labour under apartheid caused great social harm. Many communities near mines feel they carry the costs while others receive the benefits. Most experts therefore argue not for stopping mining, but for doing it much more responsibly: strict environmental laws that are enforced, money set aside to rehabilitate land after mining, safe working conditions, fair sharing of benefits with local communities, more recycling so less new ore is needed, and careful decisions about which areas, such as important water sources and protected wilderness, should not be mined at all. Weighing these views is part of being an informed citizen.

### subtopic_2 — Plastics
**Assistant:** How important are waste pickers to recycling in South Africa?

**Tutor:** Very important: studies have estimated that informal waste pickers collect most of the paper and packaging that is recycled in South Africa, perhaps as much as eighty to ninety per cent of some materials. There are tens of thousands of waste pickers, many of whom walk long distances through suburbs on collection days, pulling large bags on trolleys, or work on landfill sites, sorting out plastic, paper, cardboard, glass and metal to sell to buy-back centres and recycling companies. Their work keeps huge amounts of material out of landfills, saves municipalities money, provides raw material for recycling industries and reduces the need to produce new materials from oil, trees and ore. Yet waste pickers often earn very little, face health and safety risks from sharp objects, chemicals and traffic, and have often been treated as a nuisance rather than as workers providing a service. In recent years, organisations such as the African Reclaimers Organisation and the South African Waste Pickers Association have campaigned for recognition, fair payment and safer working conditions, and government guidelines now encourage municipalities to integrate waste pickers into their recycling systems. People can help by separating recyclables at home, keeping them clean and dry, and putting them out in a way that makes them easy to collect, and by treating waste pickers with respect. This shows that environmental solutions involve people and fairness, not only technology.

### subtopic_3 — Fuels
**Assistant:** Why is the air on the Mpumalanga Highveld so polluted, and what is being done about it?

**Tutor:** Because a very large number of coal-burning power stations and heavy industries are concentrated in a relatively small area, and the local weather often traps the pollution near the ground. The Highveld region around eMalahleni, Middelburg and Secunda holds about a dozen large coal-fired power stations, coal mines, steel and metal works and the huge coal-to-fuel plant at Secunda. Together they release large amounts of sulphur dioxide, nitrogen dioxide and fine soot particles. In winter, cold, still air near the ground is often trapped under a layer of warmer air above, an effect called a temperature inversion, so pollution cannot rise and spread out; smoke from household coal fires in nearby towns adds to it. Satellite measurements have shown some of the highest nitrogen dioxide levels in the world over this region. Fine particles and these gases damage the lungs and heart, worsening asthma and bronchitis and causing early deaths; communities living nearby carry the greatest burden. In 2007 the government declared the area a Highveld Priority Area for air quality, and in 2022 a court ruled that the poor air there violated people's constitutional right to an environment that is not harmful to their health and ordered the government to act. Responses include stricter emission limits, fitting pollution-control equipment to power stations, gradually closing the oldest power stations, developing renewable energy, and electrifying homes so families do not need to burn coal indoors.
