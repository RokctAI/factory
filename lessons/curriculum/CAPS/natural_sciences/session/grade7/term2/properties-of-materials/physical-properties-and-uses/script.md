# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains how the physical properties of a material decide what it can be used for. The anchor is a three-legged potjie pot cooking over a fire at a family gathering: the pot is cast iron, which can survive the heat of the coals and spreads heat evenly; the handle is wrapped in cloth or held with a wooden spoon; and nobody would make the pot from plastic or the spoon from copper. The governing idea is that every material has measurable physical properties, such as melting and boiling points, electrical conductivity and thermal conductivity, along with strength, hardness, flexibility and density, and that engineers, builders and cooks choose materials whose properties match the job. Each property, how it is tested, and how it explains the uses of common materials are examinable.

## Subtopic: Melting and Boiling Points

A material is any substance used to make things: metals, wood, plastics, glass, ceramics, fabrics, rubber, stone and concrete. Raw materials, such as iron ore, crude oil, sand and trees, are processed into the materials used for manufacturing.

A physical property is a feature of a material that can be observed or measured without changing it into a different substance. The melting point is the temperature at which a solid changes into a liquid; the boiling point is the temperature at which a liquid boils and changes into a gas throughout. For a pure substance, each is a fixed temperature at normal air pressure. Water melts at 0 degrees Celsius and boils at 100 degrees Celsius at sea level.

Melting points vary enormously. Butter melts at about 32 degrees, chocolate at about 34 degrees, which is why it melts in the mouth, candle wax at about 60 degrees, lead at about 327 degrees, aluminium at about 660 degrees, copper at about 1 085 degrees, iron at about 1 538 degrees and tungsten at about 3 422 degrees.

Melting points explain many uses. Cooking pots must be made of materials with melting points far above the temperature of a stove or fire, which is why pots are made of iron, steel or aluminium, never plastic. The filament of an old-style light bulb glowed at over 2 500 degrees, so it was made of tungsten. Solder, used to join electrical wires, is a mixture of metals with a low melting point, so it melts easily under a soldering iron and sets again to hold the wires. Fuses in older plugs contained a wire with a low melting point that melts if too much current flows, breaking the circuit before a fire starts.

Boiling points matter too. Alcohol boils at about 78 degrees, so it evaporates quickly and is used in hand sanitisers. The coolant in a car radiator is a mixture with a higher boiling point than water, so it does not boil away in a hot engine.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of a physical property, melting and boiling points, and uses that depend on a high or low melting point.

## Subtopic: Conductors and Insulators

Two of the most useful physical properties are how well a material conducts electricity and how well it conducts heat.

Electrical conductivity is how easily electric current flows through a material. Good conductors, such as copper, aluminium, silver, gold, steel and graphite, let current flow easily. Insulators, such as plastic, rubber, glass, dry wood, porcelain, ceramics and dry air, do not. A simple test circuit, with a cell, a light bulb and two wires with a gap between them, can classify any material: place the material across the gap, and if the bulb lights, the material is a conductor.

Both conductors and insulators are essential in electrical equipment. In a plug, the pins are brass, a good conductor, while the casing is plastic, an insulator. Electrical cables have copper wires inside plastic coatings. Overhead power lines are aluminium, chosen because it conducts well and is much lighter than copper, and they hang from glass or porcelain insulators on the pylons. Electricians use tools with rubber or plastic handles. Pure water conducts electricity poorly, but tap water and the water on wet skin contain dissolved substances that let it conduct, which is why electrical appliances must never be handled with wet hands or used near baths.

Thermal conductivity is how easily heat flows through a material. Metals are good thermal conductors, which is why a metal spoon left in hot soup becomes hot. Wood, plastic, cloth, cork, polystyrene, wool and air are poor thermal conductors, or thermal insulators. Trapped air is an especially good insulator, which is why fluffy and foamy materials keep things warm or cold.

Thermal properties explain the potjie pot: its cast-iron body conducts heat from the coals to the food and spreads it evenly, while a wooden spoon or a cloth-covered handle, both poor conductors, protects the cook's hand. Kettles have metal or glass bodies but plastic handles; oven gloves are thick cloth; cool boxes are lined with polystyrene foam.

The questions for this section are with you now: conductors and insulators of electricity, how to test them, conductors and insulators of heat, and how both are used together in one object.

## Subtopic: Strength, Hardness, Flexibility and Density

Several other physical properties help decide which material to use.

Strength is the ability of a material to carry a load without breaking. Steel is very strong, which is why it is used in bridges, building frames and the reinforcing bars inside concrete. Concrete is strong when squeezed but weak when stretched, which is why steel bars are set inside it. Hardness is resistance to scratching and denting. Diamond is the hardest natural material and is used in drill bits for mining; glass is hard but brittle; soft materials such as lead and rubber dent easily. Hardness and strength are not the same: glass is hard but breaks easily under a blow.

Flexibility is the ability to bend without breaking; brittleness is the opposite, the tendency to crack or shatter. Rubber and many plastics are flexible, so they are used for hoses and tyres; glass and ceramics are brittle. Elasticity is the ability to return to the original shape after being stretched or bent, as a rubber band does.

Density is how much mass is packed into a given volume of a material. Lead is very dense, about 11 grams per cubic centimetre; steel about 8; aluminium about 2,7; water 1; wood about 0,5 to 0,8; and polystyrene foam well below 0,1. Materials less dense than water float. Low density is important where weight matters, which is why aircraft are built mostly from aluminium alloys and composite materials rather than steel, and why life jackets contain foam.

Other properties include absorbency, how well a material soaks up liquid, important for towels and nappies; transparency, letting light through, for windows; and resistance to corrosion, for outdoor use. Stainless steel is used for sinks and cutlery because it resists rusting.

Choosing a material means matching several properties to the job at once. A bicycle frame must be strong, fairly light and not too expensive, so steel or aluminium alloy is chosen. A window must be transparent, hard and waterproof, so glass is used. A potjie pot must have a high melting point, conduct heat well, be strong and hold heat, so cast iron is ideal.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying hard means strong; glass is hard but brittle. Two: saying water is always a good conductor; pure water is not, but tap water is. Three: calling all metals light; lead and steel are dense, aluminium is light. Four: choosing a material for one property and ignoring others. Five: saying insulators are useless; electrical and thermal insulators are essential for safety.

The questions for this section are with you now: strength, hardness, flexibility, elasticity and density, and choosing materials by matching several properties to a job.

## Subtopic: Will It Melt?

Every material has a temperature at which it melts. That is its melting point. Chocolate melts at about thirty-four degrees, so it melts in your mouth. Candle wax melts at about sixty. Iron needs about one and a half thousand degrees.

That decides what we use things for. A potjie pot sits right in the hot coals, so it is made of iron, which will not melt. A plastic pot would melt into a puddle.

Liquids have a boiling point. Water boils at a hundred degrees at the coast, and a little lower up in Johannesburg, because the air is thinner.

Some jobs need a low melting point. Solder, the soft metal used to join wires, melts easily and then hardens to hold them.

## Subtopic: Let It Through or Keep It Out

Some materials let electricity through. They are called conductors: copper, aluminium and other metals. Some block it. They are insulators: plastic, rubber, glass and dry wood.

You can test any material with a little circuit: a battery, a bulb and a gap. Put the material in the gap. If the bulb lights, it is a conductor. If not, it is an insulator.

We need both. A cable has copper inside to carry the electricity and plastic outside to keep it from your hands.

Heat works in a similar way. Metals let heat through quickly; that is why a metal spoon in hot soup burns your fingers. Wood, plastic, cloth and anything full of trapped air keep heat out. That is why the cook stirs the potjie with a wooden spoon and holds the handle with a cloth.

And remember: tap water can carry electricity, so never touch plugs with wet hands.

## Subtopic: Pick the Right Material

There are more properties to think about.

Strong materials, like steel, hold heavy loads, so bridges are built with steel. Hard materials resist scratches, like glass and diamond. But hard is not the same as strong: drop a glass and it shatters, because it is brittle. Flexible materials, like rubber, bend without breaking. Elastic materials, like a rubber band, spring back.

Density is how heavy something is for its size. A block of lead is very heavy; the same size block of polystyrene is super light. Things less dense than water float, like wood.

So when you pick a material, check all the properties the job needs. A window: see-through, hard, waterproof, so glass. An aeroplane: strong but light, so aluminium. A potjie pot: will not melt, carries heat, strong, so iron.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Physical properties can be measured without changing the material. Melting and boiling points decide whether a material survives heat. Conductors let electricity or heat through; insulators block them, and we often need both together. Strength, hardness, flexibility, elasticity and density also matter. The best material matches all the properties the job needs.
