# Part 1 — Expert

This session asks what producing and using materials does to the environment. The anchor is the life story of three everyday things in South Africa: a steel can of beans, a plastic cooldrink bottle and the coal burned to generate the electricity for a kettle. Each began as a raw material dug, pumped or mined from the Earth, was processed using energy, was used for a short time, and then had to go somewhere. The governing idea is that every stage in the life of a material, extraction, processing, transport, use and disposal, can affect land, water, air, living things and people, and that reducing, reusing and recycling, together with careful choices, can lessen the impact. The environmental impacts of metals, plastics and fuels, and ways of reducing them, are examinable, and the theme continues in waste management later this term.

## Subtopic: Metals: Mining and Processing

Metals come from ores, rocks that contain enough of a metal compound to be worth mining. South Africa is one of the world's great mining countries, producing platinum, gold, iron ore, manganese, chromium, coal and diamonds, and mining has provided jobs, export income and much of the country's infrastructure. It also has serious environmental and social impacts.

Mining changes the land. Open-pit mines and quarries remove vegetation and soil over large areas, destroying habitats. Underground mines produce huge heaps of waste rock, and the processing of ore leaves enormous dumps of finely ground waste called tailings. The yellow and grey mine dumps around Johannesburg are tailings from more than a century of gold mining. Dust blown from dumps can harm the health of nearby communities.

Mining affects water. In many mines, rock containing sulphur compounds is exposed to air and water, forming sulphuric acid. This acid mine drainage dissolves heavy metals such as iron, manganese and sometimes uranium from the rock, and when it flows or seeps into rivers and groundwater, it can kill aquatic life and make water unsafe. Old flooded gold mines beneath the Witwatersrand have been a major source of acid mine drainage, and treatment plants have had to be built to pump and neutralise the water.

Processing ores into metals uses a great deal of energy, mostly from burning coal in South Africa, and releases gases. Smelting iron ore to make steel in a blast furnace uses coke, made from coal, and releases large amounts of carbon dioxide, the main gas causing climate change. Smelting some ores also releases sulphur dioxide, which causes acid rain and breathing problems.

Ways to reduce the impact include rehabilitating mines after closure by reshaping the land, replacing soil and replanting; treating acid mine drainage; controlling dust; recycling metals, which saves ore, energy and landfill space; and designing products that last longer. Recycling steel saves most of the energy needed to make new steel from ore, and steel can be recycled again and again without losing quality.

The questions for this section are with you now: how mining affects land and water, what acid mine drainage is, why processing metals affects the air, and how recycling reduces the impact.

## Subtopic: Plastics

Plastics are made mostly from crude oil and natural gas, fossil fuels formed over millions of years. In South Africa some are also made from coal. Plastics are extremely useful: they are light, cheap, waterproof, strong, can be moulded into any shape and are good insulators. They keep food fresh, make medical equipment sterile and safe, and insulate electrical cables. But the properties that make them useful also make them an environmental problem.

Most plastics do not biodegrade, meaning that microorganisms cannot break them down. A plastic bottle may persist in the environment for hundreds of years. Sunlight and waves break plastics into smaller and smaller pieces, eventually forming microplastics, fragments smaller than five millimetres, which are now found in oceans, rivers, soil, drinking water and the bodies of animals and people.

Plastic litter harms wildlife. Turtles mistake plastic bags for jellyfish; seabirds, such as albatrosses, feed plastic pieces to their chicks; seals and dolphins become entangled in fishing line and packaging straps. Animals with stomachs full of plastic can starve. Litter blocks storm-water drains, causing flooding, and washes down rivers such as the Umgeni in Durban into the sea; after heavy rains, Durban's beaches and harbour have been covered in plastic and other waste washed down from upstream.

Producing plastics uses fossil fuels as both raw material and energy, releasing carbon dioxide. Burning plastic waste, which happens on many informal dumps, releases toxic smoke that harms people's lungs.

Ways to reduce the impact follow the waste hierarchy. Reduce: avoid single-use plastics such as straws and bags where possible; South Africa introduced a levy on plastic shopping bags in 2003 to discourage their use. Reuse: use durable bottles, containers and shopping bags many times. Recycle: many plastics, especially PET cooldrink bottles, marked with the number 1 in the recycling triangle, and HDPE milk bottles, number 2, are widely recycled in South Africa, often collected by informal waste pickers who play a major role in recycling. Some new plastics are made from plants, and some are designed to break down, though these need the right conditions.

The questions for this section are with you now: why plastics are useful, why they are a long-lasting environmental problem, what microplastics are, and how to reduce, reuse and recycle plastics.

## Subtopic: Fuels

Fuels are materials that are burned to release energy. South Africa depends heavily on coal: most of the country's electricity comes from coal-fired power stations, many of them in Mpumalanga, and coal is also turned into liquid fuels such as petrol and diesel at Secunda. Petrol, diesel and paraffin come from crude oil; liquefied petroleum gas, LPG, is used for cooking and heating; wood and charcoal are still used for cooking and heating in many households.

Burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide, which traps heat in the atmosphere and is the main cause of climate change, bringing higher temperatures, more intense droughts and floods, and rising sea levels. South Africa is one of the largest emitters of carbon dioxide in Africa, largely because of coal.

Burning fuels also causes air pollution. Coal power stations release sulphur dioxide, nitrogen oxides and fine particles. The Mpumalanga Highveld has some of the worst air quality in the world because of the concentration of power stations and industries there, and studies have linked this pollution to asthma, lung disease and early deaths. Indoor smoke from burning wood, coal and paraffin in poorly ventilated homes causes serious breathing problems, especially for children and the elderly, and paraffin stoves cause many fires and burns. Vehicle exhausts add pollution in cities.

Extracting fuels has impacts too: coal mining damages land and causes acid mine drainage, and oil spills from ships harm coastal life, including African penguins.

Reducing the impact means using less energy and switching to cleaner sources. Using energy efficiently, with efficient light bulbs, insulated homes and switching off appliances, reduces the coal burned. Renewable sources, such as solar and wind, produce electricity without burning fuel; South Africa has built many solar and wind farms since 2011, and rooftop solar panels have spread rapidly. Cleaner cooking options, such as improved stoves and gas, reduce indoor smoke. Public transport, walking and cycling reduce vehicle fuel use.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: thinking recycling is the first step; reducing and reusing come first. Two: saying plastics rot away quickly; most last hundreds of years. Three: thinking mining affects only the land; it affects water and air too. Four: saying burning fuel only causes local smoke; it also releases carbon dioxide that changes the global climate. Five: assuming any material is impact-free; every material has a life story.

The questions for this section are with you now: the impacts of burning coal and other fuels on air, climate and health, and ways of reducing energy use and switching to cleaner sources.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a can, a bottle and a lump of coal, each telling its life story — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Story of a Can

Every material has a life story. Let's follow a steel can of beans.

It begins as iron ore, dug out of a huge open pit in the Northern Cape. Digging the pit removes plants and soil, and leaves piles of waste rock. Some mines also leak acid water that can poison rivers.

The ore is taken to a factory and heated with coal in a furnace to make steel. That uses lots of energy and puffs out carbon dioxide, the gas that is heating up our planet.

The can is filled with beans, sold, and emptied in a few minutes. Then what? If it is thrown away, it sits in a landfill. If it is recycled, it is melted down and made into new steel, saving ore, energy and landfill space. And steel can be recycled over and over.

Keep the can's story in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: The Story of a Bottle

Now a plastic cooldrink bottle. Plastic is made from oil, gas or coal. It is light, cheap, waterproof and tough, which is why it is everywhere.

But here is the problem. Tiny living things that rot away food and leaves cannot eat plastic. So a bottle thrown away can last for hundreds of years. The Sun and waves break it into tiny pieces called microplastics, which end up in the sea, in fish, and even in our drinking water.

Plastic bags in the sea look like jellyfish to turtles. Seabirds feed plastic bits to their chicks. Litter blocks drains and washes down rivers onto beaches.

What can we do? Reduce: say no to straws and extra bags. Reuse: carry a water bottle and a shopping bag. Recycle: cooldrink bottles marked with a number 1 can be made into new things. And thank the waste pickers who collect most of the recycling in South Africa.

Hold the bottle's story in mind and try this part's questions.

## Subtopic: The Story of a Lump of Coal

Switch on a kettle in South Africa and you are probably using electricity made by burning coal, mostly in Mpumalanga.

Coal is dug from mines, which scar the land and can leak acid water. Then it is burned in a power station. The smoke carries gases and soot that make the air dirty and cause coughing and asthma. And it releases carbon dioxide, which is heating up the whole planet, bringing worse droughts and floods.

At home, burning wood, coal or paraffin indoors fills rooms with smoke that harms children's lungs, and paraffin stoves can cause fires.

The answers: use less electricity, switch things off, use efficient bulbs, and get more energy from the Sun and the wind, which do not burn anything.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Every material has a life story, and each stage can harm the environment. Mining damages land and water, and making metals uses energy and releases carbon dioxide. Plastics come from fossil fuels, do not rot, and harm wildlife and oceans. Burning coal and other fuels pollutes the air and causes climate change. We can reduce, reuse and recycle, use energy carefully, and switch to cleaner energy.

Hold the can, the bottle and the lump of coal, and take the final questions of the lesson.
