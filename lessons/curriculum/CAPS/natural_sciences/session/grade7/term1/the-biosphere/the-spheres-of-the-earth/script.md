# Part 1 — Expert

This session opens Grade 7 Life and Living by describing the place where all life is found. The anchor is a single handful of soil from a riverbank in KwaZulu-Natal: it holds rock grains, water, air and living things, and so it contains, in miniature, every part of the Earth that supports life. The governing idea is that the Earth can be described as a set of overlapping spheres, the lithosphere of rock and soil, the hydrosphere of water and the atmosphere of air, and that the biosphere is the thin zone where these three meet and life exists, including all living organisms and the dead organic matter they leave behind. The names of the spheres, what each contains, how far the biosphere extends and how materials move between the spheres are all examinable, and the idea returns in Grade 8 ecology and in Grade 10 Life Sciences and Geography.

## Subtopic: The Lithosphere, Hydrosphere and Atmosphere

Scientists find it useful to describe the Earth as a set of layers or spheres, each named after what it is made of. The word sphere comes from the Greek for ball, because each layer wraps around the planet.

The lithosphere is the solid, rocky outer layer of the Earth. The name comes from the Greek lithos, meaning stone. It includes the rocks of the crust, the mountains, the ocean floor, and the soil that forms on top of the rock when rock is slowly broken down by weather and mixed with dead plant and animal material. The Drakensberg, the sandstone of Table Mountain and the red soils of Limpopo are all part of the lithosphere. Soil is the part of the lithosphere that matters most for life: plants anchor their roots in it and take up water and minerals from it.

The hydrosphere is all the water on, under and above the Earth's surface. The name comes from the Greek hydor, meaning water. It includes the oceans, which hold about ninety-seven per cent of all the Earth's water, as well as rivers such as the Orange and the Limpopo, lakes, dams, groundwater stored in rocks underground, ice and snow, and the water vapour in clouds. Only about three per cent of the Earth's water is fresh, and most of that is frozen in ice caps and glaciers, so the fresh water that people, plants and land animals can use is a very small fraction of the total. South Africa is a water-scarce country, which makes this fact especially important here.

The atmosphere is the layer of gases that surrounds the Earth, held in place by gravity. It is mostly nitrogen, about seventy-eight per cent, and oxygen, about twenty-one per cent, with small amounts of argon, carbon dioxide and water vapour. The atmosphere provides the oxygen that animals breathe, the carbon dioxide that plants use to make food, and a blanket that keeps the Earth warm enough for life and shields it from much of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet radiation. Most of the air is packed into the lowest ten or so kilometres; higher up, the air becomes so thin that climbers on the highest mountains need bottled oxygen.

The three spheres are not separate boxes. Water seeps into soil and rock, so the hydrosphere reaches into the lithosphere. Water evaporates into the air, so the hydrosphere reaches into the atmosphere. Air fills the spaces between soil particles and dissolves in water, where fish use it. That overlapping is the key to the next idea.

The questions for this section are with you now: the names of the three spheres, what each one contains, and examples of each from South Africa.

## Subtopic: The Biosphere

The biosphere is the part of the Earth where life exists. The name comes from the Greek bios, meaning life. It is not a separate layer sitting on top of the others; it is the zone where the lithosphere, hydrosphere and atmosphere overlap and where conditions allow living things to survive. The biosphere includes all living organisms, from bacteria to baobab trees to whales, and it also includes dead organic matter: fallen leaves, dead wood, animal droppings, the bodies of dead organisms and the dark, crumbly humus in soil that forms as they decay.

Dead organic matter is included because it is still part of the living system. Decomposers such as fungi, bacteria and earthworms feed on it and break it down, and in doing so they return nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus to the soil, where plants take them up again. Without the dead material and the decomposers, the nutrients would stay locked up and life would run out of raw materials.

The biosphere is surprisingly thin. Most living things are found from a few metres below the soil surface to a few hundred metres above it, and in the top two hundred metres or so of the oceans, where sunlight reaches. If the Earth were the size of an apple, the biosphere would be thinner than its skin. Some life reaches further: bacteria have been found several kilometres underground in South African gold mines, birds and insects are carried kilometres up into the air, and animals live in the deepest ocean trenches. But the great bulk of life lives in that thin film.

Life in the biosphere depends on all three of the other spheres at once. A thorn tree in the Kruger National Park takes water and minerals from the soil of the lithosphere, uses rainwater from the hydrosphere, and takes in carbon dioxide from the atmosphere to make food by photosynthesis, releasing oxygen back into the air. A frog in a wetland breathes air, lays eggs in water and shelters in mud. Every organism is a meeting point of the spheres.

The anchor handful of soil shows the same thing. Rub it between the fingers and there are sand and clay grains from the lithosphere; squeeze it and water from the hydrosphere darkens the skin; the tiny gaps between grains hold air from the atmosphere; and in it live roots, earthworms, insects, fungi and billions of bacteria, with rotting leaves and twigs mixed through. One handful, all four spheres.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of the biosphere, why dead organic matter is part of it, and how far the biosphere extends.

## Subtopic: How the Spheres Interact

Because the spheres overlap, materials and energy move between them all the time. Two of the most important movements are the water cycle and the cycling of carbon and oxygen.

In the water cycle, the Sun's heat evaporates water from oceans, lakes and the soil, moving it from the hydrosphere into the atmosphere as water vapour. Plants add more water vapour by losing it through tiny pores in their leaves, a process called transpiration, so the biosphere pumps water into the atmosphere too. As the vapour rises and cools it condenses into clouds, and it falls as rain, hail or snow. Some of that water runs off into rivers and back to the sea; some soaks into the soil and rock of the lithosphere to become groundwater; and some is taken up by plant roots and drunk by animals, entering the biosphere again.

Carbon and oxygen move in a linked cycle. Plants in the biosphere take carbon dioxide from the atmosphere and, using sunlight, build it into sugars, releasing oxygen. Animals eat plants and take in the carbon; plants and animals both release carbon dioxide back into the air when they respire. When organisms die, decomposers break down their bodies, and more carbon dioxide returns to the atmosphere while nutrients return to the soil. Over millions of years some buried dead material became coal, oil and gas in the lithosphere, a store of carbon that comes back into the atmosphere when fuels are burned.

The lithosphere and atmosphere also interact without life. Rain, wind and changing temperatures slowly crack and wear rock down, a process called weathering, and the fragments become the mineral part of soil. Volcanoes release gases from deep in the Earth into the air.

People are part of the biosphere and affect every sphere. Burning fuels adds carbon dioxide to the atmosphere; clearing vegetation exposes soil to erosion, washing the lithosphere into rivers; pollution and over-use reduce the fresh water in the hydrosphere. Because the spheres are connected, a change in one shows up in the others: soil eroded from overgrazed land in the Eastern Cape ends up as mud that silts up dams downstream.

The questions for this section are with you now: how water moves between the spheres, how plants and animals cycle carbon and oxygen, and an example of people changing one sphere and affecting another.

## Subtopic: Describing the Spheres in Examinations

Questions on the spheres usually ask for one of three things: a definition, an example, or an interaction. Each has a reliable layout.

For a definition, name the sphere, give the Greek root and state what it contains. For example: the hydrosphere, from hydor meaning water, is all the water on, under and above the Earth's surface, including oceans, rivers, groundwater, ice and water vapour. The biosphere definition needs both parts: all living organisms and dead organic matter, in the zone where the other three spheres overlap.

For an example, choose something specific. A mark is safer for the Vaal River, the Drakensberg or the air above Durban than for water, rock or air in general.

For an interaction, name both spheres and the process that links them. For example: the atmosphere and the lithosphere interact through weathering, when rain and temperature changes break rock into the particles that form soil. Or: the biosphere and the atmosphere interact through photosynthesis, when plants take carbon dioxide from the air and release oxygen.

A useful check is to classify the parts of one object. A pond contains water (hydrosphere), mud and stones (lithosphere), dissolved air and bubbles (atmosphere), and frogs, reeds, algae and rotting leaves (biosphere).

The error museum, five exhibits. One: calling the biosphere a fourth layer above the atmosphere; it is the zone where the other three overlap. Two: leaving dead organic matter out of the biosphere; it is included because decomposers recycle it. Three: saying the hydrosphere is only the oceans; it includes groundwater, ice and water vapour too. Four: saying the atmosphere is mostly oxygen; it is mostly nitrogen. Five: describing soil as part of the atmosphere because it holds air; soil is part of the lithosphere, with air in its spaces.

The questions for this section are with you now: defining each sphere with its root word, classifying the parts of a pond or a handful of soil, and naming an interaction with its process.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a handful of mud, a layered sandwich and a planet the size of an apple — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Handful of Everything

Imagine you scoop up a handful of damp soil from the bank of a river. It looks like plain dirt, but it is actually a sample of the whole planet.

Rub it between your fingers and you feel tiny grains of sand and clay. Those grains are bits of broken rock. Rock and soil are the lithosphere: litho means stone.

Squeeze it and your fingers get wet. That water is part of the hydrosphere: hydro means water. The hydrosphere is every bit of water on Earth, in the sea, in rivers and dams, underground, in ice and even floating in clouds.

Look closely and the soil is full of tiny gaps. Those gaps hold air. Air is the atmosphere, the blanket of gas around the Earth. It is mostly nitrogen, with about one fifth oxygen.

And then there is life. Thin roots, a wriggling earthworm, a beetle, white threads of fungus, and billions of bacteria too small to see. Bits of rotting leaf, too. All of that is the biosphere: bio means life.

So one handful holds four spheres: rock, water, air and life. That is why soil matters so much. It is where all four meet.

Keep that handful in mind and try the questions for this part: the four spheres, their root words, and what each one contains.

## Subtopic: The Planet Sandwich

Think of the Earth as a sandwich. The bread at the bottom is the rocky lithosphere. The filling poured over it in places is water, the hydrosphere. Over the whole sandwich is a see-through cover of air, the atmosphere.

So where is the biosphere? It is not another slice. It is where the layers touch. Life lives where rock, water and air meet: in soil, in the shallow sea, on the land surface, in rivers and in the lower air.

And it is thin. If the Earth were shrunk to the size of an apple, the part where almost all life lives would be thinner than the apple's skin. Fish swim in the top part of the sea where sunlight reaches. Roots go a few metres down. Birds fly a few hundred metres up. That thin skin is home to every plant, animal, fungus and microbe we know.

The biosphere also includes dead things. A fallen leaf, a dead beetle, a cow pat. These are not rubbish to nature. Fungi, bacteria and earthworms eat them and turn them back into food for plants. That is nature's recycling.

Hold the sandwich picture in mind and try this part's questions: why the biosphere is not a separate layer, how thin it is, and why dead things belong to it.

## Subtopic: Everything Is Connected

The spheres are always swapping things.

Water travels. The Sun warms the sea and water rises into the air. Plants breathe water out of their leaves too. The water makes clouds, falls as rain, runs into rivers or soaks into the ground, and gets drunk by animals and sucked up by roots. Then round it goes again.

Gases travel. Plants take carbon dioxide out of the air and give out oxygen. Animals breathe in oxygen and breathe out carbon dioxide. So plants and animals keep swapping gases through the air.

Soil travels too. Rain and wind slowly break rock into tiny pieces that become soil. But if people let cattle eat all the grass, the bare soil washes into rivers and fills dams with mud.

That last example shows the big idea. Change one sphere and the others feel it. Lose the grass, lose the soil, muddy the water.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The lithosphere is rock and soil. The hydrosphere is all the water. The atmosphere is the air, mostly nitrogen with some oxygen. The biosphere is where the three overlap and life exists, and it includes all living things and dead organic matter. The spheres are always exchanging water, gases and soil, so a change in one affects the others.

Hold the handful of soil, the sandwich and the muddy dam, and take the final questions of the lesson: how water travels, how plants and animals swap gases, and what happens when grass is lost.
