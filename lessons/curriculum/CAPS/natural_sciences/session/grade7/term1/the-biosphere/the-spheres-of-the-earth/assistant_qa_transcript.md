### subtopic_1 — The Lithosphere, Hydrosphere and Atmosphere
**Assistant:** If only three per cent of Earth's water is fresh, why do we not simply use seawater?

**Tutor:** The short answer is that seawater is full of dissolved salt, about thirty-five grams in every litre, and that salt makes it harmful for drinking, for most crops and for most of our machines. If a person drinks seawater, the body has to get rid of the extra salt through the kidneys, and to do that it uses more water than the seawater provided, so the person becomes more dehydrated, not less. Most crop plants are damaged by salty water too, because salt in the soil draws water out of their roots instead of letting it in. Seawater can be made fresh by a process called desalination, which either boils the water and collects the steam, or pushes it at high pressure through very fine filters that let water through but hold back the salt. Some coastal towns in South Africa, such as Mossel Bay and parts of Cape Town during the drought of 2017 and 2018, have used desalination plants. The difficulty is cost: desalination needs a great deal of energy, and the very salty water left over has to be disposed of carefully so that it does not harm sea life. Inland areas such as Gauteng are also far from the sea and high above it, so pumping desalinated water there would be extremely expensive. That is why saving fresh water, protecting rivers and storing water in dams remain the main way South Africa looks after its supply.

### subtopic_2 — The Biosphere
**Assistant:** Why are bacteria found kilometres underground in gold mines, when there is no sunlight there?

**Tutor:** Because not all life runs on sunlight, even though almost all the life we see does. The bacteria discovered in water flowing through cracks in rock nearly three kilometres down in South African gold mines near Johannesburg get their energy from chemical reactions instead. Radioactive elements in the surrounding rock slowly split water into hydrogen and other substances, and some of these bacteria use the hydrogen, together with sulphur compounds in the rock, as a source of energy to build their bodies and reproduce. They live extremely slowly, and some scientists estimate that individual cells may take years or even centuries to divide. These discoveries matter for two reasons. First, they show that the biosphere is deeper than people once thought, extending well into the lithosphere wherever there is liquid water and a usable energy source. Second, they help scientists imagine where life might exist on other worlds, such as underneath the frozen surface of Mars or inside icy moons of Jupiter and Saturn, where sunlight never reaches but water and chemical energy might be present. For Grade 7, the examinable idea stays simple: the biosphere is the zone where life exists, it overlaps the other three spheres, and most life depends on energy from the Sun, with a few remarkable exceptions in places like these.

### subtopic_3 — How the Spheres Interact
**Assistant:** How can soil eroded in one province end up harming a dam in another province?

**Tutor:** Because rivers carry soil wherever they flow, and rivers ignore provincial borders. When land is overgrazed or cleared of plants, the roots that held the soil together are gone and the ground is left bare. Heavy summer thunderstorms then wash the loose topsoil into streams, which is why rivers in flood often look brown or red: they are carrying soil. The streams join larger rivers, and the soil travels with the water until the river slows down, which happens when it enters a dam. In still water the soil particles sink to the bottom and build up as a layer of mud called silt. Over many years the silt fills part of the dam, so it holds less water, and the water that remains can be muddier and more expensive to clean for drinking. The Orange River, for example, carries soil from the highlands of Lesotho and the Free State, and large dams along it have lost some of their storage capacity to silt. The farmer whose soil washed away loses too, because topsoil is the most fertile layer and takes hundreds of years to form. This is a clear example of the spheres interacting: a change in the biosphere, the loss of plant cover, changes the lithosphere by removing soil, and that changes the hydrosphere by filling dams with silt. Protecting grass cover, avoiding overgrazing and building contour barriers on slopes reduce the problem at its source.

### subtopic_4 — Describing the Spheres in Examinations
**Assistant:** Why is it useful to split one connected planet into four spheres at all?

**Tutor:** Because naming the parts makes it possible to ask clear questions about how they affect one another, which is exactly what scientists need to do to solve real problems. If we only said that the Earth is one big connected system, which is true, we would struggle to say where a problem starts and where it ends. Dividing the Earth into spheres gives us a vocabulary. A hydrologist can study how much water a river carries and how much soaks into the ground; a soil scientist can study how quickly topsoil forms and erodes; an atmospheric scientist can measure how much carbon dioxide is in the air; an ecologist can study how a wetland's plants and animals depend on all three. When the different experts meet, the spheres let them describe the links precisely: overgrazing in the biosphere causes erosion of the lithosphere, which silts the hydrosphere. The danger is to forget the overlap and treat the spheres as separate boxes, which is why the definition of the biosphere deliberately describes it as the zone where the others meet. A good habit is to use the spheres as a checklist. Faced with any environmental question, such as building a new mine or a new dam, ask what it will do to the rock and soil, to the water, to the air and to the living things, and then ask how a change in one will spread into the others.
