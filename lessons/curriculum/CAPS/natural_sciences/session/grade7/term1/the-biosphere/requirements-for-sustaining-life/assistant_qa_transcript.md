### subtopic_1 — Energy from the Sun
**Assistant:** How can animals live at the bottom of the deep ocean, where sunlight never reaches?

**Tutor:** Most deep-sea animals still depend on sunlight, just indirectly and from far above. In the sunlit surface waters, phytoplankton photosynthesise and are eaten by small animals, which are eaten by larger ones. When these organisms die, or produce droppings, the material slowly sinks through the dark water like a gentle snow, which scientists call marine snow. Animals in the deep sea, such as sea cucumbers, worms, crabs and strange deep-sea fish, feed on this falling material or on each other. A whale carcass that sinks to the sea floor can feed a whole community for decades. So even in total darkness, the energy was captured from the Sun at the surface. There is one remarkable exception. At hydrothermal vents on the ocean floor, hot water rich in dissolved chemicals such as hydrogen sulphide gushes out of cracks in the Earth's crust. Bacteria there obtain energy from chemical reactions with these substances, a process called chemosynthesis, and use it to make food without any light. Giant tube worms, clams and shrimps live around the vents, many of them feeding on or housing these bacteria. Vent communities were only discovered in 1977, and they changed how scientists think about where life can exist. For Grade 7, the rule is that the Sun is the energy source for almost all life, and vent communities are the famous exception.

### subtopic_2 — Gases and Water
**Assistant:** Why do fish suffocate in a dam on a hot day when the water is full of algae?

**Tutor:** Because the amount of oxygen dissolved in water is small, and two things together can use it up faster than it is replaced. Water holds only a little dissolved oxygen even in good conditions, far less than the same volume of air, and warm water holds less oxygen than cold water. So on a hot day the dam starts with less oxygen. Now add a bloom of algae, which often happens when fertiliser or sewage washes into a dam and feeds them. During the day the algae photosynthesise and actually add oxygen, but at night they stop photosynthesising and respire, using oxygen like every other living cell. Worse, when the algae die in huge numbers, bacteria decompose them, and decomposing bacteria use large amounts of oxygen too. In the hours before dawn, the oxygen level can fall so low that fish cannot get enough through their gills, and they gasp at the surface and die. This is why fish kills are often discovered early in the morning after a run of hot, still weather. It is also why rivers polluted with sewage can lose almost all their fish. Preventing nutrient pollution, keeping wetlands that filter runoff, and avoiding overloading dams with fertiliser all help. The lesson for this topic is that the right gases must be available in the right form and the right place: oxygen dissolved in water is just as essential for a fish as oxygen in air is for us.

### subtopic_3 — Soil and Temperature
**Assistant:** If soil takes hundreds of years to form, how do farmers stop it from being lost?

**Tutor:** By keeping the soil covered, slowing water down and feeding the soil life that builds it. Soil is lost mainly by erosion: rain splashes and washes bare soil away, and wind blows dry loose soil off. So the first rule is never to leave soil bare for long. Farmers plant cover crops between main crops, leave crop leftovers on the surface instead of burning them, and rotate grazing so that animals move on before the grass is eaten to the ground. The second rule is to slow down running water. On slopes, farmers plough along the contour, across the slope rather than up and down it, so that each furrow catches water; they build terraces and contour banks, and they plant grass strips in places where water collects so that gullies cannot start. The third rule is to keep the soil alive. Adding compost and manure increases humus, which holds soil particles together, holds water like a sponge and feeds earthworms and bacteria; the burrows of earthworms let rain soak in instead of running off. Reduced tillage, disturbing the soil less, keeps its structure intact. South Africa has seen the cost of ignoring these rules: large dongas, deep erosion gullies, scar parts of the Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal, and the soil lost from them has gone for good on any human timescale. Protecting soil is therefore one of the most important parts of keeping the requirements for life in place.
