### subtopic_1 — Nutrition, Respiration and Excretion
**Assistant:** If plants respire all the time, why do we say plants give us oxygen?

**Tutor:** Because during daylight a plant makes much more oxygen by photosynthesis than it uses up by respiration, so overall it gives oxygen out. Both processes happen at once in a leaf in the sunshine. Respiration in every cell uses a little oxygen and releases a little carbon dioxide, exactly as it does in our own cells. At the same time photosynthesis in the green cells uses carbon dioxide and releases oxygen, and in good light it runs many times faster than respiration. The difference is released into the air, and that surplus is the oxygen we depend on. At night photosynthesis stops because there is no light, but respiration continues, so for those hours a plant takes in a small amount of oxygen and gives out a small amount of carbon dioxide. Over a whole day and night a growing plant still produces more oxygen than it uses, because it is building new material out of carbon dioxide, and that carbon is locked up in its stems, leaves and roots. A common myth says it is dangerous to sleep in a room with plants because they use up the oxygen at night. The amount a few pot plants use is tiny compared with what one sleeping person uses, so there is no danger. The examinable point is that respiration is continuous in all living cells, while photosynthesis happens only in green cells and only in light.

### subtopic_2 — Growth, Reproduction, Sensitivity and Movement
**Assistant:** How can a plant sense light and bend towards it without eyes or a brain?

**Tutor:** Because the tip of a shoot contains cells that are sensitive to light and produce a chemical messenger, a plant hormone called auxin, which controls how fast cells grow. When light shines evenly from above, auxin spreads evenly down the shoot, the cells on all sides grow at the same rate, and the shoot grows straight up. When light comes from one side, for example through a window, the auxin moves towards the shaded side of the shoot. There it makes the cells grow longer than the cells on the lit side. A shoot whose shaded side grows faster than its lit side must bend, and it bends towards the light. This response is called phototropism, from photo meaning light and tropism meaning a turning growth response. It is slow, taking hours, but it is effective: the leaves end up facing the light they need for photosynthesis. Scientists first tested this in the late 1800s, when Charles Darwin and his son covered the tips of grass seedlings with little caps and found that the seedlings stopped bending; the tip was where the light was detected. Roots respond to gravity in a similar way, which is why a seed planted upside down still sends its root downwards. So a plant has no brain and no nerves, but it does have sensitivity: it detects a stimulus, light or gravity, and responds by changing the way it grows.

### subtopic_3 — Living or Non-living
**Assistant:** Is a seed in a packet alive, even though it does nothing for years?

**Tutor:** Yes, a seed is alive, but it is in a resting state called dormancy, in which its life processes have slowed down almost to nothing. Inside the seed coat is a tiny plant, the embryo, with a small root, a small shoot and one or two seed leaves, together with a store of food. While the seed is dry, its cells contain very little water, and the chemical reactions of life, including respiration, run extremely slowly. That allows the seed to survive cold, heat and drought for months or years without using up its food. We can show it is alive by giving it water, warmth and oxygen: the seed absorbs water, its respiration speeds up, it uses its food store for energy, and it germinates, sending out a root and a shoot. A boiled seed, by contrast, will not germinate however well it is treated, because heat has killed its cells. Seeds can survive for astonishing lengths of time. Scientists have germinated a date palm seed about two thousand years old that was found in an ancient fortress in Israel, and seed banks around the world, including one at Kirstenbosch in Cape Town, store seeds of indigenous plants in cool, dry conditions so that they can be grown again if wild populations are lost. So a seed is a good reminder that being alive does not mean being busy at every moment; it means being able to carry out the life processes when conditions allow.
