### subtopic_1 — What an Adaptation Is
**Assistant:** How can giraffes have long necks if animals do not stretch their necks and pass it on?

**Tutor:** This is exactly the question that separated an older idea from the one scientists accept today. About two hundred years ago, the French naturalist Jean-Baptiste Lamarck suggested that giraffes stretched their necks to reach high leaves, that each giraffe's neck became a little longer through use, and that the longer neck was passed on. It is an appealing idea, but it does not match what we know about inheritance: changes to the body during a lifetime, like stretched muscles or a scar, are not written into the genetic instructions in sex cells, so they are not inherited. The explanation accepted today comes from Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace in the 1850s. Giraffes, like all species, vary: some are born with slightly longer necks than others, because of differences in the genetic instructions they inherited. In times when food is scarce, those that can reach higher leaves, beyond the reach of other browsers, are more likely to survive and raise young, and their young tend to inherit the longer necks. Over very many generations this shifts the whole population towards longer necks. This process is natural selection, and it is the topic of Grade 12. Scientists today also think long necks help giraffe males in neck-swinging contests over mates, so more than one advantage may be involved. For Grade 7 the key point is that adaptations arise in populations over many generations, through inherited variation, not by individuals trying.

### subtopic_2 — Adaptations to Hot, Dry Places
**Assistant:** Why do so many desert plants have spines instead of ordinary leaves?

**Tutor:** Spines solve two desert problems at once: losing water and being eaten. A broad, flat leaf is excellent at catching sunlight, but it also has a large surface through which water evaporates, especially in hot, dry wind. Water leaves a plant mainly through tiny pores called stomata on the leaves. Turning leaves into narrow spines removes most of that surface, so the plant loses much less water. Photosynthesis then takes place in the green, fleshy stem instead, which stores water and has fewer pores. The second problem is that a plant full of stored water is a tempting drink for thirsty animals in a dry land, and spines make it painful to bite. Some spines also shade the stem slightly and trap a thin layer of still air near the surface, which reduces drying. It is worth knowing that many spiny succulents in South Africa, such as the euphorbias, are not cactuses at all; cactuses come naturally from the Americas, while euphorbias evolved in Africa. Yet they look remarkably similar because they faced the same desert challenges and evolved similar solutions independently. Biologists call this convergent evolution, and it is strong evidence that the features really are adaptations to the habitat: unrelated plants, separated by an ocean, arrived at the same design. Prickly pear, which is a true cactus, was brought to South Africa and became an invasive weed in the Eastern Cape for the same reason: it was already well adapted to the dry conditions it found.

### subtopic_3 — Adaptations to Water, Cold and Forest
**Assistant:** Why would a poisonous animal want to be brightly coloured, when it could hide instead?

**Tutor:** Because for a poisonous or bad-tasting animal, being seen and remembered is safer than being found by accident and bitten. A well-camouflaged poisonous frog might still be grabbed by a bird that did not notice it until the last moment; even if the bird then spits it out, the frog may be injured or killed. A brightly coloured poisonous animal advertises itself instead. A predator that tries to eat one has an unpleasant experience, such as a burning mouth, vomiting or worse, and it learns quickly to avoid anything with those colours. After that, every animal of the same species with the same colours is left alone. This kind of signal is called warning colouration, and the colours chosen by evolution are usually bold combinations such as red, yellow, orange and black, which are easy to see and easy to remember. In South Africa the foam grasshopper is a good example: it is brightly coloured, and if disturbed it oozes a foul-smelling toxic foam made partly from poisonous plants it has eaten. Some harmless animals take advantage of the system by evolving the same colours as dangerous ones, so predators avoid them too; this is called mimicry. So hiding and advertising are two different solutions to the same problem of avoiding predators, and which one evolves depends on whether the animal has a chemical defence that makes the warning honest.
