# Part 1 — Expert

This session asks what living things need in order to stay alive, and why life is found in some parts of the biosphere and not others. The anchor is a comparison between two South African places on the same day: a lush patch of forest in the Tsitsikamma on the Garden Route, and the dry gravel plains of the Richtersveld in the Northern Cape. Both receive sunlight, both have air, and yet one is crowded with trees, ferns and birds while the other has scattered succulents and few animals. The governing idea is that life depends on five requirements, a source of energy, the right gases, water, soil and favourable temperatures, and that where one of them is in short supply, life is scarcer or must be specially adapted. Each requirement, why it is needed and how it limits life are examinable, and the ideas return in Grade 8 ecology.

## Subtopic: Energy from the Sun

Every living thing needs a continuous supply of energy, because all seven life processes use energy. Almost all of that energy comes, in the end, from the Sun.

Green plants capture sunlight by photosynthesis. In their leaves, chlorophyll absorbs light energy, and the plant uses it to join carbon dioxide and water into sugar, releasing oxygen. The energy is now stored in the sugar as chemical energy. The plant uses some of the sugar for its own respiration and builds the rest into its body. Because plants make food from sunlight, they are called producers, and they are the entry point for energy into the biosphere.

Animals cannot use sunlight directly for food. A herbivore such as a kudu gets energy by eating plants; a carnivore such as a leopard gets energy by eating herbivores; a decomposer such as a mushroom gets energy from dead material. Each feeding step passes on only part of the energy, because most is used for life processes and lost as heat. So the energy that keeps a leopard alive was first captured from sunlight by grass or leaves, perhaps only weeks earlier.

Sunlight also provides warmth. Without the Sun the Earth's surface would freeze, and the water cycle, which depends on the Sun's heat to evaporate water, would stop.

The amount of sunlight varies from place to place. A forest floor beneath a thick canopy is dim, so only shade-tolerant plants grow there; the sunlit tops of the trees capture most of the light. In the sea, sunlight reaches only the top two hundred metres or so, which is why most marine producers, the tiny drifting algae called phytoplankton, live near the surface.

The questions for this section are with you now: why living things need energy, how plants capture the Sun's energy, and how animals obtain it.

## Subtopic: Gases and Water

Living things depend on the gases of the atmosphere and on water.

Oxygen is needed for respiration in almost all organisms. Land animals take it from the air through lungs or, in insects, through tiny tubes; fish take it from oxygen dissolved in water, using their gills. Plants need oxygen for respiration too, including their roots, which is why most plants die if the soil stays waterlogged: the water fills the air spaces and the roots suffocate. Carbon dioxide is needed by plants for photosynthesis; although it makes up only about 0,04 per cent of the air, it is the source of the carbon in every living body. Nitrogen, the main gas of the air, cannot be used directly by plants or animals, but certain bacteria in the soil and in the root nodules of plants such as beans and peas turn it into compounds that plants can absorb and use to make proteins.

Water is essential to every organism. Most living things are between sixty and ninety per cent water; a human body is about sixty per cent. Water is the substance in which the chemical reactions of the cells take place; it carries dissolved food, oxygen and wastes around the body in blood and in plant sap; it is a raw material for photosynthesis; it helps regulate temperature, as when sweating cools the skin; and for aquatic organisms it is the home itself. A person can survive several weeks without food but only a few days without water.

Water availability is one of the strongest limits on life in South Africa. Annual rainfall ranges from more than a thousand millimetres in parts of the eastern escarpment to less than fifty millimetres along parts of the west coast. The Tsitsikamma forest, with rain in every month, supports tall trees with broad leaves. The Richtersveld, with very little rain, supports mainly succulent plants that store water in thick leaves and stems, and animals that get most of their water from their food or from fog.

The questions for this section are with you now: why organisms need oxygen and carbon dioxide, how bacteria make nitrogen usable, and four reasons living things need water.

## Subtopic: Soil and Temperature

Soil and temperature complete the list.

Soil is a mixture of rock particles, humus, water, air and living organisms. It anchors plants by their roots, and it supplies water and dissolved mineral nutrients such as nitrogen compounds, phosphorus and potassium that plants need to build proteins and other substances. Soil also houses huge numbers of decomposers, earthworms and burrowing animals. Different soils suit different plants. Sandy soils drain quickly and hold few nutrients; clay soils hold water and nutrients but can become waterlogged; loam, a mixture of sand, clay and humus, is the best soil for most crops. Topsoil, the dark upper layer rich in humus, forms extremely slowly, in the order of hundreds of years for a few centimetres, so losing it to erosion is a serious problem for farming.

Temperature matters because the chemical reactions of life work only within a certain range. Most organisms live between about zero and forty-five degrees Celsius. Below zero, water in cells can freeze, and ice crystals damage cells; above about forty-five degrees, many of the proteins that run the cell's reactions begin to break down. Temperature also controls how fast reactions happen, so cold-blooded animals such as lizards are slow on cold mornings and bask in the Sun to warm up. Birds and mammals keep their body temperature constant, about thirty-seven degrees in humans, but must use energy to do so.

Favourable temperatures differ between organisms. A Cape fynbos plant tolerates hot, dry summers; a tropical plant from Mpumalanga's lowveld is killed by frost; some bacteria in hot springs survive near boiling point. But every species has a range, and outside it the organism cannot survive.

Putting the five requirements together explains the two anchor places. The Tsitsikamma has sunlight, plenty of water, deep forest soil, mild temperatures and air, so life is abundant. The Richtersveld has sunlight and air, but little water, thin rocky soils and very hot summer days, so life is sparse and specially adapted. Water is the limiting factor there: the one requirement in shortest supply that holds life back.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying plants need only water and sunlight; they need carbon dioxide, oxygen, minerals and a suitable temperature too. Two: saying fish do not need oxygen; they use dissolved oxygen. Three: treating soil as dead dirt; it contains air, water, humus and organisms. Four: saying all organisms need the same temperature; ranges differ. Five: forgetting that the Sun is the original energy source for animals too.

The questions for this section are with you now: what soil provides, why temperature limits life, and how the idea of a limiting factor explains the difference between the forest and the desert.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a backpack for survival, a forest and a desert — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Survival Backpack

Imagine every living thing carries a survival backpack. Inside there are five things it cannot do without.

First, energy. Plants get it straight from the Sun, using sunlight to make food. Animals get it by eating plants, or eating animals that ate plants. So the energy in your lunch came from the Sun.

Second, gases. Almost every living thing needs oxygen to release energy from food. Plants also need carbon dioxide to make food. Fish need oxygen too; they get it from the water through their gills.

Third, water. Your body is about sixty per cent water. Water carries food around your body, and every reaction in your cells happens in water. You can live weeks without food but only a few days without water.

Fourth, soil. Plants stand in it, drink from it and get their minerals from it, a bit like vitamins.

Fifth, the right temperature. Too cold and cells freeze; too hot and they get damaged. Most living things like it between about zero and forty-five degrees.

Energy, gases, water, soil, temperature. That is the survival backpack.

Keep the backpack in mind and try the questions for this part: the five requirements and why each one matters.

## Subtopic: Forest and Desert

Let's visit two places in South Africa on the same day.

First, the Tsitsikamma forest on the Garden Route. It rains in every month. The soil is deep and dark. It is never very hot or very cold. Tall trees, ferns, mosses, birds and insects are everywhere. Every item in the backpack is easy to find.

Now the Richtersveld in the Northern Cape. There is just as much sunlight and air. But hardly any rain falls, the soil is thin and stony, and summer days are scorching. Here you see only scattered plants with fat, juicy leaves that store water, and a few tough animals.

Why the difference? The desert is short of one thing in the backpack: water. That one missing item holds everything back. Scientists call it the limiting factor. A forest has plenty of everything; a desert has a shortage of water.

Hold the two places in mind and try this part's questions: why the forest is crowded and why the desert is sparse.

## Subtopic: Looking After the Backpack

People can empty the backpack without meaning to.

If we pollute a dam with fertiliser or sewage, green algae grow too much. When they die, bacteria use up the oxygen in the water, and fish suffocate. So pollution can take away the gas fish need.

If we let all the grass be eaten, rain washes the soil away. Good topsoil takes hundreds of years to form, and it can be lost in one stormy summer.

If we waste water, there is less for people, crops and wild animals, and South Africa is already a dry country.

So keeping life going means keeping the backpack full: clean water, healthy soil, clean air and enough plants to capture the Sun's energy.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Living things need five things: energy, mostly from the Sun through plants; gases, oxygen for respiration and carbon dioxide for photosynthesis; water for every life process; soil for plants to anchor, drink and get minerals; and a favourable temperature. Where one is in short supply, like water in a desert, it becomes the limiting factor and life is sparse.

Hold the backpack, the forest and the desert, and take the final questions of the lesson: what pollution and erosion take away from living things.
