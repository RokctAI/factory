# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains why living things look and behave the way they do. The anchor is the gemsbok, or oryx, of the Kalahari, an antelope that can go for weeks without drinking, standing in temperatures above forty degrees, beside a quiver tree with its waxy, water-storing branches. The governing idea is that an adaptation is a feature of structure, body function or behaviour that helps an organism survive and reproduce in its particular environment, and that organisms are adapted to the conditions of their habitat: water, temperature, food, light and predators. The meaning of adaptation, the three kinds of adaptation and South African examples from deserts, water, cold mountains and forests are examinable, and adaptation is the starting point of evolution in Grade 12 Life Sciences.

## Subtopic: What an Adaptation Is

A habitat is the place where an organism lives, such as a desert, a river, a forest or a rocky shore. Each habitat presents particular challenges: how hot or cold it is, how much water there is, what food is available, how much light, and which predators or competitors are present. An adaptation is any inherited feature that helps an organism survive and reproduce in its habitat.

Adaptations are usually grouped into three kinds. Structural adaptations are features of the body's shape or parts: the thick fur of a mountain animal, the webbed feet of a duck, the long neck of a giraffe, the spines of a cactus. Physiological adaptations, sometimes called functional adaptations, are features of how the body works inside: producing very concentrated urine to save water, making venom, changing body temperature, or a plant opening its leaf pores only at night. Behavioural adaptations are things an organism does: resting in shade at midday, migrating to find food, hunting in packs, burrowing, or calling to attract a mate.

Adaptations are inherited, passed from parents to offspring in their genetic instructions. They are not changes an individual makes because it wants or needs to. A gemsbok does not grow better at saving water because it is thirsty; gemsbok are good at saving water because, over many generations, individuals that happened to save water best survived droughts and had more offspring. This distinction matters. A person who goes to the gym builds muscle, but the muscle is not passed on to their children, so it is not an adaptation in the biological sense.

An adaptation suits a particular habitat and may be a disadvantage elsewhere. The thick fur that keeps a mountain animal warm would overheat it in the Kalahari; the gills that let a fish breathe in water are useless in air.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of habitat and adaptation, the three kinds of adaptation with an example of each, and why adaptations are inherited rather than chosen.

## Subtopic: Adaptations to Hot, Dry Places

Deserts and semi-deserts cover much of the western half of South Africa, including the Kalahari, the Karoo and the Namaqualand coast. Their challenges are scarce water, high daytime temperatures and often cold nights.

The gemsbok shows all three kinds of adaptation. Structurally, its pale coat reflects sunlight, and a network of blood vessels near its nose cools the blood flowing to its brain. Physiologically, it can let its body temperature rise by several degrees during the day instead of sweating, saving water, and its kidneys produce very concentrated urine. Behaviourally, it feeds at night and early morning on plants and roots that contain more water at those times, and rests in whatever shade it can find at midday.

Desert plants have their own set. Succulents such as the quiver tree, vygies and the many Karoo stone plants store water in thick leaves, stems or roots. Their surfaces are covered with a waxy layer that reduces water loss. Many have small leaves, or leaves reduced to spines, so less surface is exposed to the Sun and drying wind; the spines also protect the stored water from thirsty animals. Some open their leaf pores only at night, when it is cool, to take in carbon dioxide while losing less water. Roots may be shallow and spread wide to catch the briefest rain, or very deep to reach groundwater, like those of the camel thorn tree. Many Namaqualand annuals survive the dry season as seeds and flower in a spectacular burst in spring after the winter rains.

Smaller desert animals have adaptations too. The barking gecko and many beetles shelter in burrows in the heat of the day. In the Namib, just across the border in Namibia, the fog-basking beetle stands head-down on dune crests at dawn so that fog condenses on its back and runs down into its mouth. Meerkats have dark patches around their eyes that reduce glare, and they sunbathe in the cold early morning before foraging.

The questions for this section are with you now: three adaptations of the gemsbok, three adaptations of succulent plants, and how small animals avoid the heat.

## Subtopic: Adaptations to Water, Cold and Forest

Other South African habitats bring other challenges.

In water, the problems are getting oxygen, moving through a dense medium and, in the sea, salt. Fish have gills that extract dissolved oxygen, streamlined bodies and fins. The Cape clawless otter has webbed feet, waterproof fur and a streamlined shape. Water lilies have air spaces in their stems and leaves that keep their leaves floating in the sunlight, with leaf pores on the upper surface instead of the lower. Mangrove trees in the estuaries of KwaZulu-Natal grow breathing roots that stick up out of the airless mud, and they can get rid of excess salt through their leaves. On rocky shores, limpets and mussels clamp tightly to rocks to survive waves and close up to avoid drying out at low tide.

In cold places, such as the high Drakensberg in winter, the problem is losing body heat. Mammals there have thick fur; the eland and the grey rhebok have dense coats. Many animals, such as the ice rat of the Drakensberg, live in burrows where the temperature stays steady. Some animals reduce activity in winter; some birds migrate to warmer areas. Mountain plants grow low and close to the ground, out of the wind, often in tight cushions, and some have hairy leaves that trap a layer of insulating air.

In forests, the main challenge is competing for light. Tall trees such as the yellowwood grow straight and high to reach the canopy. Climbers and vines use other plants for support to reach the light without building thick trunks. Plants on the dim forest floor have large, dark green leaves that capture as much of the weak light as possible. Forest animals have adaptations for moving through trees, such as the grasping hands and tails of monkeys, and for camouflage among leaves, like the bright green of the Knysna turaco's feathers, which make it hard to see despite its colour.

Camouflage and warning colours are adaptations in every habitat. The chameleon changes colour; the stick insect looks like a twig; and brightly coloured animals such as some frogs and the foam grasshopper warn predators that they are poisonous.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying an animal adapts because it wants to; adaptations are inherited and develop over many generations. Two: confusing a habitat with an adaptation. Three: calling every feature a structural adaptation; behaviours and body functions count too. Four: assuming an adaptation is useful everywhere; it suits one habitat. Five: saying cactuses are indigenous to South Africa; the similar plants here are mostly euphorbias and other succulents.

The questions for this section are with you now: adaptations to water, cold and forest life, and how camouflage and warning colours protect animals.

## Subtopic: Built for Home

Every living thing is built for the place where it lives. That place is its habitat. The special features that help it survive there are adaptations.

There are three kinds. Some are body parts, like a duck's webbed feet for swimming. Some are about how the body works inside, like making very little sweat to save water. And some are behaviours, things the animal does, like hiding in a burrow when it is hot.

Here is the important bit. An animal does not decide to get an adaptation. A giraffe did not stretch its neck and then have long-necked babies. Instead, giraffes are born a bit different from each other. The ones with longer necks reached more food in hard times, survived and had babies with long necks. Over a very long time, the whole group ended up with long necks.

So adaptations are passed down from parents, and they fit one home. Thick fur is great on a cold mountain and terrible in a hot desert.

Keep that idea in mind and try the questions for this part: what an adaptation is and the three kinds.

## Subtopic: Living Without Water

The Kalahari is hot, dry and harsh. Let's see how its residents cope.

The gemsbok can go weeks without drinking. Its pale coat bounces sunlight away. It lets its body get hotter during the day instead of sweating, so it keeps its water. It wees very little, and what it does wee is strong. And it eats at night, when plants hold more water.

The quiver tree is a living water bottle. Its thick branches store water, and a waxy coat stops the water from escaping, like cling film on a bowl.

Many desert plants have spines instead of big leaves. Big leaves lose lots of water, like washing drying on a line. Spines lose very little, and they stop thirsty animals from biting.

Small animals hide. Geckos and beetles dig burrows and come out when it is cooler. One clever beetle in the Namib desert even stands on its head in the morning fog so that drops roll down its back into its mouth.

Hold the gemsbok and the water bottle tree in mind and try this part's questions: how desert animals and plants save water.

## Subtopic: Wet, Cold and Shady Homes

Now three other homes.

In water, fish have gills to breathe the oxygen in water and smooth shapes to slip through it. Otters have webbed feet and waterproof fur. Water lilies have air pockets in their leaves so they float in the sunshine.

On a freezing mountain, animals have thick fur and hide in burrows, like the little ice rat of the Drakensberg. Plants grow in low, tight cushions out of the wind, some with hairy leaves like a warm jersey.

In a forest, the race is for light. Tall yellowwood trees grow straight up to the sunny top. Vines climb on other plants to get there without building a trunk. Plants on the dark floor have big dark leaves to catch every bit of light.

And everywhere, animals play hide and seek. A chameleon changes colour, a stick insect looks like a twig. But some animals do the opposite and wear bright colours. That is a warning: I taste horrible, do not eat me.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A habitat is where an organism lives, and an adaptation is an inherited feature that helps it survive and reproduce there. Adaptations can be body parts, inside body workings or behaviours. Desert life saves water; water life breathes and moves in water; cold-place life keeps warm; forest plants race for light. Camouflage hides animals, and bright colours warn that an animal is poisonous.

Hold the gemsbok, the water bottle tree and the yellowwood, and take the final questions of the lesson.
