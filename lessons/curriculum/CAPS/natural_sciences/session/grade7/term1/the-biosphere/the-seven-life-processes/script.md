# Part 1 — Expert

This session answers a question that sounds simple and is not: how do we decide whether something is alive? The anchor is a comparison between a goat grazing on a hillside in the Eastern Cape, a mealie plant in a garden and a car parked on the road. The car moves, uses fuel, gives off waste gases and even responds when a key is turned, yet it is not alive. The governing idea is that a living thing carries out all seven life processes, nutrition, growth, reproduction, respiration, excretion, sensitivity and movement, on its own and as part of one organism, whereas a non-living object may show one or two of them only by imitation. Each process, what it means in plants and in animals, and how to use the full list to classify an object as living or non-living are examinable, and the processes become whole chapters in Grade 8 and in Life Sciences.

## Subtopic: Nutrition, Respiration and Excretion

Three of the life processes deal with taking in materials, releasing energy from them and getting rid of what is left over.

Nutrition is the process of obtaining food, which provides the energy and the raw materials an organism needs. Plants and some micro-organisms make their own food. A green plant uses the energy of sunlight to combine carbon dioxide from the air with water from the soil, making sugars and releasing oxygen; this is photosynthesis, and organisms that feed in this way are called producers or autotrophs, meaning self-feeders. Animals, fungi and most bacteria cannot do this. They must take in food made by other organisms, by eating plants, eating other animals or absorbing food from dead matter; they are consumers or heterotrophs, meaning other-feeders. The goat on the hillside is a consumer, eating grass; the mealie plant is a producer.

Respiration is the release of energy from food inside the cells. It is not the same as breathing, although the two are connected. In most organisms, respiration uses oxygen to break down sugar, releasing energy and producing carbon dioxide and water as waste. That energy is used for everything the organism does: moving, growing, keeping warm, repairing damage. Every living cell respires, all the time, day and night, in plants as well as animals. Breathing is only the way that many animals move air in and out of their lungs to bring in oxygen for respiration and remove the carbon dioxide; plants have no lungs, and gases simply move in and out through tiny pores on their leaves.

Excretion is the removal of waste substances made by the organism's own chemical processes. Carbon dioxide from respiration is one such waste; animals breathe it out. Animals also break down excess proteins and produce urea, which the kidneys remove in urine, and sweat removes some water and salts. Plants excrete too: they release oxygen and water vapour through their leaves, and some store wastes in leaves that later fall. Excretion must be kept separate from egestion, which is getting rid of undigested food as faeces. Faeces were never made by the body's cells, so passing them is not excretion in the strict scientific sense.

The car can be compared with these three. It takes in petrol, which looks like nutrition; it burns the petrol with oxygen to release energy, which looks like respiration; and it pushes waste gases out of the exhaust, which looks like excretion. But the car does not find or make its own fuel, and it is not made of cells, so these are only imitations.

The questions for this section are with you now: the two ways organisms obtain food, the difference between respiration and breathing, and the difference between excretion and egestion.

## Subtopic: Growth, Reproduction, Sensitivity and Movement

The remaining four life processes deal with getting bigger, making more of the same kind, detecting changes and moving.

Growth is a permanent increase in size, usually because the organism makes new cells and new material. A mealie grows from a seed to a plant taller than a person in a few months by making new cells at the tips of its roots and shoots. A goat grows from a kid to an adult. Growth uses the materials from nutrition and the energy from respiration. Crystals and icicles get bigger too, but only by adding more of the same substance to the outside; they do not take in food or make cells, so this is not growth in the biological sense.

Reproduction is the making of new individuals of the same kind. Without reproduction a species would die out when its last members died. In sexual reproduction two parents each give a sex cell, and the offspring are a mixture of both, like goat kids or most mealie seeds. In asexual reproduction one parent produces offspring identical to itself, as when a potato sprouts new plants or a bacterium divides in two. An individual organism does not have to reproduce to be alive; a mule cannot reproduce, but it is still alive. What matters is that the kind of organism can reproduce.

Sensitivity, also called irritability or responsiveness, is the ability to detect changes in the surroundings, called stimuli, and to respond to them. Animals have sense organs such as eyes, ears, nose, tongue and skin, and a nervous system that carries messages; the goat lifts its head when it hears a dog. Plants are sensitive too, but usually respond more slowly by growing in a particular direction: shoots bend towards light, roots grow downwards with gravity and towards water. Some plants respond quickly: the leaves of the sensitive plant fold when touched, and a Venus flytrap snaps shut on an insect.

Movement is a change in position of the whole organism or of a part of it. Most animals move from place to place to find food, shelter and mates, using muscles. Plants cannot walk, but they do move parts of themselves: flowers open and close, sunflowers turn to follow the Sun, leaves turn towards the light, and tendrils of a climbing bean wrap around a pole. Movement in plants is usually slow, so it is easiest to see with time-lapse photography.

The car again: it moves, and it can respond to its key or its steering wheel, but it does not grow, does not reproduce, and responds only because a person operates it. Fire spreads, gets bigger and uses oxygen, yet it has no cells and does not reproduce its kind. Neither is alive.

The questions for this section are with you now: growth as permanent increase in size, sexual and asexual reproduction, how plants and animals respond to stimuli, and how plants move.

## Subtopic: Living or Non-living

The seven life processes together give a test for life. A useful way to remember them is the made-up name MRS GREN or a similar sentence: Movement, Respiration, Sensitivity, Growth, Reproduction, Excretion, Nutrition. Any order is acceptable, provided all seven are there.

To classify an object, ask whether it carries out all seven processes by itself. A goat does: it eats, respires, excretes urea and carbon dioxide, grows, reproduces, senses danger and walks. A mealie plant does too, although its nutrition is by photosynthesis, its movement is slow and its sensitivity shows as growth towards light and water. Both are living organisms. A car and a fire each show only some features and only by imitation; they are non-living.

Two further groups need care. Things that were once alive, such as a dead leaf, a piece of wood, a cotton shirt or a leather shoe, are dead or made from dead organisms; they no longer carry out any life processes. Things that have never been alive, such as a stone, water or plastic, are called non-living. Some textbooks use three categories for this reason: living, dead (once living) and non-living (never living). A seed is a special case: it is alive but dormant, carrying out respiration so slowly that it is hard to measure, and it will begin to grow when it gets water, warmth and oxygen.

All seven processes depend on each other. Nutrition supplies food; respiration releases its energy; that energy powers growth, reproduction, movement and the nerves and muscles of sensitivity; and excretion removes the wastes these processes produce. If one process stops for long, the organism dies.

Living things are also made of cells, the small building blocks visible under a microscope, and this is a second strong test. Every living organism is made of one or more cells, while a car, a fire or a crystal is not.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: calling respiration breathing; respiration is energy release in cells, breathing is gas exchange. Two: calling faeces excretion; faeces are egested. Three: saying plants do not move or respond; they do, slowly. Four: classifying a fire or car as living because it uses oxygen and moves; it must show all seven processes and be made of cells. Five: saying something is not alive because it cannot reproduce on its own as an individual, like a mule; the test applies to the kind of organism.

The questions for this section are with you now: listing all seven processes, classifying objects as living, once living or never living, and explaining why a car or fire is non-living.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a goat, a car and a checklist of seven — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Seven Things Every Living Thing Does

Every living thing, from a tiny bacterium to a huge baobab tree, does seven things. Let's meet them using a goat.

The goat eats grass. That is nutrition, getting food. Plants are clever here: they make their own food from sunlight, air and water.

Inside the goat's body, the food is used to release energy. That is respiration. It is not the same as breathing. Breathing just brings the oxygen in; respiration happens inside every tiny cell, like a little fire burning food for energy.

The goat gets rid of waste its body made, like carbon dioxide breathed out and urine. That is excretion. Careful: dung is not excretion, because that is just food the goat never used.

The goat grows from a small kid into a big adult. That is growth.

The goat has babies. That is reproduction.

The goat hears a dog barking and lifts its head. That is sensitivity, noticing a change and reacting.

The goat walks to a new patch of grass. That is movement.

Nutrition, respiration, excretion, growth, reproduction, sensitivity and movement. Seven things.

Keep the goat in mind and try the questions for this part: the seven processes and what each one means.

## Subtopic: Plants Do It Slowly

People sometimes say plants are not really doing much. But a plant does all seven things too, just slowly and quietly.

A plant makes its own food from sunlight. That is nutrition. Its cells respire day and night, releasing energy. It gives off waste gases through tiny holes in its leaves. That is excretion.

A mealie grows from a seed into a plant taller than you in a few months. It makes seeds that grow into new mealies. That is growth and reproduction.

Now the slow ones. Put a plant near a window and after a few days it bends towards the light. Turn a seed upside down and the root still grows downwards. That is sensitivity. And sunflowers turn their heads to follow the Sun. A bean plant's tendril curls around a stick. That is movement, just very slow, like a film played at the wrong speed.

So plants are fully alive. They just live at a slower pace than we do.

Hold the window plant in mind and try this part's questions: how plants show sensitivity and movement.

## Subtopic: Is It Alive? The Checklist

Now the tricky one. A car. It moves. It takes in petrol. It burns the petrol and puffs out waste gas. It even reacts when you turn the key. Is it alive?

No. A car cannot grow. It cannot have baby cars. It only reacts because a person drives it. And it is not made of cells. A living thing must do all seven things by itself and be made of cells.

Fire is another trick. It spreads, it gets bigger and it uses oxygen. But it has no cells, it does not grow new parts and it does not make baby fires of the same kind. Not alive.

What about your cotton shirt? Cotton came from a plant, so it was once alive, but it is not alive now. A stone was never alive.

And a seed in a packet? It looks dead, but it is alive and sleeping. Give it water and warmth and it wakes up and grows.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Living things carry out seven life processes: nutrition, respiration, excretion, growth, reproduction, sensitivity and movement. Respiration is releasing energy in cells, not breathing. Excretion removes waste the body made, not faeces. Plants do all seven, slowly. A non-living thing may copy one or two processes, but only a living thing does all seven by itself and is made of cells.

Hold the goat, the window plant and the car, and take the final questions of the lesson: living, once living or never living.
