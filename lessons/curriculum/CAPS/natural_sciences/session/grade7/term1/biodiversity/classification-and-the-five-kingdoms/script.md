# Part 1 — Expert

This session begins the study of biodiversity, the enormous variety of living things, and the system scientists use to organise it. The anchor is a walk through a South African school garden, where in a few minutes we might find a bean plant, a mushroom on a rotting log, an earthworm, a green scum on a puddle and the bacteria that make the soil smell earthy after rain. The governing idea is that living things are classified into groups according to the characteristics they share, and that at the largest scale all life can be sorted into five kingdoms: Bacteria, Protista, Fungi, Plants and Animals. Why we classify, the features used and the distinguishing features of each kingdom are examinable, and classification returns in Grade 10 Life Sciences.

## Subtopic: Why We Classify Living Things

Biodiversity is the variety of all living things: the number of different kinds of organisms and the differences among them. Scientists have named about two million species so far, and estimates of the total range from several million to many more. South Africa is one of the most biodiverse countries in the world, with more than twenty thousand kinds of flowering plants alone, about ten per cent of the world's total, packed into about one per cent of its land area. The Cape Floral Kingdom, around Cape Town, is the smallest of the world's six floral kingdoms and among the richest.

With so many organisms, scientists need a way to organise them, just as a library sorts millions of books into sections and shelves so that any book can be found. Classification is the sorting of living things into groups based on the characteristics they share. Organisms in the same group are more similar to each other than to organisms in other groups.

Classification is useful in several ways. It makes the variety of life manageable to study; it lets scientists in every country use the same names and know exactly which organism is meant; it helps identify an unknown organism by narrowing it down group by group; and it shows relationships, because organisms that share many features are usually closely related through common ancestors.

The features used for classification must be reliable. Scientists use characteristics such as whether the organism has cells with a nucleus, whether it is single-celled or many-celled, how it obtains food, whether it has a cell wall and what that wall is made of, and its body structure. Features that vary with circumstances, such as size or where it happens to be living, are poor choices. A whale lives in the sea like a fish, but it breathes air with lungs, is warm-blooded and feeds its young on milk, so it is classified as a mammal, not a fish.

## Subtopic: Bacteria, Protista and Fungi

At the highest level, the five-kingdom system places all living things in five kingdoms. Three of them contain mostly microscopic organisms.

Kingdom Bacteria contains single-celled organisms whose cells have no nucleus; their genetic material lies free in the cytoplasm. They are the smallest and simplest cells, usually only a few thousandths of a millimetre long, with a cell wall. They reproduce by splitting in two. Some make their own food, like the cyanobacteria that form green scums on ponds; most absorb food, including the decomposers in soil that help produce the earthy smell after rain. A few cause diseases, such as tuberculosis and cholera; many are useful, like those that make yoghurt and those in our intestines.

Kingdom Protista contains mostly single-celled organisms whose cells do have a nucleus. It is a mixed group of organisms that do not fit into the other kingdoms. Some are animal-like, such as the amoeba and paramecium, which move and take in food; some are plant-like, such as many algae, which photosynthesise; and some, such as the Plasmodium that causes malaria, are parasites. The green scum on a puddle may contain protist algae as well as cyanobacteria. Seaweeds such as kelp along the Cape coast are large many-celled algae and are often grouped with the protists in the five-kingdom system.

Kingdom Fungi contains organisms whose cells have a nucleus and a cell wall made of chitin, but no chlorophyll, so they cannot photosynthesise. They feed by releasing digestive enzymes onto dead or living material and absorbing the dissolved food; most are decomposers. Most fungi are made of thin threads called hyphae that form a network, the mycelium, through their food; the mushroom on the rotting log is only the reproductive part, which releases spores. Yeast is a single-celled fungus used in baking bread and brewing. Some fungi cause diseases, such as athlete's foot and ringworm, and some, like Penicillium, gave us antibiotics.

## Subtopic: Plants and Animals

The two remaining kingdoms contain the large organisms we see every day.

Kingdom Plants contains many-celled organisms whose cells have a nucleus, a cell wall made of cellulose, and chloroplasts containing the green pigment chlorophyll. Plants make their own food by photosynthesis, so they are producers. They do not move from place to place, although parts of them move slowly in response to light and gravity. They store food as starch. The kingdom ranges from mosses and ferns to the huge yellowwood trees, the bean plant in the garden and the grasses of the Highveld.

Kingdom Animals contains many-celled organisms whose cells have a nucleus but no cell wall and no chlorophyll. Animals cannot make their own food; they must eat other organisms, so they are consumers. Most can move from place to place at some stage of their lives, and most have nerves and muscles that let them respond quickly to their surroundings. They store food as glycogen and fat. The kingdom includes the earthworm in the garden soil, insects, spiders, fish, frogs, lizards, birds and mammals, including humans.

A comparison of the five kingdoms makes the system clear. Bacteria: one cell, no nucleus. Protista: mostly one cell, with nucleus. Fungi: threads or single cells, nucleus, chitin wall, absorb food. Plants: many cells, nucleus, cellulose wall, chlorophyll, make food. Animals: many cells, nucleus, no wall, eat food, usually move.

Some organisms test the system. Euglena, a pond protist, can photosynthesise like a plant and swim like an animal; this is one reason the protists form a separate kingdom. Viruses are not placed in any of the five kingdoms at all, because they are not cells.

The five-kingdom system is not the only one. Some modern systems use six kingdoms or three larger groups called domains, because genetic studies showed that some single-celled organisms once called bacteria are very different from the rest. Grade 7 uses the five-kingdom system, which is still widely taught.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: putting fungi in the plant kingdom; they have no chlorophyll and chitin walls. Two: saying bacteria have a nucleus; they do not. Three: classifying a whale as a fish because it lives in water. Four: placing viruses in Kingdom Bacteria; viruses are not cells. Five: thinking only large organisms count in biodiversity; most of life's variety is microscopic.

The questions for this section are with you now: the features of plants and animals, a comparison of all five kingdoms, and why euglena and viruses are difficult to place.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a messy cupboard, a garden walk and five big boxes — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Sorting the Cupboard

Imagine a cupboard with every kind of thing thrown in together: shoes, spoons, books, socks. Finding anything would be a nightmare. So you sort it. Shoes together, spoons together, books together. Now you can find things, and you can see how many of each you have.

Scientists have the same problem with living things, except there are millions of kinds. The huge variety of life is called biodiversity. South Africa has loads of it: more than twenty thousand kinds of flowering plants.

Sorting living things into groups is called classification. Things that share features go in the same group. But you must sort by the right features. You would not sort shoes by colour and put a black shoe with a black sock. Scientists sort by things like: does the creature have one cell or many, does it make its own food, does it have a cell wall.

A whale lives in the sea, but it breathes air and feeds its babies milk. So it goes with the mammals, not the fish. Where you live is not the right feature.

Keep the cupboard in mind and try the questions for this part: biodiversity, classification and good features to sort by.

## Subtopic: Five Big Boxes

All living things fit into five big boxes, called kingdoms. Let's fill them on a walk around a school garden.

Box one, Bacteria. Tiny single cells with no nucleus, no control centre. The earthy smell after rain comes partly from soil bacteria.

Box two, Protista. Tiny single cells that do have a nucleus. The green scum on a puddle has some, and so does a drop of pond water.

Box three, Fungi. The mushroom on a rotting log. Fungi cannot make food from sunlight; they eat dead stuff by digesting it from the outside. Mould and yeast are fungi too.

Box four, Plants. The bean plant. Many cells, green, makes its own food from sunlight, stays in one place.

Box five, Animals. The earthworm, the beetle, the bird, and you. Many cells, eats other living things, usually moves around.

Bacteria, Protista, Fungi, Plants, Animals. Five boxes for all of life.

Hold the garden walk in mind and try this part's questions: which box each thing goes into.

## Subtopic: The Tricky Ones

Most living things are easy to sort. A few are tricky.

Mushrooms grow from the ground, so people used to call them plants. But they are not green, they cannot make food from sunlight, and their cell walls are made of different stuff. So they get their own box.

Euglena is a tiny pond creature that can make food from sunlight like a plant and swim with a tail like an animal. It sits in the Protista box, which is a bit like the box for things that do not fit anywhere else.

And viruses do not fit in any box. They are not even cells, so they are left out of the five kingdoms.

Scientists keep improving their boxes as they learn more. Some now use six kingdoms or three even bigger groups. But five boxes is a great place to start.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Biodiversity is the variety of life, and classification sorts living things by shared features. All life fits into five kingdoms: Bacteria, single cells with no nucleus; Protista, mostly single cells with a nucleus; Fungi, which absorb food and have no chlorophyll; Plants, which make their own food; and Animals, which eat food and usually move. Mushrooms are fungi, not plants, and viruses are not in any kingdom.

Hold the cupboard, the five boxes and the tricky ones, and take the final questions of the lesson.
