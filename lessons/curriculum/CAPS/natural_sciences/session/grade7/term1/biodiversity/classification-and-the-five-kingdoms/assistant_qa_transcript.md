### subtopic_1 — Why We Classify Living Things
**Assistant:** Why is South Africa so rich in plant species compared with much larger countries?

**Tutor:** Several factors combine. First, South Africa has an unusual range of climates and landscapes in a small area: winter-rainfall regions in the south-west, summer-rainfall grasslands and savanna in the north and east, deserts in the west, subtropical coast in the east, and high mountains, all within a day's drive. Each supports different plants. Second, the Cape region in particular has many old, nutrient-poor sandstone soils, mountains that separate valleys from one another, and regular natural fires, and over millions of years plant populations in neighbouring valleys and on different soils became separated and gradually developed into distinct species. Many fynbos species grow naturally only on one mountain or in one small valley; these are called endemic species, found nowhere else. Third, the region was not covered by ice sheets during the ice ages, so plant lineages have survived for a very long time without being wiped out. The result is that the Cape Floral Kingdom has around nine thousand plant species, about seventy per cent of them endemic, in an area smaller than many countries' single provinces. This richness brings responsibility. Because so many species occur in such small areas, a single housing development, invasive plant or uncontrolled fire regime can push a species to extinction. That is why the Cape Floral Region is a World Heritage Site and why classification matters practically: you cannot protect a species you have not identified and named.

### subtopic_2 — Bacteria, Protista and Fungi
**Assistant:** If fungi grow from the ground like plants, why are mushrooms not in the plant kingdom?

**Tutor:** Because the characteristics that matter for classification are structure and nutrition, and fungi differ from plants in both. The most important difference is food: plants have chlorophyll and make their own food by photosynthesis, while fungi have none and must absorb food made by other organisms, usually by digesting dead material outside their bodies and absorbing the result. This is why mushrooms can be farmed in dark sheds, which would kill a plant. Second, the cell walls are made of different substances: cellulose in plants, chitin in fungi, the same tough material found in the outer skeleton of insects. Third, plants store food as starch, while fungi store it as glycogen, as animals do. Fourth, the body plan is different: a plant has roots, stems and leaves, while a fungus is mainly a hidden network of threads, with the mushroom pushed up only to release spores. Older classification systems did put fungi with plants, because both seemed to stay in one place and grow from the ground. As scientists studied cells under microscopes and later compared genetic material, it became clear that fungi are a separate group, and genetic evidence even shows they are more closely related to animals than to plants. This history is a good example of how classification improves as scientists learn more about the features that really matter.

### subtopic_3 — Plants and Animals
**Assistant:** Why do some scientists now use domains instead of the five kingdoms?

**Tutor:** Because comparing the genetic material of organisms revealed differences that could not be seen by looking at cells under a microscope. In the 1970s the American scientist Carl Woese compared a particular molecule found in all cells and discovered that some single-celled organisms that looked like bacteria, many of them living in extreme places such as hot springs, salt lakes and the guts of cows, were as different from ordinary bacteria as bacteria are from humans. He proposed that these organisms, now called Archaea, form a separate group. That led to the three-domain system: Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya, which includes all organisms whose cells have a nucleus, meaning protists, fungi, plants and animals. Some textbooks also use six kingdoms, splitting the old Kingdom Bacteria into two. None of this makes the five-kingdom system wrong for Grade 7. Classification systems are tools that scientists improve as they learn more, and different levels of detail suit different purposes, in the same way that a map of South Africa and a street map of one town are both correct but show different things. The five kingdoms give a clear, useful first sorting of life, based on features you can understand and observe: nucleus or not, single-celled or many-celled, cell wall or not, and how the organism gets its food. You will meet the domains again later in Life Sciences.
