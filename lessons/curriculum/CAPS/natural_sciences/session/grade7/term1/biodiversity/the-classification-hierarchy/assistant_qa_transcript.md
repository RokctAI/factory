### subtopic_1 — The Seven Levels
**Assistant:** Why do plants use Division instead of Phylum, if both mean the same level?

**Tutor:** It is mainly a matter of history and of the separate rule books that botanists and zoologists developed. Scientists who study plants and those who study animals worked largely in separate traditions for about two centuries, and each group developed its own international code of rules for naming. The botanical code traditionally used the word division for the rank just below kingdom, while zoologists used phylum, a word coined in the 1860s from the Greek for race or tribe. Both words name the same level in the hierarchy, a large group of organisms sharing a basic body plan: for example, the division of flowering plants or the phylum of arthropods. In the 1990s the botanical code was changed to accept phylum as an equal alternative, so many modern books now use phylum for plants as well, but division is still widely found in school textbooks, including those that follow CAPS. For exams, either is acceptable for plants, and the key idea is that it is the second level, between kingdom and class. The history is a useful reminder that classification is a human system created to organise nature, and that its details reflect how science developed. The organisms themselves do not change when we rename the level; what matters is that every scientist agrees on which organisms belong in which group.

### subtopic_2 — Species and Scientific Names
**Assistant:** Who decides the scientific name when a new species is discovered?

**Tutor:** The scientist who first formally describes the species chooses the name, but must follow strict international rules. When researchers find an organism they believe has never been described, they must compare it carefully with all known species in its group, often examining specimens in museums and comparing genetic material, to be sure it is truly new. They then write a formal scientific description, explaining exactly how it differs from its relatives, and publish it in a scientific journal. One preserved specimen, called the type specimen, is deposited in a museum or herbarium so that other scientists can always check what the name refers to. The name must be in Latin form, must use an existing genus or a properly described new one, and must not already be in use for another animal or plant in that group. Within these rules, scientists have freedom: names may describe a feature, a place, or honour a person. South African scientists describe new species every year, especially plants, insects, frogs and reptiles. For example, new species of Cape fynbos plants and of tiny frogs in the mountains of the Western Cape and the Eastern Cape have been named in recent years. Once published, the name belongs to the whole scientific community, and if two names were given to the same species by accident, the older one usually wins. This careful process is why a scientific name can be trusted in every country.

### subtopic_3 — Using the Hierarchy
**Assistant:** Can two species that look completely different be more closely related than two that look alike?

**Tutor:** Yes, and it happens surprisingly often, because appearance can be shaped by the way of life rather than by ancestry. A good example is the dolphin and the shark. Both are streamlined swimmers with fins and similar shapes, but a dolphin is a mammal that breathes air, has a little hair at birth and feeds its young on milk, while a shark is a fish with gills. The dolphin is much more closely related to a hippopotamus, which looks nothing like it, than to the shark. Their similar shape evolved separately because both hunt fast in water, where a streamlined body is the best design. On the other hand, a Great Dane and a Chihuahua look extremely different but are the same species, because they can interbreed and produce fertile puppies; their differences come from breeding by people. In South Africa, the golden moles of the Namaqualand dunes look and live very like moles elsewhere in the world, but genetic studies have shown they are more closely related to elephants, tenrecs and aardvarks, a group of mammals that originated in Africa. This is why modern classification uses many features at once, including internal structure, development and genetic material, rather than overall appearance. It is also why the hierarchy is so useful: it records real relationships, not just resemblances.
