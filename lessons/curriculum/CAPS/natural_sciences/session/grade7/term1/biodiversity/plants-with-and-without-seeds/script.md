# Part 1 — Expert

This session sorts the plant kingdom by the way plants reproduce. The anchor is a walk up a forested kloof in the Magaliesberg: soft green moss on the damp rocks, ferns along the stream, a cycad on the slope above, a pine plantation on the far hill, and wild flowers and fruit trees all around. The governing idea is that plants can be divided into those that reproduce by spores and have no seeds, the mosses and ferns, and those that produce seeds; and that seed plants are divided again into gymnosperms, whose seeds develop exposed on cones, and angiosperms, whose seeds develop enclosed inside a fruit formed from a flower. The features and examples of each group are examinable, and plant diversity returns in Grade 10 Life Sciences.

## Subtopic: Plants Without Seeds

All plants are many-celled, have cell walls of cellulose and contain chlorophyll, so they make their own food by photosynthesis. But they differ greatly in how they are built and how they reproduce. The oldest and simplest groups reproduce by spores, not seeds.

A spore is a tiny reproductive cell, usually a single cell, that can grow into a new plant. Spores are produced in huge numbers, are so light that wind carries them long distances, and contain almost no stored food. They need damp conditions to grow.

Mosses are small, low-growing plants that form soft green cushions on damp rocks, tree trunks and soil. They have no true roots, stems or leaves in the scientific sense; they are anchored by thin threads called rhizoids, and they have no transport tissues, the tubes that carry water and food through larger plants. Because of this, water simply soaks through their cells, which is why mosses stay small and grow in damp, shady places. Mosses need water for reproduction too: the male sex cells must swim through a film of water to reach the female sex cells. After fertilisation, a thin stalk with a capsule grows up from the moss, and the capsule releases spores.

Ferns are larger and more complex. They have true roots, stems and leaves, called fronds, and they have transport tissues, so they can grow taller than mosses. Tree ferns in the forests of the Eastern Cape and the Drakensberg foothills can reach several metres. The stem of many ferns is an underground stem called a rhizome. On the underside of the fronds are clusters of brown dots called sori, which produce spores. A spore lands in a damp place and grows into a tiny, heart-shaped plant that produces sex cells; as in mosses, the male cells need water to swim to the female cells, and the fertilised cell grows into a new fern. This dependence on water explains why ferns grow mostly along streams and in damp forests.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a spore is, the features of mosses and ferns, and why both groups need damp places.

## Subtopic: Gymnosperms

Most plants alive today reproduce by seeds. A seed is very different from a spore. It contains a young plant, the embryo, with a tiny root and shoot; a store of food to feed the embryo when it starts to grow; and a protective seed coat. Seeds can survive dry, cold or hot conditions for long periods and germinate when conditions improve. Seed plants also do not need water for their sex cells to meet, because the male sex cells are carried inside pollen grains, moved by wind or animals. These two advantages allowed seed plants to spread into dry habitats and become the dominant plants on land.

Seed plants are divided into two groups. The gymnosperms, meaning naked seeds, produce seeds that lie exposed on the scales of cones rather than enclosed in a fruit. They do not have flowers. Male cones produce pollen, which is usually carried by wind to female cones; after fertilisation, seeds develop on the scales of the female cone, which opens when the seeds are ripe to let them fall or be blown away. Many pine seeds have a papery wing.

Gymnosperms include conifers, such as pines and cypresses, and cycads. Most conifers are trees with needle-like or scale-like leaves that stay on the tree all year, and they contain resin. South Africa's native conifers are the yellowwoods, Podocarpus and Afrocarpus; the real yellowwood is South Africa's national tree, and giant yellowwoods in the Knysna and Tsitsikamma forests are hundreds of years old. The pines in plantations in Mpumalanga and the Western Cape are not native: they were brought from the Northern Hemisphere for timber, and some species have spread as invasive aliens, using water and crowding out fynbos.

Cycads are ancient gymnosperms that look a little like palm trees, with a stout trunk and a crown of stiff, divided leaves, and they bear large cones. They were widespread when dinosaurs lived. South Africa has about thirty-eight cycad species, many of them found nowhere else. Cycads are among the most threatened plants on Earth, because they grow very slowly and are stolen from the wild for gardens; several South African species are extinct in the wild, and the Woods cycad survives only as cloned plants in botanical gardens.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a seed contains, two advantages of seeds over spores, and the features and South African examples of gymnosperms.

## Subtopic: Angiosperms

The angiosperms, meaning seeds in a container, are the flowering plants. They produce flowers, and after fertilisation part of the flower, the ovary, develops into a fruit that encloses and protects the seeds. Angiosperms are by far the largest plant group, with around three hundred thousand species, and they include almost all the plants people eat and almost all the plants in a South African garden, veld or fynbos: grasses, mealies, wheat, beans, roses, proteas, aloes, acacias, baobabs, marula trees and vegetables.

Flowers are reproductive organs. Many flowers attract insects, birds or other animals with colour, scent and nectar; the animals carry pollen from flower to flower. Other flowers, such as grasses, are small and dull and are pollinated by wind. The fruit protects the seeds and helps scatter them: fleshy fruits such as marula and mango are eaten by animals, which drop the seeds elsewhere; dry fruits may split open, have hooks that catch on fur, or have wings and parachutes carried by wind. The word fruit in science means any structure that develops from the ovary and contains seeds, so a bean pod, a tomato, a mealie grain and a sunflower seed in its husk are all fruits.

Angiosperms are divided further into monocotyledons and dicotyledons, which are the subject of the next lesson.

A comparison brings the four groups together. Mosses: spores, no true roots or transport tissue, need water for reproduction. Ferns: spores, roots, stems, fronds and transport tissue, need water for reproduction. Gymnosperms: seeds exposed on cones, no flowers, pollen carried by wind. Angiosperms: flowers, seeds enclosed in fruits, pollen carried by wind or animals.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying ferns have seeds; they reproduce by spores. Two: saying pine cones are fruits; cones are not fruits, and gymnosperms have no flowers. Three: thinking a fruit must be sweet; in science any seed-container from an ovary is a fruit, including bean pods and tomatoes. Four: calling all trees angiosperms; yellowwoods and pines are gymnosperms. Five: confusing spores and seeds; a spore is usually a single cell with no food store, a seed contains an embryo and food.

The questions for this section are with you now: the features of angiosperms, the scientific meaning of fruit, and a comparison of all four plant groups.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with dust-like spores, a lunchbox seed and seeds with or without a wrapper — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Dust Instead of Seeds

Walk up a damp valley and look at the rocks by the stream. That soft green carpet is moss. Along the stream are ferns with their big feathery leaves. Neither of them ever makes a seed.

Instead, they make spores. A spore is like a speck of dust that can grow into a new plant. Turn a fern leaf over and you will see little brown dots. Those dots are full of spores. The wind blows them away, and if one lands somewhere damp, a new plant can start.

Moss is very simple. It has no real roots and no pipes inside to carry water, so water just soaks through it like a sponge. That is why moss is always small and always in damp places.

Ferns are a step up. They have roots, stems and leaves, and pipes inside to carry water, so they can grow bigger. Some tree ferns are taller than a person.

Both mosses and ferns need water to make new plants, because their tiny sex cells have to swim. So they love damp, shady spots.

Keep the damp valley in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: A Seed Is a Packed Lunchbox

A seed is much more than a spore. Open a bean seed and you find three things: a baby plant, a big store of food, and a tough coat around the outside. It is like a baby packed into a lunchbox with a strong lid.

That lunchbox means the seed can wait for months or years until there is rain, then grow. And seed plants do not need water for their sex cells to swim; pollen carries the male cells by wind or by animals. That is why seed plants can live in dry places where moss never could.

Seed plants come in two kinds. The first kind keeps its seeds open on the scales of cones, with no wrapper. These are the gymnosperms, which means naked seeds. Pine trees, our national tree the yellowwood, and the ancient cycads are gymnosperms. They have no flowers.

Cycads were around with the dinosaurs. Today many are in danger because they grow very slowly and people steal them for gardens.

Hold the lunchbox in mind and try this part's questions.

## Subtopic: Seeds in a Wrapper

The second kind of seed plant has flowers. After the flower is pollinated, part of it swells into a fruit, and the seeds are wrapped up inside. These are the angiosperms, which means seeds in a container.

To a scientist, a fruit is anything that grows from a flower and holds seeds. So an apple is a fruit, but so is a tomato, a bean pod, a pumpkin and even a mealie grain. Fruits help seeds travel: animals eat juicy marula fruits and drop the seeds far away; some fruits have hooks that catch in fur; some have wings that spin in the wind.

Angiosperms are the biggest plant group. Grass, mealies, beans, roses, proteas, aloes and thorn trees are all flowering plants.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Mosses and ferns have no seeds; they spread by spores and need damp places. Seed plants have seeds with a baby plant, a food store and a coat. Gymnosperms, like pines, yellowwoods and cycads, have naked seeds on cones and no flowers. Angiosperms have flowers, and their seeds grow inside fruits.

Hold the spores, the lunchbox and the wrapped seeds, and take the final questions of the lesson.
