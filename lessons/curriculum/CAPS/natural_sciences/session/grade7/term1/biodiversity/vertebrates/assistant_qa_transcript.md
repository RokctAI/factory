### subtopic_1 — Vertebrates and Fish
**Assistant:** Why was the coelacanth discovered near East London such a famous scientific event?

**Tutor:** Because scientists knew the coelacanth only from fossils and believed it had been extinct for about sixty-six million years, since roughly the time the dinosaurs disappeared. In December 1938 a fishing trawler brought up a strange, large, steel-blue fish near the mouth of the Chalumna River close to East London. Marjorie Courtenay-Latimer, the curator of the East London Museum, recognised that it was unusual and sent a sketch to the chemist and fish expert Professor J.L.B. Smith in Makhanda, then called Grahamstown. Smith identified it as a coelacanth and named it Latimeria chalumnae, honouring both the curator and the river. The discovery caused a sensation around the world: it was like finding a living dinosaur. The coelacanth is important because of its fleshy, lobe-shaped fins, which contain bones arranged a little like the bones in the limbs of land animals. Scientists study coelacanths to understand how fish with lobed fins were related to the ancestors of the first four-legged animals, the amphibians, which moved onto land hundreds of millions of years ago. A population was later found near the Comoros Islands, and in 2000 divers found coelacanths living in deep canyons in Sodwana Bay in KwaZulu-Natal. They are protected, and the story is a reminder that the oceans still hold surprises, and that local South African scientists made one of the great discoveries of twentieth-century biology.

### subtopic_2 — Amphibians and Reptiles
**Assistant:** Why are frogs disappearing in many parts of the world, including South Africa?

**Tutor:** Because frogs face several threats at once, and their biology makes them especially sensitive. Their moist, thin skin absorbs water and oxygen, but it also absorbs pollutants, such as pesticides and fertilisers that wash into wetlands, so frogs are affected quickly by poor water quality. Their eggs and tadpoles need clean, unpolluted water, and many species need temporary pools that fill in the rainy season, so draining wetlands and building over them destroys their breeding sites. In the Cape Town area, the western leopard toad must cross roads each year to reach its breeding ponds, and many are killed by cars; volunteers now patrol roads on breeding nights to carry toads across. A further threat is a fungal disease called chytrid, which infects the skin of amphibians and interferes with how they take in water and salts. It has caused dramatic declines and extinctions of frogs in Central and South America and Australia. Interestingly, the disease may have spread around the world partly through the international trade in the African clawed frog, a South African species once widely exported for pregnancy testing and laboratory research, which carries the fungus without becoming very ill. Climate change, which alters rainfall patterns, adds to the pressure. Because frogs react so quickly to changes in their environment, scientists treat them as indicator species: when frogs decline, it is a warning that a wetland is in trouble.

### subtopic_3 — Birds and Mammals
**Assistant:** Why does a small bird or mammal need to eat so much more than a lizard the same size?

**Tutor:** Because birds and mammals burn food to make heat all the time, while a lizard borrows heat from the Sun. An endothermic animal keeps its body at a fairly constant temperature, around thirty-seven degrees in most mammals and close to forty-one in many birds, however cold the air is. Heat is constantly lost from the skin to the surroundings, and to replace it the animal must release energy from food by respiration, continuously, day and night. An ectothermic lizard does not do this: when it is cold, its body cools down and its life processes slow, so it uses very little energy; when it needs to be active, it basks in the Sun. As a result, a mammal can need roughly five to ten times more food than a reptile of the same mass. Size matters too. A small animal has a large surface area compared with its volume, so it loses heat quickly. Tiny shrews must eat more than their own body mass in food every day, and sunbirds feed on nectar almost constantly. Some small birds and mammals save energy by letting their temperature drop at night, a state called torpor. The advantage of endothermy is independence from the weather: birds and mammals can hunt on cold nights, live in cold mountains and the polar regions, and stay active when reptiles are sluggish. The cost is a constant, large demand for food.
