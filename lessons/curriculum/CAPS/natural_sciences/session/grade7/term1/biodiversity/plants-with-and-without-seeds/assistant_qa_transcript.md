### subtopic_1 — Plants Without Seeds
**Assistant:** If mosses are so simple, are they of any real use to the environment?

**Tutor:** Yes, mosses do important jobs out of proportion to their size. First, they are pioneers. Mosses can grow on bare rock where nothing else can, and as they grow and die they trap dust and form a thin layer of organic material, the start of a soil in which larger plants can later grow. Second, they hold water. A cushion of moss soaks up water like a sponge and releases it slowly, which reduces erosion on slopes and keeps streams flowing in dry spells. Peat mosses, called Sphagnum, can hold many times their own weight in water, and over thousands of years they form peat bogs that store enormous amounts of carbon; South Africa has peatlands in places such as the Mfabeni swamp in the iSimangaliso Wetland Park, which contains some of the oldest peat in Africa. Third, mosses provide homes and food for tiny invertebrates, such as mites and springtails, and nesting material for birds. Fourth, mosses are sensitive to air pollution because they absorb water and nutrients directly through their leaves, with no protective layers; scientists use their presence or absence as an indicator of air quality. Finally, mosses can survive drying out completely for months and recover within hours of rain, which makes them interesting to scientists studying how living things survive drought. So their simplicity is a strategy, not a weakness.

### subtopic_2 — Gymnosperms
**Assistant:** Why are cycads so badly threatened, when they survived since the age of the dinosaurs?

**Tutor:** Because they are very slow and very valuable, and that combination is dangerous when people want them. Cycads survived for two hundred million years and through the extinction that ended the dinosaurs, so they are tough plants in natural conditions. But they grow extremely slowly; some species add only a few centimetres of trunk a year, and a large wild plant may be hundreds of years old. They also reproduce slowly. Male and female cones grow on separate plants, cones are produced only every few years, and in some species pollination depends on particular beetles or weevils that live in the cones. A population that loses its older plants cannot replace them for decades. At the same time, large, old cycads are prized as garden plants, and a single specimen can sell illegally for very large sums of money. Thieves dig plants out of the wild, sometimes removing every adult from a hillside. Habitat loss to farming and building adds to the pressure. As a result, several South African cycad species are now extinct in the wild, and others survive as a handful of plants. The Woods cycad, Encephalartos woodii, was found as a single male plant in the Ngoye forest in KwaZulu-Natal in 1895, and no female has ever been found, so all existing plants are clones of that one male. South Africa now protects cycads under strict laws, microchips some wild plants and requires permits for owning them.

### subtopic_3 — Angiosperms
**Assistant:** Why did flowering plants become so much more common than gymnosperms?

**Tutor:** Flowering plants appeared later in Earth's history, about one hundred and thirty million years ago, but within a few tens of millions of years they had become the dominant plants in most habitats. Scientists think several features gave them an advantage. First, flowers allowed partnerships with animals. Insects, birds and bats that carry pollen directly from one flower to another of the same species make pollination much more accurate than wind, which wastes huge amounts of pollen, so a plant can reproduce successfully even when its neighbours are scattered. Second, fruits gave new ways to disperse seeds, by animals eating fleshy fruits or carrying hooked ones, so seeds could be spread far from the parent plant and into new places. Third, many flowering plants grow and reproduce quickly. Many herbs and grasses can go from seed to seed in a single season, while a pine or a cycad takes years to produce cones. This allowed flowering plants to colonise disturbed ground and to evolve rapidly into new species suited to different conditions. Fourth, flowering plants have more efficient water-transport tissues, helping them grow fast. The partnership with animals also drove diversity among the animals: bees, butterflies, many beetles, sunbirds and fruit bats evolved alongside flowers. In South Africa the fynbos, with its proteas pollinated by sugarbirds and its many plants pollinated by particular insects, is one of the finest examples of this partnership.
