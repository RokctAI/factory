# Part 1 — Expert

This session sorts the animals with backbones, the vertebrates, into their five classes. The anchor is a visit to a river in Mpumalanga, where in one afternoon we could see a yellowfish in the water, a river frog on the bank, a Nile crocodile basking on a sandbar, a fish eagle overhead and a herd of impala coming down to drink. All five have a backbone, yet each belongs to a different class. The governing idea is that vertebrates are classified by a small set of reliable features: body covering, how they breathe, how they control body temperature and how they reproduce. The features of fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals, and the ability to classify an unknown vertebrate from its features, are examinable, and vertebrate classes return in Grade 10 Life Sciences.

## Subtopic: Vertebrates and Fish

Animals can be divided into two large groups: vertebrates, which have a backbone, and invertebrates, which do not. The backbone, or vertebral column, is a chain of small bones called vertebrae running along the back. It supports the body, protects the spinal cord, the main nerve running from the brain, and gives muscles something to pull against. Vertebrates also have an internal skeleton of bone or cartilage, a skull that protects the brain, and usually two pairs of limbs or fins. Although vertebrates include the largest animals, they make up only about five per cent of all animal species; most animals are invertebrates.

Vertebrates are divided into five classes: fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals. Four features separate them: body covering, breathing, temperature control and reproduction.

Fish live in water throughout their lives. Most are covered in overlapping scales and a layer of slime, which protect them and help them slip through water. They breathe with gills, which take dissolved oxygen from water flowing over them. They have fins for swimming and steering, and most have a streamlined shape. Fish are cold-blooded, the scientific term being ectothermic: their body temperature changes with the temperature of their surroundings. Most fish reproduce by external fertilisation: the female releases eggs into the water, the male releases sperm over them, and the eggs develop outside the body, usually with little or no parental care, which is why fish lay huge numbers of eggs.

South Africa has many examples. The yellowfish lives in rivers such as the Vaal; the sardines of the KwaZulu-Natal sardine run are sea fish; the coelacanth, once thought extinct for millions of years, was discovered alive off East London in 1938. Sharks and rays are fish with skeletons made of cartilage rather than bone, and most have internal fertilisation.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of vertebrate, the four features used to classify vertebrates, and the features of fish.

## Subtopic: Amphibians and Reptiles

Amphibians are vertebrates that usually live part of their lives in water and part on land; the name means double life. Frogs, toads and salamanders are amphibians. Their skin is smooth, moist and has no scales, and it must stay damp because adult amphibians absorb some oxygen through their skin as well as breathing with simple lungs. Amphibians are ectothermic. They lay soft, jelly-covered eggs in water or damp places, usually with external fertilisation, and the young hatch as tadpoles that live in water, breathe with gills and have a tail. Over weeks the tadpole changes into an adult with legs and lungs, a process called metamorphosis. South Africa has more than one hundred and thirty frog species, including the large giant bullfrog of the Highveld, the tiny rain frogs and the endangered western leopard toad of Cape Town.

Reptiles are vertebrates with dry skin covered in hard scales or plates. Snakes, lizards, tortoises, turtles and crocodiles are reptiles. Their scaly, waterproof skin stops them drying out, so they can live in dry places away from water. They breathe with lungs throughout their lives, even sea turtles and crocodiles, which must come to the surface to breathe. Reptiles are ectothermic, which is why lizards bask in the morning Sun and become sluggish in cold weather. They have internal fertilisation, and most lay eggs with a leathery or hard shell on land; the shell stops the egg drying out, so reptiles do not need water to breed. A few snakes and lizards give birth to live young. South African reptiles include the Nile crocodile, the leopard tortoise, the puff adder, the Cape cobra, the flat lizards of the Augrabies area and the loggerhead and leatherback turtles that nest on the beaches of northern KwaZulu-Natal.

The key differences between amphibians and reptiles are the skin, moist and scaleless versus dry and scaly, and the egg, jelly-covered and laid in water versus shelled and laid on land. These two features explain why amphibians stay near water and reptiles can live in deserts.

The questions for this section are with you now: the features of amphibians and reptiles, metamorphosis, and why reptiles can live in dry places while amphibians cannot.

## Subtopic: Birds and Mammals

Birds and mammals are the two classes that are warm-blooded, or endothermic: they keep a constant body temperature using heat produced by their own respiration, whatever the temperature outside. This lets them stay active on cold nights and in cold places, but it uses a great deal of energy, so they need much more food than ectothermic animals of the same size.

Birds are covered in feathers, which no other living animal has. Feathers provide insulation and make flight possible. Birds have a beak with no teeth, and their front limbs are wings. Most can fly, helped by light, hollow bones and powerful chest muscles; some, like the ostrich of the Karoo and the African penguin of Boulders Beach, cannot fly but run or swim instead. Birds breathe with lungs connected to air sacs, an efficient system that supports flight. They have internal fertilisation and lay eggs with hard, chalky shells, which are usually kept warm, or incubated, by the parents, who also feed and protect the chicks. The African fish eagle over the river, the hadeda ibis on school fields and the tiny sunbirds of the fynbos are all South African birds.

Mammals are covered in hair or fur, at least at some stage of life. Female mammals feed their young on milk produced in mammary glands, which gives the class its name. Mammals breathe with lungs, have internal fertilisation, and almost all give birth to live young that develop inside the mother's body, nourished through a placenta. Mammals usually care for their young for a long time. The exceptions are interesting: the platypus and echidnas of Australia lay eggs, and marsupials such as kangaroos give birth to tiny young that develop in a pouch. Bats are mammals that fly; whales and dolphins are mammals that live in the sea, breathe air, and feed their calves on milk. The impala at the river, the elephant, the dassie and humans are all mammals.

A summary table brings the five classes together. Fish: scales, gills, ectothermic, mostly external fertilisation, eggs in water. Amphibians: moist skin, gills then lungs and skin, ectothermic, mostly external fertilisation, jelly eggs in water. Reptiles: dry scales, lungs, ectothermic, internal fertilisation, shelled eggs on land. Birds: feathers, lungs with air sacs, endothermic, internal fertilisation, hard-shelled eggs. Mammals: hair, lungs, endothermic, internal fertilisation, mostly live birth, milk.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: calling a whale or dolphin a fish; they are mammals. Two: calling a bat a bird; it has fur and feeds its young on milk. Three: calling a crocodile an amphibian because it lives near water; it has dry scales and shelled eggs. Four: saying cold-blooded animals have cold blood; their temperature follows their surroundings and can be warm. Five: saying all birds fly; ostriches and penguins do not.

The questions for this section are with you now: the features of birds and mammals, endothermic versus ectothermic, and classifying unusual vertebrates such as bats, whales and penguins.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a river, five visitors and a checklist of four questions — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Five Visitors at the River

Picture a river in Mpumalanga on a hot afternoon. Five visitors arrive, and all five have a backbone, a chain of bones down the back. Animals with backbones are vertebrates.

In the water is a yellowfish. It is a fish: scales, fins, and gills to breathe the oxygen in water.

On the muddy bank sits a frog. It is an amphibian: smooth, wet skin and eggs laid in the water.

On a sandbar lies a crocodile. It is a reptile: dry, scaly skin and eggs with a shell laid on land.

Overhead flies a fish eagle. It is a bird: feathers, a beak and wings.

And down to drink comes an impala. It is a mammal: fur, and its babies drink milk.

Fish, amphibian, reptile, bird, mammal. Five classes of vertebrates, all at one river.

Keep the river in mind and try the questions for this part: what a vertebrate is and which visitor is which.

## Subtopic: Four Questions

To sort any vertebrate, ask four questions.

What covers its body? Scales and slime: fish. Wet, smooth skin: amphibian. Dry, hard scales: reptile. Feathers: bird. Hair or fur: mammal.

How does it breathe? Gills: fish, and baby frogs. Lungs: reptiles, birds and mammals. Frogs use lungs and their wet skin.

Is it warm-blooded or cold-blooded? Birds and mammals make their own heat, so they stay warm all the time and need lots of food. Fish, frogs and reptiles take their heat from around them, which is why lizards sunbathe in the morning.

How does it have babies? Fish and frogs usually lay soft eggs in water. Frog eggs hatch into tadpoles that change into frogs. Reptiles and birds lay eggs with shells on land. Most mammals give birth to live babies and feed them milk.

Covering, breathing, warmth, babies. Four questions sort all five classes.

Hold the four questions in mind and try this part's questions.

## Subtopic: Tricky Customers

Some vertebrates try to trick you.

A whale lives in the sea and has fins. Fish? No. It breathes air with lungs and feeds its baby milk. Mammal.

A bat flies. Bird? No. It has fur, not feathers, and feeds its babies milk. Mammal.

A penguin swims and cannot fly. Fish? No. It has feathers and lays eggs with shells. Bird.

A crocodile lives in the river. Amphibian? No. It has dry scales and lays shelled eggs on land. Reptile.

The rule: ignore where it lives and how it moves. Check the covering, the breathing, the warmth and the babies.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Vertebrates have a backbone and come in five classes. Fish have scales and gills and lay eggs in water. Amphibians have moist skin, start as tadpoles in water and change into adults. Reptiles have dry scales and lay shelled eggs on land. Birds have feathers and lay hard-shelled eggs. Mammals have hair and feed their young on milk. Birds and mammals are warm-blooded; the others are cold-blooded.

Hold the river, the four questions and the tricky customers, and take the final questions of the lesson.
