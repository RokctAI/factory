# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains how scientists sort living things below the level of kingdom, all the way down to a single kind of organism. The anchor is the address on an envelope addressed to a learner in Soweto: country, province, city, suburb, street and house number, each line narrowing the possibilities until only one home is left. The governing idea is that classification is a hierarchy of seven main levels, Kingdom, Phylum or Division, Class, Order, Family, Genus and Species, and that at each step down the groups get smaller and their members share more characteristics. The order of the levels, the meaning of species and genus, and the two-part scientific name are examinable, and the system returns in Grade 10 Life Sciences.

## Subtopic: The Seven Levels

The classification hierarchy has seven main levels, or ranks. From the largest and most general to the smallest and most specific they are: Kingdom, Phylum, Class, Order, Family, Genus and Species. For plants, the second level is usually called Division instead of Phylum, but it means the same rank. A memory sentence helps to keep the order: King Philip Came Over For Good Soup. Each first letter matches a level: K for Kingdom, P for Phylum, C for Class, O for Order, F for Family, G for Genus, S for Species.

The hierarchy works like the address on an envelope. Country narrows the world to South Africa; province narrows it to Gauteng; city to Johannesburg; suburb to Soweto; street to one road; house number to one home. Each level contains everything in the levels below it. In the same way, a kingdom contains many phyla, each phylum contains many classes, and so on down to species.

Two patterns follow from this. Going down the hierarchy, each group contains fewer kinds of organisms. And going down, the organisms in a group share more and more characteristics. Kingdom Animals contains everything from sponges to elephants, which share only a few features: many cells, no cell walls, and feeding on other organisms. The species Panthera leo, the lion, contains only lions, which share almost every feature.

The levels can be traced for the lion. Kingdom: Animals, because it is many-celled and eats other organisms. Phylum: Chordata, animals with a nerve cord along the back, which includes all vertebrates. Class: Mammalia, because it has hair and feeds its young on milk. Order: Carnivora, mammals with teeth adapted for eating meat. Family: Felidae, the cats. Genus: Panthera, the big roaring cats, which also includes the leopard. Species: leo, the lion.

The questions for this section are with you now: the seven levels in order, the two patterns going down the hierarchy, and the classification of the lion.

## Subtopic: Species and Scientific Names

A species is a group of organisms that are so similar that they can breed with each other and produce offspring that can themselves breed. All lions belong to one species; a lion and a leopard belong to different species, even though they are in the same genus. A horse and a donkey can mate, but their offspring, the mule, is almost always unable to reproduce, so horses and donkeys are separate species.

Every species has a two-part scientific name. This system is called binomial nomenclature, meaning two-name naming, and it was introduced by the Swedish scientist Carl Linnaeus in the 1750s. The first part of the name is the genus, written with a capital letter; the second part is the species name, written with a small letter. The full name is printed in italics or, when handwritten, underlined. The lion is Panthera leo; the leopard is Panthera pardus; humans are Homo sapiens; the springbok is Antidorcas marsupialis; the king protea, South Africa's national flower, is Protea cynaroides.

Scientific names solve a real problem with common names. The same animal may have many common names in different languages: the lion is ingonyama in isiZulu, tau in Sesotho and leeu in Afrikaans. Worse, the same common name may refer to different animals in different places; a bird called a robin in South Africa is not the same as a robin in Britain or America. A scientific name is unique and the same in every country, so scientists anywhere know exactly which species is meant. The names are usually in Latin or Greek, or are Latinised, because Latin was the shared language of scholars in Linnaeus's time and no longer changes.

Scientific names also show relationships. Panthera leo and Panthera pardus share the genus name, which tells us immediately that lions and leopards are closely related. Many South African species have names honouring local places or people, or use local words, for example plants named capensis, meaning from the Cape.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of a species, the rules for writing a scientific name, and why scientific names are better than common names.

## Subtopic: Using the Hierarchy

Classification hierarchies are used to identify organisms and to compare how closely they are related.

To compare two organisms, find the lowest level they share. The more levels they share, the more closely related they are. A lion and a leopard share every level down to genus, so they are very closely related. A lion and a domestic cat share levels down to family, Felidae, but are in different genera. A lion and a dog share levels down to order, Carnivora. A lion and a human share levels down to class, Mammalia. A lion and a frog share only kingdom and phylum.

A table helps. For the lion, the leopard and the human: Kingdom, all Animals; Phylum, all Chordata; Class, all Mammalia; Order, Carnivora for the cats and Primates for the human; Family, Felidae for the cats and Hominidae for the human; Genus, Panthera for both cats and Homo for the human; Species, leo, pardus and sapiens.

Identification keys use the same idea in reverse. A dichotomous key asks a series of questions, each with two possible answers, that split organisms into smaller and smaller groups until only one is left. For example: Does it have a backbone? If no, go to question two; if yes, go to question three. Each question uses a clear, observable feature. Field guides to South African birds, trees and insects use similar keys and pictures.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: putting the levels in the wrong order, especially swapping Class and Order or Family and Genus. Two: writing the species name with a capital letter, or the genus with a small letter. Three: thinking organisms in the same kingdom are closely related; they share very few features. Four: thinking a species is any group of similar-looking organisms; they must be able to breed and produce fertile offspring. Five: forgetting that each level contains all the levels below it.

The questions for this section are with you now: deciding which pair of organisms is most closely related using a table, and using a simple key.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with an address on an envelope, a family surname and a two-way game — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: From Country to House

Think about the address on a letter to someone in Soweto. South Africa. Gauteng. Johannesburg. Soweto. Then a street. Then a house number. Each line makes the area smaller until only one home is left.

Scientists sort living things the same way, with seven levels. Kingdom, Phylum, Class, Order, Family, Genus, Species. Here is a sentence to remember them: King Philip Came Over For Good Soup.

At the top, Kingdom Animals is like the whole country: it holds sponges, worms, snakes, elephants and you. They do not have much in common. As you go down, each group gets smaller and its members are more alike. At the bottom, a species is like one house: only one kind of creature lives there, such as lions.

For plants, the second level is often called Division instead of Phylum. Same level, different word.

Keep the envelope in mind and try the questions for this part: the seven levels in order.

## Subtopic: Surname First

Every kind of living thing has a scientific name with two parts, a bit like a name with the surname first.

The lion is Panthera leo. Panthera is like the surname, the genus, and it starts with a capital letter. Leo is like the first name, the species, with a small letter. The whole name is written in italics, or underlined when you write by hand.

The leopard is Panthera pardus. Same surname! That tells you lions and leopards are close cousins. You are Homo sapiens.

Why not just say lion? Because people say ingonyama, tau, leeu or lion, depending on their language. And sometimes one common name means different animals in different countries. The scientific name is the same everywhere in the world, so there is no confusion.

And what makes a species? Members can breed together and have babies that can also have babies. A horse and a donkey can have a mule, but mules cannot have babies, so horses and donkeys are different species.

Hold the surname idea in mind and try this part's questions: writing scientific names and what a species is.

## Subtopic: How Close Are We?

Here is a game. How closely related are two animals? Find the lowest level they share.

Lion and leopard: same genus. Very close cousins. Lion and house cat: same family, the cats. Close. Lion and dog: same order, the meat-eaters. Further apart. Lion and you: same class, mammals. Hair and milk, but not much more. Lion and frog: only the same phylum, animals with backbones.

The deeper down the levels you match, the closer the relatives.

Scientists also use keys to identify living things. A key is like the game twenty questions, but each question has just two answers. Does it have a backbone? Yes or no. Does it have feathers? Yes or no. Step by step, you narrow it down to one.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Classification has seven levels: Kingdom, Phylum or Division, Class, Order, Family, Genus and Species. Going down, groups get smaller and members share more features. A species can breed and produce fertile offspring. Scientific names have two parts, genus with a capital and species with a small letter, in italics. Two organisms that share a lower level are more closely related.

Hold the envelope, the surname and the relatives game, and take the final questions of the lesson.
