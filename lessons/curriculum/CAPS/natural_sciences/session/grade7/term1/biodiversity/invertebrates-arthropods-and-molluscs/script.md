# Part 1 — Expert

This session turns to the animals without backbones, the invertebrates, which make up about ninety-five per cent of all animal species. The anchor is a single rocky pool and its surrounding rocks on the Wild Coast of the Eastern Cape: crabs scuttling sideways, a spider on the rocks above, a dragonfly overhead, and limpets, mussels and an octopus in the water. The governing idea is that two of the largest invertebrate groups, the phylum Arthropoda and the phylum Mollusca, can be recognised by their body plan: arthropods have an exoskeleton and jointed legs, while molluscs have a soft body, usually protected by a shell. The features of arthropods, the differences between insects, arachnids and crustaceans, and the features of molluscs are examinable, and invertebrate diversity returns in Grade 10 Life Sciences.

## Subtopic: Invertebrates and Arthropods

Invertebrates are animals without a backbone. They include sponges, jellyfish, many kinds of worms, starfish, molluscs and arthropods. Although many are small, invertebrates are the great majority of animal species, and they are vital to every ecosystem: they pollinate plants, decompose dead material, aerate soil and form the food of countless other animals.

Arthropods, phylum Arthropoda, are the largest group of animals on Earth, with more than a million described species. The name means jointed foot. All arthropods share three key features. First, they have an exoskeleton, a hard outer skeleton made mainly of chitin, which supports the body from the outside and protects it, and which also prevents water loss. Second, they have jointed legs, and other jointed appendages such as antennae and mouthparts, which can bend at the joints because the exoskeleton is thin there. Third, their bodies are divided into segments, often grouped into distinct body regions.

The exoskeleton has one big drawback: it cannot grow. To get bigger, an arthropod must shed its exoskeleton, a process called moulting, and expand its body before the new, soft exoskeleton hardens. During that time the animal is soft and vulnerable, which is why crabs hide after moulting. The papery empty cases of cicadas found on tree trunks are moulted exoskeletons.

Arthropods are divided into several classes. The three studied in Grade 7 are the insects, the arachnids and the crustaceans. Others include the millipedes, with two pairs of legs on most body segments, and the centipedes, with one pair per segment.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of invertebrate, the three features of arthropods, and why arthropods must moult.

## Subtopic: Insects, Arachnids and Crustaceans

The three main arthropod classes are recognised by counting body sections, legs and antennae.

Insects have three body parts: a head, a thorax and an abdomen. They have three pairs of legs, six legs in total, all attached to the thorax. They have one pair of antennae on the head, used for smell and touch, and compound eyes made of many small units. Most adult insects have one or two pairs of wings, also attached to the thorax; insects were the first animals to fly. Insects are the most numerous group of all, with beetles alone counting several hundred thousand species. Examples include the dragonfly, honeybee, termite, dung beetle, mopane worm, which is the caterpillar of a moth and an important food in Limpopo, and the mosquito. Many insects undergo metamorphosis, changing from larva to pupa to adult, as a caterpillar becomes a butterfly.

Arachnids have two body parts: a cephalothorax, which is a joined head and thorax, and an abdomen. They have four pairs of legs, eight legs in total, and no antennae and no wings. Spiders, scorpions, ticks and mites are arachnids. Many spiders produce silk from spinnerets at the end of the abdomen, and most have fangs that inject venom to subdue prey. Scorpions have large pincers and a venomous sting at the tip of the tail. South African examples include the rain spider that enters houses before storms, the baboon spider, the thick-tailed scorpions of the dry west, whose large tails warn of strong venom, and the ticks that carry tick-bite fever.

Crustaceans have a hard exoskeleton strengthened with chalk-like minerals, usually two body parts, a cephalothorax and an abdomen, two pairs of antennae and many pairs of legs, often five pairs of walking legs, with the front pair often formed into pincers. Most live in water and breathe with gills. Crabs, prawns, crayfish and lobsters, barnacles and the tiny copepods of the plankton are crustaceans. The West Coast rock lobster is an important catch in South Africa. Woodlice, found under stones in gardens, are among the few crustaceans that live on land, and they still need damp places.

A quick count separates them: insects three body parts, six legs, antennae; arachnids two body parts, eight legs, no antennae; crustaceans two body parts, ten or more legs, two pairs of antennae, gills.

The questions for this section are with you now: the body parts, legs and antennae of insects, arachnids and crustaceans, and classifying a spider, a crab and a beetle.

## Subtopic: Molluscs

Molluscs, phylum Mollusca, form the second-largest group of animals, with tens of thousands of species living in the sea, in fresh water and on land. The name comes from the Latin for soft. Their main features are a soft, unsegmented body; a muscular foot used for movement or digging; and a fold of tissue called the mantle, which covers the body organs and, in most molluscs, makes a shell of calcium carbonate, the same chalky substance as limestone. Molluscs have no jointed legs and no exoskeleton of chitin. Many have a rasping tongue covered with tiny teeth, the radula, which they use to scrape food.

Three main groups are usually described. Gastropods, meaning stomach-foot, include snails, slugs, whelks, limpets and the abalone, called perlemoen in South Africa. They usually have a single, often coiled shell, or none at all in slugs, and they glide on a large flat foot. Limpets clamp tightly to rocks on the shore. Bivalves have two hinged shells that can close tightly: mussels, oysters and clams. Most filter food particles from water drawn in through the gaps between the shells. Cephalopods, meaning head-foot, include octopuses, squids and cuttlefish. Their foot has become a ring of arms or tentacles around the head; most have a small internal shell or none; and they are the most intelligent invertebrates, able to change colour, solve problems and jet away by squirting water. The common octopus of South African rock pools can squeeze through a gap the size of its beak.

Molluscs matter to people. Mussels and oysters are harvested and farmed along the coast; perlemoen is so valuable that illegal poaching has made it endangered; and some snails are crop pests or carry the parasites that cause bilharzia in fresh water.

The rock pool shows the two phyla side by side. The crab, with its jointed legs and hard exoskeleton, is an arthropod; the octopus, limpets and mussels, with soft bodies and, in two of them, shells, are molluscs. A shell does not make an animal a crab, and a hard covering does not make a mussel an arthropod: what matters is jointed legs versus a soft body with a muscular foot.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: calling spiders insects; they have eight legs and two body parts. Two: calling a crab a mollusc because it has a hard shell; it has jointed legs, so it is a crustacean, an arthropod. Three: saying an octopus is not a mollusc because it has no outer shell; it has a soft body and a mantle. Four: counting a centipede's legs as six because it is a bug; it is a separate arthropod class. Five: thinking invertebrates are a small, unimportant group; they are about ninety-five per cent of animal species.

The questions for this section are with you now: the features of molluscs, the three groups with examples, and how to tell a crab from a mussel.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a rock pool, a counting game and soft bodies in shells — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Animals With No Backbone

Most animals in the world do not have a backbone. They are called invertebrates, and they are about ninety-five out of every hundred kinds of animal. Worms, jellyfish, spiders, snails and insects are all invertebrates.

The biggest group of all is the arthropods. The name means jointed legs. Instead of bones inside, an arthropod wears its skeleton on the outside, like a suit of armour. That armour is called an exoskeleton. It protects the animal and stops it drying out, and its legs bend at joints, like the joints in a knight's armour.

But armour has one problem: it cannot grow. So when an arthropod gets too big, it climbs out of its old armour and grows a new, bigger one. That is moulting. The empty cases you sometimes find on tree trunks are old cicada armour.

Keep the armour suit in mind and try the questions for this part: what an invertebrate is and what makes an arthropod.

## Subtopic: Count the Legs

Here is how to tell three kinds of arthropod apart: count.

An insect has three body parts, a head, a middle and a back end, and six legs. It has feelers, called antennae, and many insects have wings. Bees, beetles, ants, butterflies and mosquitoes are insects.

An arachnid has two body parts and eight legs. No feelers and no wings. Spiders and scorpions are arachnids. So a spider is not an insect: count its legs and you get eight.

A crustacean usually has ten or more legs and two pairs of feelers, and most live in water and breathe with gills. Crabs, prawns and lobsters are crustaceans. The little grey woodlice under garden stones are crustaceans too, which is why they like damp places.

Six legs, insect. Eight legs, arachnid. Ten or more, and gills, crustacean.

Hold the counting game in mind and try this part's questions.

## Subtopic: Soft Bodies in Shells

In the same rock pool as the crab, there are animals with no jointed legs at all. Their bodies are soft and squishy. These are molluscs, and the name means soft.

Most molluscs make a shell to protect their soft body. A snail or a limpet has one shell and moves on one big, flat, muscly foot. A mussel or an oyster has two shells joined by a hinge, which snap shut when the tide goes out. And then there is the octopus. It has no outside shell at all, and its foot has turned into eight arms. It is the cleverest invertebrate: it can change colour, open jars and squeeze through a tiny gap.

So in the rock pool: the crab with jointed legs is an arthropod; the snail, the mussel and the octopus, with soft bodies, are molluscs.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Invertebrates have no backbone and are most of the animal kingdom. Arthropods have an exoskeleton and jointed legs, and moult to grow. Insects have three body parts and six legs; arachnids have two body parts and eight legs; crustaceans have ten or more legs and gills. Molluscs have soft bodies and a muscular foot, usually with a shell: snails have one, mussels have two, and the octopus has none.

Hold the armour, the leg count and the rock pool, and take the final questions of the lesson.
