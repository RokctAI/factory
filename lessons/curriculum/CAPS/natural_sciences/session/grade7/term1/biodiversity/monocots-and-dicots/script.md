# Part 1 — Expert

This session divides the flowering plants into their two great groups. The anchor is a South African vegetable garden and the veld beside it: a row of mealies and a clump of grass on one side, and bean plants, a pumpkin vine and a young marula tree on the other. The governing idea is that angiosperms are divided into monocotyledons and dicotyledons, named after the number of seed leaves in the embryo, and that this one difference comes with a matching set of differences in roots, stems, leaves, flowers and seeds that can be seen without a microscope. The six differences, how to classify a plant from its features, and South African examples of each group are examinable, and the distinction returns in Grade 10 Life Sciences.

## Subtopic: Seeds and Seed Leaves

Inside every angiosperm seed is an embryo, a young plant with a tiny root and shoot, attached to one or two special leaves called cotyledons, or seed leaves. Cotyledons either store food for the seedling or absorb it from a food store around the embryo. The number of cotyledons gives the two groups their names: monocotyledons, or monocots, have one cotyledon, and dicotyledons, or dicots, have two.

The difference can be seen by soaking seeds. A soaked bean seed, a dicot, splits easily into two halves; each half is a fat cotyledon full of stored food, and between them lies the tiny embryo. A soaked mealie grain, a monocot, does not split into halves. It has a single small cotyledon pressed against a large store of food called the endosperm, which makes up most of the grain and is the starchy part we grind into mealie meal.

When the seeds germinate, the difference shows again. A bean seedling usually pushes its two fat cotyledons up out of the soil, where they turn green before shrivelling as the first true leaves grow. A mealie seedling sends up a single narrow, pointed shoot, and the cotyledon stays below the ground.

Monocots and dicots are both angiosperms: both have flowers, and both enclose their seeds in fruits. About a quarter of flowering plant species are monocots and most of the rest are dicots, though the monocots include the grasses, which supply most of humanity's food.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of cotyledon, how a bean seed differs from a mealie grain, and how their seedlings differ.

## Subtopic: Leaves, Roots and Stems

The number of seed leaves is only the start. Monocots and dicots also differ in their leaves, roots and stems, which makes them easy to tell apart in the garden.

Leaves. Monocot leaves are usually long and narrow, and their veins run parallel to each other along the length of the leaf, like the lines on a page. The leaf often wraps around the stem at its base, as in mealie and grass leaves. Dicot leaves are usually broader, often with a leaf stalk, and their veins form a branching network, with a main vein in the middle and smaller veins spreading out from it like a net; bean, pumpkin and rose leaves show this. Holding a leaf up to the light makes the pattern clear.

Roots. Most monocots have a fibrous root system: many thin roots of about equal size growing from the base of the stem, forming a dense mat. Pulling up a clump of grass shows this mat. Fibrous roots hold topsoil together very well, which is why grass is planted to stop erosion. Most dicots have a tap root system: one main root growing straight down, with smaller side roots branching from it. Carrots and beetroot are swollen tap roots used to store food, and the long tap roots of thorn trees can reach deep water.

Stems. In a monocot stem, the bundles of transport tissue, the vascular bundles that carry water and food, are scattered throughout the stem. In a dicot stem, the vascular bundles are arranged in a ring near the outside. Because of this ring, many dicots can grow wider each year, adding new wood in rings, and so form woody trunks; the growth rings of a tree are the result. Most monocots cannot grow wider in this way, so they are mostly soft-stemmed herbs and grasses. Palms and aloes, which are monocots, form tall trunks by other means, but they do not have true annual wood rings.

The questions for this section are with you now: the vein patterns of monocot and dicot leaves, fibrous and tap roots, and the arrangement of vascular bundles in stems.

## Subtopic: Flowers and Using the Differences

Flowers provide the last difference. In monocots, the flower parts, such as petals, usually come in threes or multiples of three: three, six or nine. Lilies, arum lilies, agapanthus, irises, red-hot pokers and the flowers of aloes show this, often with three petals and three petal-like sepals that look alike. In dicots, flower parts usually come in fours or fives or multiples of these: a bean flower has five petals, a rose five, a hibiscus five. Grass flowers are tiny and wind-pollinated, so their parts are hard to count, but grasses are clearly monocots by their leaves and roots.

A full comparison: monocots have one cotyledon, parallel-veined leaves, fibrous roots, scattered vascular bundles, mostly soft stems, and flower parts in threes. Dicots have two cotyledons, net-veined leaves, a tap root, vascular bundles in a ring, often woody stems, and flower parts in fours or fives.

South African examples are everywhere. Monocots: mealies, sorghum, wheat, sugar cane in KwaZulu-Natal, all the veld grasses, aloes, agapanthus, arum lilies, the bird-of-paradise flower, strelitzia, from the Eastern Cape, and palms. Dicots: beans, pumpkins, potatoes, tomatoes, sunflowers, proteas, the marula, the baobab, thorn trees, rooibos and honeybush.

To classify an unknown flowering plant, use several features together, because a few plants are exceptions to one rule. A leaf with parallel veins, a fibrous root and flower parts in threes is certainly a monocot. Aloes have thick, succulent leaves, but their parallel veins, flower parts in sixes and fibrous roots place them among the monocots.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: deciding from a single feature only; check several. Two: calling grass a non-flowering plant; grasses have tiny flowers. Three: confusing tap roots and fibrous roots by size; it is the pattern that matters, one main root or many equal roots. Four: thinking all trees are dicots; palms are monocot trees. Five: saying monocots and dicots are not angiosperms; both are flowering plants.

The questions for this section are with you now: counting flower parts, the full comparison table, and classifying aloes, grasses and beans from their features.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a split bean, a leaf held up to the light and a five-question checklist — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: One or Two

Soak a bean and a mealie grain in water overnight. Then try to split them open.

The bean splits easily into two fat halves, like a book opening. Each half is a seed leaf full of food for the baby plant, which is tucked between them. Two seed leaves: the bean is a dicot. Di means two.

The mealie grain will not split into halves. It has only one small seed leaf, and most of the grain is a big store of starch, the part that becomes mealie meal. One seed leaf: the mealie is a monocot. Mono means one.

Plant them and watch. The bean pushes its two fat halves up into the light. The mealie sends up one thin, pointy shoot.

Both are flowering plants. They just sort into two teams: Team One and Team Two.

Keep the split bean in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: Read the Leaf, Pull the Roots

You do not need to split seeds to tell the teams apart. Look at the grown plant.

Hold a leaf up to the light. A mealie or grass leaf is long and thin, and its lines, the veins, run side by side from bottom to top, like train tracks. That is Team One, monocot. A bean or pumpkin leaf is wide, with a middle vein and lots of little veins branching off like a net. That is Team Two, dicot.

Now pull up a clump of grass. You get a messy mat of thin roots, all about the same size, like a beard. Those are fibrous roots: Team One. Pull up a carrot or a bean plant. You find one main root going straight down with small roots off the side. That is a tap root: Team Two.

Because Team Two stems carry their pipes in a ring, many of them can grow thick and woody. That is why most big trees with rings are dicots.

Hold the leaf and the roots in mind and try this part's questions.

## Subtopic: Count the Petals

Last check: count the flower parts.

Team One flowers come in threes. An agapanthus flower has six petal-like parts, an arum lily or an aloe flower works in threes too. Team Two flowers come in fours or fives. A bean flower has five petals. So does a rose.

Now use everything together. An aloe has thick, fat leaves, which might confuse you. But its veins run side by side, its roots are a beard and its flower parts come in sixes. Team One: monocot.

Monocots in South Africa: mealies, sugar cane, grasses, aloes, agapanthus and the strelitzia, the bird-of-paradise flower. Dicots: beans, pumpkins, proteas, the marula, the baobab and rooibos.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Flowering plants are monocots or dicots. Monocots have one seed leaf, narrow leaves with parallel veins, fibrous roots, scattered pipes in the stem and flower parts in threes. Dicots have two seed leaves, broad leaves with net veins, a tap root, pipes in a ring and flower parts in fours or fives. Always check more than one feature.

Hold the split bean, the leaf and the petals, and take the final questions of the lesson.
