### subtopic_1 — Invertebrates and Arthropods
**Assistant:** Why are there no insects as big as dogs today, when giant insects once existed?

**Tutor:** The leading explanation involves oxygen and the way insects breathe. Insects do not have lungs. Air enters through small holes along the sides of the body, called spiracles, and travels through a branching network of tubes, the tracheae, directly to the tissues. Oxygen moves along these tubes mainly by spreading out, which works well over short distances but becomes less effective as the tubes get longer. So the bigger an insect is, the harder it is to supply its innermost tissues with oxygen. About three hundred million years ago, the air contained much more oxygen than today, perhaps thirty per cent or more instead of twenty-one per cent, and in that atmosphere giant insects lived, including dragonfly-like insects with wingspans of about seventy centimetres. As oxygen levels fell, the giants disappeared. The exoskeleton adds another limit. A large exoskeleton would have to be very thick and heavy to support the animal's weight, and every time the animal moulted it would be soft and unable to support itself, which is why the largest arthropods today, such as the giant crabs of Japan, live in water, which helps support their weight. Today's biggest insects, like some beetles and stick insects, are only a few tens of centimetres long. So the small size of insects is not an accident but a consequence of their body plan.

### subtopic_2 — Insects, Arachnids and Crustaceans
**Assistant:** Is a spider an insect, and does the difference really matter?

**Tutor:** A spider is not an insect, and the difference matters to scientists because it reflects a deep difference in body plan and history, not just a different number of legs. A spider has two body parts instead of three, eight legs instead of six, no antennae and no wings, and it has fangs and silk glands that no insect has. Its mouthparts work differently: a spider usually injects digestive juices into its prey and sucks up the liquid. Spiders and insects have been separate lineages for hundreds of millions of years. The difference also has practical importance. Most spiders are predators that eat insects, so they help control pests in homes, gardens and crops; a single garden may contain thousands of spiders catching flies, mosquitoes and aphids. Pesticides aimed at insects often kill spiders too, which can make pest problems worse once the pests return. In medicine, knowing the difference helps with bites and stings. In South Africa only a few spiders, such as the button spiders, the violin spider and the six-eyed sand spider, have venom that is dangerous to people, and most spider bites cause only mild effects. Scorpions are arachnids too; a useful rule is that South African scorpions with thick tails and thin pincers tend to have more powerful venom than those with thin tails and big pincers. So getting the classification right helps people respond sensibly instead of fearing every creature with legs.

### subtopic_3 — Molluscs
**Assistant:** How can an octopus be so intelligent when its relatives are snails and mussels?

**Tutor:** The octopus has followed a very different path from its slow relatives, and its intelligence seems to be linked to its way of life as an active hunter with a soft, unprotected body. Its ancestors had shells, like snails, but over hundreds of millions of years the octopus line lost the outer shell. That made it vulnerable, but also flexible: an octopus can squeeze into tiny cracks, change its shape and texture and alter its colour in a fraction of a second. To use such a flexible body, and to hunt crabs and fish while avoiding predators, it needs a large and complex nervous system. An octopus has about five hundred million nerve cells, a number similar to a dog's, and about two thirds of them are not in the brain but in the eight arms, so each arm can explore, taste and grasp with some independence. In experiments octopuses have learned to open jars, find their way through mazes and recognise individual people. Divers in South African waters have watched them carry coconut shells or bivalve shells to use as portable shelters. It is a striking example of how intelligence evolved separately in very different animals, in octopuses as well as in mammals and birds. It also shows why classification depends on body plan and ancestry, not on behaviour: by its soft body, mantle and mouthparts, the octopus is clearly a mollusc, even though it behaves nothing like a mussel.
