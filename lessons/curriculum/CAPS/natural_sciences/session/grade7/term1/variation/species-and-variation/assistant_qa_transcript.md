### subtopic_1 — What a Species Is
**Assistant:** How do scientists decide whether two similar-looking animals are separate species?

**Tutor:** The breeding test in the definition is the starting point, but in practice scientists often cannot watch animals breed, especially wild animals in remote places or species known only from museum specimens. So they combine several kinds of evidence. First, they look for consistent differences in body structure, such as skull shape, teeth, scales or markings, that are not just individual variation. Second, they study behaviour, such as mating calls and courtship. Many frogs, birds and insects look almost identical, but each species has its own call, and females respond only to their own species' call, so in nature they do not interbreed. South African scientists have identified new frog species partly by recording their calls. Third, they compare genetic material. If two populations have been breeding separately for a long time, differences build up in their DNA, and large consistent differences suggest separate species. Fourth, they look at where the animals live: populations that overlap in the same area but never interbreed are clearly separate species. Sometimes the evidence shows the opposite. Genetic studies of giraffes in Africa have suggested there may be four species rather than one, which is still debated, while some animals once given different names turned out to be one variable species. Classification therefore changes as evidence improves, but the central idea of the definition remains: a species is a group whose members can and do breed together to produce fertile offspring.

### subtopic_2 — Variation Within a Species
**Assistant:** Why does a graph of the heights of a whole school make a bell shape?

**Tutor:** Because height is influenced by many separate factors, each of which pushes a little up or a little down, and when many small influences are added together, most people end up near the middle and few at the extremes. Height is affected by hundreds of inherited factors, each with a small effect, together with environmental factors such as nutrition in childhood, health, sleep and, for a school, age. For any one person, some factors push towards tall and some towards short. To be very tall, a person would need almost all factors pushing upwards at once, which is unlikely, just as tossing ten coins and getting all heads is unlikely. Most people get a mixture, so most heights cluster around the average. Plotting the number of learners in each height range, such as 140 to 144 centimetres, 145 to 149 centimetres and so on, therefore gives a smooth hump that rises to a peak in the middle and falls away on both sides: the bell-shaped or normal distribution. Many continuous features, such as mass, hand span and the length of leaves on a tree, give similar curves. This pattern is useful in medicine: doctors compare a child's height and mass with growth charts based on these distributions to check whether the child is growing normally. A discontinuous feature such as blood group cannot give a bell curve, because there are no in-between values; it gives separate bars for each group.

### subtopic_3 — Causes of Variation
**Assistant:** If identical twins have the same genes, why are they not exactly the same?

**Tutor:** Because genes give the instructions, but the environment influences how those instructions play out, and no two people, even twins raised together, experience exactly the same environment. Identical twins form when one fertilised egg splits into two early in development, so they start with the same genetic instructions. That is why they look so alike and always share the same blood group and natural eye colour. But even in the uterus they may lie in different positions and receive slightly different amounts of nourishment, so one twin is often born a little heavier. After birth the differences grow: they may eat differently, play different sports, have different illnesses or accidents, spend different amounts of time in the sun, and make different friends and choices. Fingerprints, which form in the uterus partly through small random effects of pressure and position, are always different between identical twins, which is why fingerprints can tell them apart. Scientists have also found that chemical tags on the genetic material, which switch genes on or off, change during life in response to diet, stress and other influences, and these differ more and more between twins as they get older. Studies of twins raised in different homes are especially useful: when such twins are still similar in a feature, inheritance probably plays a big role; when they differ a lot, the environment probably does. For most human features, both matter.
