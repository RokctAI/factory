# Part 1 — Expert

This session turns the class itself into a scientific investigation of inherited variation in humans. The anchor is a Grade 7 class in Polokwane collecting data on themselves: measuring heights against a wall chart, checking who can roll their tongue, comparing earlobes and thumbs, and recording the results in a table that will become a graph. The governing idea is that humans vary in many inherited features, some continuous and some discontinuous; that genes passed from both parents are the basis of this inheritance; and that investigating variation requires careful measurement, fair recording, the right kind of graph and honest conclusions that respect privacy and dignity. Examples of inherited human features, the planning of a variation survey, data handling and the interpretation of graphs are examinable.

## Subtopic: Inherited Features in Humans

Every human inherits genetic instructions, carried in genes, from both parents: one set of instructions from the mother's egg cell and one set from the father's sperm cell. Genes are found on long thread-like structures called chromosomes in the nucleus of almost every cell. Humans have forty-six chromosomes in most cells, in twenty-three pairs; one chromosome of each pair came from each parent. Because each sex cell carries a random selection of one chromosome from each pair, every child receives a unique combination, which is why brothers and sisters differ.

Some human features commonly used to study inherited variation are the following. Height has a strong inherited component, with hundreds of genes involved, together with environmental effects such as nutrition. Natural eye colour ranges from very dark brown, the most common colour worldwide and in South Africa, through lighter browns, hazel and green to blue; it is controlled by several genes. Natural hair type, straight, wavy, curly or tightly coiled, and natural hair colour are inherited. Blood group, A, B, AB or O, is controlled by one gene with three versions and is a clear example of discontinuous variation. The ability to roll the tongue into a U shape is often used in school surveys; it was once thought to be controlled by a single gene, but twin studies have shown that the environment and practice also play a part, so it is a useful classroom example rather than a perfect one. Earlobes may hang free or be attached to the side of the head, and studies show a range of in-between forms. Other features used include the presence of dimples, freckles, a cleft chin, and whether the thumb bends back sharply, sometimes called hitchhiker's thumb.

Skin colour is an inherited feature controlled by many genes, influenced also by sunlight exposure. Variation in skin colour across human populations relates mainly to the strength of sunlight where ancestors lived: darker skin protects against strong ultraviolet light, and lighter skin allows more vitamin D to be made in weak sunlight. All humans are one species and share almost all of their genetic instructions; the genetic differences between any two people are tiny compared with what they share.

The questions for this section are with you now: how genes are passed from both parents, why siblings differ, and examples of inherited human features.

## Subtopic: Planning a Variation Survey

A variation survey is a scientific investigation, so it follows the same steps as any investigation.

First, ask a clear question. For example: how does height vary among the learners in our Grade 7 class? Or: what proportion of learners in the school can roll their tongues?

Second, choose features carefully and decide how to observe them. A continuous feature such as height must be measured with an instrument and a unit; a discontinuous feature such as tongue rolling is observed and classified into groups. Each must have a clear definition. What counts as rolling the tongue? A U shape held for three seconds, perhaps. What counts as attached earlobes? The lower edge joins the head without hanging below.

Third, plan for fair and accurate results. Measure everyone the same way: for height, shoes off, back against a wall, heels together, looking straight ahead, a ruler or book laid flat on the head, and the mark read at eye level, recorded to the nearest centimetre or half centimetre. Use the same measuring tape and the same person if possible. Measure twice and take the average if readings differ. Survey as many people as possible, because a larger sample gives more reliable results.

Fourth, think about ethics. People must agree to take part; no one should be forced to share information about their body. Data should be recorded with numbers or codes instead of names where possible, and features that some people feel sensitive about, such as mass, should only be collected privately and with care. Nobody's results should be used to tease or embarrass. Scientists follow similar rules when they study people.

Fifth, record results in a table as they are collected: for height, a column for learner number and a column for height in centimetres; for tongue rolling, a tally of yes and no. Tables should have headings and units.

The questions for this section are with you now: the steps of planning a variation survey, how to measure height accurately, and the ethical rules for collecting data about people.

## Subtopic: Graphs and Conclusions

Once results are collected, they are organised and displayed so that patterns can be seen.

For continuous data such as height, first group the measurements into equal class intervals, for example 135 to 139 cm, 140 to 144 cm, 145 to 149 cm, 150 to 154 cm and so on, and count how many learners fall into each interval using a tally. Then draw a histogram: the height intervals along the horizontal axis and the number of learners on the vertical axis, with bars touching each other because the data are continuous. With enough data, the histogram usually shows a hump in the middle, the most common heights, with fewer learners in the shortest and tallest groups.

For discontinuous data such as tongue rolling or blood group, draw a bar graph with a separate bar for each category and gaps between the bars, because the categories are separate. A pie chart can also show the proportion in each group. For example, if 21 out of 30 learners can roll their tongues, then 21 ÷ 30 = 0,7, so 70 per cent can and 30 per cent cannot.

The range and the average summarise continuous data. If the shortest learner is 138 cm and the tallest is 165 cm, the range is 165 - 138 = 27 cm. The mean height is found by adding all the heights and dividing by the number of learners.

Conclusions must answer the question and stay within the evidence. A good conclusion might be: in our class of 30, heights ranged from 138 cm to 165 cm, with most learners between 145 and 154 cm; height shows continuous variation. A poor conclusion would claim something the data cannot show, such as that tall learners are better at sport.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: drawing gaps between bars for continuous data; histogram bars touch. Two: drawing joined bars for categories like blood group. Three: generalising from a tiny sample to all humans. Four: forgetting units and headings. Five: using results to label or tease individuals.

The questions for this section are with you now: choosing the right graph, grouping continuous data, calculating range and percentages, and writing a conclusion that stays within the evidence.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a family photo, a measuring wall and a class graph — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Your Family Recipe

Think of a family photo. You might have your mother's eyes, your father's hair, your grandmother's smile. That is inheritance.

Inside almost every cell in your body are tiny instruction books called genes. You got half of your instructions from your mother and half from your father. Each brother or sister got a different mix of the same two sets, like shuffling two decks of cards together and dealing a different hand each time. That is why siblings look related but not identical.

Some features you can check with your friends: can you roll your tongue into a tube? Do your earlobes hang free or are they attached? Do you have dimples? What is your natural eye colour and hair type? Your blood group, A, B, AB or O, is inherited too.

And remember: all humans share almost all of their instructions. The differences we see on the outside are tiny compared with what we have in common.

Keep the family photo in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: The Measuring Wall

Let's investigate variation in a Grade 7 class, like real scientists.

First, a clear question: how does height vary in our class?

Second, measure fairly. Everyone takes their shoes off, stands with their back flat against the wall, heels together, looking straight ahead. Lay a book flat on the head, mark the wall, and read the measurement at eye level. Do it the same way for everyone.

Third, be kind and fair. Everyone must agree to be measured. Use numbers instead of names in your table. Nobody's height is a joke.

Fourth, write results in a neat table with a heading and units, centimetres.

For tongue rolling, just make a tally: can or cannot.

Hold the measuring wall in mind and try this part's questions.

## Subtopic: Drawing the Picture

Now turn the numbers into a picture.

For height, sort everyone into groups: 140 to 144 centimetres, 145 to 149, 150 to 154 and so on. Count how many are in each group. Draw bars that touch each other, because height changes smoothly. You will probably see a hill, with most learners in the middle.

For tongue rolling, draw two separate bars with a gap: can and cannot. Separate groups, separate bars.

You can also work out percentages. If 21 out of 30 can roll their tongues, that is 21 divided by 30, which is 0,7, or 70 per cent.

Then write a conclusion that answers your question using only your data. Most of our class is between 145 and 154 centimetres tall: height shows continuous variation. Do not claim more than the data show.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Humans inherit genes from both parents, so each child gets a unique mix. Inherited features include height, eye colour, hair type, blood group, tongue rolling and earlobe shape. To survey variation, ask a clear question, measure fairly, respect people's privacy, and record in a table. Continuous data like height go on a histogram with touching bars; groups like blood type go on a bar graph with gaps.

Hold the family photo, the measuring wall and the class graph, and take the final questions of the lesson.
