### subtopic_1 — Inherited Features in Humans
**Assistant:** If all humans share almost all their genes, why do people look so different?

**Tutor:** Because a very small proportion of differences in the genetic instructions can have very visible effects, and because the features we notice most, such as face shape, hair and skin colour, are on the outside, where small changes are easy to see. Any two humans share about ninety-nine point nine per cent of their DNA sequence. The remaining small fraction still amounts to millions of individual differences spread across the genetic material, because the whole set of instructions is enormous, about three thousand million units long. Some of these differences affect how much of the pigment melanin is made in the skin, hair and eyes, or the shape of facial bones and the texture of hair. These are easy to notice, but they are a tiny part of what makes a human body. Interestingly, studies have found that there is more genetic variation among people living within Africa than among all other human populations combined. This is because modern humans originated in Africa, and populations here have had the longest time to accumulate differences, while the groups that migrated out of Africa carried only part of that variation with them. South Africa is home to some of the most genetically diverse populations on Earth, including San and Khoe communities whose ancestry goes back a very long way. This scientific evidence matters socially too: it shows that dividing people into a few biological races does not match how human genetic variation is actually distributed.

### subtopic_2 — Planning a Variation Survey
**Assistant:** Why is a survey of one class less reliable than a survey of the whole school?

**Tutor:** Because a small sample is much more affected by chance, and a small group may not represent the larger population you want to describe. Suppose you want to know what proportion of Grade 7 learners can roll their tongues. In a class of thirty, a few unusual results, such as a set of twins or three cousins who share the trait, can shift the percentage by ten per cent or more. In a sample of three hundred, those same few people make only a small difference, so random chance tends to even out and the result is closer to the true value. The sample should also be representative, meaning it should be like the whole population in the ways that matter. Measuring height only in the school's basketball team would give an average that is far too tall; measuring only Grade 7s cannot tell you about Grade 12s, because age affects height; and a class that is mostly girls or mostly boys might not represent a mixed population, since boys and girls grow at different times during puberty. Scientists therefore try to use large samples chosen fairly, report how many people they studied, and treat conclusions from small samples with caution. A good habit is to compare results: if two classes give very different percentages, that is a sign that the samples are too small or not representative, and more data are needed before drawing a firm conclusion.

### subtopic_3 — Graphs and Conclusions
**Assistant:** Why must the bars of a histogram touch, while bar graph bars have gaps?

**Tutor:** Because the gaps, or the lack of them, tell the reader what kind of data is being shown. In a histogram of height, the class intervals follow on from each other with no gaps: someone who is 144,9 centimetres tall is in the 140 to 144 group, and someone who is 145 centimetres tall is in the next group, and every possible height between the shortest and tallest learner belongs to some interval. Height is continuous; it can take any value. Drawing the bars touching shows that the scale is continuous and that the intervals cover the whole range without breaks. A bar graph of blood groups shows categories that are completely separate: there is nothing between group A and group B, and the order of the categories along the axis does not even matter, because O is not bigger or smaller than A. The gaps between the bars show that the categories are distinct. If you drew the blood groups touching, a reader might think there was some in-between value; if you drew the height bars with gaps, a reader might think some heights were missing. This is part of a bigger idea: the type of graph should match the type of data. Using the right graph lets anyone see at a glance whether a feature shows continuous or discontinuous variation, which is exactly the distinction this topic is about.
