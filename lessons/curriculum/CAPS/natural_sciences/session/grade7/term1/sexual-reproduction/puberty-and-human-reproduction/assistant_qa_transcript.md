### subtopic_1 — Puberty
**Assistant:** Why does puberty start at different ages for different people?

**Tutor:** Because the timing is controlled by a combination of inherited factors and the body's condition, and these differ from person to person. Inheritance plays a large part: children tend to start puberty at roughly similar ages to their parents, so if a parent was an early or late developer, the child may be too. Body condition matters as well. The brain seems to check whether the body has enough energy reserves to support the big changes of puberty and, eventually, reproduction, so good nutrition tends to bring puberty earlier, while undernourishment, serious illness or very intense physical training can delay it. This is one reason why, across the world, the average age of first menstruation has fallen over the past hundred and fifty years as nutrition and health have improved. Girls, on average, begin puberty about one to two years before boys, which is why in Grade 6 and 7 many girls are taller than boys of the same age, and why boys often catch up and overtake them later. Within these averages the range is wide: starting puberty at eight or at thirteen can both be normal for a girl, and at nine or at fourteen for a boy. Comparing yourself with friends can cause unnecessary worry. A health worker should be consulted only if there are no signs of puberty at all by about thirteen in girls or fourteen in boys, or if it starts very early, because a doctor can then check that hormones are working properly.

### subtopic_2 — The Reproductive Organs
**Assistant:** Why are sperm made in millions when only one sperm is needed to fertilise an egg?

**Tutor:** Because the journey from the vagina to the egg is long and difficult, and only a tiny fraction of sperm survive it. Each release of semen contains roughly forty million to several hundred million sperm. Many are killed by the slightly acidic conditions of the vagina, which protect the female body against infection. Many others leak out or swim in the wrong direction. Of those that pass through the cervix into the uterus, a large proportion are destroyed by the body's white blood cells, which treat them as foreign cells. Sperm must then find the correct oviduct, since an egg is usually released into only one, and swim against the movement of its lining. Measured against its own size, a sperm cell's journey of about fifteen to twenty centimetres is like a person swimming several kilometres. Scientists estimate that only a few hundred sperm, out of the many millions released, reach the egg. Several are needed even then, because the egg is surrounded by a protective layer of cells and a coat that the sperm must break through using chemicals released from their heads; once one sperm enters the egg, the egg's coat changes immediately so that no other sperm can enter. So producing huge numbers is the male body's way of making sure that at least one sperm succeeds. This also explains why fertility can be reduced if the sperm count is low or if many sperm are poor swimmers.

### subtopic_3 — Menstruation, Fertilisation and Pregnancy
**Assistant:** Why is drinking alcohol during pregnancy so harmful, even in small amounts?

**Tutor:** Because alcohol passes easily from the mother's blood through the placenta into the baby's blood, and the developing baby cannot break it down well. An adult's liver contains chemicals that gradually remove alcohol from the blood, but a foetus's liver is immature, so the alcohol stays in its body for longer, at levels similar to or higher than in the mother's blood. The organ most affected is the developing brain, which grows throughout pregnancy. Alcohol interferes with how brain cells form, move into position and connect, and the damage is permanent. The result can be foetal alcohol spectrum disorder, which ranges from mild learning and behaviour difficulties to the severe form, foetal alcohol syndrome, with a smaller head, particular facial features, poor growth, and lifelong problems with learning, memory, attention and controlling behaviour. There is no cure, but it is completely preventable. Studies in parts of the Western Cape and Northern Cape have found some of the highest rates of foetal alcohol syndrome in the world, linked partly to the history of the dop system, under which some farm workers were paid partly in wine, and to continuing patterns of heavy drinking. Because damage can happen early, sometimes before a woman knows she is pregnant, and because no amount of alcohol has been shown to be safe, health authorities advise not drinking at all during pregnancy or when trying to become pregnant. Partners, families and communities can help by supporting pregnant women to avoid alcohol.
