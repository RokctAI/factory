# Part 1 — Expert

This session deals with how people can prevent unplanned pregnancy and sexually transmitted infections, and why delaying sexual activity protects young people's health and futures. The anchor is a South African school clinic day, where a nurse answers learners' questions honestly: how HIV is and is not passed on, what a condom does, where to get tested, and who to talk to. The governing idea is that abstaining from sex is the only completely effective way to avoid pregnancy and sexually transmitted infections; that for people who are sexually active, condoms used correctly every time greatly reduce the risk of both; that HIV is a virus that attacks the immune system and can be prevented, tested for and treated; and that early sexual activity carries serious health, emotional and social consequences. These facts are examinable and, more importantly, they protect lives. The content is presented factually and without judgement.

## Subtopic: Contraception

Contraception means any method used to prevent pregnancy. Pregnancy happens when a sperm cell fertilises an egg cell, so contraceptive methods work either by stopping sperm from reaching the egg or by stopping an egg from being released or implanting.

Abstinence, choosing not to have sexual intercourse, is the only method that is completely effective against both pregnancy and sexually transmitted infections. Many young people choose to delay sex until they are older, in a committed relationship, and ready for the responsibilities involved, and this is a valid and healthy choice.

The male condom is a thin sheath, usually made of latex rubber, rolled onto an erect penis before any sexual contact. It collects semen and stops sperm entering the vagina, and it also forms a barrier that stops the exchange of body fluids, so it reduces the risk of sexually transmitted infections, including HIV. A female condom, a soft pouch placed inside the vagina, works in the same way. Condoms are the only contraceptive method that also protects against infections. To work, condoms must be used correctly every time, from start to finish: checked for the expiry date and damage, opened carefully without teeth or scissors, put on before any contact, used only once, and never combined with oil-based lubricants such as petroleum jelly, which weaken latex. Condoms are available free at public clinics and in many public places in South Africa.

Other methods, provided by clinics, include hormonal methods such as the pill, injections and implants, which stop the ovaries releasing eggs; and intra-uterine devices placed in the uterus by a health worker. These are very effective against pregnancy but give no protection against sexually transmitted infections, so health workers recommend dual protection: a condom together with another method. Emergency contraception, sometimes called the morning-after pill, can prevent pregnancy if taken as soon as possible after unprotected sex, ideally within seventy-two hours, and is available at clinics and pharmacies; it is not meant for regular use.

In South Africa, young people have the right to confidential advice and contraception at public clinics from the age of twelve.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of contraception, how condoms work and must be used, and why dual protection is recommended.

## Subtopic: HIV, AIDS and Other STIs

Sexually transmitted infections, or STIs, also called sexually transmitted diseases or STDs, are infections passed from one person to another mainly through sexual contact. Common examples are chlamydia, gonorrhoea and syphilis, caused by bacteria and curable with antibiotics if treated early; and herpes, genital warts caused by the human papillomavirus, hepatitis B and HIV, caused by viruses. Many STIs have no symptoms at first, so a person can be infected and pass on the infection without knowing it. Untreated STIs can cause pain, infertility, harm to babies during pregnancy and birth, and they make HIV infection more likely. Anyone with sores, unusual discharge or pain when urinating should go to a clinic, where treatment is free and confidential. Girls in Grade 5 at public schools are offered a vaccine against the human papillomavirus, which protects against cervical cancer later in life.

HIV, the human immunodeficiency virus, attacks the immune system, the body's defence against disease. It infects and destroys particular white blood cells called CD4 cells, which coordinate the body's defences. Over years, if untreated, the number of CD4 cells falls so low that the body cannot fight off infections such as tuberculosis and pneumonia, which it would normally control. This late stage is called AIDS, acquired immune deficiency syndrome. HIV and AIDS are not the same thing: HIV is the virus; AIDS is the condition it can cause if untreated.

HIV is found in blood, semen, vaginal fluids and breast milk of an infected person. It is passed on through unprotected sexual intercourse, which is the most common route in South Africa; through sharing needles or other sharp objects contaminated with blood; from mother to baby during pregnancy, birth or breastfeeding if the mother is not on treatment; and, very rarely now, through contaminated blood products. HIV is not passed on by hugging, shaking hands, sharing cups or plates, toilet seats, swimming pools, coughing, sneezing, tears, sweat or mosquito bites. People living with HIV should never be treated differently or excluded; stigma harms people and discourages testing.

The only way to know one's HIV status is to test. Testing is free and confidential at clinics, and self-testing kits are available. HIV cannot be cured, but antiretroviral therapy, or ART, medicine taken every day, stops the virus multiplying, protects the immune system and allows people to live long, healthy lives. When treatment reduces the virus in the blood to undetectable levels, the person does not pass HIV on through sex. South Africa has the largest HIV treatment programme in the world. Treatment of pregnant mothers has made it possible for the great majority of babies born to mothers living with HIV to be born free of HIV.

The questions for this section are with you now: what an STI is, the difference between HIV and AIDS, how HIV is and is not transmitted, and how it is prevented and treated.

## Subtopic: Consequences of Early Sexual Activity

Early sexual activity, meaning sexual activity while still a child or young teenager, carries risks that go beyond biology.

Physical risks include unplanned pregnancy and STIs, including HIV. Young girls' bodies are still growing, and pregnancy and childbirth in early adolescence carry higher risks of complications for both mother and baby. Young people are also less likely to use protection consistently.

Emotional consequences can include regret, anxiety, guilt, damaged self-esteem and pressure in relationships. Young teenagers may feel pushed into sex before they are ready, by partners, friends or social media, and sex used to keep a relationship or gain status rarely makes people feel valued.

Social and educational consequences can include leaving school early. Teenage pregnancy is a major reason why girls in South Africa do not finish school, which affects their future work and income and that of their children. Young fathers also face responsibilities they are not ready for.

There are also legal facts. In South Africa the age of consent to sexual activity is sixteen. Any sexual activity with a child under twelve is always a crime. Adults who pressure or force children into sexual activity commit serious crimes, and relationships between children and much older adults are exploitative. Nobody ever has the right to touch someone without consent, and anyone who is abused is never to blame. Help is available from trusted adults, teachers, school counsellors, clinic nurses, the police, and Childline South Africa, a free helpline on 116.

Making good decisions is a skill. It helps to know the facts, to decide on personal values in advance, to choose friends who respect those values, and to practise ways of saying no clearly. Delaying sexual activity keeps future options open.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: thinking you cannot get pregnant the first time; you can. Two: thinking the pill protects against HIV; only condoms and abstinence protect against STIs. Three: believing HIV spreads by hugging, sharing cups or mosquitoes; it does not. Four: confusing HIV and AIDS. Five: thinking a person who looks healthy cannot have HIV or another STI; many have no symptoms, so testing is the only way to know.

The questions for this section are with you now: the physical, emotional and social consequences of early sexual activity, the age of consent, and where to get help.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a clinic visit, a body's security guards and some straight answers — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Preventing Pregnancy

Contraception means ways to stop a pregnancy from starting.

The only way that works one hundred per cent of the time is not having sex at all. That is called abstinence, and many young people choose to wait until they are older and ready. That is a smart, healthy choice.

A condom is a thin rubber cover that stops sperm reaching the egg. It is also a barrier against infections, which makes it the only contraceptive that protects against HIV and other sexual infections. But it only works if it is used properly every single time, from the very start. Condoms are free at clinics.

Clinics also offer other methods, like the pill, injections and implants. These stop pregnancy well, but they do not stop infections. So nurses suggest using a condom as well.

In South Africa, young people can get confidential advice at a clinic from the age of twelve.

## Subtopic: The Body's Security Guards

Your body has security guards: white blood cells that fight germs. HIV is a virus that attacks those guards. Little by little, if it is not treated, it knocks out so many guards that the body cannot fight off other illnesses like TB. That late stage is called AIDS. So HIV is the virus, and AIDS is what can happen years later without treatment.

HIV lives in blood, semen, vaginal fluid and breast milk. It spreads through sex without a condom, through sharing needles, and from a mother to her baby if she is not on treatment.

It does not spread through hugging, holding hands, sharing a cup or a plate, toilet seats, swimming pools, sweat, tears or mosquito bites. So people living with HIV should be treated just like everyone else.

You cannot tell if someone has HIV by looking at them. The only way to know is a test, which is free and private at clinics. There is no cure yet, but daily medicine called ART keeps the virus down so people can live long, healthy lives.

## Subtopic: Worth Waiting For

Starting sex very young comes with real risks. There is the chance of pregnancy and of infections. A young girl's body is still growing, so pregnancy can be dangerous. Many girls who fall pregnant leave school, which changes their whole future. And people often feel regret, worry or pressure afterwards.

The law protects young people. In South Africa, the age of consent is sixteen. Nobody is ever allowed to touch you without your permission, and if someone abuses you, it is never your fault. Tell a trusted adult, a teacher, a nurse or the police, or call Childline on 116 for free.

Saying no is a skill. Decide what you want before you are in a tricky spot. Use clear words: no, I'm not ready. Choose friends who respect you. Anyone who keeps pushing is not respecting you.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Contraception prevents pregnancy; abstinence is the only completely safe choice, and condoms, used correctly every time, protect against both pregnancy and infections. HIV attacks the immune system, and without treatment can lead to AIDS. It spreads through sex, blood and from mother to baby, not through everyday contact. Testing is free, and treatment keeps people healthy. Delaying sex protects your health and your future.
