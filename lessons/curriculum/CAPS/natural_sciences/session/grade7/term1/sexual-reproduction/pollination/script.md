# Part 1 — Expert

This session follows pollen on its journey from anther to stigma. The anchor is an apple orchard in the Elgin valley of the Western Cape in spring, where farmers hire beekeepers to bring hives into the orchard, because without bees carrying pollen from tree to tree there would be almost no apples. The governing idea is that pollination is the transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma, that it can be carried by wind, water or animals, and that flowers show clear adaptations to their method of pollination. Self-pollination and cross-pollination, the adaptations of wind-pollinated and animal-pollinated flowers, and the importance of pollinators for food crops are examinable, and they lead into fertilisation in the next lesson.

## Subtopic: What Pollination Is

Pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from an anther to a stigma. It must happen before fertilisation can take place, because the male gametes are inside the pollen and must reach the female part of a flower of the same species. Pollen landing on the stigma of a different species normally fails.

There are two types of pollination. Self-pollination is the transfer of pollen from an anther to the stigma of the same flower, or of another flower on the same plant. It is reliable, because the plant does not depend on outside help, but the offspring are very similar to the parent and to each other. Peas, beans and wheat often self-pollinate. Cross-pollination is the transfer of pollen from the anther of one plant to the stigma of a flower on another plant of the same species. It depends on an agent, such as wind or an animal, to carry the pollen, and much pollen is wasted, but it mixes the genetic material of two parents and so produces more varied offspring, which helps a species survive changing conditions and disease.

Many plants have features that encourage cross-pollination: separate male and female flowers, as in mealies; separate male and female plants, as in the marula; anthers and stigmas that ripen at different times; or chemical systems that reject a plant's own pollen. Many apple varieties cannot set fruit with their own pollen at all, which is why orchards plant rows of two or more varieties together and bring in bees to move pollen between them.

Pollen is moved by three main agents: wind, water and animals. Each is matched by a particular set of flower features.

## Subtopic: Wind, Water and Animal Pollination

Wind-pollinated flowers are adapted to release and catch pollen in moving air. Their petals are small, dull and often absent, because there are no animals to attract, and they have no scent and no nectar. The anthers are large and hang outside the flower on long, loose filaments, so the wind shakes pollen out of them. They produce enormous quantities of small, light, smooth, dry pollen, because most of it will be lost. The stigmas are large, feathery or sticky and hang outside the flower to comb pollen from the air. Grasses, mealies, wheat, sorghum and many trees such as pines are wind-pollinated. A single mealie tassel can release millions of pollen grains.

Water pollination is rare. A few plants that live completely underwater, such as some pondweeds and seagrasses along the coast, release pollen that floats or drifts through the water to the stigmas of other plants.

Animal-pollinated flowers are adapted to attract a particular kind of animal and to make sure it carries pollen. They usually have large, brightly coloured petals, often with nectar guides; scent; and nectar, a sugary reward, at the base of the flower. Their pollen is larger, sticky or spiky, and produced in smaller amounts, because animals carry it accurately. The anthers and stigma are usually inside the flower, placed so that a visitor brushes against them while reaching for nectar: pollen is dusted onto the animal's body at one flower and rubbed off onto the stigma at the next.

Different animals visit different flowers. Bees, the most important pollinators, favour blue, purple, yellow and white flowers with a landing platform and a sweet scent. Butterflies favour brightly coloured flowers with long tubes, reached with their long tongues. Moths favour white, strongly scented flowers that open at night. Birds such as sunbirds and Cape sugarbirds favour red or orange, tube-shaped flowers with lots of nectar and little scent; proteas, aloes, ericas and the strelitzia, whose bird-perch petal opens to dust the bird's feet with pollen, are bird-pollinated. Some South African plants are pollinated by beetles, flies, bats or even mice and gerbils: certain low-growing proteas produce yeasty-smelling flowers near the ground that are visited at night by small mammals.

## Subtopic: Pollinators and Our Food

Pollinators matter to people because a large part of our food depends on them. Scientists estimate that about three quarters of the world's main food crops benefit from animal pollination, and around a third of the food we eat comes from crops that need pollinators to give a good yield. Grains such as maize, wheat and rice are wind-pollinated, so they provide most of our energy; but most fruits, many vegetables, nuts, oil seeds and seeds for animal feed depend heavily on insects, mainly bees.

South African examples are plentiful. Apples and pears in the Elgin and Ceres valleys, macadamia nuts in Mpumalanga and Limpopo, avocados, citrus, litchis, sunflowers, pumpkins, butternuts, watermelons, canola and lucerne seed all depend on bees. Deciduous fruit farmers rent tens of thousands of hives each spring. Honey itself, such as fynbos or eucalyptus honey, is a product of bees feeding on nectar.

Pollinators are under pressure worldwide. Threats include the loss of the wild flowers and nesting places they depend on when land is cleared, pesticides that kill or weaken bees, diseases and parasites such as the varroa mite, which reached South Africa in the late 1990s, and climate change, which can shift flowering times so that flowers and pollinators are no longer active together. South Africa has two native honeybee subspecies, the Cape honeybee and the African honeybee, as well as hundreds of species of solitary wild bees that also pollinate crops.

People can help by planting indigenous flowering plants, leaving patches of wild vegetation on farms, using pesticides carefully and not spraying crops while bees are foraging, and supporting beekeepers.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: confusing pollination with fertilisation; pollination is the transfer of pollen, fertilisation is the joining of gametes. Two: saying pollen moves from stigma to anther; it moves from anther to stigma. Three: thinking only bees pollinate; wind, birds, butterflies, moths, beetles and even mammals do too. Four: describing wind-pollinated flowers as brightly coloured; they are usually dull. Five: thinking pollinators matter only for flowers in gardens; much of our food depends on them.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a delivery service, three kinds of courier and an orchard full of hired bees — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Pollen Delivery Service

Pollen has a job to do: it must get from the anther, where it is made, to a stigma, the sticky landing pad. That trip is called pollination.

Sometimes the pollen only travels a short way, to the stigma of the same flower or another flower on the same plant. That is self-pollination. It is easy, but the baby plants all turn out almost exactly like the parent.

Sometimes the pollen travels to a flower on a different plant. That is cross-pollination. It is harder and lots of pollen gets lost, but the baby plants are a mix of two parents, so they are more varied. Variety is useful: if a disease comes along, some of the mixed babies may be able to survive it.

Pollen cannot walk or fly by itself, so it needs a courier: the wind, water, or an animal.

## Subtopic: Three Kinds of Courier

Flowers are designed for their courier.

Wind flowers do not bother to look pretty. Who would they impress? They have tiny or no petals, no smell and no nectar. Their anthers dangle out in the breeze and puff out clouds of light, dusty pollen. Their stigmas are like feather dusters hanging out to catch it. Grass and mealies work this way.

Animal flowers are all about advertising. Bright petals, nice smells and a sweet drink of nectar as a reward. While a bee or bird pushes in for the nectar, sticky pollen rubs onto its body, and at the next flower some rubs off onto the stigma.

Different animals like different flowers. Bees love blue, yellow and purple. Birds like sunbirds love red and orange tubes full of nectar, like aloes and proteas. Moths come at night to white flowers with a strong smell. The strelitzia even has a perch: when a bird stands on it, the flower opens and dusts the bird's feet with pollen.

## Subtopic: Hire a Bee

In spring, apple farmers in the Western Cape do something surprising: they rent beehives. Trucks carry hives into the orchards, because without bees moving pollen from tree to tree, there would be almost no apples.

Bees and other pollinators help produce about one third of the food we eat: apples, pears, avocados, pumpkins, butternuts, sunflowers and macadamia nuts. Maize and wheat use the wind, so they would still grow, but without pollinators our plates would be very boring and much more expensive.

Bees are in trouble in many places because of pesticides, diseases, a tiny mite that attacks hives, and the loss of wild flowers. We can help by planting indigenous flowers, leaving wild patches on farms and not spraying while bees are busy.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Pollination moves pollen from an anther to a stigma. Self-pollination happens on the same plant; cross-pollination moves pollen to another plant and gives more varied offspring. Wind flowers are dull, with dangling anthers, light pollen and feathery stigmas. Animal flowers are bright and scented, with nectar and sticky pollen. Pollinators, especially bees, are vital for much of our food.
