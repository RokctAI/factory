### subtopic_1 — What Pollination Is
**Assistant:** If self-pollination is reliable, why do so many plants go to such trouble to cross-pollinate?

**Tutor:** Because the long-term advantage of variation usually outweighs the short-term convenience of reliability. Self-pollinated offspring receive their genetic instructions from one parent only, so they are very similar. If a new disease, pest or change in climate arrives that the parent cannot cope with, the offspring are likely to share the same weakness, and a whole population can be wiped out together. Cross-pollinated offspring receive a mixture from two different parents, so each seedling is a slightly different combination. Some will cope better with heat, some with drought, some with a particular fungus, and the chance that at least some survive is much higher. Repeated self-pollination over many generations can also bring together harmful inherited features that are hidden when they come from only one parent, leading to weaker plants, a problem called inbreeding depression. That said, self-pollination is not a mistake. It is very useful for plants that live where pollinators are scarce, or for weeds and crops that need to reproduce quickly and reliably, and many plants can do both: they are built for cross-pollination but will self-pollinate if no pollen arrives from another plant before the flower closes. Farmers use both. Wheat varieties are kept true by self-pollination, while hybrid maize seed is produced by deliberately cross-pollinating two chosen varieties, which gives stronger, higher-yielding plants for one season.

### subtopic_2 — Wind, Water and Animal Pollination
**Assistant:** How did the strelitzia flower get the nickname bird-of-paradise, and how does it use birds?

**Tutor:** The strelitzia's flower looks remarkably like the head of a brightly crested bird, with an orange crest of sepals and a blue beak-like structure sticking out of a stiff green sheath, which is why it is called the bird-of-paradise flower. It grows naturally in the Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal and is now planted in gardens all over the world. The flower is a clever machine for using birds. The blue structure is made of two petals joined together, enclosing the anthers and with the style running along it, and it forms a firm perch. Birds, especially the Cape weaver and various sunbirds in the wild, land on this perch to reach the nectar at the base of the flower. Under the bird's weight, the blue petals spread apart and the anthers spring out, dusting pollen onto the bird's feet and belly. When the bird visits another strelitzia flower, the stigma, which sticks out at the tip of the perch, is the first thing its feet touch, so pollen is transferred. The bright orange and blue colours suit bird vision, the flower holds a large amount of nectar for a bird-sized visitor, and it has almost no scent, because birds rely on sight rather than smell. When strelitzias are grown in countries without suitable birds, they often set few or no seeds unless gardeners pollinate them by hand, which shows how closely the flower is matched to its partners.

### subtopic_3 — Pollinators and Our Food
**Assistant:** If bees were to disappear, would people starve?

**Tutor:** People would not all starve, but the world's diet would become much poorer, more expensive and less healthy. The main energy crops, maize, wheat, rice, sorghum and the other cereals, are pollinated by wind, so the basic supply of starch would continue. Root crops such as potatoes and cassava are grown from pieces of the plant rather than seed, so they would also survive. What would collapse is much of the variety. Most fruits, such as apples, pears, cherries, avocados and berries, many vegetables such as pumpkins, butternuts, cucumbers and melons, nuts such as macadamias and almonds, oil seeds such as sunflower and canola, and coffee and cocoa depend partly or entirely on insect pollinators, mostly bees. Yields would fall dramatically and prices would soar. Animal feed such as lucerne seed would be harder to grow, affecting milk and meat. These crops provide many of the vitamins, minerals and healthy fats in our diet, so nutrition would suffer even if people still had enough calories. People can pollinate by hand, and in parts of south-western China, where wild bees declined after heavy pesticide use, workers have pollinated pear and apple trees with brushes, flower by flower, which shows how slow and costly the alternative is. Other insects, birds and wild bees would partly fill the gap, which is one reason to protect them too. So bees and other pollinators are not optional extras; they are part of the system that feeds us.
