### subtopic_1 — Sexual Reproduction and the Flower
**Assistant:** Why do some flowers have markings that people cannot see?

**Tutor:** Because many pollinating insects, especially bees, see light that human eyes cannot detect. Bees cannot see red well, but they can see ultraviolet light, which is invisible to us. Many flowers have patterns on their petals that reflect or absorb ultraviolet light, forming lines, spots or a dark centre that bees see clearly. These patterns are nectar guides: they point the way to the nectar and pollen at the centre of the flower, like landing lights on a runway. When scientists photograph flowers with cameras sensitive to ultraviolet light, a plain yellow flower such as a buttercup or some daisies can be revealed to have a striking bull's-eye pattern. This is a good example of how flowers are adapted to their pollinators rather than to us. Flowers pollinated by birds, such as the sunbirds and sugarbirds of South Africa, are often red or orange, because birds see red well, and these flowers usually have little scent, because birds have a weak sense of smell. Flowers pollinated at night by moths are often white or pale, so they show up in dim light, and they have a strong sweet scent. Flowers pollinated by flies, such as the carrion flowers of the Karoo, can smell like rotting meat. Studying the colour, scent and shape of a flower therefore lets a scientist predict which animal probably pollinates it, before ever seeing the visit.

### subtopic_2 — The Male Parts: Stamens
**Assistant:** How do scientists use pollen grains to learn about the past?

**Tutor:** Because pollen grains have extremely tough outer walls that can survive for thousands or even millions of years in the right conditions, and because each kind of plant produces pollen with its own recognisable shape. When pollen falls onto a lake, a swamp or a peat bog, it sinks into the mud and is buried layer upon layer, with the oldest layers at the bottom. Scientists drill out long cylinders of this mud, called cores, and examine samples from different depths under the microscope, counting the pollen grains of each type. Because each layer can be dated, the pollen tells them which plants grew in the area at each time. In South Africa, cores from places such as the Wonderkrater spring in Limpopo and wetlands in KwaZulu-Natal show how vegetation changed between grassland, forest and bushland over tens of thousands of years, which tells us how the climate changed, since forests need more rain than grassland. Pollen is also used in police work: pollen on clothing, shoes or vehicles can show where a person or object has been, because different places have different plants. And pollen in honey reveals which flowers the bees visited, which helps to check where honey truly came from. All this is possible because of the two features of pollen learned in this lesson: its tough wall, which protects the male gametes, and its distinct shape for each plant.

### subtopic_3 — The Female Parts: the Carpel
**Assistant:** Why do some plants have separate male and female flowers instead of both in one?

**Tutor:** Mainly to encourage cross-pollination, in which pollen travels from one plant to another, rather than self-pollination, in which a flower's pollen reaches its own stigma or another flower on the same plant. Cross-pollination mixes the genetic instructions of two different parents, so the offspring vary more. Variation is valuable because, if conditions change or a new disease appears, some of the varied offspring are likely to survive. Repeated self-pollination, by contrast, can lead over generations to weaker offspring in many species. Plants have evolved several ways to promote cross-pollination. Separating the sexes into different flowers, as in mealies and pumpkins, makes it less likely that pollen lands on the same flower, though it can still reach another flower on the same plant. Separating them onto different plants, as in the marula, where only female trees bear fruit, guarantees cross-pollination, but means only half the plants produce seeds. Many bisexual flowers achieve the same result by timing: their anthers release pollen before or after their own stigma is ready to receive it. Others have chemical systems that reject their own pollen. Farmers use this knowledge. In producing hybrid maize seed, for example, the tassels of one variety are removed so that its cobs can only be pollinated by another variety planted nearby. So the arrangement of male and female parts is a strategy for producing healthy, varied offspring.
