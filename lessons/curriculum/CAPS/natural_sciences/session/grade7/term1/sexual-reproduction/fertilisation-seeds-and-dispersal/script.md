# Part 1 — Expert

This session follows what happens after a pollen grain lands on a stigma, all the way to a new seedling growing some distance from its parent. The anchor is a marula tree in the Lowveld in late summer: its flowers were pollinated months earlier, its fallen fruit now lies under the tree, and elephants, baboons and people carry the fruit away and drop the seeds far from where they grew. The governing idea is that fertilisation is the fusion of a male gamete with a female gamete, made possible by a pollen tube growing from the stigma to the ovule; that after fertilisation the ovule becomes a seed and the ovary becomes a fruit; and that seeds are dispersed by animals, wind, water and the plant itself to reduce competition and colonise new places. The stages of fertilisation, seed and fruit formation, and the methods of seed dispersal with their adaptations are examinable.

## Subtopic: The Pollen Tube and Fertilisation

Pollination brings pollen to the stigma, but the male gametes inside the pollen grain are still a long way from the female gamete, the egg cell, deep inside an ovule in the ovary. Fertilisation is the process that joins them.

When a pollen grain of the right species lands on a ripe stigma, the sugary liquid on the stigma stimulates it to germinate. The pollen grain grows a thin tube, the pollen tube, out through one of the pores in its wall. The tube grows down through the tissue of the stigma and the style, digesting a path as it goes, towards the ovary. The male gametes move down inside the tube, near its tip. The tube grows towards an ovule, guided by chemical signals from it, and enters the ovule through a tiny opening called the micropyle.

The tip of the pollen tube then bursts and releases the male gametes. One male gamete fuses with the egg cell; this joining is fertilisation. The cell formed by fertilisation is called a zygote, and it contains genetic instructions from both parents. In flowering plants a second male gamete also fuses with other cells in the ovule to form the tissue that will become the seed's food store, the endosperm; this is why scientists speak of double fertilisation in flowering plants, a feature found in no other group.

Each ovule needs its own pollen grain and its own pollen tube. A watermelon with hundreds of seeds needed hundreds of pollen grains to land and grow tubes, which is why poorly pollinated fruits are often small or misshapen: only the parts with fertilised ovules develop properly. The pollen tube can be several centimetres long; in a mealie, it must grow down the full length of the silk, which can exceed twenty centimetres.

The questions for this section are with you now: the path of the pollen tube, the definition of fertilisation, and why each seed needs its own pollen grain.

## Subtopic: Seed and Fruit Formation

After fertilisation the flower changes dramatically. The petals, stamens, stigma and style are no longer needed; they wither and usually fall off. Sometimes the sepals remain, as the little green star at the top of a tomato or the tuft on a strawberry shows.

Inside each fertilised ovule, the zygote divides many times to form the embryo, the young plant, with a tiny root called the radicle, a tiny shoot called the plumule, and one or two cotyledons. Food is stored in the cotyledons, as in beans, or in the endosperm, as in maize. The outer layers of the ovule harden into the seed coat, or testa, which protects the embryo. Water is removed until the seed is dry, and the embryo becomes dormant, able to survive long periods until conditions are right for germination. So each fertilised ovule becomes a seed. On many seeds a small scar, the hilum, shows where the seed was attached to the ovary wall, and the tiny hole of the micropyle remains beside it; both are easy to see on a soaked bean.

At the same time, the wall of the ovary grows and develops into the fruit, which surrounds and protects the seeds and helps disperse them. In a botanical sense a fruit is a ripened ovary containing seeds. Fruits are either fleshy or dry. Fleshy fruits, such as marula, tomato, grape, orange and pumpkin, have a juicy, often sweet wall. Dry fruits have a hard or papery wall: the bean pod, the acacia pod, the mealie grain, the sunflower seed in its striped husk, and the nut.

The relationship can be summarised: ovule becomes seed; ovary becomes fruit; ovary wall becomes the fruit wall; integuments of the ovule become the seed coat; zygote becomes the embryo.

Some fruits form without fertilisation, giving seedless fruit. Bananas and some seedless grapes and citrus are produced this way, and farmers grow them from cuttings rather than seeds.

The questions for this section are with you now: what happens to each part of the flower after fertilisation, the parts of a seed, and the difference between fleshy and dry fruits.

## Subtopic: Seed Dispersal

Seed dispersal is the spreading of seeds away from the parent plant. It matters because seedlings that germinate under the parent compete with it, and with each other, for light, water and minerals, and most would die. Dispersal also lets plants colonise new areas, such as a gap in a forest or ground cleared by fire, and spreads a species so that a local disaster cannot wipe it out. Fruits and seeds show clear adaptations to their method of dispersal.

Dispersal by animals happens in two ways. Fleshy, brightly coloured, sweet fruits are eaten by animals. The seeds have tough coats that survive the animal's digestion and are dropped in its droppings, often far from the parent and with a little fertiliser. Elephants, baboons, monkeys, birds and bats disperse marula, fig, wild plum and many other seeds this way. Other fruits have hooks, barbs or sticky surfaces that catch on fur, feathers or clothing; the blackjack seeds that cling to socks after a walk in the veld and the devil's claw of the Kalahari are examples. Some animals, such as squirrels and harvester ants, carry seeds away to store and forget some; many fynbos seeds have a fleshy attachment called an elaiosome that ants carry into their nests, where the seeds are safe from fire and seed-eaters.

Dispersal by wind uses light seeds or fruits with wings or hairs. Dandelions and many daisies have parachutes of fine hairs; the seeds of the pod mahogany and of pines have papery wings that spin as they fall, slowing descent so the wind carries them further. Very tiny seeds, such as those of orchids, are as light as dust.

Dispersal by water uses fruits that float. The coconut has a fibrous, air-filled husk and a waterproof coat and can drift for weeks across oceans; seeds of mangroves and of many river and wetland plants float and are carried downstream.

Self-dispersal, or mechanical dispersal, happens when a dry fruit bursts open as it dries, flinging seeds away from the parent. Pea and bean pods twist and split; the pods of some acacias and of the wild balsam explode. Some plants simply drop heavy fruits that roll.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: confusing pollination and fertilisation; pollen landing is pollination, gametes fusing is fertilisation. Two: saying the ovary becomes the seed; the ovule becomes the seed. Three: saying a tomato is not a fruit; in science it is. Four: thinking dispersal and pollination are the same; one moves pollen, the other moves seeds. Five: thinking seeds are dispersed to make the parent plant more comfortable; the main reason is to reduce competition and reach new places.

The questions for this section are with you now: why seeds are dispersed, the four methods of dispersal with adaptations and examples, and how ants protect some fynbos seeds.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a tunnel to the nursery, a packed seed and a marula travelling in an elephant — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Digging a Tunnel

When a pollen grain lands on the sticky stigma, the job is not finished. The male cell inside it still has to reach the egg cell, hidden deep in an ovule inside the ovary.

So the pollen grain does something amazing. It grows a long, thin tube, like a tunnel, down through the style. The male cells travel down inside the tunnel. When the tunnel reaches an ovule, it bursts open, and a male cell joins with the egg cell.

That joining is called fertilisation. It is different from pollination: pollination is the landing, fertilisation is the joining.

Every ovule needs its own pollen grain and its own tunnel. So a watermelon with hundreds of seeds needed hundreds of pollen grains. If bees did not bring enough, the melon grows lopsided, because only the fertilised bits grow properly.

Keep the tunnel in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: From Flower to Fruit

After fertilisation, the flower changes. The petals are not needed anymore, so they dry up and fall off. The stamens and stigma wither too.

Each fertilised ovule grows into a seed. Inside the seed is a baby plant with a tiny root and a tiny shoot, plus a food store, all wrapped in a tough seed coat. Soak a bean and you can see the little scar where it was attached inside the pod.

Meanwhile the ovary around the seeds swells into a fruit. Some fruits are juicy, like marulas, tomatoes and oranges. Some are dry, like bean pods, acacia pods and nuts.

Remember: in science a fruit is anything that grows from the ovary and holds seeds. So a tomato, a pumpkin and a cucumber are fruits, even though we cook them as vegetables.

Hold the flower-to-fruit change in mind and try this part's questions.

## Subtopic: Seeds on the Move

A seed that falls right under its parent has a hard life. The parent hogs the light and water, and all its brothers and sisters are crowded there too. So plants send their seeds away. That is seed dispersal.

Animals carry seeds. Elephants and baboons eat marula fruits and drop the seeds far away in their dung. Blackjacks hook onto your socks. Some fynbos seeds have a tasty bit that ants carry into their nests, where the seeds hide safely from fire.

The wind carries seeds. Dandelion seeds have parachutes; pine seeds have wings that spin like helicopters.

Water carries seeds. A coconut floats across the sea for weeks.

And some plants throw their own seeds. Bean pods dry, twist and pop, flinging their seeds out.

Pocket summary of the lesson. After pollination, the pollen grain grows a tube to an ovule, and a male gamete fuses with the egg cell: fertilisation. The ovule becomes a seed and the ovary becomes the fruit. Seeds are dispersed by animals, wind, water or the plant itself, to avoid competition and reach new places.

Hold the tunnel, the swelling ovary and the marula in the elephant, and take the final questions of the lesson.
