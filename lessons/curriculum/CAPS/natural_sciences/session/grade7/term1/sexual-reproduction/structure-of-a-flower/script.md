# Part 1 — Expert

This session opens the topic of sexual reproduction with the organ that flowering plants use to reproduce: the flower. The anchor is a single large flower that can be taken apart on a desk, such as a hibiscus from a school garden or a lily from a florist, alongside a king protea, South Africa's national flower, whose many small flowers are packed into one head. The governing idea is that a flower is a set of parts arranged in rings, each with a job in reproduction: sepals protect, petals attract, stamens produce pollen, and the carpel, made of stigma, style and ovary, receives pollen and contains the ovules that become seeds. The names, positions and functions of the flower parts, and the difference between the male and female parts, are examinable, and flower structure leads directly into pollination and fertilisation in the next lessons.

## Subtopic: Sexual Reproduction and the Flower

Sexual reproduction is reproduction that involves two sex cells, also called gametes, one male and one female, which join to form a new individual. Because the offspring receives a mixture of genetic instructions from two parents, it is different from both, which produces variation in a species. Flowering plants, the angiosperms, reproduce sexually using flowers. The male gametes are carried inside pollen grains, and the female gametes are inside ovules in the ovary.

A typical flower is built on a short stalk, the flower stalk, which widens at the top into a platform called the receptacle. The flower parts are attached to the receptacle in rings, or whorls, from the outside inwards: sepals, petals, stamens and carpels.

The sepals form the outermost ring. They are usually green and leaf-like, and their job is to protect the flower while it is a bud; when the bud opens they are often folded back beneath the petals. Together the sepals are called the calyx.

The petals form the next ring. In flowers pollinated by animals they are usually large, brightly coloured and often scented, and they may have markings called nectar guides that lead insects to the nectar at the base of the flower. Their main job is to attract pollinators. Together the petals are called the corolla. In wind-pollinated flowers, such as grasses, the petals are tiny or absent, because there is no need to attract animals.

A note on names: in some flowers, such as lilies, the sepals and petals look alike, and both are then called tepals.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of sexual reproduction and gamete, the order of the rings in a flower, and the functions of sepals and petals.

## Subtopic: The Male Parts: Stamens

The stamens are the male parts of the flower. Each stamen has two parts. The filament is a thin stalk that holds the anther up in a good position, often among the petals where a visiting insect will brush against it. The anther, at the top of the filament, is the part that makes pollen. An anther usually has four small sacs in which pollen grains develop; when the pollen is ripe, the anther splits open and releases it. The stamens of a flower together are sometimes called the androecium, meaning the male house.

Pollen grains are tiny, usually between a hundredth and a tenth of a millimetre across, and each contains male gametes. Pollen grains have a tough outer wall that protects the gametes from drying out on their journey to another flower. Under a microscope, pollen grains show beautiful patterns of spikes, ridges and pores, and the pattern is different for each kind of plant. Pollen from insect-pollinated flowers is often sticky or spiky so that it clings to insects; pollen from wind-pollinated flowers is light, smooth and dry, and produced in enormous quantities so that some reaches its target by chance. That light pollen is what causes hay fever in many people in spring.

The number of stamens varies: a lily has six, many dicot flowers have five or ten, and a hibiscus has dozens joined into a column around the style.

The questions for this section are with you now: the two parts of a stamen and their functions, and how pollen differs in insect-pollinated and wind-pollinated flowers.

## Subtopic: The Female Parts: the Carpel

The female part of the flower is the carpel, also called the pistil, found in the centre. A flower may have one carpel or several, sometimes joined together. Each carpel has three parts.

The stigma is at the top. It receives pollen, and it is adapted to catch and hold pollen grains: it is often sticky, hairy or feathery. In wind-pollinated grasses the stigmas are large and feathery and hang out of the flower to comb pollen from the air; in a mealie, the long silks of the cob are the styles and stigmas, one silk for each grain.

The style is the stalk that connects the stigma to the ovary and holds the stigma in a position where it will receive pollen.

The ovary is the swollen base of the carpel. Inside it are one or more ovules, each containing a female gamete, the egg cell. After fertilisation, each ovule develops into a seed and the ovary develops into the fruit. The number of seeds in a fruit shows how many ovules were fertilised: a pea pod with six peas came from an ovary with at least six ovules.

Many flowers also have nectaries, small glands near the base of the petals or ovary that produce nectar, a sugary liquid that rewards visiting animals.

Most flowers contain both stamens and carpels, so they are called bisexual or perfect flowers. Some plants have separate male and female flowers. A mealie plant carries male flowers in the tassel at the top and female flowers in the cob lower down; pumpkins have separate male and female flowers on the same vine; and in a few plants, such as the marula and many cycads, male and female structures are on separate plants.

The king protea is not one flower but a flower head: hundreds of small flowers packed together, surrounded by large, colourful bracts that look like petals. Each small flower has its own stamens and carpel.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: calling the anther the stigma; the anther makes pollen, the stigma receives it. Two: saying the ovary makes pollen; it contains ovules. Three: thinking all flowers have bright petals; wind-pollinated flowers usually do not. Four: saying the ovule becomes the fruit; the ovule becomes the seed, the ovary becomes the fruit. Five: calling the protea one big flower; it is a head of many small flowers.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three parts of the carpel and their functions, what the ovule and ovary become, and examples of plants with separate male and female flowers.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a flower as a house, a pollen factory and a landing pad — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Flower Has Rings

Pick a big flower, like a hibiscus, and take it apart gently, from the outside in.

First come the green bits underneath, the sepals. Before the flower opened, they wrapped around the bud like a jacket to protect it.

Next come the petals, the bright, colourful part. Their job is advertising. They say to bees, birds and butterflies: come here, there is food. Some petals even have lines that point the way to the sweet nectar, like arrows on a road.

Inside the petals are the parts that actually make baby plants. A flower is how a plant has babies, by joining a male cell and a female cell. Scientists call that sexual reproduction.

So from outside in: sepals that protect, petals that advertise, then the male parts, then the female parts in the very middle.

Keep the rings in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: The Pollen Factory

The male parts are called stamens. Each stamen looks like a little stick with a blob on top.

The stick is the filament. It holds the blob up high where an insect will brush against it.

The blob is the anther, and it is a pollen factory. Pollen is the yellow dust that comes off on your fingers or on your nose when you sniff a lily. Each grain of pollen carries the male cells.

Flowers that use insects make sticky, spiky pollen that clings to a bee's hairy body. Flowers that use the wind, like grass, make huge clouds of light, dusty pollen. That is the pollen that makes some people sneeze in spring.

Hold the pollen factory in mind and try this part's questions.

## Subtopic: The Landing Pad and the Nursery

The female part is in the very centre of the flower. It is called the carpel, and it has three parts.

On top is the stigma, the landing pad. It is sticky or hairy, so pollen grains that land on it get stuck there.

Below it is the style, a neck that holds the landing pad up.

At the bottom is the ovary, the nursery. Inside it are ovules, and each ovule holds a female egg cell. Later, each ovule will grow into a seed, and the whole ovary will swell up into a fruit. That is why a pea pod is full of peas: each pea was once an ovule.

Some plants keep their male and female parts in different flowers. On a mealie plant, the tassel at the top is male and the cob is female; the silky threads of the cob are its landing pads.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A flower has rings: sepals protect the bud, petals attract animals, stamens are the male parts, and the carpel is the female part. A stamen has a filament holding up an anther that makes pollen. The carpel has a sticky stigma that catches pollen, a style, and an ovary with ovules inside. Ovules become seeds and the ovary becomes the fruit.

Hold the jacket, the factory and the landing pad, and take the final questions of the lesson.
