### subtopic_1 — The Pollen Tube and Fertilisation
**Assistant:** How does a pollen tube know which way to grow to find an ovule?

**Tutor:** It follows chemical signals, rather like following a scent. Scientists have studied this closely using microscopes and plants such as the small weed Arabidopsis, which is easy to grow in laboratories. The first step happens on the stigma: the stigma recognises whether the pollen is from the right species, and in some plants whether it is the plant's own pollen, and only accepts suitable grains. Pollen from the wrong species usually fails to germinate or stops growing early. Once a tube is growing, it is guided through the style by a pathway of special cells and chemical substances that support and direct it. As it nears the ovules, special cells next to the egg cell release small proteins that act as attractants. The tip of the pollen tube can detect very small differences in the concentration of these signals and bends towards where they are strongest, so it homes in on an unfertilised ovule. After an ovule has been entered by one pollen tube, it stops sending the signal, so a second tube is usually not attracted to the same ovule and goes to another one instead. The growth itself is fast: in some plants a pollen tube grows a centimetre or more an hour, among the fastest-growing plant cells known. Understanding this guidance helps plant breeders who want to cross varieties or overcome barriers that stop some crop species from being crossed with their wild relatives.

### subtopic_2 — Seed and Fruit Formation
**Assistant:** If a fruit is a ripened ovary, why do we call rhubarb a fruit and tomato a vegetable?

**Tutor:** Because kitchen language and scientific language sort plants for different purposes. In everyday cooking, fruit usually means a sweet plant part eaten as a dessert or snack, and vegetable means a savoury plant part eaten with a meal. Science sorts by structure and origin instead: a fruit is the part that develops from the ovary of a flower and contains seeds. By that definition, tomatoes, cucumbers, pumpkins, butternuts, green peppers, brinjals, avocados, mealie grains, bean pods and peas in their pods are all fruits, even though we cook them as vegetables. Rhubarb, on the other hand, is a leaf stalk, so scientifically it is not a fruit at all, even though it is usually stewed with sugar and served as dessert. Vegetable is not a scientific word: it covers many different plant parts, such as roots like carrots and beetroot, stems like potatoes, which are underground stems, leaves like spinach and cabbage, flower buds like broccoli and cauliflower, and many botanical fruits. Neither system is wrong. A cook needs to know how a food tastes and how to prepare it; a botanist needs to know which part of the plant it is and how it formed. In a Natural Sciences answer, though, always use the scientific meaning: if it grew from the ovary and contains seeds, it is a fruit.

### subtopic_3 — Seed Dispersal
**Assistant:** Why do some seeds need to pass through an animal's gut, or a fire, before they will grow?

**Tutor:** Because their tough seed coats evolved to delay germination until the seed is in a good place at a good time, and passing through a gut or a fire is the signal that this has happened. Many seeds have hard, waterproof coats that stop water entering, so they stay dormant. In an animal's gut, acids and grinding scratch and soften the coat, a process called scarification, so that when the seed is dropped, water can enter and germination can begin. The seed also arrives in a pile of dung, often far from the parent. Seeds of some acacias, marula and other trees germinate much better after passing through animals such as elephants, antelope or birds. Fire works in a related way in fynbos. Many fynbos plants produce seeds that lie dormant in the soil or are held in closed cones on the plant for years. When a fire passes through, heat cracks seed coats, smoke contains chemicals that trigger germination, and the fire clears away old plants and releases nutrients in the ash, so the seeds germinate together with the first winter rains into open, sunny ground. Scientists at Kirstenbosch discovered that smoke alone could trigger germination in many fynbos species, and smoke-treated paper is now used to grow these plants. This is why fynbos needs fire at intervals of roughly ten to twenty years, and why fires that are too frequent, or prevented for too long, both threaten it.
