# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains how humans reproduce sexually and how the body prepares for it during puberty. The anchor is the ordinary experience of a Grade 7 class, in which some learners have already grown taller, noticed changes in their voices or bodies, or started menstruating, and others have not yet: all of this is normal, because puberty starts at different ages for different people. The governing idea is that puberty is the stage when hormones cause the reproductive organs to mature and the body to change; that the male reproductive system produces sperm cells and the female system produces egg cells and supports a developing baby; and that fertilisation, the fusion of a sperm cell and an egg cell, can lead to pregnancy. The changes of puberty, the main reproductive organs and their functions, the menstrual cycle, fertilisation and pregnancy are examinable. The content is taught with accurate scientific names, because accurate knowledge helps young people look after their health.

## Subtopic: Puberty

Puberty is the stage of development during which a child's body changes into an adult body capable of reproduction. It usually begins between about eight and thirteen years of age in girls and between about nine and fourteen in boys, and it takes several years to complete. The exact timing varies a great deal from person to person, and starting earlier or later than friends is normal.

Puberty is controlled by hormones, chemical messengers carried in the blood. A gland at the base of the brain, the pituitary gland, releases hormones that stimulate the reproductive organs. In girls, the ovaries then produce the hormone oestrogen; in boys, the testes produce the hormone testosterone. These hormones cause the changes of puberty.

Some changes happen in both girls and boys: a growth spurt, when height increases quickly; hair growing in the armpits and pubic area; more active sweat glands, so body odour increases and daily washing becomes more important; oilier skin, which can cause pimples, or acne; and emotional changes, including stronger feelings, mood swings and new interest in relationships.

Changes in girls include the development of breasts, the widening of the hips, and the start of menstruation, also called having a period, which shows that the ovaries have begun releasing egg cells. Changes in boys include the growth of facial and body hair, the deepening of the voice as the voice box grows, sometimes with a period of cracking, broadening shoulders and more muscle, growth of the penis and testes, and the start of sperm production, which can be shown by wet dreams, the release of semen during sleep, which is normal.

Puberty is a physical process; becoming an adult in a social and emotional sense takes longer. A body that can reproduce is not yet the body or the life of a person ready to be a parent. Many South African cultures mark this stage with initiation, coming-of-age ceremonies or celebrations, and families, teachers, school nurses and clinics can answer questions.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of puberty, the hormones involved, and the changes in girls, in boys and in both.

## Subtopic: The Reproductive Organs

The male reproductive system produces sperm cells, the male gametes, and delivers them into the female body. The two testes produce sperm cells and testosterone. They hang outside the body in a pouch of skin called the scrotum, because sperm develop best at a temperature slightly below normal body temperature. Sperm are produced continuously from puberty onwards, millions every day. Each testis connects to a sperm duct, a tube that carries sperm towards the penis. Along the way, glands, including the seminal vesicles and the prostate gland, add fluids that nourish the sperm and help them swim; sperm and fluid together are called semen. The urethra is the tube that runs through the penis and carries either urine or semen out of the body, never both at once. The penis carries semen into the female body during sexual intercourse.

A sperm cell is tiny, about five hundredths of a millimetre long, and consists of a head containing the father's genetic material, a middle section that releases energy, and a long tail that lashes to swim.

The female reproductive system produces egg cells, also called ova, receives sperm, and provides a place for a baby to develop. The two ovaries, each about the size of an almond, contain the egg cells, which are present from birth, and produce oestrogen and another hormone, progesterone. From puberty, usually one egg is released each month, a process called ovulation. Each ovary lies close to the open end of an oviduct, also called a fallopian tube, which carries the egg towards the uterus; fertilisation normally takes place in the oviduct. The uterus, or womb, is a hollow, muscular organ about the size of a pear, with a thick, blood-rich lining, where a fertilised egg can implant and develop into a baby. Its lower end is the cervix, a narrow neck that opens into the vagina. The vagina is a muscular tube leading to the outside of the body; it receives the penis during intercourse and forms the birth canal through which a baby is born. The external parts are called the vulva.

An egg cell is much larger than a sperm cell, about a tenth of a millimetre across, just visible to the eye, because it contains a store of food for the first days of development. It cannot move by itself; tiny hairs lining the oviduct sweep it along.

The questions for this section are with you now: the male and female reproductive organs and their functions, and the differences between sperm cells and egg cells.

## Subtopic: Menstruation, Fertilisation and Pregnancy

The menstrual cycle is a monthly cycle of changes in the female reproductive system, controlled by hormones, that prepares the body for a possible pregnancy. A cycle is usually about twenty-eight days long, although anything from about twenty-one to thirty-five days is normal, and cycles are often irregular in the first years after puberty. Day one is the first day of menstruation.

During menstruation, which lasts about three to seven days, the thickened lining of the uterus breaks down and passes out of the body through the vagina as menstrual blood. After menstruation, the lining begins to thicken again under the influence of oestrogen. Around the middle of the cycle, about day fourteen in a twenty-eight-day cycle, ovulation occurs: an egg is released from an ovary into an oviduct. The lining keeps thickening and becomes rich in blood vessels, ready to receive a fertilised egg. The egg can be fertilised only for about a day after ovulation, but sperm can survive in the female body for several days, so intercourse in the days before ovulation can also lead to pregnancy. If the egg is not fertilised, it dies, hormone levels fall, the lining breaks down, and menstruation begins again. Menstrual products such as pads, tampons and menstrual cups keep a person comfortable and clean; access to them is an important part of keeping girls in school.

Fertilisation occurs when a sperm cell fuses with an egg cell in the oviduct, forming a zygote with genetic instructions from both parents. The zygote divides repeatedly as it moves down the oviduct, forming a ball of cells, which after about a week implants in the thick lining of the uterus. This is the start of pregnancy, and menstruation stops while it lasts.

During pregnancy, the developing baby is called an embryo for the first eight weeks and then a foetus. A placenta grows into the uterus wall and is connected to the baby by the umbilical cord. Through the placenta, oxygen and food pass from the mother's blood to the baby's, and carbon dioxide and wastes pass back, without the two bloods mixing. The baby floats in a fluid-filled sac, the amnion, which cushions it. Harmful substances such as alcohol, nicotine and some drugs can cross the placenta, which is why pregnant women are advised to avoid them; alcohol in pregnancy can cause foetal alcohol syndrome, a lifelong condition that is unfortunately common in parts of South Africa. Pregnancy lasts about nine months, or around forty weeks, and ends with birth, when strong contractions of the uterus push the baby out through the cervix and vagina.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: calling the uterus the stomach; the baby grows in the uterus, not in the digestive system. Two: saying fertilisation happens in the uterus; it normally happens in the oviduct. Three: saying the urethra carries urine and semen at the same time. Four: thinking pregnancy cannot happen the first time or during the first periods; it can happen whenever an egg is released. Five: confusing menstruation with ovulation; menstruation is the shedding of the lining, ovulation is the release of an egg.

The questions for this section are with you now: the main events of the menstrual cycle, fertilisation and implantation, and the role of the placenta.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with body changes, two sets of organs and a monthly calendar — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Body Under Construction

Somewhere between about eight and fourteen, your body starts a big building project called puberty. It is when a child's body slowly changes into an adult body that could have babies.

Chemical messengers called hormones run the project. In girls, the ovaries make oestrogen. In boys, the testes make testosterone.

Everyone gets some changes: a sudden growth spurt, hair under the arms and around the private parts, more sweat, so washing every day matters, and often some pimples. Feelings can get stronger too, and moods can swing.

Girls develop breasts and wider hips, and start their periods. Boys' voices get deeper, sometimes cracking for a while, they grow facial hair and broader shoulders, and their bodies start making sperm.

And it starts at different ages for different people. Early, late or in between: it is all normal. Being able to have a baby does not mean being ready to be a parent; that takes many more years.

## Subtopic: Two Teams of Organs

A baby starts when a sperm cell from a man joins an egg cell from a woman. Each body has organs for its part of the job.

The male organs: two testes, held in a pouch of skin called the scrotum, make millions of sperm cells every day. Tubes carry the sperm, and glands add a liquid to make semen. The urethra carries semen or urine out through the penis, never both at the same time.

The female organs: two ovaries, each the size of an almond, hold egg cells and usually release one each month. Each egg travels down a tube called an oviduct. The uterus, or womb, is a strong, hollow muscle the size of a pear, where a baby can grow. Its neck is the cervix, which opens into the vagina, the passage to the outside, which is also the birth canal.

A sperm cell is tiny and swims with a tail. An egg cell is much bigger, carries a little food store, and cannot swim.

## Subtopic: The Monthly Calendar

Once periods start, a woman's body runs a monthly calendar of about twenty-eight days, though it is often irregular at first.

At the start, the soft lining of the uterus, which is full of blood, is shed. It comes out through the vagina for a few days. That is a period, or menstruation.

Then the lining builds up again, like making a soft bed. Around the middle of the month, an ovary releases an egg. That is ovulation.

If the egg meets a sperm in the oviduct, they join: fertilisation. The tiny new ball of cells travels to the uterus and settles into the soft bed. That is the start of pregnancy, and periods stop.

If no sperm comes, the egg dies, the bed is not needed, and it is shed in the next period. The calendar starts again.

During pregnancy, the baby gets food and oxygen through the placenta and the umbilical cord. Anything harmful, like alcohol or cigarette smoke, can reach the baby that way, so pregnant women should avoid them. After about nine months, the baby is born.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Puberty is when hormones change a child's body into one that can reproduce, at different ages for different people. Testes make sperm; ovaries release eggs. In the menstrual cycle the uterus lining builds up, an egg is released about halfway through, and if there is no fertilisation the lining is shed as a period. If a sperm fertilises the egg in the oviduct, the new cells implant in the uterus and grow for about nine months, fed through the placenta.

Hold the building project, the two teams and the monthly calendar, and take the final questions of the lesson.
