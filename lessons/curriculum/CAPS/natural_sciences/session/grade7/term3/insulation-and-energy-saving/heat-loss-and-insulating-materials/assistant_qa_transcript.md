### subtopic_1 — How Heat Is Lost and Gained
**Assistant:** Why does a windy winter day feel so much colder than a still day at the same temperature?

**Tutor:** Because the wind strips away the thin layer of warm air that normally surrounds your body and replaces it with fresh cold air, so your body loses heat much faster by convection. On a still day, the air right next to your skin and clothing is warmed by your body heat. This layer of warm air, only a few millimetres thick, stays roughly in place and acts as a natural insulator, slowing the flow of heat from your skin into the colder air further away. When the wind blows, this warm layer is constantly blown away and replaced with cold air at the full outside temperature. Heat flows quickly from your skin into the new cold air, which is then blown away too, and the process repeats. Your skin cools much faster, and you feel much colder. Weather forecasters describe this with a wind-chill temperature: on a day when the thermometer reads five degrees Celsius, a strong wind may make it feel as cold as minus five on bare skin. The effect is strongest on exposed parts such as the face, ears and hands. That is why windproof jackets, scarves and beanies make such a difference in winter, by blocking the wind and keeping the warm layer of air in place. The same idea explains why blowing on hot soup cools it: your breath replaces the warm, moist air above the spoon with cooler air, speeding up convection and evaporation.

### subtopic_2 — Insulating Materials in Cool Boxes and Flasks
**Assistant:** Why does a cool box also keep hot food hot, if it is called a cool box?

**Tutor:** Because an insulator does not care which way the heat is trying to go; it simply slows the flow of heat in both directions, so the same box keeps cold things cold and hot things hot. A cool box works by putting a thick layer of trapped-air foam between the inside and the outside. When the inside is colder than the outside, heat tries to flow inward, and the foam slows it, so the drinks stay cold. If you put a pot of hot food inside instead, the heat tries to flow outward, and the same foam slows it, so the food stays hot. Many caterers and taxi-rank food sellers use cool boxes exactly this way, to keep pap, rice and stew hot for lunchtime customers. The name only describes its most common use. The same is true of a blanket, a flask and a wonder bag. Wrap an ice block in a blanket and it melts more slowly than an unwrapped one; wrap a pot of hot food in a blanket and it stays hot longer. What matters is the difference in temperature between inside and outside, because heat always flows from hotter to colder, and the insulator's job is to slow that flow, whichever direction it runs. The one thing an insulator cannot do is create heat or cold: a cool box with warm drinks and no ice will never make them cold.

### subtopic_3 — Insulation in Ceilings, Clothing and Blankets
**Assistant:** Why do birds fluff up their feathers on cold mornings?

**Tutor:** To trap a thicker layer of still air among their feathers, which turns their plumage into a better insulator and slows the loss of body heat. A bird's body temperature is high, around forty degrees Celsius, higher than ours, and small birds lose heat very quickly because they have a large surface area for their small size. Feathers, especially the soft, fluffy down feathers close to the skin, are excellent insulators because they hold air in countless tiny spaces between their branches. When a bird is cold, tiny muscles at the base of each feather pull it upright, so the feathers stand out from the body. This doubles or triples the thickness of the air layer, and since trapped still air is a very poor conductor, the bird's heat escapes much more slowly. The bird looks round and puffy, like a small ball. People do something similar: goosebumps are caused by tiny muscles that try to raise our body hairs, a leftover from ancestors who had more hair. Birds also tuck their beaks and feet into their feathers, reducing the area exposed to the cold, and huddle together on branches or wires to share warmth. Down feathers from ducks and geese are used in the warmest sleeping bags and jackets, because they trap so much air for so little weight.
