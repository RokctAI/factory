# Part 1 — Expert

This session applies heat transfer to whole buildings: how they gain and lose heat, how we can conserve energy in them, and what we can learn from energy-efficient indigenous South African homes. The anchor is a village in the Eastern Cape where a family lives in a round, thatched rondavel with thick mud walls next to a newer house of cement blocks with a corrugated-iron roof, and the rondavel stays cooler in summer and warmer in winter. The governing idea is that a building's materials, shape, orientation, colour and insulation control how heat flows in and out, and that good design keeps people comfortable while using little or no electricity. Describing ways to conserve heat in buildings and explaining features of indigenous homes are examinable.

## Subtopic: How Buildings Gain and Lose Heat

A building gains and loses heat through its roof, walls, windows, floor and any gaps, by all three methods of heat transfer.

Through the roof, the biggest culprit: sunlight radiates onto the roof all day, heating it; a thin metal roof conducts that heat straight through into the room below. In winter, warm air rises by convection to the roof and its heat is conducted out into the cold night.

Through the walls: thin walls of a single layer of material conduct heat quickly. Thick walls, or walls with a cavity of trapped air, slow it down.

Through windows: glass lets sunlight in, which is helpful on winter days but can overheat rooms in summer. A single pane of glass also conducts heat out quickly on cold nights.

Through gaps: draughts around doors and windows and under roofs let warm air escape and cold air in by convection.

Through the floor: heat is conducted into cold concrete or ground.

In South Africa, most electricity comes from coal power stations, and heating and cooling homes uses a lot of it. Designing and fitting buildings to need less heating and cooling saves money, reduces load-shedding pressure, and reduces pollution and carbon dioxide emissions.

Heat flow through a building is driven by the temperature difference between inside and outside. On the Highveld, winter nights can drop below freezing while people want their homes at about twenty degrees, a difference of more than twenty degrees, so heat pours out. In the hot Lowveld or Northern Cape, summer afternoons can exceed forty degrees, and heat pours in. Buildings in different climates therefore need different designs, but every design must manage the same three methods of heat transfer.

## Subtopic: Conserving Heat Energy in Buildings

Several features keep buildings comfortable with less energy.

Ceilings and roof insulation, such as fibreglass, recycled-paper fibre or polystyrene, trap still air and slow heat flow through the roof. A reflective foil layer under the roof reflects radiant heat.

Thick walls, or cavity walls with an air gap between two layers of bricks, slow conduction. Heavy materials like brick, stone and mud also store heat during the day and release it slowly at night, evening out temperature changes; this is called thermal mass.

Orientation: in South Africa, which is in the Southern Hemisphere, the Sun is in the northern part of the sky, especially in winter. Placing large windows on the north side lets the low winter sun shine deep into rooms, while a roof overhang above them shades them from the high summer sun. Fewer windows on the west side reduce the hot afternoon sun.

Colour: light-coloured roofs and walls reflect sunlight, keeping buildings cooler in hot regions.

Sealing gaps: draught strips around doors and windows, and draught snakes along the bottom of doors, stop warm air escaping.

Curtains, double-glazed windows, carpets and rugs add insulation.

Ventilation: openings low on one side and high on the other let hot air escape by convection in summer.

Trees: deciduous trees on the north side shade the house in summer and lose their leaves to let sunlight in during winter.

Many energy-saving features cost little or nothing. Closing curtains at sunset traps a layer of air against the cold glass. Opening curtains on sunny winter mornings lets free solar heat in. Hanging a heavy curtain over a draughty door, laying a rug on a cold concrete floor, and closing doors to unused rooms all help. In summer, closing curtains on the sunny side during the day and opening windows in the cool evening lets the house breathe and stay comfortable without fans.

The questions for this section are with you now: ceiling insulation, thermal mass, north-facing windows and overhangs, colour, sealing gaps and ventilation.

## Subtopic: Energy-Efficient Indigenous South African Homes

Long before electricity, South African communities designed homes that stayed comfortable in hot summers and cold winters using local, natural materials.

The rondavel, common in the Eastern Cape, KwaZulu-Natal and elsewhere, has thick walls of mud, clay bricks or stone, mixed with dung and straw. These walls are poor conductors and have high thermal mass: they absorb heat during the hot day and release it slowly during the cold night, keeping the inside temperature steady. The thick thatched roof of grass is full of trapped air, an excellent insulator that keeps heat out in summer and in during winter, and its steep slope sheds rain. The round shape has the smallest wall area for the floor space, so less heat is lost, and it resists strong winds.

The Zulu beehive hut, or iqhugwane, was a dome of woven saplings covered with thick thatch, light enough to build quickly, with the thatch insulating the inside.

Ndebele homes have thick mud walls, often painted with bright geometric patterns. The light-coloured lime-washed background reflects sunlight.

In the dry Karoo, corbelled houses were built with domes of flat stones layered inward, because there was little timber. Their thick stone walls keep the inside cool by day and warm at night.

The Nama people of the Northern Cape build matjieshuise, portable domes of reed mats over a frame. The reed mats let breezes through when it is hot, and when it rains, the reeds swell and close the gaps.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: thinking modern materials are always better; indigenous designs often perform better thermally. Two: thinking thatch is a poor insulator because it is "only grass". Three: forgetting that thermal mass works by storing heat and releasing it later. Four: putting the main windows on the south side in South Africa. Five: thinking energy saving is only about switching off lights.

Many of these ideas appear in modern green buildings in South Africa. Some new schools and offices have thick rammed-earth walls, made by compacting damp soil in moulds, which copy the thermal mass of mud walls. Others use thatch-inspired green roofs covered with plants and soil, which insulate and keep the building cool. Learning from indigenous homes reminds us that local materials, which need little transport and little factory energy to produce, can be the most sustainable choice of all.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a rondavel and a tin-roofed house side by side — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Where Houses Leak Heat

A house leaks heat through its roof, walls, windows, floor and little gaps.

A thin metal roof is the worst. In summer, the sun bakes it and the heat comes straight through. In winter, the heat inside escapes straight out.

Thin walls let heat through too. Gaps around doors let warm air out and cold air in.

Heating and cooling houses uses a lot of electricity, and most of our electricity comes from burning coal. Saving it saves money and helps the planet.

## Subtopic: Making Houses Comfy

Put in a ceiling with a thick insulation blanket above it. Fill gaps around doors with draught strips.

Build thick walls, which soak up heat by day and give it back at night.

In South Africa, the Sun is in the northern sky, so put big windows on the north side. Add a roof overhang to shade them from the high summer sun but let in the low winter sun.

Paint roofs light colours to bounce sunlight away. Plant trees for shade.

## Subtopic: Wisdom of Traditional Homes

A rondavel has thick mud walls and a thick grass roof. The grass is full of trapped air, a great insulator. The mud walls soak up heat in the day and give it back at night. So inside, it stays cool in summer and warm in winter, with no electricity at all.

Round walls mean less wall to lose heat through, and the wind slides around them.

In the Karoo, people built thick stone domes. The Nama people make domes of reed mats that let breezes in, and close up when the rain makes the reeds swell.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Buildings gain and lose heat through roofs, walls, windows, floors and gaps by conduction, convection and radiation. Ceilings with insulation, thick walls, sealed gaps, north-facing windows with overhangs, light colours and trees save energy. Indigenous homes, such as thatched rondavels, Karoo stone houses and Nama reed huts, use local materials, thermal mass and trapped air to stay comfortable without electricity.
