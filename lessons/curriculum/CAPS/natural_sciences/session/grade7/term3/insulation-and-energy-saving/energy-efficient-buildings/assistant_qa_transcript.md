### subtopic_1 — How Buildings Gain and Lose Heat
**Assistant:** Why are so many RDP and shack homes extremely hot in summer and freezing in winter?

**Tutor:** Because many were built from thin materials that conduct heat easily, often with no ceiling and no insulation, so heat passes straight through the walls and roof in both directions. Consider a house with a corrugated-iron roof and no ceiling. Iron is a metal and a good conductor of heat, and the roof is only a millimetre or so thick. On a summer afternoon, the sun radiates onto it, the grey or rusty metal absorbs most of that radiation, and its temperature can rise above sixty degrees Celsius. Heat passes straight through by conduction and radiates down into the room, which becomes oven-hot. At night in winter, the opposite happens: warm air from the room rises by convection to the roof, and the metal conducts its heat out into the cold sky, so the room loses heat as fast as the family can make it. Thin walls of single cement blocks or iron sheets add to the problem, and gaps around doors, windows and the roof let draughts through. People then burn paraffin, wood or coal indoors to keep warm, causing dangerous smoke and fires. Simple changes help enormously: fitting a ceiling with insulation, painting the roof white, sealing gaps, and planting trees for shade. Government programmes have retrofitted ceilings in many low-cost houses, and studies found that indoor temperatures became much more comfortable, and families used less fuel.

### subtopic_2 — Conserving Heat Energy in Buildings
**Assistant:** Why should big windows face north in South Africa, but south in Europe?

**Tutor:** Because South Africa is in the Southern Hemisphere, where the Sun is always in the northern part of the sky at midday, while Europe is in the Northern Hemisphere, where the Sun is always in the southern part of the sky. The Sun rises roughly in the east and sets in the west, but it does not pass directly overhead in most places. Seen from Johannesburg or Cape Town, which lie south of the Tropic of Capricorn, the Sun arcs across the northern sky. In winter, it is low in the north, so its rays come in at a shallow angle; a large window facing north lets these low rays shine deep into the room, warming the floor and walls, which store heat for the evening. In summer, the Sun is much higher in the sky at midday, so a roof overhang of the right width above the north windows blocks the steep summer rays, keeping the room shaded and cool. Windows facing south in South Africa receive almost no direct sunlight in winter, so they lose heat without gaining any, and rooms facing south are often the coldest in the house. In London or Paris, everything is reversed: the Sun is in the southern sky, so architects face windows south. This kind of design, which uses the Sun's path rather than machines to heat and cool a building, is called passive solar design.

### subtopic_3 — Energy-Efficient Indigenous South African Homes
**Assistant:** Why does a thatched rondavel stay so comfortable without any electricity?

**Tutor:** Because almost every part of its design works together to slow and smooth out heat flow, using materials found close by. Start with the roof, where houses gain and lose the most heat. Thatch is thick, often thirty centimetres or more, made of layers of dry grass stems, each a hollow tube, packed with countless pockets of still air. That makes it an excellent insulator: on a summer afternoon, the outside of the thatch may be very hot, but little heat reaches the room below, and in winter the warm air inside cannot easily escape. The walls are thick mud or clay bricks, sometimes stone, which conduct heat slowly and have high thermal mass. During a hot day, the walls absorb heat gradually; by the time it has worked through to the inside, the sun has set, and the stored warmth is released into the cold night. So the inside stays cooler than outside by day and warmer by night. The floor, traditionally of mud and dung smoothed and polished, adds more thermal mass. The round shape gives the least wall area for the space enclosed, so there is less surface to lose heat through, and wind flows smoothly around it rather than battering a flat wall. Small windows limit heat loss. A fire in the centre, with smoke escaping through the thatch, provides warmth on the coldest nights. Many modern architects now study these homes to design energy-efficient buildings.
