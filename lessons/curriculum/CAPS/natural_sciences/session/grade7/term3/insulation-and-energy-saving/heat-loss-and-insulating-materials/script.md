# Part 1 — Expert

This session uses the three methods of heat transfer to explain how heat is lost and gained, and how insulating materials slow it down. The anchor is a family preparing for a winter rugby match in Bloemfontein: packing a cool box with cold drinks, filling a flask with hot soup, and dressing the baby in a woolly beanie and fleece blanket. The governing idea is that heat is always gained or lost by conduction, convection and radiation, and that a good insulator works by slowing one or more of these, usually by trapping still air and using shiny surfaces. Identifying heat loss and gain by each method, and explaining how cool boxes, ceilings, clothing and blankets use insulating materials, are examinable.

## Subtopic: How Heat Is Lost and Gained

Any object that is warmer than its surroundings loses heat, and any object that is cooler gains heat, until temperatures are equal. It happens in three ways, often all at once.

By conduction, heat passes through solids that touch: a hot pot loses heat into the cold counter it stands on; a person sitting on a cold concrete step loses body heat into it; heat from a hot day passes through a metal roof into a house.

By convection, moving air or water carries heat away: wind blowing past your skin carries body heat away, which is why a windy day feels colder than a still day at the same temperature; warm air from a room escapes through open windows and gaps under doors, and cold air flows in.

By radiation, heat travels as invisible waves: your body radiates heat into a cold night; the Sun radiates heat into a car through its windows; a hot stove radiates heat into the kitchen.

People also lose heat by evaporation of sweat, which helps cool the body on hot days, and by breathing out warm, moist air.

To keep something warm, we must slow all the ways it can lose heat; to keep something cold, we must slow all the ways it can gain heat. The same insulating idea works in both directions.

Heat gain matters as much as heat loss. Milk left on a sunny windowsill gains heat by radiation through the glass and by conduction from the warm sill, and it soon goes sour. Ice cream carried home from the shop in a plastic bag on a hot afternoon gains heat from the air by convection and from your warm hand by conduction. Food safety depends on keeping cold food cold and hot food hot, which is why delivery drivers carry insulated bags.

The questions for this section are with you now: identifying heat loss and gain by conduction, convection and radiation in everyday situations.

## Subtopic: Insulating Materials in Cool Boxes and Flasks

An insulating material slows the transfer of heat. The best insulators usually contain lots of small pockets of trapped, still air, which is a very poor conductor and cannot move to carry heat by convection.

Good insulating materials include polystyrene foam, expanded plastic foam, wool, fleece, feathers and down, cork, fibreglass, straw, cardboard and newspaper.

A cool box is a plastic box with thick walls filled with foam. The foam slows conduction of heat from the warm outside air through the walls. The tight lid stops warm air flowing in, preventing convection. A light colour, usually white, reflects sunlight, reducing radiation. Ice packs inside absorb heat that does leak in, keeping the drinks cold for hours.

A vacuum flask keeps soup hot by slowing all three methods. It has a double wall with a vacuum between the layers, which stops conduction and convection because there are no particles to carry heat. The inner surfaces are silvered, like mirrors, which reflect radiant heat back. An insulating stopper slows heat escaping through the top.

A simple investigation compares insulators: wrap identical cans of hot water at the same starting temperature in equal thicknesses of different materials, such as newspaper, wool, foil, cotton and polystyrene, leave one unwrapped as a control, and measure the temperatures every five minutes. The water in the best insulator cools most slowly.

Insulating materials are chosen for more than how well they insulate. A good material for a cool box should also be light, strong enough not to crush, waterproof, cheap and easy to clean. Polystyrene is excellent but breaks easily and is hard to recycle, so many people reuse polystyrene boxes many times. Natural materials such as straw, sawdust and wool were used for centuries: ice was once carried long distances packed in sawdust, and farmers still wrap water pipes in sacking to stop them freezing.

The questions for this section are with you now: what makes a good insulator, how a cool box and a flask work, and the investigation comparing insulators.

## Subtopic: Insulation in Ceilings, Clothing and Blankets

Homes lose a great deal of heat in winter, and gain it in summer, through their roofs. A metal or tiled roof without a ceiling lets heat pass by conduction straight through, and warm air rising by convection meets the cold roof. A ceiling traps a layer of air, and insulation laid above the ceiling, such as fibreglass wool, mineral wool, recycled-paper fibre or polystyrene boards, slows heat flow even more. A shiny foil layer under the roof sheets reflects radiant heat, keeping the house cooler in summer and warmer in winter. South African building regulations now require ceiling insulation in new houses.

Clothing works by trapping layers of air next to the body. Several thin layers are warmer than one thick layer, because each layer traps air between it and the next. Wool, fleece and down jackets trap a lot of air. A beanie is important, because babies and small children lose much of their heat through their heads. A windproof outer layer stops convection.

Blankets trap still air in their fibres and slow the loss of body heat. A shiny survival blanket also reflects radiant heat.

Animals use the same ideas: sheep and Angora goats grow thick fleece, penguins on the Cape coast have densely packed feathers with an oily coat and a layer of fat beneath the skin, and many birds fluff up their feathers on cold mornings to trap more air.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: thinking insulators make heat. Two: saying a jersey "keeps the cold out"; it keeps your heat in. Three: forgetting that trapped air is the real insulator. Four: thinking one thick layer is always better than several thin ones. Five: thinking a ceiling only helps in winter; it helps in summer too.

Insulation also matters for people working in extreme conditions. Firefighters wear thick, layered suits with a shiny outer layer to reflect radiant heat from flames. Miners deep underground, where the rock can be hotter than fifty degrees Celsius, wear cooling vests packed with ice. Scientists at South Africa's research base in Antarctica wear many layers, windproof shells and insulated boots, and their buildings stand on legs above the snow, with walls thickly insulated against temperatures far below freezing.

The questions for this section are with you now: insulation in ceilings and roofs, layers of clothing, and animal insulation.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a cool box, a flask and a baby's beanie — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Three Ways to Lose Heat

Heat escapes in three ways.

Touching: sit on a cold step and heat goes from you into the step. That is conduction.

Moving air: wind blows your warm air away. That is convection. That is why windy days feel extra cold.

Waves: your body sends invisible heat waves out into a cold night. That is radiation.

To keep something warm, block all three. To keep something cold, block all three too.

Keep the cold step and the wind in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: Boxes and Flasks

A cool box has thick foam walls. Foam is full of tiny bubbles of trapped air, and trapped air is a great insulator. The lid stops air moving in, and the white colour bounces sunlight away.

A flask has a gap with no air at all between two walls, so heat cannot be passed through. The shiny walls bounce heat waves back.

Here is a surprise: a cool box can keep hot food hot too. Insulators just slow heat down, whichever way it is going.

Hold the cool box in mind and try this part's questions.

## Subtopic: Ceilings and Clothes

A house without a ceiling loses heat through the roof in winter and bakes in summer. A ceiling with a thick blanket of insulation above it keeps the warmth in, or the heat out.

Wear several thin layers in winter, because each layer traps air. Add a beanie and a windproof jacket.

Birds fluff up their feathers to trap more air. Sheep have thick wool.

A jersey does not keep the cold out. It keeps your own heat in.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Heat is lost and gained by conduction, convection and radiation. Insulators slow heat flow, usually by trapping still air, and shiny surfaces reflect radiation. Cool boxes, flasks, ceiling insulation, layers of clothing, blankets, fleece and feathers all use these ideas, keeping hot things hot and cold things cold.
