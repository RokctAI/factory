### subtopic_1 — Convection in Liquids
**Assistant:** Why does warm water rise if it is still the same water?

**Tutor:** Because heating makes the same water take up more space, so each cupful of warm water weighs a little less than a cupful of cold water, and lighter-for-its-size fluid is pushed upwards by the heavier fluid around it. When water is heated, its particles gain kinetic energy, move faster and push one another slightly further apart. The amount of water, its mass, does not change, but its volume increases. Density is the mass packed into a given volume, so the warm water becomes less dense. Now picture a blob of this warm water surrounded by cooler, denser water. Gravity pulls more strongly on the denser cold water around it, and the cold water pushes in and down beneath the warm blob, forcing it upwards, just as a cork is pushed up when held under water. This upward push is called buoyancy. So the warm water does not rise by itself; it is pushed up by the sinking cold water. When the warm water reaches the top, it gives up heat to the air and cooler water, contracts, becomes denser again and sinks, completing the loop. The effect is small, warm water at sixty degrees is only about two percent less dense than water at twenty, but it is enough to keep a pot of water steadily churning, and enough to drive enormous currents in the oceans, carrying warm water from the tropics towards the poles.

### subtopic_2 — Convection in Air
**Assistant:** Is it really true that the floor of a room is colder than the ceiling?

**Tutor:** Yes, and it is easy to show with two thermometers, one held near the floor and one on a high shelf, in a room warmed by a heater on a winter evening. The difference can be several degrees, sometimes five or more in a room with a high ceiling. The reason is convection. Air heated by the heater, by people's bodies, by the stove and by lights becomes less dense and rises, collecting near the ceiling. Cooler, denser air sinks and pools near the floor, especially if cold air leaks in under the doors or through gaps near the ground. In a house without a ceiling, the rising warm air touches the metal roof sheets directly, and because metal is a good conductor, the heat is quickly lost to the cold night air outside, which is why houses with only a corrugated-iron roof are so cold in winter. Installing a ceiling with insulation above it traps the warm layer inside the room. Cats and dogs know about this: on a cold night, a pet often chooses the top of a cupboard. People living in very cold climates have used it for centuries: in old houses, sleeping lofts were built high above the fireplace. It also explains why in summer, an upstairs room can be uncomfortably hot, and why opening a high window lets the hottest air out quickly.

### subtopic_3 — Convection in Nature
**Assistant:** Why do Highveld thunderstorms usually happen in the afternoon, not the morning?

**Tutor:** Because the storms are powered by convection, and convection needs the ground to be heated strongly by the Sun first, which takes most of the day. In the early morning, after a clear night, the ground and the air above it are cool, and the air is calm and stable. As the Sun climbs, its radiation heats the ground, and the ground heats the layer of air touching it. By midday and early afternoon, this lowest layer of air has become much warmer, and less dense, than the air higher up. Bubbles of warm air begin to rise in strong convection currents, the same thermals that vultures ride. Summer air on the Highveld is often moist, carrying water vapour from the Indian Ocean. As the warm, moist air rises, it expands and cools; at a certain height, its water vapour condenses into tiny droplets, forming flat-bottomed, puffy cumulus clouds. Condensation releases heat, which makes the air rise even more strongly, and the clouds tower upward to great heights, becoming dark cumulonimbus thunderclouds with flat, anvil-shaped tops. Inside, water droplets and ice crystals collide, building up electric charges that discharge as lightning, and the rain or hail falls in a short, heavy burst, usually between about three and six in the afternoon. By night, without sunlight, the convection dies down, the sky often clears, and the cycle repeats the next day.
