# Part 1 — Expert

This session begins the study of heat transfer with what heating actually is and with the first way heat moves, conduction. The anchor is a three-legged potjie pot on a fire in a backyard in Soweto, with a metal spoon and a wooden spoon both resting in the stew. The governing idea is that heating is the transfer of energy from a hotter object to a cooler one, continuing until both reach the same temperature, and that in conduction heat passes through a material from particle to particle without the material itself moving. Explaining heating, describing conduction in terms of particles, and classifying materials as conductors or insulators of heat are examinable.

## Subtopic: Heating as Energy Transfer

Heat is energy that moves from a hotter object to a cooler object because of the difference in their temperatures. Temperature is a measure of how hot or cold something is, measured in degrees Celsius with a thermometer. It is linked to how fast the particles of a substance are moving: the hotter it is, the faster the particles move.

Heat always flows from hot to cold, never the other way round on its own. When the potjie pot sits on the coals, energy flows from the very hot coals into the cooler pot, and from the pot into the cooler stew. When a cup of tea is left on the table, energy flows from the hot tea into the cooler cup, table and air, and the tea cools. When you hold an ice cube, energy flows from your warm hand into the cold ice, which melts, while your hand feels cold because it is losing energy.

The flow continues until the objects reach the same temperature, called thermal equilibrium. A cup of tea cools to room temperature and then stops cooling; a cold drink from the fridge warms to room temperature and then stops warming.

Notice that "cold" is not something that flows. When you touch a cold tap, cold does not move into your hand; heat moves out of your hand into the tap.

The bigger the difference in temperature, the faster heat flows. Boiling water in a cold mug cools quickly at first, then more and more slowly as it approaches room temperature.

Thermometers themselves work by heat transfer. When a thermometer is placed in warm water, heat flows from the water into the bulb until the liquid inside reaches the same temperature as the water, and only then is the reading correct. That is why you must wait a little before reading a thermometer, and why you must not hold the bulb in your fingers, since heat from your hand would flow in and give a false reading. A clinical thermometer under the tongue works the same way, reaching the temperature of the body.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of heat, the direction heat flows, and thermal equilibrium.

## Subtopic: Conduction

Conduction is the transfer of heat through a material from particle to particle, without the material itself moving. It happens mainly in solids, where the particles are packed tightly together.

Picture the metal spoon in the potjie. The particles at the hot end, in the stew, gain energy and vibrate more strongly. They bump into their neighbours and pass some energy on; those particles vibrate more and bump into the next ones, and so on along the spoon. After a minute or two, the handle is hot, even though no part of the spoon has moved along. In metals, there are also free electrons that move easily through the metal and carry energy very quickly, which is why metals conduct heat so well.

Conduction can be shown with a simple investigation: stick drawing pins to a metal rod with candle wax at equal distances and heat one end over a flame. The wax nearest the flame melts first and that pin drops, then the next, then the next, showing heat moving along the rod.

A fair test can compare rods of copper, aluminium, iron, glass and wood of the same length and thickness, heated the same way. The pins drop fastest along copper, which is the best conductor of the five; on glass and wood they may not drop at all.

Conduction also happens between objects that touch: heat passes from the hot plate of a stove into the bottom of a pot by conduction, and from a hot-water bottle into cold feet.

Conduction is also at work in cooking methods all over South Africa. A cast-iron potjie heats slowly, because iron is a less effective conductor than copper or aluminium, but once hot, it holds and spreads heat evenly, so food cooks gently without burning. Meat on a braai grid is seared where it touches the hot metal bars by conduction, which makes the dark stripes. A flat iron or a hair straightener presses hot metal against cloth or hair, passing heat directly by conduction.

The questions for this section are with you now: conduction explained with vibrating particles, and the wax-and-pin investigation.

## Subtopic: Conductors and Insulators of Heat

A conductor of heat is a material that allows heat to move through it easily. Most metals are good conductors: copper, aluminium, iron, steel and silver.

An insulator of heat is a material that does not allow heat to move through it easily. Wood, plastic, rubber, cloth, wool, paper, cork, glass, polystyrene and still air are insulators.

Air is a very good insulator when it is trapped and cannot move, so materials full of tiny pockets of air, such as wool, feathers, fur, polystyrene foam and fleece, are excellent insulators. Liquids and gases are generally poor conductors of heat.

We choose materials according to the job. A pot needs a conducting base so that heat reaches the food quickly, and an insulating handle so that you do not burn your hand. An oven glove is made of thick cloth with padding, an insulator. Electrical wires are copper inside, and plastic outside: the plastic is an electrical and heat insulator.

Insulators are used to keep hot things hot and cold things cold. A cool box keeps drinks cold because its foam walls stop heat from the warm outside reaching the drinks; a flask keeps tea hot.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying "cold" moves into your hand; heat moves out. Two: thinking a metal bench is colder than a wooden one at the same temperature. Three: thinking insulators make heat; a blanket only slows the escape of your own body heat. Four: forgetting that trapped air is an excellent insulator. Five: saying the particles of the spoon move along to the handle; only energy moves.

Builders use insulators too. Bricks and concrete are only fair insulators, so modern walls may have a cavity, a gap of still air between two layers of brick, and roofs have a thick blanket of fibreglass or other insulating material laid above the ceiling. Double-glazed windows have two panes of glass with a thin layer of trapped air or gas between them. In each case, a layer of still air or foam slows the flow of heat between the warm inside and the cold outside.

The questions for this section are with you now: classifying materials as conductors or insulators, why trapped air insulates, and choosing materials for a pot.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a potjie pot and two spoons — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Hot to Cold

Heat is energy on the move, and it always moves from something hotter to something colder.

A hot pot of stew warms your hands. An ice cube in your hand melts, because heat leaves your hand and goes into the ice. Your hand feels cold because it is losing heat, not because cold is coming in.

Leave hot tea on the table. It cools until it is the same temperature as the room, then stops cooling. Heat keeps flowing until both things are the same temperature.

Temperature tells us how hot something is. We measure it with a thermometer, in degrees Celsius.

Keep the tea in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: Passing Heat Along

Put a metal spoon in hot stew. Soon the handle is hot too. How did the heat travel?

The tiny particles in the spoon at the hot end start shaking harder. They bump the next particles, which shake harder and bump the next ones. The heat is passed along like a message down a line of people. This is called conduction.

The spoon does not move. Only the energy moves.

Metals pass heat fastest. Copper is one of the best. Wood hardly passes heat at all.

Hold the spoon in mind and try this part's questions.

## Subtopic: Let It Through or Hold It Back

Conductors let heat through easily: metals like copper, aluminium and iron.

Insulators hold heat back: wood, plastic, wool, cloth, cork and foam.

Trapped air is a great insulator. That is why woolly jerseys, feathers and foam boxes keep heat in, or keep it out.

A good pot has a metal bottom to let heat in and a plastic or wooden handle so you do not burn your hand.

A blanket does not make heat. It just slows down your own body heat from escaping.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Heat is energy that moves from hotter to colder until temperatures are equal. In conduction, vibrating particles pass energy along a material, mainly solids, and metals are the best conductors because of their free electrons. Conductors such as copper let heat through; insulators such as wood, wool and foam, especially with trapped air, hold it back.
