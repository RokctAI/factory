### subtopic_1 — What Radiation Is
**Assistant:** How can heat travel through empty space if there is nothing there to carry it?

**Tutor:** Because radiant heat is not carried by particles at all; it is a type of wave, made of electric and magnetic energy, that carries itself. In conduction, energy is passed from particle to particle, so without particles it stops. In convection, energy is carried by moving particles of a liquid or gas, so again, no particles means no convection. Radiation is different. Every object is made of particles that are constantly vibrating and moving, and moving electric charges in those particles produce waves that spread outward. These waves belong to the same family as visible light, radio waves, microwaves and X-rays, called electromagnetic waves. Each has a different wavelength; infrared, the main form of radiant heat, has waves a little longer than red light, so our eyes cannot see it, but our skin feels it as warmth. Electromagnetic waves do not need a medium to travel through, so they can cross a vacuum, which is why we can see the stars. When the waves strike an object, their energy is passed to the object's particles, which vibrate faster, and the object becomes warmer. Special infrared cameras can show this radiation: they display warm objects, like people and animals, as bright shapes even in total darkness, and they are used by firefighters to find people in smoke and by game rangers to track poachers at night.

### subtopic_2 — Dark and Light, Dull and Shiny Surfaces
**Assistant:** Why do people in hot desert regions sometimes wear black robes, if black absorbs more heat?

**Tutor:** This is a puzzle scientists have actually tested, and the answer shows that radiation is only one part of the story; convection and the way the clothing is worn matter just as much. The Bedouin people of the Sinai and Arabian deserts traditionally wear loose, flowing robes, some of them black. In a study, researchers measured volunteers standing in the desert sun wearing black robes, white robes, a uniform, or just shorts. The black robe did absorb more radiation: its outer surface became several degrees hotter than the white one. Yet the people inside were no warmer. Why? The robes are loose and long, with openings at the neck and bottom. The hot black cloth heats the air under the robe, which rises by convection and flows out at the top, drawing in cooler air at the bottom, setting up a steady breeze across the skin. Black cloth also absorbs the sunlight before it reaches the skin, so less radiation gets through. So a loose black robe can be about as cool as a white one, though a tight black T-shirt certainly is not. For most situations, the simple rule still holds: light-coloured clothing reflects more sunlight and keeps you cooler in the sun. That is why tennis and cricket kits are white, why many houses in hot towns are painted white, and why roofs coated with white paint can make the rooms beneath them noticeably cooler in summer.

### subtopic_3 — Radiation at Work
**Assistant:** How does a simple solar cooker cook food without any fire or electricity?

**Tutor:** It collects sunlight from a large area, focuses it onto a small dark pot, and then traps the heat so that the temperature builds up high enough to cook. A common South African design is a box cooker: a cardboard or wooden box, lined with insulation such as newspaper or polystyrene, with the inside walls covered in shiny aluminium foil and a black-painted floor and pot. A lid of glass or clear plastic covers the top, and a large foil-covered flap is propped up to reflect extra sunlight into the box. Each part uses one idea from this lesson. The shiny reflector and foil walls reflect radiation onto the pot instead of letting it be absorbed by the walls. The black, dull pot is a good absorber, so it changes the radiation into heat efficiently. The clear lid lets sunlight in, while slowing the escape of heat from the warm air inside, which also stops convection currents from carrying warm air away. The insulation in the walls slows conduction. On a sunny day, the inside can reach over one hundred degrees Celsius, hot enough to cook rice, beans, vegetables and even bread, though more slowly than a stove. Solar cookers save money on wood, paraffin or electricity, reduce smoke indoors, and help protect trees. They work best in sunny places such as the Northern Cape and Limpopo, but are useless at night or under thick cloud.
