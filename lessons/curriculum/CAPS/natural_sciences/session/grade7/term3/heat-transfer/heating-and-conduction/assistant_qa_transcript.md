### subtopic_1 — Heating as Energy Transfer
**Assistant:** Why does a metal bench feel colder than a wooden bench on the same winter morning?

**Tutor:** Because the metal draws heat out of your skin much faster than the wood does, even though both benches are at exactly the same temperature. Overnight, both benches have reached the temperature of the cold air around them, perhaps five degrees Celsius; a thermometer placed on each would read the same. Your skin, at around thirty-three degrees, is warmer than both, so heat flows from your body into whichever bench you touch. Your nerves cannot measure the temperature of the bench directly; they sense how fast your own skin is losing heat. Metal is a very good conductor of heat. As soon as your hand touches it, heat flows from your skin into the metal and is quickly carried away from the point of contact deeper into the bench, so the surface under your hand stays cold and keeps pulling heat from you. Your skin cools fast, and your brain reads that as "very cold". Wood is a poor conductor, an insulator. Heat flows from your skin into the thin layer of wood touching you, but it cannot travel onward easily, so that thin layer warms up nearly to your skin temperature, and the flow slows down. Your skin hardly cools, and the wood feels only slightly cool. The same effect explains why a tiled floor feels colder to bare feet than a carpet in the same room, and why a metal spoon left in hot stew burns your fingers while a wooden spoon does not.

### subtopic_2 — Conduction
**Assistant:** Why do metals conduct heat so much better than other solids?

**Tutor:** Because metals have two ways of passing heat along, while most other solids have only one, and the second way is very fast. In every solid, the particles are held in fixed places but vibrate. When one end is heated, its particles vibrate harder and jostle their neighbours, passing energy along from particle to particle like people in a crowd nudging one another. This works in wood, plastic, glass and stone, but slowly, because each particle hands on only a little energy at a time. Metals are built differently. Their atoms are arranged in a regular pattern, and some of the electrons are not tied to any one atom; they drift freely throughout the whole piece of metal, a "sea" of electrons. When one end of a metal rod is heated, these free electrons gain energy, speed up and race through the metal, colliding with atoms far from the heat and passing energy to them. They act like messengers carrying energy quickly across the whole object. The same free electrons explain why metals are also good conductors of electricity: electricity is the flow of these electrons. That is why the most useful heat conductors are copper, used in the bottoms of good pots, aluminium, used in pots and car radiators, and iron in potjie pots and frying pans. Silver is the very best conductor of heat, but it is too expensive to use in kitchenware.

### subtopic_3 — Conductors and Insulators of Heat
**Assistant:** Does a blanket actually make you warmer, or does it just keep heat in?

**Tutor:** It only keeps heat in; a blanket produces no heat at all, and the warmth you feel is your own body heat that it slows from escaping. Your body continuously releases energy from food through respiration, at about the same rate as a bright light bulb. Without covering, on a cold night heat flows quickly from your warm skin into the cooler air and bed, and you feel cold. A blanket is made of fibres, such as wool, cotton or polyester, which trap millions of tiny pockets of air between them. Still air is a very poor conductor, and because the air is trapped, it cannot flow away and carry heat with it. So heat leaving your body passes only very slowly through the blanket, the layer of air next to your skin warms up, and you stay warm. The proof is simple: wrap a blanket round a block of ice and it does not melt faster; it melts more slowly, because the blanket slows the flow of heat from the warm room into the ice. A blanket on a bowl of hot porridge keeps it hot; a blanket on a frozen chicken keeps it frozen. The blanket simply slows heat flow in either direction. This is also the idea behind wonder bags, insulated cooking bags filled with polystyrene beads, used in many South African homes. Food is brought to the boil on the stove, then placed in the bag, where it continues cooking slowly for hours using the heat trapped inside, saving electricity or gas.
