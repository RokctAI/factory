# Part 1 — Expert

This session studies the third way heat moves, radiation, the only one that can cross empty space. The anchor is a clear summer day in Upington in the Northern Cape: a black car and a white car parked side by side in the sun, a metal roof shining, and a solar water heater on a house nearby. The governing idea is that hot objects give off radiant heat that travels as invisible waves, needs no particles to carry it, and is absorbed well by dark, dull surfaces and reflected well by light, shiny surfaces. Explaining radiation, comparing it with conduction and convection, and investigating how colour and shine affect absorption are examinable.

## Subtopic: What Radiation Is

Radiation is the transfer of heat energy in the form of invisible waves, called infrared radiation or radiant heat, which travel in straight lines at the speed of light. Unlike conduction and convection, radiation does not need any particles to carry it. It can pass through empty space, called a vacuum.

This is how the Sun's energy reaches Earth. Between the Sun and Earth there are about 150 million kilometres of almost empty space, with no air to conduct or convect heat. The Sun's energy crosses that gap as radiation, including light and infrared, in about eight minutes.

All objects give off some radiant heat, and the hotter an object is, the more it radiates. Very hot objects, like the Sun, a stove plate on high or the coals of a fire, glow and radiate strongly. You can feel radiation from a fire on your face and hands even when the air between you and the fire is cool, and if someone steps between you and the fire, the warmth stops at once, because their body blocks the straight-line waves.

When radiation reaches an object, it can be absorbed, which warms the object; reflected, which bounces it away; or transmitted, which lets it pass through, as sunlight passes through window glass.

Comparing the three ways: conduction needs particles in contact, mostly in solids; convection needs a fluid that can flow; radiation needs no particles at all.

Radiation also explains why you feel the warmth of a heater or the sun on one side of your body only: the side facing the source is struck by the straight-line waves, while the other side is in shadow. It explains why a car parked in the shade stays cooler, and why standing under a tree on a hot day feels so much better: the leaves absorb and block much of the Sun's radiation before it reaches you, while the air temperature may be almost the same.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of radiation, how the Sun's energy reaches Earth, and the comparison of conduction, convection and radiation.

## Subtopic: Dark and Light, Dull and Shiny Surfaces

The colour and texture of a surface affect how much radiation it absorbs or reflects.

Dark, dull surfaces are good absorbers of radiant heat: they take in most of the radiation that falls on them and warm up quickly. They are also good emitters, or radiators: when hot, they give off heat quickly.

Light, shiny surfaces are good reflectors: they bounce most of the radiation away, so they warm up slowly. They are poor emitters, so a shiny hot object loses heat slowly by radiation.

The two cars in Upington show this. After an hour in the sun, the black car's roof is too hot to touch, while the white car is much cooler. Wearing a black T-shirt on a sunny day feels hotter than wearing a white one.

A fair test can investigate this. Take two identical cans or bottles of water, one painted matt black and the other covered with shiny aluminium foil, with thermometers in each. Place them in the sun, or at the same distance from a lamp, and record their temperatures every five minutes. The black can warms faster. Then fill both with hot water, leave them in the shade and measure how fast they cool: the black can cools faster, because it is the better emitter.

Variables to keep the same: the size and type of container, the amount of water, the starting temperature and the distance from the heat source. The only variable changed is the surface.

Light-coloured surfaces help keep whole cities cooler. Dark tarred roads, dark roofs and car parks absorb large amounts of sunlight during the day and give it off as heat into the evening, so large cities stay several degrees warmer than the surrounding countryside, an effect called the urban heat island. Planting trees, painting roofs white and using lighter paving are ways that town planners can reduce this effect and make summers more bearable for people living in towns.

The questions for this section are with you now: which surfaces absorb, reflect and emit radiation, and planning a fair test with painted cans.

## Subtopic: Radiation at Work

Knowing how surfaces behave lets us design useful things.

A solar water heater on a roof has a black-painted panel of metal pipes, often behind glass. The black surface absorbs as much sunlight as possible, and the glass lets sunlight in but slows heat from escaping. Water flowing through the pipes is heated and stored in an insulated tank.

A solar cooker, or solar oven, uses shiny reflectors to bounce sunlight onto a black pot. The reflectors concentrate the radiation; the black pot absorbs it. With a simple solar cooker made from a box, foil and a pot lid painted black, rice or beans can be cooked on a sunny day without any fuel.

Survival blankets used by paramedics and marathon runners are thin, shiny plastic sheets. The shiny surface reflects body heat back towards the person, keeping them warm.

Tankers carrying fuel and many factory roofs are painted white or silver, to reflect sunlight and stay cool. Firefighters' suits often have a shiny silver outer layer to reflect radiant heat from flames.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying radiation needs air to travel; it crosses empty space. Two: thinking shiny surfaces absorb best because they shine brightly; they reflect. Three: forgetting that good absorbers are also good emitters. Four: confusing this radiant heat with dangerous nuclear radiation. Five: thinking only the Sun radiates; all warm objects do.

A vacuum flask, or thermos, uses all three ideas together. It has a double glass or steel wall with a vacuum between the layers, which stops conduction and convection, because there are no particles to carry the heat. The inner surfaces are silvered, so they reflect radiant heat back, hot drinks keeping their heat in and cold drinks keeping it out. A stopper made of plastic or cork, both insulators, closes the top. Tea can stay hot in a good flask for most of a day.

The questions for this section are with you now: how a solar water heater and a solar cooker work, and uses of shiny and white surfaces.

## Subtopic: Heat Through Empty Space

The Sun is very far away, and between it and Earth there is almost nothing, not even air. So how does its heat reach us?

It travels as invisible waves, called radiation. These waves need nothing to travel through. They zoom through empty space in straight lines.

Sit near a fire and you feel the heat on your face. If someone steps in front of you, the warmth stops, because they block the waves.

Every warm thing gives off some radiation. The hotter it is, the more it gives off.

## Subtopic: Black Soaks It Up, Shiny Bounces It Back

Park a black car and a white car in the sun. After an hour, the black car is much hotter.

Dark, dull things soak up heat waves. Light, shiny things bounce them away.

Dark things also give off heat faster when they are hot.

Try it: one can painted black, one wrapped in shiny foil, both filled with water, both in the sun. Check with thermometers. The black can warms up faster.

## Subtopic: Using the Sun

Solar water heaters on roofs have black pipes that soak up sunlight to heat water.

A solar cooker uses shiny foil to bounce sunlight onto a black pot, which soaks it up and cooks the food. No fire needed!

Shiny silver blankets keep injured people warm by bouncing their body heat back to them.

Fuel tankers are painted white or silver to stay cool.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Radiation carries heat as invisible waves in straight lines and needs no particles, so it can cross empty space, which is how the Sun warms Earth. Dark, dull surfaces absorb and give off radiation well; light, shiny surfaces reflect it. We use this in solar water heaters, solar cookers, survival blankets and white or silver tankers.
