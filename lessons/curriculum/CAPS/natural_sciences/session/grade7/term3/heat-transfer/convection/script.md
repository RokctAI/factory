# Part 1 — Expert

This session studies the second way heat moves, convection, which carries heat through liquids and gases. The anchor is a pot of mealies boiling on a stove in a kitchen in Polokwane, with a paraffin heater warming the room on a winter evening. The governing idea is that when part of a fluid is heated, it expands, becomes less dense and rises, while cooler, denser fluid sinks to take its place, setting up a circulating flow called a convection current that carries heat with the moving fluid. Explaining convection in water and air, drawing convection currents and recognising convection in everyday life and the weather are examinable.

## Subtopic: Convection in Liquids

Convection is the transfer of heat by the movement of a fluid, a liquid or a gas. Unlike conduction, in convection the heated particles themselves move from one place to another, carrying their energy with them.

Think about the pot of water on the stove. The heat enters at the bottom by conduction through the metal. The water at the bottom warms, and its particles move faster and spread further apart: the warm water expands. The same mass of water now takes up more space, so it is less dense than the cooler water above it. Less dense fluid rises, so the warm water rises to the top. Cooler, denser water from the top sinks down to replace it, is heated at the bottom, and rises in turn. This circular flow is a convection current, and it soon carries heat through the whole pot.

You can see a convection current with a simple investigation. Place a few crystals of potassium permanganate, a purple dye, at one side of the bottom of a beaker of water and heat gently below that side. A stream of purple rises above the heat, spreads across the top, sinks down the far side and returns along the bottom, tracing a loop.

Convection is why a kettle element is placed at the bottom, and why boiling water bubbles and churns. If you heated water at the top instead, the warm water would stay at the top and the bottom would remain cool for a long time.

Convection also explains how a geyser, or hot-water cylinder, works. The heating element sits near the bottom of the tank, and the hot water rises to the top, where the outlet pipe takes it to the taps. Cold water enters through a pipe near the bottom to replace it. So the hottest water is always drawn from the top. A pot of soup on a stove left unstirred is hottest at the top, and cooks evenly because convection currents keep the liquid circulating.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of convection, why heated water rises, and the potassium permanganate investigation.

## Subtopic: Convection in Air

Air is a fluid too, so convection carries heat through gases in the same way.

In a room warmed by a heater, the air next to the heater is heated, expands, becomes less dense and rises to the ceiling. It spreads across the top of the room, cools a little, becomes denser and sinks on the far side, then flows back along the floor towards the heater to be warmed again. That is why the top of a room is warmer than the floor, and why a heater works best low down near the floor.

An air conditioner or a cooling unit in a fridge works best high up. Cool air from it is dense and sinks, pushing warmer air up to be cooled. In a fridge, the coldest part used to be the freezer compartment at the top, so cold air sank through the fridge.

Smoke rises above a fire because the hot air carrying it is less dense. A paper spiral hung above a candle or heater turns, pushed by the rising warm air. Hot-air balloons rise because the air inside is heated by a burner and becomes less dense than the cooler air outside.

Chimneys use convection: hot gases from the fire rise up the chimney, drawing fresh air in at the bottom to keep the fire burning. In a well-designed home, warm air can escape through high windows or vents and cooler air enters through low openings, a natural way to cool the house.

Convection also carries smells and smoke around a room. Smoke from a candle or an incense stick first rises straight up in the warm air, then curls and spreads as it meets cooler air near the ceiling, tracing the convection currents. Fire safety advice uses this: in a burning building, hot smoke and poisonous gases collect near the ceiling first, so people are taught to crawl low under the smoke, where the air is cooler and cleaner, when escaping.

The questions for this section are with you now: convection currents in a heated room, why heaters go low and coolers go high, and examples such as smoke and hot-air balloons.

## Subtopic: Convection in Nature

Convection shapes the weather and climate of South Africa and the whole planet.

Sea breezes are a daytime convection current. On a sunny day at the beach in Durban, the land heats up faster than the sea. Air above the land is warmed, expands and rises. Cooler air from above the sea flows in to take its place, giving a cool breeze blowing from sea to land. At night, the land cools faster than the sea, the air above the sea is warmer and rises, and a land breeze blows from land to sea.

Thunderstorms on the Highveld in summer begin with convection. The Sun heats the ground strongly during the day; warm, moist air rises in powerful currents, cools as it climbs, and its water vapour condenses into towering clouds, which can bring afternoon thunderstorms.

Birds of prey such as vultures and eagles glide upwards without flapping on thermals, rising columns of warm air above sunny ground, and paragliders use them in the same way.

On a much larger scale, convection in the oceans and atmosphere carries heat from the hot equator towards the cold poles.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: saying "heat rises"; it is the heated fluid that rises, carrying heat. Two: thinking convection happens in solids; solid particles cannot flow. Three: thinking warm water is lighter because it has less water; it has the same mass in a bigger volume. Four: drawing convection currents as straight lines instead of loops. Five: mixing up sea and land breezes.

Convection inside the Earth may surprise you: deep below the crust, rock in the mantle is so hot that, over millions of years, it flows very slowly like an extremely thick liquid. Hot rock rises, cooler rock sinks, and these slow convection currents are believed to drag the huge plates of the Earth's crust, moving continents a few centimetres each year, about as fast as your fingernails grow. This links convection to earthquakes and volcanoes.

The questions for this section are with you now: sea breezes and land breezes, thermals and thunderstorms, and convection in the atmosphere.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a pot of mealies and a room heater — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Moving Water Carries Heat

Heat a pot of water from the bottom. The water at the bottom warms up and spreads out a little, so it becomes lighter for its size. It floats up to the top.

Cooler, heavier water from the top sinks down to take its place. It gets heated, then it rises too.

Round and round the water goes, carrying heat with it. This loop is called a convection current, and moving heat this way is called convection.

Drop a little purple dye at the bottom and you can actually see the loop.

Keep the pot in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: Moving Air Carries Heat

Air does the same thing. A heater warms the air around it. The warm air rises to the ceiling, cools, sinks down the other side and flows back along the floor.

That is why the ceiling is warmer than the floor, and why heaters should sit low down. Air conditioners go high up, because cool air sinks.

Smoke rises above a fire. A hot-air balloon rises because the air inside is heated and becomes lighter for its size.

Hold the heater in mind and try this part's questions.

## Subtopic: Wind and Weather

At the beach on a sunny day, the land heats up faster than the sea. Warm air rises over the land, and cool air from the sea rushes in: a sea breeze. At night it swaps, and the breeze blows from land to sea.

On the Highveld in summer, the hot ground makes warm, wet air rise. It cools high up and makes huge clouds, and by afternoon, you get a thunderstorm.

Vultures and eagles float up on rising warm air without flapping.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Convection carries heat through liquids and gases. Heated fluid expands, becomes less dense and rises; cooler fluid sinks to take its place, forming a convection current. In rooms, warm air collects near the ceiling, so heaters go low and coolers go high. In nature, convection makes sea and land breezes, thermals and afternoon thunderstorms.
