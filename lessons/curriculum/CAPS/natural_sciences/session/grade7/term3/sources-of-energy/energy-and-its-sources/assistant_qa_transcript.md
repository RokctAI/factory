### subtopic_1 — Energy Makes Things Happen
**Assistant:** Why do food labels give energy in kilojoules, and how much energy does a person need?

**Tutor:** Because food is a fuel for the body, and its energy can be measured in the same unit as the energy in petrol or electricity, the joule. Scientists measure the energy in food by burning a dried sample in a sealed container surrounded by water and measuring how much the water warms up; the body releases a similar amount of energy more slowly, by respiration. Food labels in South Africa show kilojoules per serving and per hundred grams, because whole foods contain many thousands of joules. Some countries still use an older unit, the calorie, on labels; one food calorie equals about 4,2 kilojoules. How much energy a person needs depends on age, sex, body size and activity. A typical Grade 7 learner needs somewhere around eight thousand to ten thousand kilojoules a day, more during growth spurts and for very active learners; an adult doing heavy physical work may need more than twelve thousand. That energy is used not only for movement but also for the basic work of staying alive: keeping the heart beating, breathing, maintaining body temperature, growing and repairing tissues and running the brain, which alone uses about a fifth of the body's energy at rest. If a person regularly takes in more energy than they use, the extra is stored as fat; if they take in less, the body uses its stores and they lose mass. Reading kilojoules on labels helps people compare foods; a 500 millilitre sugary cooldrink, for example, can contain around 900 kilojoules with almost no other nutrients.

### subtopic_2 — Non-renewable Sources
**Assistant:** Why can South Africa not simply stop using coal tomorrow?

**Tutor:** Because coal is woven into almost every part of the country's energy system and economy, and replacing it takes time, money and careful planning, even though most experts agree the country must move away from it. Coal-fired power stations generate most of South Africa's electricity, and the country's homes, factories, mines, hospitals and trains depend on that supply. The power stations, mines, railway lines and workers' skills represent decades of investment. Tens of thousands of people work in coal mining, and many towns in Mpumalanga depend on coal for jobs and local businesses; closing mines suddenly would cause great hardship. Replacing coal power requires building large amounts of solar and wind generation, together with ways to store energy or provide power when the Sun is not shining and the wind is not blowing, such as batteries, pumped water storage and gas; and it requires new transmission lines to carry electricity from sunny and windy areas, mostly in the Northern, Western and Eastern Cape, to where it is used. All of this costs a great deal and takes years. At the same time, there are strong reasons to move: coal pollution harms health, especially on the Highveld; the old power stations are increasingly unreliable, which contributed to load-shedding; and climate change threatens South Africa with more droughts and heat. South Africa has therefore committed to a just energy transition, aiming to shift gradually to cleaner energy while retraining workers and supporting coal communities, a process that people continue to debate.

### subtopic_3 — Renewable Sources
**Assistant:** How does a biogas digester turn cow dung into cooking gas?

**Tutor:** By letting bacteria do the work in a sealed tank without oxygen. A biogas digester is a closed container, often a brick or plastic tank partly buried in the ground. Fresh animal manure, such as cow dung, is mixed with water and fed into the tank through an inlet pipe; food scraps and crop waste can also be added. Inside, with no oxygen present, communities of bacteria break down the organic matter in stages, first into simpler substances and acids, and finally into a mixture of gases called biogas, which is mostly methane with a large share of carbon dioxide. This is the same process that produces gas in landfills and swamps. The biogas collects at the top of the tank, and a pipe carries it to a simple gas stove or lamp in the home, where the methane burns with a clean blue flame. The leftover material, called digestate or slurry, flows out of an outlet and makes an excellent fertiliser for gardens and fields. A household digester fed with dung from a few cows can supply much of a family's cooking gas. Biogas has several advantages for rural households in South Africa: it replaces firewood, saving time spent collecting it and protecting trees; it removes smoky indoor fires, protecting the family's lungs; it uses waste that would otherwise attract flies; and it produces fertiliser. Several projects in the Eastern Cape, KwaZulu-Natal and Limpopo have installed household digesters, though they need regular feeding and maintenance to keep working.
