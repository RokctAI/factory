# Part 1 — Expert

This session opens the Energy and Change strand by asking what energy is and where it comes from. The anchor is a single evening in a South African home during load-shedding: the lights go off, the stove stops, the cellphone is on its last bar, and the family switches to a gas cooker, a battery lamp and a small solar light charged on the windowsill during the day. In one evening the household uses electricity from coal, gas from oil fields, chemical energy in batteries and energy from sunlight. The governing idea is that energy is needed to make everything work, move or live, that energy comes from sources, and that sources are either non-renewable, which will run out because they are used far faster than they form, or renewable, which are replaced naturally. The meaning of energy, the main sources and their advantages and disadvantages are examinable.

## Subtopic: Energy Makes Things Happen

Energy is what is needed to make things happen: to make things work, move, grow, heat up, light up or make sound. A car needs energy to move, a kettle needs energy to heat water, a cellphone needs energy to light its screen and send messages, a plant needs energy to grow, and a person needs energy to walk, think, keep warm and keep the heart beating. Without energy, nothing would change.

Energy is measured in joules, named after the English scientist James Joule. One joule is a small amount: roughly the energy needed to lift an apple one metre. Larger amounts are measured in kilojoules, where one kilojoule is one thousand joules. Food labels in South Africa show energy in kilojoules: a slice of bread provides roughly 300 to 400 kilojoules.

Energy exists in different forms and can change from one form to another. The forms met in Grade 7 include: chemical potential energy stored in food, fuels and batteries; kinetic energy, the energy of moving things; gravitational potential energy stored in raised objects; elastic potential energy in stretched or squashed objects; heat, or thermal energy; light; sound; and electrical energy carried by an electric current. A gas stove changes chemical energy in the gas into heat and light; a cellphone changes chemical energy in its battery into electrical energy, then into light and sound; a person changes chemical energy from food into kinetic energy and heat.

Living things need energy too. Plants capture the energy of sunlight in photosynthesis and store it as chemical energy in food. Animals obtain that energy by eating plants or other animals, and release it by respiration. So the energy in a plate of pap and vegetables began as sunlight.

## Subtopic: Non-renewable Sources

An energy source is something from which useful energy can be obtained. Non-renewable sources are those that cannot be replaced within a human lifetime, or at all, so they will eventually run out.

Fossil fuels are coal, crude oil and natural gas. They formed over millions of years from the remains of plants and tiny sea organisms that were buried and squeezed under heat and pressure. Coal formed mostly from ancient swamp forests; oil and gas formed mostly from microscopic sea plankton. When fossil fuels are burned, the chemical energy stored in them, which originally came from sunlight captured by those ancient organisms, is released as heat. South Africa depends heavily on coal, mined mainly in Mpumalanga and Limpopo: most of the country's electricity is generated by burning coal in power stations, and coal is also turned into liquid fuels. Petrol, diesel, paraffin and LPG are made from oil, most of which South Africa imports.

Fossil fuels have advantages: they are concentrated stores of energy, relatively cheap to use with existing equipment, and available at any time of day or night in any weather. But they have serious disadvantages. They are being used millions of times faster than they form, so they will run out; burning them releases carbon dioxide, the main cause of climate change; burning coal releases air pollutants that harm health; and mining and drilling damage land and water.

Nuclear fuels are the other non-renewable source. Uranium, a radioactive metal, is used in nuclear power stations, where the nuclei of uranium atoms are split in a controlled chain reaction, releasing enormous amounts of heat, which is used to boil water and drive turbines. South Africa's only nuclear power station, Koeberg, near Cape Town, supplies a few per cent of the country's electricity. Nuclear power produces very little carbon dioxide and a tiny amount of fuel gives a huge amount of energy, but it produces radioactive waste that remains dangerous for thousands of years and must be stored safely, power stations are very expensive to build, and accidents, though rare, can be severe, as at Chernobyl in 1986 and Fukushima in 2011.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of non-renewable, how fossil fuels formed, the advantages and disadvantages of fossil fuels and of nuclear fuel.

## Subtopic: Renewable Sources

Renewable sources of energy are replaced naturally as fast as they are used, so they will not run out on a human timescale.

Solar energy is energy from sunlight. Solar panels, made of silicon cells, change light directly into electricity; solar water heaters use sunlight to heat water in pipes and tanks on roofs. South Africa has some of the strongest sunshine in the world, especially in the Northern Cape, where large solar farms have been built, and rooftop panels have spread rapidly since load-shedding. Concentrated solar power plants, such as those near Upington, use mirrors to focus sunlight and produce steam. Solar energy does not work at night and is reduced by cloud, so it needs batteries or other sources as backup.

Wind energy uses moving air to turn the blades of wind turbines, which drive generators. Wind farms have been built in the Eastern Cape, Western Cape and Northern Cape, where winds are strong and steady. Wind does not always blow, and turbines can affect birds and bats if poorly sited.

Hydro power uses flowing or falling water to turn turbines. Water stored behind a dam is released through pipes to turn turbines at the bottom. South Africa has limited rivers suitable for hydro power, but the Gariep and Vanderkloof dams on the Orange River produce some, and the country imports hydro power from Cahora Bassa in Mozambique. Pumped storage schemes, such as Drakensberg and Ingula, pump water uphill when electricity is cheap and release it to generate power at times of high demand.

Biofuel is fuel made from recently living material, called biomass. Wood and charcoal are traditional biofuels; others include biogas, made by bacteria breaking down animal manure and food waste in sealed tanks, and liquid fuels such as bioethanol from sugar cane and biodiesel from plant oils. Biofuels are renewable if the plants are regrown, but burning wood indoors causes smoke pollution, and growing fuel crops can compete with food crops for land and water.

Renewable sources produce little or no carbon dioxide or air pollution while generating electricity, and they will not run out, but many are intermittent, depending on weather and time of day, and need large areas and initial investment.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: calling electricity a source; it is a way of carrying energy produced from a source such as coal or sunlight. Two: saying renewable means free; equipment costs money. Three: saying fossil fuels are renewable because plants still grow; they take millions of years to form. Four: saying solar panels work equally well at night. Five: thinking nuclear energy is renewable; uranium is mined and will run out.

The questions for this section are with you now: the main renewable sources with South African examples, their advantages and disadvantages, and a comparison of renewable and non-renewable sources.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a load-shedding evening, a fuel that took millions of years, and the Sun on your roof — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Nothing Happens Without Energy

It is load-shedding. The lights are off, the stove is cold and the TV is silent. Suddenly you notice how much we use energy.

Energy is what makes things happen. It makes things move, heat up, light up, make sound and grow. A car needs it to drive. A kettle needs it to boil. You need it to run, think and keep warm.

We measure energy in joules. Food labels show kilojoules; a slice of bread gives a few hundred kilojoules.

Energy comes in different forms, and it can change from one form to another. The gas cooker changes the energy stored in gas into heat. A torch changes the energy in its battery into light. You change the energy in your food into movement and warmth. And the energy in your food came from the Sun, captured by plants.

## Subtopic: Energy That Runs Out

Most of South Africa's electricity comes from burning coal in power stations in Mpumalanga. Coal, oil and gas are called fossil fuels, because they formed from plants and tiny sea creatures that died millions of years ago and were squashed underground.

They are non-renewable. That means they took millions of years to make, but we are using them up in a few hundred years. One day they will run out.

They also cause problems. Burning them makes smoke that hurts people's lungs, and gives off carbon dioxide, which is heating up the planet.

Nuclear power uses uranium, a metal dug from the ground. South Africa's Koeberg power station near Cape Town uses it. It makes very little smoke, but its waste stays dangerous for thousands of years, and uranium will run out too.

Hold the coal power station in mind and try this part's questions.

## Subtopic: Energy That Comes Back

Some energy sources will never run out, because nature keeps refilling them. They are renewable.

Sunlight: solar panels on roofs turn sunlight into electricity, and solar geysers heat water. South Africa is one of the sunniest countries in the world.

Wind: giant wind turbines in the Cape turn moving air into electricity.

Water: water rushing out of a dam spins turbines. That is hydro power.

Biofuel: wood, and even gas made by bacteria from cow dung, can be used for cooking.

These make little or no smoke. The catch? The Sun sets every evening and the wind does not always blow, so we need batteries or backup. That is why the family in the dark used a small solar light charged on the windowsill during the day.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Energy makes things work, move and live, and it is measured in joules. Non-renewable sources, coal, oil, gas and uranium, will run out, and fossil fuels pollute and cause climate change. Renewable sources, sunlight, wind, water and biofuel, are refilled by nature and are cleaner, but they depend on weather and need backup.
