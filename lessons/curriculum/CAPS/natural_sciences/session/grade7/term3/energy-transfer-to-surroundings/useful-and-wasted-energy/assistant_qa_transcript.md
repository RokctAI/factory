### subtopic_1 — Useful and Wasted Energy Outputs
**Assistant:** Is the heat from a light bulb always wasted, even on a cold night?

**Tutor:** In the strict scientific sense of a light bulb's job, yes, the heat is wasted, because the bulb's purpose is to give light, and the heat does not help it do that; but whether the heat is useful to you depends on what you want at the time. Scientists judge waste against the device's intended purpose. A light bulb is designed to change electrical energy into light. An old filament bulb changes only about five to ten percent of its input into light; the rest becomes heat. So we say ninety percent or more is wasted. On a cold night in a small room, you might feel that heat warming you slightly, and in that sense it is not entirely useless. Some people even used old bulbs to keep chicks warm or to stop pipes freezing. But if you want warmth, a bulb is a poor heater: a proper heater turns almost all its input into heat, while a bulb gives you a little warmth at the cost of making light you may not need. And in summer, the heat from many bulbs makes rooms hotter, so air conditioners must work harder, wasting even more energy. That is why South Africa phased out most old filament bulbs in favour of LED bulbs, which give the same light while wasting far less as heat, using roughly a fifth of the electricity. So the rule in examinations is: decide what the device is for, and any other output is wasted energy.

### subtopic_2 — Output Is Always Less Than Input
**Assistant:** Why does a petrol car waste so much of its fuel's energy?

**Tutor:** Because a petrol engine works by burning fuel to make hot gases push pistons, and most of the heat produced simply cannot be turned into movement; it has to escape through the exhaust and the cooling system. Inside each cylinder, a mixture of petrol and air is squeezed and then ignited by a spark. It burns in a tiny explosion, producing very hot, high-pressure gases that push the piston down; the pistons turn the crankshaft, and through the gearbox, the wheels. But to keep working, the engine must get rid of the hot gas after every push to make room for fresh fuel, and that gas is still extremely hot when it leaves through the exhaust pipe, carrying away perhaps a third of the fuel's energy. Another large share heats the engine metal itself; this must be removed by the cooling system, where water circulates through the engine and radiator and fans blow air through it, or the engine would overheat and seize. Friction between moving parts, in the engine, gearbox and wheel bearings, turns more energy into heat; the engine noise is more waste. Of each litre of petrol, only about a quarter ends up moving the car, and then braking turns that kinetic energy into heat as well. Electric motors waste much less, because they do not need to burn anything, and many electric and hybrid cars also use regenerative braking, turning some kinetic energy back into stored electrical energy instead of brake heat.

### subtopic_3 — Reducing Wasted Energy
**Assistant:** Does leaving a phone charger plugged in at the wall really waste electricity?

**Tutor:** Yes, a little, and many devices on standby together waste a surprising amount over a year. A phone charger contains a small transformer and circuits that change the high-voltage alternating current from the wall into the low-voltage direct current a phone needs. While it is plugged in and switched on at the wall, these circuits stay powered, even with no phone attached, and a small current flows, turning electrical energy into heat. You can sometimes feel that an idle charger is slightly warm: that warmth is wasted energy. A modern charger wastes very little, less than half a watt, but older ones wasted more. The bigger culprits are appliances on standby: televisions, decoders, sound systems, microwave ovens with clocks, computers and game consoles. Each may use a few watts constantly, day and night, to keep a small light on or listen for a remote control. In a home with ten such devices, standby can add up to several percent of the electricity bill, and across millions of households in the country, it equals the output of a large part of a power station. Switching off at the wall, or using a multi-plug with a switch, stops this waste completely. During load-shedding, it is also wise to switch appliances off, since a surge when the power returns can damage them.
