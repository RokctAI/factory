# Part 1 — Expert

This session finds potential and kinetic energy at work in four kinds of system. The anchor is a single morning on a sugar-cane farm near Stanger in KwaZulu-Natal: a worker eats breakfast and swings a cane knife, a tractor's diesel engine roars, a kettle boils on a gas ring, and the farm's water pump runs on electricity from the grid. A system is a group of parts that work together and transfer energy. The governing idea is that the same two kinds of energy, stored and moving, appear in mechanical, thermal, electrical and biological systems, and that each system has an input of energy, changes it from one form to another and produces outputs. Identifying potential and kinetic energy in each kind of system, and describing the energy changes in simple systems, are examinable.

## Subtopic: Mechanical Systems

A system is a set of parts that work together. In energy studies, it is useful to describe a system by its energy input, the energy it takes in; the changes that happen inside it; and its output, the useful energy it gives out, together with any energy that is wasted.

Mechanical systems involve forces and movement: machines with moving parts, such as levers, wheels, pulleys, gears, springs and engines, and simple moving objects such as balls and swings. In mechanical systems, potential energy is usually gravitational or elastic, and kinetic energy is the energy of the moving parts.

A bicycle is a mechanical system. The input is the rider pushing the pedals. The gears and chain transfer the energy to the back wheel, which turns, and the bicycle and rider gain kinetic energy. Riding up a hill, some of that kinetic energy changes into gravitational potential energy; free-wheeling down the other side, the potential energy changes back into kinetic energy. Some energy is always lost as heat and sound, from friction in the chain, the tyres on the road and air pushing against the rider.

A wind-up toy shows elastic potential energy: winding the key stores energy in a coiled spring; releasing it lets the spring unwind and turn the wheels, changing elastic potential energy into kinetic energy. A bow and arrow, a trampoline, and a pole vaulter's bending pole work in the same way.

A water mill, or a hydro-electric turbine, takes in the kinetic energy of moving water, often gained from gravitational potential energy as the water fell from a height, and changes it into the kinetic energy of the turning wheel, which can grind grain or drive a generator.

A pendulum clock and a playground swing show potential and kinetic energy changing back and forth, while friction slowly removes energy so that, without a push, the swing gradually stops.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of system, input and output, and the potential and kinetic energy in a bicycle, a wind-up toy and a water mill.

## Subtopic: Thermal and Electrical Systems

Thermal systems involve heating and cooling. Here, energy is stored as the chemical potential energy of fuels and released as heat, and heat itself is linked to kinetic energy: all matter is made of tiny particles that are constantly moving, and when a substance is heated, its particles move faster, so their kinetic energy increases. A hotter object has particles with more kinetic energy than a colder one.

A gas stove and kettle form a thermal system. The input is the chemical potential energy stored in the gas. When the gas burns, this energy is released as heat and light. The heat flows into the kettle and the water, making the water particles move faster; the water's temperature rises until it boils, when the particles have enough kinetic energy to escape as steam. Some heat is lost to the air around the kettle. A fire, a solar water heater, a car engine and the human body, which keeps itself warm, are other thermal systems.

Electrical systems involve electric circuits. Here, potential energy is stored in batteries and cells as chemical potential energy, or provided by a power station; kinetic energy is linked to the movement of charges through the wires, carrying electrical energy around the circuit. At the components, electrical energy changes into other forms: light in a bulb, heat in a kettle element or iron, kinetic energy in a fan or electric motor, and sound in a speaker.

A torch is a simple electrical system: chemical potential energy in the batteries is changed into electrical energy, which flows through the circuit when the switch is closed and is changed into light, and some heat, in the bulb. An electric water pump on the farm changes electrical energy into the kinetic energy of the pump and the water, and some of that into gravitational potential energy when water is pumped up to a tank on a stand.

Many systems combine types. In a power station, the chemical potential energy of coal is released as heat, a thermal process, which boils water into high-pressure steam; the steam's kinetic energy spins a turbine, a mechanical process; and the turbine turns a generator, which produces electrical energy.

The questions for this section are with you now: how heating increases the kinetic energy of particles, the energy changes in a gas kettle and a torch, and the chain of changes in a coal power station.

## Subtopic: Biological Systems

Living organisms are biological systems, and they too store and use potential and kinetic energy.

Plants are the entry point. In photosynthesis, they take in light energy from the Sun and store it as chemical potential energy in sugars and starch. A mealie plant stores this energy in its grains; sugar cane stores it in the sweet juice of its stems; a potato stores it as starch in its tubers.

Animals take in chemical potential energy in their food. In their cells, respiration releases this energy, and the animal uses it for its life processes. Muscles change chemical energy into kinetic energy when the animal moves: the cane cutter swinging a knife, a cheetah sprinting, a bird flapping its wings, a heart beating. Some energy is also changed into heat, which keeps birds and mammals warm. An animal can also have gravitational potential energy, such as a leopard resting on a high branch, and elastic potential energy, such as a kangaroo's stretched leg tendons, which store and return energy as it hops.

Energy flows through ecosystems in food chains. Grass captures sunlight and stores it; a cow eats the grass and stores some of the energy in its body; a person eats meat or drinks milk from the cow. At each step, much of the energy is used for life processes and lost as heat, so only a small part is passed on. That is why there are many more plants than herbivores, and many more herbivores than carnivores.

A human body is a complex system: the input is chemical potential energy in food; respiration releases it; the outputs are kinetic energy in muscles, heat to maintain body temperature, electrical signals in nerves, sound when speaking, and chemical energy stored in new tissues during growth, with any extra stored as fat.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: thinking potential and kinetic energy belong only to moving machines; they appear in every type of system. Two: saying hot objects have more potential energy because they are hot; their particles have more kinetic energy. Three: thinking a plant uses energy from the soil to grow; it uses sunlight. Four: forgetting the wasted heat in every system. Five: mixing up input and output.

The questions for this section are with you now: how plants store energy, how animals use stored energy for movement and warmth, energy in food chains, and the human body as an energy system.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a farm morning, a kettle and a cane cutter — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Machines That Move

A system is a set of parts that work together. Every system takes energy in, changes it, and gives energy out.

Ride a bicycle. Your legs push the pedals, the chain turns the back wheel, and you move: that is movement energy, kinetic energy. Ride up a hill and you slow down, because your speed turns into height energy. Coast down the other side and the height energy turns back into speed.

Wind up a toy car. The spring inside stores stretch energy. Let go and the spring unwinds, turning the wheels.

A water wheel takes the movement of a river and uses it to turn and grind grain.

But in every machine, a little energy leaks away as heat and squeaking sounds, because parts rub together. That is why a swing stops if nobody pushes it.

Keep the bicycle in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: Hot and Electric

Heat your tea and the tiny particles in the water start moving faster and faster. So a hot thing has particles with more movement energy.

A gas kettle: the gas stores chemical energy. Burn it and you get heat, which makes the water particles move faster until the water boils.

A torch: the batteries store chemical energy. Press the switch and electricity flows through the wires to the bulb, which gives out light and a little heat.

A coal power station does a whole chain: burning coal makes heat, heat boils water into steam, rushing steam spins a turbine, and the turbine spins a generator that makes electricity for your house.

Hold the kettle and the torch in mind and try this part's questions.

## Subtopic: Living Engines

You are a living energy system too.

Plants catch sunlight and store it as chemical energy in their leaves, stems, grains and roots: a mealie cob, a stick of sugar cane, a potato.

Animals eat plants, or eat animals that ate plants. Inside their cells, the stored energy is released. Muscles turn it into movement, like a cane cutter swinging a knife or a cheetah sprinting. Some becomes heat, which keeps you warm.

A leopard lying on a high branch has stored height energy too.

In a food chain, from grass to cow to person, only a little energy passes along at each step. Most of it is used up or lost as heat.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A system takes in energy, changes it and gives energy out, with some always wasted as heat. In mechanical systems, energy moves between height, stretch and movement. In thermal systems, heat makes particles move faster. In electrical systems, batteries and power stations provide electrical energy that becomes light, heat or movement. In biological systems, plants store sunlight as chemical energy and animals turn it into movement and warmth.

Hold the bicycle, the kettle and the cane cutter, and take the final questions of the lesson.
