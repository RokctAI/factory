### subtopic_1 — The Law of Conservation of Energy
**Assistant:** If energy can never be destroyed, why do we worry about running out of energy?

**Tutor:** Because what runs out is not energy itself but useful, concentrated stores of energy that we can easily control, and every time we use them, the energy spreads out into forms we cannot gather up again. The law of conservation of energy is completely true: when a car burns a tank of petrol, every bit of the chemical potential energy that was in the fuel still exists afterwards. But where is it? Some moved the car and was then changed into heat in the brakes and tyres; much went straight out of the exhaust and radiator as heat; some became the sound of the engine. All of it ends up as a slight warming of the air, roads and surroundings, spread over a huge area. That warmth is real energy, but it is so thinly spread that no machine can collect it and use it to drive the car again. A litre of petrol is a concentrated, portable store that took millions of years to form from ancient plants and animals; once burned, it is gone as a useful source. The same is true for coal in power stations. So the problem is a shrinking supply of useful energy sources, and the pollution caused by burning them. That is why we try to use energy carefully, with efficient appliances that waste less, and why renewable sources such as sunlight and wind matter: the Sun keeps sending fresh, useful energy every day.

### subtopic_2 — Energy Transfer Within a System
**Assistant:** How does a wind-up radio work without batteries or plugs, if energy cannot be created?

**Tutor:** It does not create energy; it stores the energy you put in with your own arm, and that energy came from your food, which came from sunlight. When you turn the crank, your muscles change chemical potential energy from your breakfast into kinetic energy of the handle. Inside, gears turn a small generator, a coil of wire spinning near magnets, which changes the kinetic energy into electrical energy. That electrical energy charges a small rechargeable battery or a capacitor, where it is stored as potential energy, or in older designs, the handle winds a strong spring. When you switch the radio on, the stored energy is released slowly as electrical energy to the circuits, which pick up radio signals, and to the speaker, which changes electrical energy into sound. About a minute of winding can give many minutes of listening, because a radio needs only a small amount of energy each second, not because the radio makes any extra. Some energy is wasted at every stage: the gears get slightly warm, and the generator and circuits give off heat. Wind-up radios were designed for places with no electricity and expensive batteries, and many were made in Cape Town in the 1990s for use across Africa, so that people in rural areas could hear news and health information. The radio shows the law of conservation clearly: energy in from your arm equals sound out plus heat wasted, never more.

### subtopic_3 — Energy Transfer Between Systems
**Assistant:** Could someone ever build a machine that keeps running forever without any energy input?

**Tutor:** No, and the law of conservation of energy, together with the fact that every real machine wastes some energy as heat, explains why. People have tried for hundreds of years to build such perpetual-motion machines: wheels with weights that are supposed to keep turning, magnets arranged to push each other round in a circle, water wheels that pump their own water back up. Every one has failed. Think about what such a machine would need. If it gives out useful energy, say to light a bulb, that energy must come from somewhere; with no input, it would have to be created, which the law forbids. Even a machine that does no useful work at all, only keeping itself moving, cannot last, because every moving part rubs against something, and friction changes a little kinetic energy into heat each turn. Air resistance takes more. The energy for movement steadily drains away into the surroundings, and the machine slows and stops, just like a swing that nobody pushes. Patent offices in many countries refuse to consider perpetual-motion designs at all, because the idea has been tested so often. What does exist are machines with very low friction that run for a long time, like a spinning top on a smooth surface or a flywheel in a vacuum, but they still slow down eventually. Every real engine, generator and body needs a continuing supply of energy from fuel, food, electricity, wind or sunlight.
