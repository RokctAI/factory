### subtopic_1 — Potential Energy: Stored Energy
**Assistant:** Why does water behind a high dam store more energy than water behind a low dam?

**Tutor:** Because gravitational potential energy depends on both the mass of the object and the height it is raised above the point where it will fall, so each kilogram of water high up behind a tall dam wall stores more energy than each kilogram behind a low one. To raise any object, energy must be used to lift it against the pull of gravity; that energy is then stored in the raised object as gravitational potential energy and can be released when it falls back down. In a natural river, the Sun's heat does the lifting: it evaporates water from the sea, the vapour rises, forms clouds and falls as rain on high ground, so water in the mountains has a lot of stored energy, which is why mountain streams rush. A dam holds back a large mass of this water at a height. When water is released through pipes from near the top of the reservoir to turbines at the bottom, it falls through the height difference, called the head, and its potential energy changes into kinetic energy, which spins the turbines that drive generators. The greater the height of the fall and the more water flows each second, the more electricity is produced. That is why hydro-electric schemes are built where there is a big drop, such as in mountain gorges or near waterfalls. South Africa's pumped storage schemes, such as Ingula in the Drakensberg, use spare electricity to pump water up into a high reservoir, storing energy, and release it to generate electricity when demand peaks.

### subtopic_2 — Energy in Food
**Assistant:** Why does the body store extra energy as fat rather than as sugar?

**Tutor:** Because fat is the most compact and efficient way for the body to store large amounts of energy. The body does store some energy as a sugar-based substance called glycogen, in the liver and muscles, which is useful because it can be broken down quickly when energy is needed in a hurry, for example during a sprint. But glycogen is stored together with a lot of water, about three grams of water for each gram of glycogen, so it is heavy and bulky for the energy it holds, and the body can store only a limited amount, enough for roughly a day of normal activity. Fat, on the other hand, stores about 37 kilojoules per gram, more than twice as much as carbohydrate, and is stored almost without water. Taking the water into account, a kilogram of body fat holds several times as much usable energy as a kilogram of stored glycogen. If the body had to store the same energy as glycogen, a person would be much heavier and slower. This efficiency was important for survival in the past, when food was not always available, and it is why animals that hibernate or migrate long distances, such as birds crossing the Sahara on their way to and from South Africa each year, build up fat before the journey. Fat also insulates the body and cushions organs. The difficulty in modern life, where energy-dense food is easy to obtain and many people are less active, is that the same efficient storage can lead to too much body fat, which increases the risk of diseases such as diabetes and heart disease.

### subtopic_3 — Kinetic Energy: Energy of Motion
**Assistant:** Why is speeding so much more dangerous than it seems, if it only adds a little speed?

**Tutor:** Because kinetic energy increases much faster than speed does, so a modest increase in speed produces a large increase in the energy that must be got rid of in a crash or when braking. Kinetic energy depends on the square of the speed: if speed doubles, kinetic energy becomes four times as large; if speed triples, kinetic energy becomes nine times as large. So a car travelling at 120 kilometres per hour has four times the kinetic energy it would have at 60 kilometres per hour, not twice. When a driver brakes, the brakes must change all that kinetic energy into heat by friction, and since they can only do this at a certain rate, the braking distance also increases roughly four times when speed doubles. On a wet road or with worn tyres it is even longer. Add the distance travelled while the driver reacts, about one second or more, which also grows with speed, and a car at high speed may need well over a hundred metres to stop. In a crash, all that kinetic energy is changed in a fraction of a second into the energy of bending metal, breaking glass, heat, sound and, tragically, injury to the people inside and outside the vehicle. Pedestrians are especially vulnerable: research shows a pedestrian hit at 30 kilometres per hour usually survives, while one hit at 60 or more is much more likely to be killed. This is why speed limits are lower near schools and in towns, and why road safety campaigns in South Africa focus so strongly on speed.
