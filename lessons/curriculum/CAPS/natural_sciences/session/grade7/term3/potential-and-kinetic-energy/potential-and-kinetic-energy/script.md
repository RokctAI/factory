# Part 1 — Expert

This session defines the two great categories of energy: stored energy, called potential energy, and the energy of motion, called kinetic energy. The anchor is a boy on a swing at a park in Pietermaritzburg: at the top of each swing he pauses, high and still, and at the bottom he rushes through fastest, and all the while the sandwich he ate at lunch is supplying the energy his muscles use to pump the swing higher. The governing idea is that potential energy is energy stored in an object because of its position, its shape or its chemical make-up, including the chemical energy in food, which is measured in joules; and that kinetic energy is the energy an object has because it is moving, which is greater for faster and heavier objects. The kinds of potential energy, energy in food and how to read it on labels, and the factors affecting kinetic energy are examinable, without calculations.

## Subtopic: Potential Energy: Stored Energy

Potential energy is stored energy, energy that an object or substance has because of its position, its condition or what it is made of. It is called potential because it has the potential, the possibility, to do something later, when it is released.

Gravitational potential energy is stored in an object because it is raised above the ground. The higher it is lifted and the heavier it is, the more gravitational potential energy it stores. A rock at the top of a cliff, water held behind the wall of a dam, a learner at the top of a slide and the boy at the top of his swing all have gravitational potential energy. When they fall or move down, this stored energy is released.

Elastic potential energy, sometimes called strain energy, is stored in an object that has been stretched, squashed, bent or twisted, and that can spring back. A stretched catapult, a drawn bow, a wound-up spring in a toy, a compressed spring in a mattress and a stretched rubber band all store elastic potential energy. Let go, and the energy is released as the object springs back.

Chemical potential energy is stored in substances and is released when they react. It is stored in fuels such as coal, wood, petrol and gas, released when they burn; in batteries, released as electrical energy when a circuit is completed; in matches; in explosives; and in food, released in the body by respiration.

The amount of potential energy does not show; a full dam and an empty dam may look similar from far away, and a charged battery looks just like a flat one. Potential energy is hidden until it is released and changed into another form, such as kinetic energy, heat, light or sound.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of potential energy, the three kinds of potential energy with examples, and what increases gravitational potential energy.

## Subtopic: Energy in Food

Food is a store of chemical potential energy. Plants capture energy from sunlight in photosynthesis and store it in the chemical bonds of sugars, starch, fats and proteins. When we eat food, our cells release that energy by respiration, using oxygen, and use it to move, grow, repair the body, keep warm and keep every organ working.

The energy in food is measured in joules, and because foods contain large amounts, kilojoules are used on labels. In South Africa, food labels must show the energy content per serving and per one hundred grams or one hundred millilitres, together with the amounts of nutrients such as carbohydrates, protein and fat.

Different nutrients store different amounts of energy. Fats store the most, roughly 37 kilojoules per gram, more than twice as much as carbohydrates and proteins, which store roughly 17 kilojoules per gram each. That is why fatty foods such as cooking oil, butter, peanuts and fried chips are energy-dense, and why animals, including people, store excess energy as body fat: it packs a lot of energy into a small mass. Foods with lots of water and fibre, such as vegetables and fruit, contain fewer kilojoules per hundred grams.

Comparing labels is a useful skill. One hundred grams of white bread contains roughly a thousand kilojoules; one hundred grams of peanuts roughly two and a half thousand; one hundred grams of apple roughly two hundred. A person's daily needs depend on age and activity. Active people need more energy; people who take in more energy than they use, over a long time, store the extra as fat, while those who take in too little lose mass and may not grow properly. A healthy diet balances energy with a variety of nutrients, vitamins and minerals; a sugary cooldrink provides energy but very few other nutrients.

Grade 7 does not require energy calculations, but reading and comparing labels in kilojoules is examinable.

The questions for this section are with you now: why food stores chemical potential energy, how energy in food is measured and shown on labels, and why fats are energy-dense.

## Subtopic: Kinetic Energy: Energy of Motion

Kinetic energy is the energy an object has because it is moving. Everything that moves has kinetic energy: a running learner, a rolling ball, a speeding taxi, flowing water, blowing wind, a flying bird, the vibrating particles of a hot object, and the boy on the swing as he rushes through the bottom of his arc.

The amount of kinetic energy depends on two things. First, speed: the faster an object moves, the more kinetic energy it has. Speed matters a great deal; doubling the speed of a car gives it about four times as much kinetic energy, which is why speeding is so dangerous and why a crash at 120 kilometres per hour is far more destructive than one at 60. Second, mass: the heavier an object, the more kinetic energy it has at the same speed. A loaded truck moving at the same speed as a small car has much more kinetic energy, which is why it needs a much longer distance to stop. An object that is not moving has no kinetic energy.

Kinetic energy can do work when the moving object hits something or is slowed down. A hammer's kinetic energy drives a nail into wood; moving wind turns a wind turbine; moving water turns a water wheel or turbine; a thrown ball can break a window. When a moving object is brought to a stop, its kinetic energy changes into other forms, such as heat in the brakes of a car, sound and the energy of bending metal in a crash.

On the swing, energy changes back and forth. At the top of each swing the boy is momentarily still, so he has no kinetic energy but the most gravitational potential energy. As he swings down, potential energy changes into kinetic energy and he speeds up, reaching his greatest speed and kinetic energy at the lowest point. As he rises on the other side, kinetic energy changes back into potential energy and he slows down. The same pattern appears in a roller coaster, a pendulum and a ball thrown upwards.

The error museum, five exhibits. One: thinking a still object has no energy at all; it may have potential energy. Two: saying potential energy is only height; it can also be elastic or chemical. Three: saying kinetic energy depends only on speed; mass matters too. Four: thinking food energy is measured in grams; grams measure mass, kilojoules measure energy. Five: saying the swing has the most energy at the top only; it has the most potential energy there, but the most kinetic energy at the bottom.

The questions for this section are with you now: the meaning of kinetic energy, the two factors that affect it, examples of kinetic energy doing work, and the energy changes on a swing.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson again with a swing, a lunchbox and a speeding taxi — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Energy in Waiting

Some energy just sits there waiting. It is called potential energy, stored energy.

Climb to the top of a slide. Just by being up high, you have stored energy. Slide down and it turns into speed. Water held high behind a dam wall is the same: let it go and it rushes down.

Stretch a catapult or a rubber band. It stores energy in the stretch. Let go, and zing!

Food, petrol, wood and batteries hold stored energy too, in their chemicals. A battery looks the same full or flat; you cannot see the energy until it is used.

High up, stretched, or stored in chemicals: that is potential energy.

Keep the waiting energy in mind and try the questions for this part.

## Subtopic: Lunchbox Power

Your lunchbox is full of stored energy. Plants caught sunlight and stored it in food; when you eat, your body releases it to run, think and stay warm.

Food energy is measured in kilojoules, and every food label shows it. Fatty foods like peanuts, chips and oil pack in lots of kilojoules. Fruit and vegetables have fewer, because they are mostly water.

Your body needs enough energy to grow and play. If you take in much more than you use, your body stores the extra as fat. If you take in too little, you cannot grow properly. And remember: a cooldrink gives lots of energy but hardly any of the other good stuff your body needs.

Hold the lunchbox in mind and try this part's questions.

## Subtopic: On the Move

Anything that moves has kinetic energy, the energy of movement. A running learner, a rolling ball, a taxi, the wind.

Two things give more kinetic energy: going faster, and being heavier. A truck has more than a bicycle at the same speed. And speed matters a lot: double the speed and you get about four times the energy. That is why speeding cars are so dangerous.

Now watch the swing. At the top, the boy stops for a moment: lots of stored height energy, no movement energy. Swinging down, height energy turns into speed. At the bottom he is fastest, with the most kinetic energy. Going up the other side, speed turns back into height. Back and forth, back and forth.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Potential energy is stored energy: gravitational in raised objects, elastic in stretched ones, and chemical in food, fuels and batteries. Food energy is measured in kilojoules and comes from sunlight. Kinetic energy is the energy of moving things, and it is greater for faster and heavier objects. On a swing, energy changes between potential and kinetic.

Hold the swing, the lunchbox and the taxi, and take the final questions of the lesson.
