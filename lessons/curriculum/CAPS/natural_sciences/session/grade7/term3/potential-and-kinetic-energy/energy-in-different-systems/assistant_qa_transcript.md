### subtopic_1 — Mechanical Systems
**Assistant:** Why does a swing slowly stop if nobody pushes it, if energy just changes form?

**Tutor:** Because a little of the energy changes into forms that are not useful for swinging, namely heat and sound, and spreads out into the surroundings, so the swing's share of the energy gets smaller with every swing. On each swing, the energy does change between gravitational potential energy at the top and kinetic energy at the bottom, but not perfectly. The chains or ropes rub where they hang from the frame, and this friction turns a small amount of kinetic energy into heat in the hooks and chains, which you might notice as a squeaking sound, another small loss. The swing and the person also push against the air, and air resistance takes away more kinetic energy, slightly warming and stirring the air. Each time, the energy that remains for swinging is a little less, so the swing cannot rise as high as before, and after many swings it comes to rest at the bottom. The total amount of energy has not disappeared: it has been transferred to the surroundings as heat and sound, spread out so thinly that it is very hard to measure and impossible to use. To keep swinging, the person must keep adding energy, which they do by pumping with their legs, using chemical energy from food, or someone must push. Oiling the hooks reduces friction and makes the swing keep going longer. This idea, that energy is never destroyed but some always spreads out as heat, is the subject of the next lesson on the conservation of energy.

### subtopic_2 — Thermal and Electrical Systems
**Assistant:** How does a power station turn burning coal into electricity in my house?

**Tutor:** Through a chain of energy changes, each carried out by a different part of the station. It begins with chemical potential energy stored in coal, which was itself captured from sunlight by ancient plants. The coal is ground into a fine powder and blown into a huge furnace, where it burns, releasing the stored energy as heat. The heat boils water flowing through thousands of metres of tubes in a boiler, producing steam at very high temperature and pressure; the water particles now have a great deal of kinetic energy. This steam is directed onto the blades of a turbine, a large shaft with rows of blades like a giant fan, and the force of the steam makes the turbine spin at high speed, so the energy is now the kinetic energy of a rotating machine. The turbine is connected to a generator, in which coils of wire rotate near powerful magnets; this produces an electric current, changing kinetic energy into electrical energy. Transformers raise the voltage, and transmission lines carry the electricity across the country, through substations, to the distribution board in your home. After passing through the turbine, the steam is cooled back into water, often in the huge cooling towers that are visible from far away in Mpumalanga, and reused. At each stage, some energy is lost as heat, so only around a third of the energy in the coal ends up as electricity. The rest warms the surroundings, which is one reason why saving electricity at home saves so much coal.

### subtopic_3 — Biological Systems
**Assistant:** If plants store sunlight as chemical energy, why can we not eat grass like cows do?

**Tutor:** Because the energy in grass is mostly locked in cellulose, a tough substance that human digestive systems cannot break down, whereas cows have special partners that do it for them. Grass leaves are made of cells with thick walls of cellulose, which is built from long chains of glucose, the same sugar that provides energy in other foods. The chemical potential energy is there, but to release it the chains must be broken apart into glucose. Humans do not produce the necessary digestive chemicals, so cellulose passes through our gut largely undigested; it is useful as fibre, which helps digestion, but provides us with little energy. Cows, sheep, goats and antelope are ruminants with a stomach divided into four chambers. The largest chamber, the rumen, is a huge fermentation tank holding enormous numbers of bacteria, protists and fungi that can break down cellulose. The cow chews its food, swallows it into the rumen where the microbes work on it, brings it back up as cud to chew again, and swallows it again, slowly extracting the energy. The microbes produce substances the cow absorbs as food, and the cow also digests some of the microbes themselves. This partnership is why grazing animals can live on a diet that would starve a human, and why, by raising cattle and drinking milk or eating meat, people can indirectly use energy stored in grasslands. The fermentation also produces methane, which cattle release in burps, a greenhouse gas.
