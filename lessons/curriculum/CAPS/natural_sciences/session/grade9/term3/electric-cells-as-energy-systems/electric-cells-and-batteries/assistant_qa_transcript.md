### subtopic_1 — The Cell as an Energy System
**Assistant:** Why must the two electrodes be different materials?

**Tutor:** Because the voltage of a cell comes from the difference in how strongly the two electrode materials hold on to their electrons. Each material has its own tendency to give up electrons in contact with the electrolyte. Zinc gives up electrons quite readily, forming zinc ions that move into the electrolyte, and the electrons it leaves behind build up on the zinc, making it the negative electrode. Copper gives up electrons much less readily. When the two are connected by a wire, electrons flow from the zinc, where there are more, to the copper, where reactions take them up. The bigger the difference between the two materials in this tendency, the bigger the voltage. If both electrodes are made of the same metal, each has exactly the same tendency, so there is no difference to drive the electrons, and the voltage is zero. You can test this in the classroom: use two copper coins in a lemon and the voltmeter reads nothing; use zinc and copper and it reads just under 1 volt; use magnesium ribbon and copper, a bigger difference, and the reading is higher, around 1,5 to 2 volts. Chemists rank metals by this tendency in the electrochemical series, closely related to the reactivity series you met in Term 2, and in Grade 12 you will use it to calculate the voltages of cells precisely.

### subtopic_2 — Inside a Cell
**Assistant:** If a cell stores energy, why does it go flat even when I do not use it?

**Tutor:** Because slow chemical reactions continue inside the cell even when the circuit is open, gradually using up the chemicals. This is called self-discharge. No cell is perfectly sealed off: tiny side reactions take place between the electrodes and the electrolyte, and very small currents can leak through the electrolyte or across a damp, dirty case. The rate depends on the type of cell and the temperature. Alkaline cells lose only a few percent of their energy per year, which is why they can sit in a drawer for years and still work. Older nickel-cadmium and some nickel-metal hydride rechargeable cells can lose a noticeable fraction of their charge in a month. Lithium-ion cells lose a few percent per month. Heat speeds up all chemical reactions, including these unwanted ones, so cells stored in a hot car in summer go flat faster and age faster, while a cool, dry cupboard is better. Leaving devices with cells inside for a long time can cause another problem: as old cells run down, they can leak corrosive electrolyte that damages the contacts of a remote or a torch. Removing cells from devices that will not be used for months is a good habit. Car batteries also slowly self-discharge, which is why a car that stands for many weeks may not start.

### subtopic_3 — Batteries: Groups of Connected Cells
**Assistant:** How does a solar panel compare with a battery?

**Tutor:** They do different jobs: a solar panel converts light energy into electrical energy, while a battery stores energy in chemical form and releases it as electrical energy later. A solar panel is made of many solar cells, usually of silicon. When light falls on a solar cell, it gives energy to electrons in the silicon, which are pushed through an external circuit, producing a current. There is no chemical reaction and nothing is used up, so a solar cell works for decades, but only while light is falling on it, and its output falls on cloudy days and stops at night. A battery can deliver energy at any time, but only until its chemicals are used up, and then it must be recharged or replaced. That is why the two are so often used together in South Africa, especially since load shedding became common. During the day, solar panels on a roof power the home and, through a charge controller or inverter, recharge a battery bank. At night or during an outage, the batteries supply the stored energy. An inverter changes the low-voltage direct current from the batteries into the 230 volt alternating current that household appliances need. Large solar farms in the Northern Cape feed the national grid directly, and grid-scale battery systems are now being built to store their energy for the evening peak.

### subtopic_4 — Flat Cells, Recharging, Disposal and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why are lithium-ion batteries dangerous if they are damaged?

**Tutor:** Because they store a lot of energy in a small space and contain a flammable electrolyte, so a fault can release that energy as heat very quickly. A lithium-ion cell has a thin plastic separator between its positive and negative electrodes, soaked in an organic liquid electrolyte that can burn. If the cell is punctured, crushed, badly overcharged, overheated or has a manufacturing defect, the separator can fail and the electrodes can touch inside, creating a short circuit. The current then flows through the cell itself, heating it rapidly. As the temperature rises, more reactions start that release more heat, a runaway process called thermal runaway, and the cell can vent hot flammable gases, catch fire or even explode, and spread to neighbouring cells. Such fires are hard to put out. This is why airlines require spare lithium batteries and power banks to be carried in hand luggage, where any fire can be dealt with quickly, why you should only use chargers designed for your device, and why you should not leave phones charging under a pillow or in direct sun. A swollen, puffy battery is a warning sign and should be replaced and recycled safely. Used lithium batteries must never go into household rubbish, because they can be crushed in a refuse truck and start a fire. Battery recycling also recovers valuable lithium, cobalt and nickel.
