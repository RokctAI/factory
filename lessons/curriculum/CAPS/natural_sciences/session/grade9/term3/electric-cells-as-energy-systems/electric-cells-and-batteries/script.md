# Part 1 — Expert

This session describes an electric cell as an energy system in which chemical reactions cause an electric current to flow in an external circuit, explains the parts of a cell, shows how a simple fruit cell and a dry cell work, and defines a battery as a group of connected cells, including how connecting cells changes the voltage. The anchor facts are that a cell has two different electrodes and an electrolyte; that a common dry cell gives 1,5 volts; that a lemon cell with zinc and copper electrodes gives just under 1 volt; and that a 12 volt car battery consists of six 2 volt cells connected in series. The governing principle is that a cell transfers stored chemical potential energy to electrical energy, which the circuit then transfers to other forms such as light, heat, sound and movement, and that cells connected in series add their voltages.

## Subtopic: The Cell as an Energy System

A system is a set of parts that work together, with inputs, processes and outputs. An electric cell is an energy system. Its input is the chemical potential energy stored in its chemicals. Its process is a chemical reaction inside the cell. Its output is electrical energy, carried by a flow of charge, an electric current, through whatever is connected to it.

The cell itself is only part of the story. For a current to flow, the cell must be connected into a complete, closed circuit: a continuous conducting path that leaves one terminal of the cell, passes through components such as a bulb, a buzzer or a motor, and returns to the other terminal. If the circuit is broken anywhere, for example by an open switch or a loose wire, no current flows and the chemical reaction in the cell almost stops. This is why a torch left switched off does not go flat quickly.

In the external circuit, the components transfer the electrical energy into other forms. A bulb transfers it to light and heat. A buzzer or speaker to sound. A motor to movement. A heating element to heat. The cell does not store electricity; it stores chemicals that can produce an electric current when the circuit is closed.

Conventional current is shown flowing out of the positive terminal, around the circuit and back into the negative terminal. The particles that actually move in metal wires are electrons, which flow the other way, from the negative terminal around to the positive terminal. Both descriptions are used, and circuit diagrams use conventional current.

In a circuit diagram, a cell is drawn as two parallel lines of different lengths: the longer, thinner line is the positive terminal and the shorter, thicker line is the negative terminal.

The history is worth knowing. In 1800 the Italian scientist Alessandro Volta built the first battery, the voltaic pile, a stack of alternating zinc and copper discs separated by cloth soaked in salt water. It was the first source of a steady electric current, and the unit of voltage, the volt, is named after him. Before that, scientists could only study static electricity and sparks.

The questions for this section are with you now: the cell as a system, the need for a closed circuit and the energy transfers.

## Subtopic: Inside a Cell

Every chemical cell has three essential parts. Two electrodes, made of different conducting materials, usually two different metals or a metal and carbon, and an electrolyte, a substance containing ions that can move, either a liquid, a solution or a moist paste, in contact with both electrodes.

The chemical reaction happens at the electrodes. At the negative electrode, atoms of the more reactive material give up electrons, which build up there and are pushed out into the external circuit. At the positive electrode, electrons arriving from the circuit are taken up in another reaction. Inside the cell, ions move through the electrolyte to complete the circuit. The difference between the two electrodes is what drives the current; two identical electrodes produce no voltage.

A fruit cell demonstrates this. Push a galvanised nail, which is coated with zinc, and a clean copper coin or strip into a lemon, a few centimetres apart and not touching. The zinc is the negative electrode, the copper is the positive electrode, and the acidic lemon juice is the electrolyte. A voltmeter connected across them reads just under 1 volt, about 0,9 volts. One lemon cell cannot light an ordinary bulb, because it can push only a very small current, but it can run a small LED or a calculator display if several are connected together. Potatoes, apples and even salt water work as electrolytes too.

A zinc-carbon dry cell, the traditional torch cell, has a zinc case as the negative electrode, a carbon rod down the middle as the positive electrode, surrounded by manganese dioxide, and a moist paste of ammonium chloride as the electrolyte. It is called dry because the electrolyte is a paste, not a liquid that can spill. It gives 1,5 volts. Alkaline cells use similar electrodes with an alkaline electrolyte and also give 1,5 volts, but last longer.

The voltage of a cell, measured in volts, V, with a voltmeter connected across its terminals, is a measure of how hard the cell pushes charge around the circuit and how much energy it gives to each unit of charge. It depends on the materials of the electrodes, not on the size of the cell: a tiny AAA cell and a large D cell both give 1,5 volts, but the larger one contains more chemicals and lasts longer.

An investigation with fruit cells makes a fair test: keep the same two electrodes, the same distance apart and the same depth, and change only the fruit or vegetable, recording the voltmeter reading for each. Then keep the fruit the same and change the electrode metals. The results show that the electrode materials have a far bigger effect on the voltage than the type of fruit.

## Subtopic: Batteries: Groups of Connected Cells

In everyday speech, any single cell is often called a battery. In science, a battery is a group of two or more cells connected together.

When cells are connected in series, the positive terminal of one joined to the negative terminal of the next, their voltages add up. Two 1,5 volt cells in series give 3 volts; three give 4,5 volts; four give 6 volts. This is why a torch with two cells placed end to end, positive touching negative, gives 3 volts. If one cell is put in the wrong way round, its voltage subtracts, and the torch is dim or does not work. A rectangular 9 volt battery contains six small 1,5 volt cells in series, since 6 × 1,5 = 9 volts.

A car battery is a lead-acid battery made of six cells, each about 2 volts, connected in series to give 6 × 2 = 12 volts. Each cell has electrodes of lead and lead dioxide in a sulfuric acid electrolyte, the acid you met in Term 2.

Cells can also be connected in parallel, with all the positive terminals joined together and all the negative terminals joined together. Then the voltage stays the same as one cell, but the battery can deliver current for longer. This is examined more closely in the lesson on series and parallel circuits.

Laptop and phone batteries consist of lithium-ion cells, each giving about 3,7 volts, often connected in series and parallel to give the voltage and capacity needed. Electric vehicles contain thousands of such cells. Large banks of batteries store energy from solar panels for use at night and during load shedding.

Labels on cells and batteries carry useful information. The voltage is printed in volts. Rechargeable cells also show their capacity in milliampere-hours, mAh, a measure of how much charge they can deliver: a 2000 mAh cell can deliver about twice as much as a 1000 mAh cell before going flat. Phone batteries are often around 3000 to 5000 mAh.

## Subtopic: Flat Cells, Recharging, Disposal and the Error Museum

A cell goes flat when the chemicals that react inside it are used up, so it can no longer push a current. Non-rechargeable cells, called primary cells, such as zinc-carbon and alkaline cells, must then be replaced. Rechargeable cells, called secondary cells, such as lead-acid car batteries, nickel-metal hydride cells and lithium-ion cells, can be recharged: connecting them to a charger forces a current through them in the opposite direction, which reverses the chemical reaction and restores the chemicals, storing chemical potential energy again.

Cells must be disposed of responsibly. Many contain heavy metals and corrosive chemicals, such as lead, mercury in older cells, cadmium, nickel and lithium compounds, which can leak from landfill sites into soil and groundwater. Used batteries should be taken to collection points at many supermarkets and hardware shops for recycling. Car batteries are almost entirely recycled for their lead. Lithium-ion batteries should never be punctured or thrown into a fire, as they can catch fire or explode.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: a cell stores electricity; it stores chemical potential energy and produces a current only when the circuit is closed. Two: any single cell is a battery; in science a battery is two or more connected cells. Three: a bigger cell gives a bigger voltage; voltage depends on the electrode materials, while size affects how long it lasts. Four: two identical metals in lemon juice make a cell; the electrodes must be different. Five: cells in series give the voltage of one cell; in series, voltages add.

Layout for a cell question: name the energy input and output; identify the electrodes and electrolyte; state the voltage of one cell; and for a battery, show the addition of voltages in series.

## Subtopic: A Chemical Pump

Think of a cell as a pump for electric charge, powered by chemicals. Inside the cell, chemicals react. That reaction pushes charge out of one end, around a wire, through a bulb, and back into the other end.

Energy story: stored chemical energy goes in, electrical energy comes out, and the bulb turns it into light and heat. A buzzer turns it into sound. A motor turns it into movement.

But the pump only works if there is a complete loop. Wire out, through the bulb, wire back. Break the loop anywhere, with a switch or a loose wire, and the charge stops flowing and the chemicals stop reacting. That is why a torch switched off does not go flat.

A cell has two ends called terminals: positive and negative. On a circuit diagram it is drawn as a long thin line for positive and a short thick line for negative.

## Subtopic: The Lemon Cell

You can make a cell in your kitchen. Push a galvanised nail, which has a zinc coating, and a copper coin into a lemon, not touching. Connect a voltmeter, and it reads nearly 1 volt.

Why does it work? Every cell needs three things: two different metals, called electrodes, and a juicy chemical between them, called an electrolyte. Here the metals are zinc and copper, and the lemon juice is the electrolyte. The zinc gives up electrons and pushes them out into the wire.

Use two copper coins and nothing happens. The metals must be different.

One lemon is too weak to light a torch bulb, but four of them joined together can light a small LED. Potatoes work too.

A shop-bought torch cell works the same way, with a zinc case, a carbon rod in the middle and a damp chemical paste inside. It gives 1,5 volts. A tiny AAA cell and a big D cell both give 1,5 volts; the big one just lasts longer because it has more chemicals.

The very first battery was built in 1800 by Alessandro Volta, from a stack of zinc and copper discs with salty cloth between them. The volt is named after him.

Keep the lemon in mind and try this part's questions: the three parts of a cell and why the metals must be different.

## Subtopic: Stacking Cells

One cell is a cell. Two or more joined together make a battery.

Join cells nose to tail, positive to negative, and their voltages add. Two 1,5 volt cells give 3 volts. Four give 6 volts. A square 9 volt battery has six little 1,5 volt cells inside. A car battery has six 2 volt cells, giving 12 volts. Put one cell in backwards and it cancels out part of the push, so the torch is dim.

When the chemicals are used up, the cell is flat. Ordinary cells must be replaced. Rechargeable ones, like phone and car batteries, can be charged: the charger pushes current backwards through them and rebuilds the chemicals.

Old batteries contain harmful metals and chemicals, so never throw them in the bin. Take them to a battery recycling box at a supermarket or hardware shop. And never puncture or burn a phone battery.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A cell is an energy system: chemical energy becomes electrical energy when the circuit is closed. It needs two different electrodes and an electrolyte. A torch cell gives 1,5 volts; a lemon cell nearly 1 volt. A battery is a group of cells; in series their voltages add, so six 2 volt cells make a 12 volt car battery. Recharge what you can, and recycle old batteries. Grade 10 to 12 Physical Sciences builds on electric circuits and cells.

Look at a battery label. It tells you the voltage, and for rechargeable ones it gives a number in mAh. The bigger that number, the longer it lasts before it needs charging.
