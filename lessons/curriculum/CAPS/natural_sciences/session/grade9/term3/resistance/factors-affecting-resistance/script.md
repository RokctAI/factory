# Part 1 — Expert

This session identifies the four factors that affect the resistance of a conductor, the type of material, its length, its thickness and its temperature, explains each in terms of electrons moving through the material, and shows how to investigate them with fair tests. The anchor facts are that nichrome has roughly sixty times the resistance of copper for a wire of the same length and thickness; that resistance is directly proportional to length, so doubling the length doubles the resistance; that resistance decreases as thickness increases, so doubling the cross-sectional area halves the resistance; and that the resistance of a metal increases as its temperature rises. The governing principle is that resistance depends on how many obstacles the moving electrons meet and how much room they have to flow.

## Subtopic: Material and Length

Type of material. Different materials resist the flow of current by different amounts, because their atoms are arranged differently and hold their electrons differently. For wires of the same length and thickness at the same temperature, silver has the lowest resistance of all metals, followed closely by copper, then gold and aluminium. Iron has about six times the resistance of copper. Nichrome, an alloy of nickel and chromium, has about sixty times the resistance of copper. Carbon, as graphite, has more still. This property of a material is called its resistivity.

This is why different materials are chosen for different jobs. Copper is used for household wiring and connecting wires because it has very low resistance, is easy to bend into thin wire and is affordable. Silver would be even better but is far too expensive except in special electronic contacts. Aluminium, lighter and cheaper, is used for overhead power lines. Nichrome is chosen for heating elements because its high resistance means it heats up well. Tungsten, with a very high melting point, is chosen for filament bulbs.

Length. A longer wire has a greater resistance than a shorter wire of the same material and thickness. The electrons have to travel further through the material and meet more atoms on the way. In fact, resistance is directly proportional to length: if the length is doubled, the resistance doubles; if it is tripled, the resistance triples; if it is halved, the resistance halves. For example, if 1 metre of a certain nichrome wire has a resistance of 4 ohms, then 2 metres has 2 × 4 = 8 ohms, and 50 centimetres has 4 ÷ 2 = 2 ohms.

This is how a rheostat works: moving the slider changes the length of resistance wire that the current must flow through, and so changes the resistance.

The link between material and resistance comes from the structure of the material. In metals such as copper and silver, each atom releases one or more outer electrons into a shared sea of free electrons that can drift through the whole metal, so there are many charge carriers and the atoms are arranged in a regular pattern that scatters them relatively little. In an alloy such as nichrome, atoms of two different sizes are mixed together irregularly, which disrupts the pattern and scatters the electrons much more, so the resistance is far higher. This is a general rule: alloys usually have higher resistance than the pure metals they are made from.

## Subtopic: Thickness and Temperature

Thickness. A thicker wire has a smaller resistance than a thinner wire of the same material and length. A thicker wire has a larger cross-sectional area, so there is more room for the electrons to flow side by side, just as a wide road carries traffic more easily than a narrow lane. If the cross-sectional area is doubled, the resistance halves. Because area depends on the square of the diameter, doubling the diameter of a wire makes its area four times larger and its resistance four times smaller.

This explains why appliances that draw large currents need thick cables. A stove or a geyser, which draw large currents, are connected with thick cables; a bedside lamp uses thin flex. Using wire that is too thin for the current can make it overheat and start a fire. Electricians follow wiring regulations that specify the minimum thickness of wire for each circuit, and extension cords have a maximum current printed on them for the same reason.

Temperature. For metals, resistance increases as temperature increases. When a metal is heated, its atoms vibrate more strongly, so the moving electrons collide with them more often and their flow is opposed more. A tungsten filament, for example, has a resistance more than ten times higher when it is glowing than when it is cold. Cooling a metal reduces its resistance, and some materials cooled to extremely low temperatures, near minus 270 degrees Celsius, lose all resistance and become superconductors, which are used in the powerful magnets of MRI scanners.

Not all materials behave like metals. Carbon and semiconductor materials, such as those used in many thermistors, have a resistance that decreases as the temperature rises, because heating frees more charge carriers to move. That is the property that makes a thermistor useful as a temperature sensor.

A worked comparison combines thickness and length. Wire P is 1 metre long with a cross-sectional area of 1 square millimetre and a resistance of 2 ohms. Wire Q, of the same material, is 2 metres long with an area of 2 square millimetres. Doubling the length doubles the resistance to 4 ohms; doubling the area then halves it back to 2 ohms. So Q has the same resistance as P. Reasoning one factor at a time like this avoids confusion.

## Subtopic: Investigating the Factors Fairly

Each factor can be investigated with a simple circuit: a cell or battery, a switch, an ammeter, and a test wire connected between two crocodile clips. Since the cell stays the same, a smaller current on the ammeter means a larger resistance. A voltmeter connected across the test wire allows the resistance to be calculated in Grade 10.

Investigating length. Stretch a bare nichrome wire along a metre rule and fix it at both ends. Connect one crocodile clip at the zero mark and move the other to 20, 40, 60, 80 and 100 centimetres, recording the current each time. The independent variable is the length; the dependent variable is the current, from which the resistance follows; the controlled variables are the material, the thickness, the temperature and the cell. The results show the current decreasing as the length increases, so resistance increases with length.

Investigating thickness. Use equal lengths, say 50 centimetres, of nichrome wire of different thicknesses, often given as standard wire gauge numbers, and record the current for each. The thicker wires give larger currents, so smaller resistances.

Investigating material. Use equal lengths of wires of the same thickness made of different materials, such as copper, iron and nichrome, and record the current. Copper gives the largest current; nichrome the smallest. For copper, add a small resistor or bulb in series so that the current does not become dangerously large.

Investigating temperature. Connect a coil of thin iron or copper wire, and heat it gently in a beaker of water at different temperatures, or with a flame, recording the current. The current falls as the wire gets hotter.

Good practice: switch off between readings, because the current heats the wire and changes its resistance, which would spoil the fair test; use a low voltage; repeat each reading and take an average; and plot a graph of the results.

A results table for the length investigation has columns for length in centimetres, current in amperes for each repeat, and the average current. A typical set of results might show currents of about 1,2 amperes at 20 centimetres, falling to about 0,3 amperes at 100 centimetres. Plotting the average current against length gives a curve that falls steeply at first and then levels off, because the current is inversely related to the resistance. If a voltmeter is also used, resistance can be calculated for each length, and a graph of resistance against length is a straight line through the origin, which confirms that resistance is directly proportional to length.

## Subtopic: Applying the Factors and the Error Museum

The four factors together explain the design of many real objects. A heating element uses a long, thin coil of nichrome, giving a high resistance in a small space. An extension lead for a power tool uses thick copper wire to keep the resistance low, so it does not heat up. A power line uses thick aluminium cable to keep resistance and losses low over long distances. A fuse wire is thin, so that it heats and melts quickly when the current is too large.

Problems may combine factors. Wire A and wire B are made of the same material at the same temperature. A is 2 metres long and B is 1 metre long, and they have the same thickness. A has twice the resistance of B. Wire C is the same length as B but has twice the cross-sectional area; C has half the resistance of B.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: a thicker wire has more resistance; it has less, because there is more room for the current. Two: a longer wire has less resistance; it has more, because the electrons travel through more material. Three: all wires of the same size have the same resistance; the material matters greatly. Four: heating a metal lowers its resistance; it raises it. Five: the circuit can be left on between readings; the wire heats up and its resistance changes.

Layout for a resistance factor answer: name the factor, state the change, state the effect on resistance with the reason in terms of electrons and atoms, then the effect on the current.

The questions for this section are with you now: designing elements and cables, comparing wires and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through roads for electrons, hot and cold wires, and doing a fair test — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Roads for Electrons

Think of a wire as a road and electrons as cars. Some things make the trip harder.

The road surface, which is the material. Copper is a smooth highway. Nichrome is a bumpy dirt road with about sixty times more resistance. That is why house wiring is copper, but kettle elements are nichrome.

The length of the road. A longer road means more bumps along the way, so more resistance. Double the length, double the resistance. A 1 metre wire of 4 ohms becomes 8 ohms at 2 metres.

The width of the road, which is the thickness of the wire. A wide road lets lots of cars through side by side. A thick wire has less resistance. A thin wire has more.

So the easiest path for current is short, thick copper. The hardest is long, thin nichrome.

Why is nichrome so bumpy? It is a mixture of two metals with atoms of different sizes jumbled together, so electrons keep bumping into them. Mixtures of metals, called alloys, usually have more resistance than pure metals.

Keep the roads in mind and try the questions for this part: material, length and thickness.

## Subtopic: Hot and Cold Wires

Temperature matters too. Heat a metal wire and its atoms jiggle more. The electrons crash into them more often, so the resistance goes up. A light bulb filament has more than ten times as much resistance when it is glowing as when it is cold.

Cool a metal right down and its resistance drops. Some special materials, cooled to nearly minus 270 degrees, lose all their resistance. They are used to make the giant magnets in hospital scanners.

Some materials go the other way. In a thermistor, heating lowers the resistance. That is exactly why thermistors are used as temperature sensors in fridges and geysers.

Thickness also matters for safety. A stove or geyser needs thick cables because it draws a big current. A thin wire carrying a big current overheats and can start a fire. That is why extension cords say how much current they can handle.

Try reasoning one step at a time. A wire twice as long has twice the resistance. If the area of its cut end is also twice as big, that halves the resistance again. So a wire twice as long with twice the cross-section area ends up with the same resistance as the original.

Keep hot and cold wires in mind and try this part's questions: temperature and the right cable for the job.

## Subtopic: Doing a Fair Test

To test one factor, change only that factor and keep everything else the same.

Testing length: stretch a nichrome wire along a metre rule. Clip on at 0 and at 20 centimetres and read the ammeter. Move the clip to 40, 60, 80 and 100 centimetres. The current drops as the wire gets longer, so the resistance goes up. Same wire, same cell, same temperature: only the length changed.

Testing thickness: same length, same material, different thicknesses. Thicker gives more current, so less resistance.

Testing material: same length and thickness, different metals. Copper gives the most current, nichrome the least.

One important tip: switch off between readings. The current warms the wire, and a warmer wire has more resistance, which would spoil your fair test.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Resistance depends on four factors. Material: copper low, nichrome high. Length: longer means more resistance, in proportion. Thickness: thicker means less resistance. Temperature: hotter metals have more resistance, while thermistors go the other way. Test one factor at a time and switch off between readings. Grade 10 to 12 Physical Sciences turns these ideas into calculations.

Write your results in a table: length, current, and an average of repeat readings. Then draw a graph. As the length goes up, the current goes down, showing that longer wire means more resistance.
