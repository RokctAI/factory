Today we meet resistance, the opposition that every conductor offers to the flow of electric current. Even a copper wire has a little resistance, and when current is pushed through any resistance, some electrical energy is transferred to heat, and sometimes to light. Resistance is measured in ohms, written with the Greek letter omega. A component made specially to provide resistance is called a resistor. Resistors are used to limit the current in a circuit so that delicate parts such as LEDs are not damaged, and variable resistors let us control things such as the volume of a radio or the brightness of a dimmer light. High-resistance wires are used on purpose in kettles, stoves, toasters, irons and geysers, where we want heat, and the thin tungsten filament of an old-style bulb glows white-hot for the same reason. Grade 10 to 12 Physical Sciences calculates resistance using Ohm's law.
