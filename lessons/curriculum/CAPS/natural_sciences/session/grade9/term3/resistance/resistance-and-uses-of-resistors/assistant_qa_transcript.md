### subtopic_1 — What Resistance Is
**Assistant:** Does a resistor use up some of the current?

**Tutor:** No, and this is one of the most common misunderstandings in electricity. In a series circuit, the current is exactly the same at every point, before a resistor, after it and through the cell, because charge cannot pile up anywhere or disappear: every electron that goes into the resistor comes out of the other side. An ammeter placed before a bulb and another placed after it give identical readings. What a resistor does is reduce the size of the current flowing in the whole circuit compared with what it would be without the resistor, because it makes it harder for the cell to push charge around. What gets used, or more correctly transferred, in a resistor is energy, not current. The cell gives energy to the charges as they pass through it, and the charges give up that energy in the resistor, where it becomes heat, or in a bulb, where it becomes heat and light. A helpful picture is a bicycle chain: the pedals push the chain round, and a brake on the wheel makes it harder to turn. The chain moves at the same rate everywhere around the loop, the chain itself is not used up, but energy from your legs is turned into heat at the brake. In Grade 10, you will measure the energy given up per unit of charge, which is the voltage across the resistor.

### subtopic_2 — Resistors and How They Are Used
**Assistant:** Why do old-style bulbs often blow at the moment they are switched on?

**Tutor:** Because the cold filament has a much lower resistance than the hot filament, so a large surge of current flows for a fraction of a second when the bulb is switched on. The resistance of a metal increases with temperature, which you will study in the next lesson. A tungsten filament at room temperature has a resistance roughly ten to fifteen times smaller than when it is glowing white-hot at about 2500 degrees Celsius. At the instant you switch on, the filament is cold, so the current is roughly ten times larger than normal. Within a fraction of a second, the filament heats up, its resistance rises and the current settles to its normal value. Over the bulb's life, tungsten slowly evaporates from the hot filament, which is why old bulbs develop a dark coating on the inside of the glass, and the filament becomes thinner, especially at weak spots. A thinner spot has more resistance, so it gets hotter, so more tungsten evaporates there, until eventually the filament is very thin at one point. The switch-on surge then heats that thin spot so quickly that it melts and the filament breaks. LED bulbs do not have this problem, because they do not work by heating a wire, which is one reason they last many times longer, often 15 000 to 25 000 hours compared with about 1000 hours for a filament bulb.

### subtopic_3 — Using the Heating Effect
**Assistant:** If copper has low resistance, why does the electricity grid lose energy?

**Tutor:** Because even a low resistance becomes significant over very long distances and with very large currents. The power lines that carry electricity from the coal power stations of Mpumalanga to Cape Town or Durban are many hundreds of kilometres long. Every metre has a small resistance, and over hundreds of kilometres these add up to a resistance of several ohms. The heat produced in a wire depends strongly on the current: doubling the current makes the heating four times larger. Power lines are usually made of aluminium with a steel core, rather than copper, because aluminium is much lighter and cheaper, and although its resistance is a little higher, the lines can be made thicker to compensate. To keep losses down, the grid transmits electricity at very high voltages, up to 400 000 volts and even 765 000 volts on some Eskom lines, because for the same amount of power, a higher voltage means a much smaller current, and so far less heating in the wires. Transformers step the voltage up at the power station and down again near towns. Even so, some energy is lost: in South Africa, technical losses in transmission and distribution are typically several percent of the electricity generated. You will look at the national grid and transformers in more detail later this term.

### subtopic_4 — Comparing Circuits and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do the coloured bands on a resistor show its value?

**Tutor:** They are a colour code in which each colour stands for a digit, so the value can be read from any side of a tiny component. The standard code assigns black to 0, brown to 1, red to 2, orange to 3, yellow to 4, green to 5, blue to 6, violet to 7, grey to 8 and white to 9. On a common four-band resistor, the first two bands give the first two digits of the value, the third band gives the number of zeros to add, and the fourth band, often gold or silver and set slightly apart, gives the tolerance, how close the actual value is guaranteed to be to the marked value: gold means within 5 percent and silver within 10 percent. For example, bands of brown, black, red and gold mean 1, 0 and two zeros, which is 1000 ohms, or 1 kilohm, within 5 percent. Bands of yellow, violet, brown and gold mean 4, 7 and one zero, 470 ohms. Bands of red, red, orange mean 2, 2 and three zeros, 22 000 ohms, or 22 kilohms. Many people learn the colour order with a memory sentence made from the first letters. Precision resistors use five bands, with three digits before the multiplier. Very small surface-mount resistors on modern phone circuit boards are too small for bands and use printed number codes instead. You can always check a resistor's value with a multimeter set to measure resistance.
