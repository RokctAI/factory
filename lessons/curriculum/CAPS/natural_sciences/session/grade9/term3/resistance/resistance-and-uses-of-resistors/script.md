# Part 1 — Expert

This session defines electrical resistance, explains why all conductors have some resistance and what happens to energy in a resistance, introduces the ohm and the resistor, and describes the uses of fixed and variable resistors and of resistance heating. The anchor facts are that resistance opposes the flow of current; that it is measured in ohms, symbol Ω; that for the same cell, a larger resistance in a circuit means a smaller current and a dimmer bulb; that energy transferred in a resistance appears as heat and sometimes light; and that heating elements are made of high-resistance alloys such as nichrome while connecting wires are made of low-resistance copper. The governing principle is that resistance limits current and converts electrical energy into heat, which is sometimes unwanted waste and sometimes exactly what the device is for.

## Subtopic: What Resistance Is

An electric current is a flow of charge, which in metal wires is a flow of electrons. The cell pushes the electrons around the circuit. As they move through a conductor, the electrons collide with the vibrating atoms, or more precisely the positive ions, of the material, which slows their flow. This opposition to the flow of current is called resistance.

Every conductor has some resistance. Good conductors such as copper, silver and aluminium have very low resistance, so current flows through them easily. Poorer conductors such as nichrome, tungsten and carbon have more resistance. Insulators such as plastic, rubber and glass have such enormous resistance that practically no current flows through them at ordinary voltages. Only special materials called superconductors, cooled to extremely low temperatures, have no resistance at all.

Resistance is measured in ohms, symbol Ω, named after the German physicist Georg Ohm. A short piece of connecting wire has a resistance of a small fraction of an ohm; a resistor in a radio circuit might be hundreds or thousands of ohms; a kettle element about 20 to 30 ohms. Large values are given in kilohms, kΩ, and megohms, MΩ: 1 kΩ = 1000 Ω.

For a given cell or battery, the larger the total resistance in a circuit, the smaller the current. Current is measured in amperes, A, with an ammeter connected in series in the circuit. If a second bulb is added to a simple circuit in series, the total resistance increases, the current decreases and both bulbs glow more dimly than one did alone.

When current flows through a resistance, the collisions transfer energy to the atoms of the material, making them vibrate faster: the material heats up. This is called the heating effect of an electric current. If it gets hot enough, it glows and gives off light as well. So in any resistance, electrical energy is transferred mainly to heat, and sometimes light.

A useful comparison is water flowing through pipes. The cell is like a pump, the current is like the flow of water, and resistance is like a narrow or rough section of pipe that makes the water harder to push through. A narrow pipe reduces the flow throughout the loop, not just at the narrow part, just as a resistor reduces the current throughout a series circuit. The analogy has limits, but it helps to picture why adding resistance anywhere in a single loop lowers the current everywhere.

Resistance can be measured directly with a multimeter set to its ohms range, connected across a component that has been removed from the circuit. It can also be found by measuring the voltage across a component and the current through it, a method you will use with Ohm's law in Grade 10.

The questions for this section are with you now: what resistance is, the ohm, and how resistance affects the current.

## Subtopic: Resistors and How They Are Used

A resistor is a component designed to have a particular resistance. In circuit diagrams, a fixed resistor is drawn as a small rectangle; a variable resistor is a rectangle with an arrow through it.

Fixed resistors have a resistance that does not change. Small carbon or metal-film resistors are marked with coloured bands that indicate their value. They are found by the dozen on the circuit boards of phones, computers, televisions and chargers. Their main jobs are to limit the current and to divide the voltage. For example, a light-emitting diode, LED, would be damaged if connected directly to a 9 volt battery, because too much current would flow through it. A resistor of a suitable value is connected in series with the LED to reduce the current to a safe level, typically about 20 milliamperes.

Variable resistors have a resistance that can be changed, usually by turning a knob or moving a slider along a coil of resistance wire, so that the current flows through more or less of the wire. A laboratory variable resistor is called a rheostat. Variable resistors are used in volume controls of radios and speakers, in some dimmer switches, in speed controls for model trains and older fans, and in controls on stoves and heaters. In a simple circuit with a bulb and a rheostat, increasing the resistance makes the bulb dimmer; decreasing it makes the bulb brighter.

Some resistors change their resistance in response to their surroundings. A light-dependent resistor, LDR, has a high resistance in the dark and a low resistance in bright light; it is used in automatic street lights and security lights that switch on at dusk. A thermistor changes resistance with temperature and is used in electronic thermometers, fire alarms and to control the temperature of fridges, geysers and car engines.

A practical investigation shows the effect of a variable resistor. Set up a circuit with a battery, a switch, a bulb, an ammeter and a rheostat in series. Move the slider in steps and record the ammeter reading and the brightness of the bulb at each step. The table shows the current falling and the bulb dimming as more resistance wire is included, and rising again as the slider is moved back. Safety: do not leave the rheostat at its lowest setting for long with a strong battery, as the current may overheat the wire or the bulb.

The questions for this section are ready: fixed and variable resistors, the LED resistor and the LDR and thermistor.

## Subtopic: Using the Heating Effect

The heating effect of a current is a nuisance in some places and useful in others.

As a nuisance: connecting wires, power cables and the transmission lines of the national grid all have some resistance, so some energy is always wasted as heat. To keep this waste small, connecting wires are made of copper, which has a very low resistance, and power lines are made thick. Overheating can also be dangerous: if too large a current flows through thin wiring, it can melt the insulation and start a fire, which is why circuits are protected by fuses and circuit breakers, studied later this term.

As useful: many appliances are designed around a high-resistance heating element. The element is usually a coil or strip of nichrome, an alloy of nickel and chromium, which has a fairly high resistance, does not oxidise when red hot, and has a very high melting point. When current flows, the element becomes hot. Heating elements are found in kettles, stoves and ovens, toasters, irons, hair dryers, heaters, electric blankets and geysers. In South Africa, the electric geyser is usually the largest single user of electricity in a home, because heating water requires a lot of energy.

Light from resistance: in an incandescent, or filament, bulb, a very thin coil of tungsten wire is heated by the current to about 2500 degrees Celsius, hot enough to glow white. Tungsten is used because its melting point, about 3400 degrees Celsius, is the highest of all metals. The bulb is filled with an unreactive gas such as argon so that the hot tungsten does not react with oxygen. Only about 5 percent of the energy supplied to a filament bulb becomes light; the rest becomes heat, which is why these bulbs feel very hot and why they have largely been replaced by LED bulbs, which give the same light for a fraction of the energy.

A fuse uses the heating effect as a safety device: a thin wire that melts and breaks the circuit if the current becomes too large.

Cooking plates, kettles and geysers often contain a thermostat, an automatic switch, frequently based on a thermistor or a bimetallic strip, that switches the element off when the desired temperature is reached and on again when it cools. This prevents overheating and saves electricity. Setting a geyser thermostat to about 60 degrees Celsius rather than 70 or higher is a common energy-saving recommendation in South Africa.

The questions for this section are waiting: wanted and unwanted heating, nichrome elements and the filament bulb.

## Subtopic: Comparing Circuits and the Error Museum

Comparing circuits is a common examination task. Circuit A: one cell and one bulb. Circuit B: the same cell and two identical bulbs in series. Circuit B has the greater total resistance, so the current is smaller and each bulb is dimmer than the bulb in A. Circuit C: the same cell, one bulb and a rheostat; as the rheostat's slider is moved to include more resistance wire, the current falls and the bulb dims.

An ammeter reading confirms this. Placed anywhere in a series circuit, it reads the same current, and that reading drops as more resistance is added.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: copper wires have no resistance; all conductors have some resistance, copper just very little. Two: a resistor uses up current; the current is the same before and after a resistor in series, but it is smaller than it would be without it; what the resistor transfers is energy. Three: more resistance gives more current; for the same cell, more resistance gives less current. Four: heating elements are made of copper; they are made of high-resistance alloys like nichrome. Five: resistance is measured in volts; it is measured in ohms.

Layout for a resistance answer: state the effect of the change on total resistance, then on current, then on what is observed, such as the brightness of a bulb, and give the energy transfer in the resistance.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing circuits, rheostat changes and the five exhibits.

## Subtopic: The Crowded Corridor

Picture electrons moving through a wire like learners rushing down a school corridor between classes. If the corridor is clear, they flow easily. If it is crowded with people standing around, they keep bumping into them and slow down. That bumping is resistance.

In a wire, the electrons bump into the atoms of the metal. Every wire has some resistance. Copper has very little, which is why it is used for wires. Some materials, like nichrome, have a lot more. Plastic and rubber have so much that almost nothing gets through, which is why they are used to cover wires.

Resistance is measured in ohms, with the symbol that looks like a horseshoe, Ω.

Two rules to remember. More resistance means less current, so a bulb gets dimmer. And every time electrons bump into atoms, the wire warms up. That is the heating effect.

Another way to picture it: water being pumped through a pipe. A narrow, rough section of pipe slows the flow all the way round the loop, not just in that section. A resistor does the same to the current all the way round a circuit.

Keep the crowded corridor in mind and try the questions for this part: what resistance is and what it does to the current.

## Subtopic: The Current Controllers

A resistor is a little part made to have a set amount of resistance. Look at any circuit board inside a radio or charger and you will see dozens of them, tiny tubes with coloured stripes that show their value.

What are they for? Mostly to hold the current back. An LED would burn out if you connected it straight to a 9 volt battery, so you put a resistor in front of it to keep the current safe.

A variable resistor is one you can adjust. Turn the volume knob on a radio and you are changing a resistance. Slide a dimmer and the light changes. More resistance, less current, dimmer light. Less resistance, more current, brighter light.

Some resistors adjust themselves. A light-dependent resistor changes with light, so street lights switch on by themselves at sunset. A thermistor changes with temperature, so a fridge or geyser knows when to switch off.

Try it in class: a battery, a bulb, an ammeter and a sliding resistor all in one loop. Slide one way and the ammeter number drops and the bulb dims. Slide back and the number rises and the bulb brightens.

Keep the current controllers in mind and try this part's questions: fixed resistors, knobs and sliders, and self-adjusting ones.

## Subtopic: Hot Wires at Home

Remember that resistance makes heat. Sometimes we want that heat.

Inside your kettle, stove, toaster, iron and geyser is a coil of special wire called nichrome. It has a high resistance, so when current flows through it, it gets very hot without melting or rusting. The geyser is usually the biggest electricity user in a South African home.

An old-style light bulb has a tiny coil of tungsten wire that gets so hot, about 2500 degrees, that it glows white. But most of its energy comes out as heat, not light, which is why LED bulbs, which stay cool and use far less electricity, have replaced them.

Sometimes the heat is a waste. Power cables warm up and lose energy. Thin wires carrying too much current can overheat and start a fire. A fuse is a thin wire that melts on purpose if the current gets too big, cutting the power before anything dangerous happens.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Resistance is the opposition to current in every conductor, measured in ohms. More resistance means less current. Resistance turns electrical energy into heat and sometimes light. Resistors limit current; variable resistors control volume and brightness; LDRs and thermistors respond to light and temperature. Nichrome elements heat kettles and geysers; tungsten filaments glow. Grade 10 to 12 Physical Sciences calculates resistance using Ohm's law.

Kettles and geysers also have a thermostat, a switch that turns the element off when the water is hot enough. Setting the geyser thermostat lower, at about 60 degrees, saves electricity and money.
