### subtopic_1 — Material and Length
**Assistant:** Why does doubling the thickness of a wire not just halve its resistance?

**Tutor:** Because thickness usually means the diameter of the wire, while resistance depends on the cross-sectional area, the area of the circular end you would see if you cut the wire across. The area of a circle depends on the square of its diameter. If you double the diameter, the area becomes 2 × 2 = 4 times larger, so there is four times as much room for the current, and the resistance becomes four times smaller, not two times. If you triple the diameter, the area becomes 3 × 3 = 9 times larger, and the resistance nine times smaller. So the rule is that resistance is inversely proportional to the cross-sectional area: double the area, half the resistance. This is why a small increase in cable diameter makes a big difference to how much current a cable can safely carry. Wire sizes in South Africa and many other countries are given by cross-sectional area in square millimetres. Typical house circuits use about 1,5 square millimetre copper wire for lighting circuits, 2,5 for plug circuits, and thicker cables, often 4 or 6 square millimetres, for stoves and geysers, depending on the current and the length of the run. In Grade 10, you will meet the full relationship, in which resistance equals the resistivity of the material multiplied by the length, divided by the cross-sectional area, which combines three of the factors into one formula.

### subtopic_2 — Thickness and Temperature
**Assistant:** If heating increases resistance, why do we use metal in heaters at all?

**Tutor:** Because the increase is modest and predictable, and the heater is designed for the resistance it has when hot. When a heating element is first switched on, it is cold, its resistance is a little lower, and the current is slightly larger. As it heats up, its resistance rises and the current settles to a steady value. For nichrome, the change in resistance between room temperature and red heat is quite small, only about ten percent or so, which is one reason nichrome is chosen: its resistance stays fairly stable over the temperature range of a kettle or stove. Tungsten in a filament bulb changes far more, which causes the switch-on surge you may have read about. Designers simply calculate the element's resistance at its working temperature and choose its length and thickness to give the heat output they want. There is even a benefit to the rise in resistance. If an element starts to overheat, its resistance rises, the current drops a little and the heating eases slightly, which provides a small amount of natural self-limiting. Thermostats and fuses provide the real safety, though. Some special heaters, used for example in car seat heaters and some hair straighteners, use ceramic materials whose resistance rises very sharply above a certain temperature, so they automatically limit their own temperature without needing a separate thermostat.

### subtopic_3 — Investigating the Factors Fairly
**Assistant:** Why is silver not used for wires if it has the lowest resistance?

**Tutor:** Mainly because of cost, and also because copper is almost as good. Silver's resistivity is only about five to six percent lower than copper's, so a silver wire would conduct only slightly better than a copper wire of the same size. But silver costs roughly a hundred times more than copper per kilogram, so wiring a house in silver would be enormously expensive for almost no benefit. You could achieve the same improvement far more cheaply by using copper wire that is slightly thicker. Silver also tarnishes when it reacts with sulfur compounds in the air, forming a dark coating, although that coating still conducts. Silver is used where tiny amounts make a real difference: in the contacts of switches and relays, in some high-quality connectors, in conductive inks printed on circuits and solar panels, and in some specialised electronics. Gold is used to coat connectors, such as the pins of computer chips and memory cards, not because it conducts better than copper, as it does not, but because it never corrodes, so the contact stays reliable for many years. Aluminium, with about 60 percent higher resistivity than copper, is used for overhead power lines because it is much lighter and cheaper, and the cables are made thicker to compensate. So engineers choose a conductor by balancing resistance, cost, weight, strength and resistance to corrosion, not resistance alone.

### subtopic_4 — Applying the Factors and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** In the investigation, why might my results not form a perfect straight line?

**Tutor:** Because real measurements always include some error, and several sources affect this experiment. First, the wire heats up while the current flows, especially at short lengths where the current is largest, which raises its resistance and lowers the current; this is why switching off between readings matters. Second, the crocodile clips and connecting wires have a small resistance of their own, and how firmly a clip grips the wire changes the contact resistance from reading to reading. Third, the cell's voltage is not perfectly steady: as it delivers current, especially large currents, its voltage drops a little, and it has an internal resistance of its own, which you will study in Grade 12. Fourth, the ammeter may not be precisely calibrated, and reading an analogue scale involves some judgement, which introduces parallax and reading errors. Fifth, the wire may not be perfectly uniform in thickness along its length, or may have kinks. To improve the results, use a low voltage, switch off between readings, clean the wire and clips, make sure the clips grip firmly, repeat each reading two or three times and take the average, and use a digital meter if available. Also, remember that the current is not directly proportional to length; it is the resistance that is. If you calculate the resistance from a voltmeter and ammeter, the graph of resistance against length should be close to a straight line through the origin.
