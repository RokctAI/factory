# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains how cells can be connected in series and in parallel, what happens to the total voltage and to the behaviour of a bulb in each case, how to measure voltage correctly with a voltmeter, and how to draw the circuits with standard symbols. The anchor facts are that cells in series add their voltages, so three 1,5 volt cells give 4,5 volts; that identical cells in parallel give the same voltage as one cell, 1,5 volts, but supply current for longer; that a cell reversed in a series arrangement subtracts its voltage; and that a voltmeter is always connected in parallel, across the component it measures. The governing principle is that voltage is the energy given to each unit of charge, so charge passing through several cells in a row gains energy from each, while charge passing through one of several side-by-side cells gains energy from only one.

## Subtopic: Voltage and the Voltmeter

The voltage of a cell, also called its potential difference or, for a cell, its emf, is a measure of how much energy the cell gives to each unit of charge that passes through it. It is the push that drives the current around the circuit. Voltage is measured in volts, V, named after Alessandro Volta.

Voltage is measured with a voltmeter. A voltmeter is always connected in parallel with the component being measured, that is, across it, with one lead on each side of the component, not in the main path of the current. To measure the voltage of a cell or battery, connect the voltmeter's positive terminal, usually red, to the positive terminal of the cell and its negative terminal, usually black, to the negative terminal. To measure the voltage across a bulb, connect the voltmeter's leads to the two ends of the bulb.

By contrast, an ammeter, which measures current in amperes, is always connected in series, in the path of the current, so that all the current flows through it.

The standard circuit symbols used in this topic: a cell, a long thin line and a short thick line, with the long line the positive terminal; a battery, two or more cell symbols joined; a bulb, a circle with a cross inside; a switch, a break in the line with a hinged line; a resistor, a small rectangle; a voltmeter, a circle with a V; an ammeter, a circle with an A; and connecting wires drawn as straight lines with right-angled corners.

A new 1,5 volt cell may read slightly more, about 1,6 volts, and a used one less. A flat cell may still show some voltage with no current flowing, but its voltage drops sharply when it is asked to deliver a current.

Using a voltmeter safely and accurately: choose a range larger than the voltage you expect, for example the 0 to 20 volt range for a few cells; connect it with the correct polarity, or an analogue needle will try to swing backwards; read the scale at eye level; and note that a digital multimeter shows a minus sign if the leads are reversed, which is harmless and tells you the polarity. A multimeter can measure voltage, current and resistance, so always check which setting and which sockets are being used before connecting it.

The questions for this section are with you now: what voltage means, how to connect a voltmeter and an ammeter, and the circuit symbols.

## Subtopic: Cells in Series

Cells are connected in series when they are joined one after another in a single line, the positive terminal of each cell touching or wired to the negative terminal of the next. This is how cells sit in most torches and remotes: end to end, flat end against the bump.

In series, the total voltage is the sum of the individual voltages. Two 1,5 volt cells give 1,5 + 1,5 = 3 volts. Three give 4,5 volts. Four give 6 volts. Six give 9 volts, as inside a rectangular 9 volt battery. The charge passes through every cell in turn and gains energy from each one.

A bulb connected to cells in series glows more brightly as more cells are added, because the larger voltage pushes a larger current through the bulb, transferring more energy each second. If too many cells are added, the current may be too large for the bulb and its filament will melt, so bulbs are marked with the voltage they are designed for, such as 2,5 volts or 6 volts.

If one cell in a series arrangement is reversed, so that its positive terminal faces the positive terminal of its neighbour, it pushes against the others and its voltage subtracts. Three 1,5 volt cells with one reversed give 1,5 + 1,5 − 1,5 = 1,5 volts. That is why a torch with one cell inserted the wrong way round is dim or does not light at all, and why devices have diagrams showing which way the cells go.

The disadvantage of series is that all the current flows through every cell, so each cell is drained at the same rate, and if one cell is flat or broken, the whole series chain is affected.

A practical investigation: connect one, two, three and four 1,5 volt cells in series to the same bulb, measuring the total voltage with a voltmeter and the current with an ammeter each time, and noting the brightness. The voltmeter readings are close to 1,5, 3, 4,5 and 6 volts, the current increases each time, and the bulb glows more brightly, provided its rating is not exceeded. The readings are usually a little lower than the ideal values, because the cells lose some voltage inside themselves when delivering current.

The questions for this section are ready: adding voltages in series, the effect on a bulb and the reversed cell.

## Subtopic: Cells in Parallel

Cells are connected in parallel when all their positive terminals are joined together and all their negative terminals are joined together, and the circuit is connected across these two joined points. The cells sit side by side, each providing a separate path for the current.

For identical cells in parallel, the total voltage is the same as the voltage of one cell. Two 1,5 volt cells in parallel give 1,5 volts; three in parallel still give 1,5 volts. Each unit of charge passes through only one of the cells, so it gains energy from only one.

A bulb connected to two cells in parallel glows with the same brightness as it would with one cell, but the cells share the current between them. Each cell supplies only part of the current, so the chemicals in each are used up more slowly and the bulb stays lit for longer, roughly twice as long with two cells and three times as long with three.

Parallel connections are used when a longer life or a larger total current is needed at the same voltage. Large battery banks for solar systems and backup power during load shedding combine cells in series, to raise the voltage, and in parallel, to increase the capacity. Only identical cells at the same state of charge should be connected in parallel; otherwise a stronger cell drives current into a weaker one, wasting energy and possibly overheating.

A practical test of battery life compares the arrangements: connect identical new cells to identical bulbs, one cell in one circuit and two cells in parallel in the other, and record how long each bulb stays lit, or measure the voltages every hour. The bulb on parallel cells keeps glowing well after the single-cell bulb has dimmed. This takes many hours with ordinary torch bulbs, so a small motor or a low-resistance bulb that draws a larger current gives quicker results.

The questions for this section are waiting: the voltage of cells in parallel, the effect on a bulb and why cells in parallel last longer.

## Subtopic: Comparing Arrangements and the Error Museum

A comparison of the arrangements, using identical 1,5 volt cells and identical bulbs: one cell, 1,5 volts, normal brightness, normal life. Two cells in series, 3 volts, brighter, the cells run down faster. Two cells in parallel, 1,5 volts, normal brightness, the cells last longer. Two cells in series with one reversed, 0 volts, the bulb does not light.

Circuit diagrams make the arrangements clear. For series, draw the cell symbols in a row in the main loop, long line to short line. For parallel, draw the cells on separate branches between two junctions, with all the long lines on the same side.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: cells in parallel add their voltages; only cells in series do. Two: a voltmeter is connected in series; it is connected in parallel, across the component. Three: more cells always give a brighter bulb; in parallel the brightness stays the same. Four: a reversed cell makes no difference; it subtracts its voltage. Five: cells in parallel make the bulb last the same time; they share the current and last longer.

Layout for a cell arrangement answer: identify series or parallel; calculate the total voltage with the working shown; state the effect on brightness; and state the effect on how long the cells last.

Mixed arrangements combine both ideas. Four 1,5 volt cells arranged as two pairs in parallel, with the two pairs then connected in series, give 1,5 + 1,5 = 3 volts, and each pair shares the current, so the arrangement lasts about twice as long as two cells in series alone. Working out such arrangements step by step, first the parallel groups and then the series chain, is good practice for the battery banks used with solar panels.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing arrangements, drawing diagrams and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through energy pushes, cells in a queue and cells side by side — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Push Meter

Voltage is the push a cell gives the charge flowing round a circuit. More precisely, it is the energy the cell gives each bit of charge. We measure it in volts.

The tool is a voltmeter. Here is the rule to remember: a voltmeter goes across a component, one lead on each side, never in the main loop. To check a cell, touch the red lead to the positive end and the black lead to the negative end.

Its partner, the ammeter, which measures current, does the opposite: it goes into the loop, so all the current passes through it.

Learn the drawing symbols: a cell is a long thin line next to a short thick line; the long line is positive. A bulb is a circle with a cross. A switch is a gap with a little hinged line. A voltmeter is a circle with a V, and an ammeter a circle with an A.

A new torch cell reads about 1,5 volts, sometimes a bit more. A tired one reads less.

When you use a multimeter, check that the dial is set to volts and that the leads are in the right sockets. If the screen shows a minus sign, your leads are simply swapped; turn them around.

Think of voltage as height on a staircase. Each cell lifts the charge up some stairs, and the charge then walks back down through the bulb, giving up its energy as light and heat.

Keep the push meter in mind and try the questions for this part: what voltage is and how to connect the meters.

## Subtopic: Cells in a Queue

Line cells up end to end, positive to negative, like the cells in a torch. That is series. The charge goes through each one in turn and gets a push from every one, so the voltages add.

One cell: 1,5 volts. Two in a queue: 3 volts. Three: 4,5 volts. Four: 6 volts. Six: 9 volts.

More push means more current through the bulb, so it shines brighter. But do not overdo it. Put 9 volts across a small 2,5 volt torch bulb and it will blow.

What if one cell faces the wrong way? It pushes backwards and cancels one of the others. Three cells with one backwards give only 1,5 volts. That is why your remote does not work if you put a cell in the wrong way round, and why there is a little diagram in the battery compartment.

The catch with a queue: every cell carries all the current, so they all run down together.

Try it in class with a voltmeter. One cell: about 1,5. Two in a queue: about 3. Three: about 4,5. Four: about 6. The numbers are often a little lower than perfect, because the cells lose a bit of push inside themselves.

Hold the queue in mind and try this part's questions: adding up the volts and the backwards cell.

## Subtopic: Cells Side by Side

Now connect the cells side by side: all the positive ends joined together and all the negative ends joined together. That is parallel.

Each bit of charge only goes through one cell, so it only gets one push. The voltage stays at 1,5 volts, no matter how many cells you put side by side. The bulb is no brighter than with one cell.

So what is the point? The cells share the work. Each one only has to supply part of the current, so they last much longer. Two cells side by side keep the bulb going about twice as long.

Big backup batteries for solar panels and load shedding use both tricks: cells in a queue for more volts, and rows side by side so they last longer.

Only put matching cells side by side. A fresh cell next to a flat one will waste its energy pushing current into the flat one.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Voltage is the energy given to each bit of charge, measured with a voltmeter connected across a component. Cells in series add their voltages and make a bulb brighter; a reversed cell subtracts. Identical cells in parallel keep the voltage of one cell, so the bulb is no brighter, but they last longer. Grade 10 to 12 Physical Sciences builds on circuits like these.

Test it: one bulb on a single cell, another bulb on two cells side by side. Leave them on. The single-cell bulb fades first. The side-by-side pair keeps going much longer.

Hold cells side by side in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: parallel voltage, longer life and backup batteries.
