### subtopic_1 — Why Homes Are Wired in Parallel
**Assistant:** Why does a breaker sometimes trip only after a few minutes, not instantly?

**Tutor:** Because most household circuit breakers have two ways of tripping, designed for two different kinds of fault. For a small overload, such as a current of 17 or 20 amperes on a 16 ampere circuit, the breaker uses a thermal mechanism: a strip made of two different metals bonded together, called a bimetallic strip, carries the current and is warmed by it. The two metals expand by different amounts, so the strip bends as it heats. With a modest overload, it takes some time, from seconds to minutes, for the strip to bend far enough to release the switch. This delay is deliberate, because many appliances draw a brief surge when they start, and the cables can carry a small overload for a short time without harm; tripping instantly would cause annoying, unnecessary cut-outs. For a very large current, such as a short circuit, where the current may be hundreds of amperes, the breaker uses a magnetic mechanism: the current flows through a coil that acts as an electromagnet, and the strong magnetic force pulls a lever and opens the switch in a fraction of a second. So a kettle, toaster and microwave together may run for a little while before the kitchen goes dark, while a damaged cable that shorts live to neutral trips the breaker immediately. You will learn about the earth leakage unit, which protects people rather than cables, in the next topic.

### subtopic_2 — Current Depends on Resistance for the Same Voltage
**Assistant:** If the voltage is the same, why does a long extension cord matter?

**Tutor:** Because the cord itself has resistance, and it is in series with the appliance plugged into it. The wires inside an extension cord, like all wires, have a small resistance that increases with their length and decreases with their thickness. When a heavy appliance such as a heater, kettle or power tool draws a large current through a long, thin cord, two things happen. First, some of the 230 volts is used up across the cord's own resistance, so the appliance receives slightly less voltage and may work less well; a power tool may run slower, for example. Second, and more importantly, the current heats the cord. If the cord is too thin for the current, it can become hot enough to soften or melt its insulation, especially if it is coiled up on a drum, where the heat cannot escape and the turns heat each other. Cable drums are therefore marked with two ratings: a lower maximum current when wound up and a higher one when fully unrolled. To use extension cords safely, choose one rated for at least the current of the appliance, use the shortest length that will do the job, unroll it fully, never join several cords together, and never run them under carpets or through doorways where they can be crushed. Outdoor cords need to be rated for outdoor use and protected by an earth leakage unit.

### subtopic_3 — Adding Up the Current
**Assistant:** Why is the geyser such a big part of the electricity bill?

**Tutor:** Because heating water takes a great deal of energy, and the geyser draws a large current for a long time every day. A typical household geyser has a 3000 watt element, which draws about 13 amperes at 230 volts. It must heat around 150 litres of water from the temperature of the cold supply to the thermostat setting, often 60 degrees Celsius or more, and then keep reheating it as hot water is used and as heat leaks out through the tank walls and pipes. Water has a very high capacity for storing heat, so this needs a lot of energy: heating 150 litres by 40 degrees takes roughly 7 kilowatt-hours, which is about two and a third hours of the element running at full power. In many South African homes, the geyser accounts for a third to a half of the electricity bill. There are several ways to reduce this: lower the thermostat to about 60 degrees, wrap the geyser and the first few metres of pipe in an insulating blanket, fit a timer so that the geyser only heats before the times hot water is needed, take shorter showers, fix dripping hot taps, and consider a solar water heater or a heat pump, which use far less electricity. These savings also reduce the load on the national grid during peak times, which helps to reduce load shedding. You will calculate costs like these in the lessons on the cost of electrical power.

### subtopic_4 — Planning Safe Use and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do two switches control one staircase light?

**Tutor:** They use a clever arrangement called two-way switching, in which each switch can connect the circuit through one of two alternative wires. A normal light switch has two terminals and is either open or closed. A two-way switch has three terminals: a common terminal and two others, and the switch connects the common terminal to one or the other. At the bottom of the stairs, the live wire goes to the common terminal of the first switch. Two wires, often called strappers, run from that switch's other two terminals up to the two outer terminals of the second switch at the top of the stairs. The common terminal of the second switch connects to the light. The light is on when both switches select the same strapper wire, completing the path, and off when they select different ones, because then there is a gap. Flipping either switch changes which strapper it selects, so it always toggles the light from on to off or off to on, wherever the other switch is set. Electricians use the same idea for long passages and for bedrooms with switches at the door and beside the bed. With more than two switches, special intermediate switches are added between them. It is a neat example of series and parallel thinking: the light and its switches form one path in series, while the strapper wires provide two parallel alternatives.
