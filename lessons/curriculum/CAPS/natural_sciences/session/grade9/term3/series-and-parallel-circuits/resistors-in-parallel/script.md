# Part 1 — Expert

This session sets out the behaviour of resistors and bulbs connected in parallel: the voltage across each branch equals the source voltage, the current divides at junctions and the branch currents add to give the total current, and adding resistors in parallel decreases the total resistance and increases the total current drawn from the source. The anchor examples are two identical bulbs in parallel on a battery, each as bright as a single bulb, branch currents of 0,2 amperes and 0,3 amperes that add to a total of 0,5 amperes, and two 10 ohm resistors in parallel giving a total resistance of 5 ohms. The governing principle is that each branch of a parallel circuit is a separate complete path across the source, so each receives the full voltage, and adding branches gives the charge more paths to flow through.

## Subtopic: Voltage in a Parallel Circuit

Components are connected in parallel when they are on separate branches, each connected directly across the same two points of the circuit. Tracing from the battery, the path splits at a junction into two or more branches and the branches rejoin at another junction before returning to the battery. Each branch is a complete path on its own.

The first rule of parallel circuits: the voltage across each branch is the same, and equal to the voltage of the source. If two bulbs are connected in parallel across a 6 volt battery, each has 6 volts across it. A voltmeter connected across either bulb, or across the battery, reads the same.

The reason is energy. Each unit of charge passes through only one branch on its way around the circuit, so it gives up all the energy it received from the battery in that one branch. Whichever branch it takes, it gives up the same amount of energy per unit of charge, which is the battery voltage.

The practical result: identical bulbs in parallel each glow with the same brightness as a single bulb connected alone to the same battery. Adding more bulbs in parallel does not make the others dimmer, at least while the battery can supply the extra current.

Each branch can have its own switch, so that each component can be controlled independently, and a break in one branch does not affect the others. If one bulb in a parallel circuit blows or is unscrewed, the other branches remain complete and their bulbs stay lit.

An investigation confirms the voltage rule. Connect two or three bulbs in parallel across a battery, and measure the voltage across each bulb and across the battery with a voltmeter. All the readings are the same, apart from small meter errors. Then compare the brightness of each bulb with that of a single bulb connected alone to the same battery: they look the same.

Drawing a parallel circuit correctly: draw the battery and switch on the main loop, then draw two junction dots where the path splits and rejoins, with each component on its own rung between them. The junction dots matter; without them, crossing wires are assumed not to be connected.

The questions for this section are with you now: identifying a parallel circuit, the same voltage across each branch and why the other bulbs stay on.

## Subtopic: Current Divides in Parallel

The second rule: the current from the source divides at a junction, and the currents in the branches add up to the total current from the source. When the branches rejoin, the currents combine again.

If an ammeter in the main wire near the battery reads 0,5 amperes, and an ammeter in one branch reads 0,2 amperes, the other branch must carry 0,5 − 0,2 = 0,3 amperes, since 0,2 + 0,3 = 0,5 amperes. Charge is not created or lost at a junction: whatever flows in must flow out.

How the current divides depends on the resistances of the branches. Since every branch has the same voltage, a branch with a smaller resistance carries a larger current, and a branch with a larger resistance carries a smaller current. For two identical bulbs, the current divides equally: if each bulb draws 0,3 amperes, the total from the battery is 0,3 + 0,3 = 0,6 amperes. For a 10 ohm and a 20 ohm resistor in parallel on the same battery, the 10 ohm branch carries twice the current of the 20 ohm branch.

A helpful picture is a stream of water dividing around an island: more water flows down the wider, easier channel and less down the narrow one, and all of it rejoins downstream.

To measure the branch currents, place an ammeter in series within each branch, and another in the main wire before the junction.

A results table for a three-branch circuit might read: main wire 0,9 amperes; branch one 0,3; branch two 0,3; branch three 0,3; sum of branches 0,9. Small differences between real bulbs might give 0,28, 0,31 and 0,30 amperes in the branches, still adding up to 0,89, which is equal to the main reading within the precision of the meter. The conclusion: the current in the main wire equals the sum of the currents in the branches.

## Subtopic: Adding Resistors in Parallel

The third rule: adding resistors in parallel decreases the total resistance of the circuit, so the total current drawn from the source increases.

This can seem surprising, since every resistor has resistance. But each new branch provides an additional path for the charge, so it is easier overall for current to flow, just as opening an extra till at a supermarket lets more customers through each minute even though each till is just as slow. Two identical 10 ohm resistors in parallel have a total resistance of 5 ohms, half of either one. Three identical 30 ohm resistors in parallel have a total resistance of 10 ohms. The total resistance of resistors in parallel is always smaller than the smallest single resistance.

With the same battery, each new branch draws its own current, and the total current is the sum. One bulb drawing 0,3 amperes; two identical bulbs in parallel, 0,6 amperes in total; three, 0,9 amperes. Each bulb stays equally bright, but the battery must supply more and more current, so it runs down faster.

This is the important limit on parallel circuits. In a home, every appliance switched on adds another branch, so the total current drawn from the supply increases. If too many high-power appliances are switched on together on one circuit, the total current can become larger than the wiring can carry safely, which is called an overload. Fuses and circuit breakers protect against this, as you will see in the next topic.

The same idea explains why a car's headlights may dim slightly when the starter motor turns. The starter motor is in parallel with the lights and draws a very large current, so the total current from the battery increases enormously. The battery's own internal resistance then uses up some of its voltage, and slightly less is left for the lights. In an ideal battery with no internal resistance, the lights would not dim at all.

## Subtopic: Comparing Series and Parallel and the Error Museum

A side-by-side comparison for identical bulbs on the same battery. Series: one path; the same current everywhere; voltage divides; adding bulbs increases resistance, decreases current and dims all the bulbs; one break stops everything. Parallel: several paths; current divides and branch currents add; the same voltage across each branch; adding bulbs decreases resistance, increases total current and leaves each bulb equally bright; one break affects only its own branch.

A worked analysis: a 6 volt battery with three identical bulbs in parallel, each drawing 0,4 amperes. The voltage across each bulb is 6 volts; the total current is 0,4 + 0,4 + 0,4 = 1,2 amperes. If one bulb is removed, the other two stay equally bright, each still drawing 0,4 amperes, and the total current falls to 0,8 amperes.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: the voltage divides in parallel; it is the same across every branch. Two: bulbs in parallel are dimmer than one bulb; each is as bright as a single bulb. Three: adding a resistor in parallel increases the total resistance; it decreases it. Four: each branch carries the full current from the battery; the current divides and the branches add up to the total. Five: if one bulb in parallel blows, they all go out; the other branches stay complete.

Layout for a parallel answer: state that the voltage is the same across each branch; show the branch currents adding to the total with working; state how total resistance and total current change when a branch is added or removed; and state the effect on brightness.

Mixed circuits combine both arrangements. Suppose a bulb is in series with a pair of identical bulbs that are in parallel with each other. The total current flows through the first bulb, then divides equally between the two parallel bulbs, each taking half. The first bulb therefore carries twice the current of either parallel bulb and glows the brightest. Reasoning step by step, first about the current through the series part and then about how it divides, solves such questions. If one of the parallel bulbs is removed, the resistance of the parallel section rises, the total current falls, and the first bulb gets dimmer while the remaining parallel bulb gets brighter.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing circuits, analysing branch currents and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through every bulb gets the full push, the dividing stream and more paths, more flow — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Every Bulb Gets the Full Push

In a parallel circuit, the wire splits into separate branches, like the rungs of a ladder, and each bulb sits on its own branch. Each branch is a complete loop to the battery by itself.

Rule one: every branch gets the full voltage of the battery. Two bulbs in parallel on a 6 volt battery get 6 volts each. Each bit of charge only goes through one bulb, so it gives up all its energy there.

So bulbs in parallel each shine just as brightly as a single bulb on its own. Add another bulb, and the others do not get dimmer.

Even better, each branch can have its own switch, so you can turn one light on without the others. And if one bulb blows, the others stay on, because their own loops are still complete.

That is exactly how your home is wired. When the kitchen light blows, the lounge light stays on.

Check it with a voltmeter: across the battery, 6 volts; across bulb one, 6 volts; across bulb two, 6 volts. Every branch gets the lot.

## Subtopic: The Dividing Stream

Rule two: at a junction, the current splits up between the branches, and when they meet again it joins back together. The branch currents add up to the total.

If 0,5 amperes leaves the battery and one branch takes 0,2, the other branch must take 0,3. Nothing is lost at a split: what goes in comes out.

Which branch takes more? The easier one. A branch with less resistance takes more current, like a river splitting around an island: more water flows down the wide channel and less down the narrow one.

For two identical bulbs, the current splits evenly. If each takes 0,3 amperes, the battery supplies 0,6 amperes altogether.

To measure, put one ammeter in the main wire near the battery and one in each branch.

Real bulbs are never perfectly identical, so you might read 0,28, 0,31 and 0,30 amperes in three branches. Add them and you get 0,89, which matches the 0,9 in the main wire.

Here is a tricky one. A bulb sits on the main wire, then the wire splits to two identical bulbs side by side. All the current goes through the first bulb, then halves at the split. So the first bulb carries twice as much current and glows brightest.

Keep the dividing stream in mind and try this part's questions: adding up branch currents and finding a missing one.

## Subtopic: More Paths, More Flow

Rule three: every branch you add makes it easier for current to flow overall, so the total resistance goes down and the total current from the battery goes up.

Think of tills at a supermarket. Each till serves customers at the same speed, but open another till and more customers get through every minute. Two identical 10 ohm resistors side by side act like 5 ohms.

So one bulb takes 0,3 amperes, two take 0,6, three take 0,9. Each bulb is just as bright, but the battery has to work harder and runs down faster.

At home, every appliance you switch on is another branch. Kettle, toaster, heater and iron all on at once can pull so much current that the wires would overheat. That is an overload, and it is why your distribution board has circuit breakers that trip.

Pocket summary of the lesson. In parallel, each branch is its own path. The voltage across each branch equals the battery voltage, so each bulb is as bright as a single bulb. The current divides at junctions and the branch currents add to the total. Adding branches lowers the total resistance and raises the total current. One break affects only its own branch, which is why homes are wired in parallel. Grade 10 to 12 Physical Sciences calculates these values with Ohm's law.

Notice the trade-off between series and parallel. Series: dim bulbs, small current, the battery lasts a long time. Parallel: bright bulbs, big current, the battery runs down faster. You pay for bright light with energy.

Hold more paths, more flow in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: total current, the supermarket tills and overloads.
