### subtopic_1 — Current in a Series Circuit
**Assistant:** If the current is the same everywhere, what gets used up in the bulbs?

**Tutor:** Energy, not charge and not current. The battery gives energy to the charges as they pass through it, and the charges transfer that energy to the bulbs as they pass through them, where it becomes light and heat. The charges themselves are not used up: the same charges keep circulating around the loop, out of one terminal of the battery, through the bulbs and back into the other terminal, again and again. The current measures how much charge passes a point each second, and since every bit of charge that goes into a bulb must come out of it, the current going in equals the current coming out. Voltage is the quantity that changes around the loop. Measured across the battery, it tells you the energy each unit of charge gains; measured across each bulb, it tells you the energy each unit of charge gives up there. A useful picture is a conveyor belt carrying bags of sand around a loop: the belt moves at the same speed everywhere, like the current, while the bags are filled at one point and emptied at others, like the energy. Nothing about the belt itself is used up. Many learners think that current is used up because the battery eventually goes flat, but what runs out is the chemical energy stored in the battery, not the charge. This distinction becomes very important in Grade 10 and later.

### subtopic_2 — Voltage Divides in Series
**Assistant:** Why does the bigger resistor get the bigger share of the voltage?

**Tutor:** Because in a series circuit the same current flows through both resistors, and pushing that current through a bigger resistance requires more energy for each unit of charge. Voltage is the energy given up per unit of charge. Each unit of charge passes through both resistors, carrying the same current, but in the bigger resistor it meets more opposition and so gives up more of its energy there. In fact, the voltage across each resistor is in proportion to its resistance. If one resistor is twice as large as the other, it takes twice the voltage. On a 9 volt battery, a 10 ohm and a 20 ohm resistor take 3 volts and 6 volts. A 10 ohm and a 40 ohm resistor would take 1,8 volts and 7,2 volts, since 1,8 + 7,2 = 9 and the second is four times the first. You can check this with the relationship you will meet in Grade 10, Ohm's law, which says that the voltage across a resistor equals its resistance multiplied by the current through it. With 0,3 amperes flowing, a 10 ohm resistor has 10 × 0,3 = 3 volts and a 20 ohm resistor 20 × 0,3 = 6 volts. This is the principle of the voltage divider, a building block of electronic circuits, used in volume controls and in sensors such as light-dependent resistors that switch street lights on at dusk.

### subtopic_3 — Adding Resistors in Series
**Assistant:** Why are some modern festive lights still wired in series?

**Tutor:** Because series wiring lets many small, low-voltage bulbs run from a higher voltage supply without a transformer, and modern designs have ways to avoid the problem of one dead bulb killing the whole string. Suppose you want to run tiny bulbs designed for about 2,5 volts directly from the 230 volt mains. If you connect about ninety-two of them in series, the voltage divides between them, about 230 ÷ 92 = 2,5 volts each, so each bulb gets the voltage it needs. In parallel, each bulb would get the full 230 volts and burn out instantly. The problem with simple series strings is that one failed bulb opens the circuit. Modern incandescent mini-lights include a tiny device called a shunt inside each bulb: when the filament breaks, the full voltage appears across that bulb, which causes the shunt to connect and carry the current past the dead filament, so the rest of the string stays lit. LED festive lights usually run from a small adapter that supplies a safe low voltage, with the LEDs arranged in short series groups connected in parallel, so a failure affects only a small section. Because these strings are connected to the mains, damaged wires or broken bulbs should never be handled while plugged in, and outdoor lights must be designed for outdoor use and protected by an earth leakage device.

### subtopic_4 — Analysing Series Circuits and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Can a series circuit ever be useful for safety?

**Tutor:** Yes, and the most important example is the fuse or circuit breaker, which only works because it is in series. A fuse is a thin wire designed to melt when the current through it becomes too large. It must be connected in series with the appliance or circuit it protects, so that all the current flowing to that appliance also flows through the fuse. If the current becomes dangerously high, perhaps because of a fault, the fuse wire overheats and melts, breaking the only path, and the current stops everywhere in that circuit. Circuit breakers in a distribution board work the same way, opening a switch in series with each circuit. The same principle is used in many safety switches. A geyser has a thermostat and a separate safety cut-out connected in series with its element; if the thermostat fails and the water gets dangerously hot, the cut-out opens and the element switches off. Some machines have emergency stop buttons wired in series, so pressing any one of them stops the machine. Safety interlocks on microwave ovens are switches in series with the power supply, so the microwave cannot run while the door is open. In each case, the feature that makes series circuits unsuitable for house lighting, that a single break stops everything, is exactly what makes them ideal for safety.
