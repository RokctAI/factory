### subtopic_1 — Voltage and the Voltmeter
**Assistant:** Why does the voltage not add up when cells are in parallel?

**Tutor:** Because each unit of charge flowing around the circuit passes through only one of the cells, so it receives energy from only one cell. Voltage measures the energy given to each unit of charge. In series, the charge has to pass through every cell one after the other, so it collects energy from the first cell, then more from the second, and so on, and the total energy per unit of charge is the sum of the cells' voltages. In parallel, the cells provide alternative paths. At the junction before the cells, the charge splits up: some goes through the first cell, some through the second. Each unit of charge goes through just one cell and so gains the energy of just one cell, 1,5 volts. Then the paths rejoin and the charge travels through the bulb. A helpful picture is a set of stairs. Cells in series are like flights of stairs stacked one above another: climbing all of them takes you higher. Cells in parallel are like several identical staircases side by side: whichever one you climb, you end up at the same height, but more people can climb at once, and each staircase is used less. For cells, being used less means that each cell's chemicals are used up more slowly, which is why cells in parallel last longer. This idea of charge splitting at junctions also explains the behaviour of resistors in parallel, which you will study in a later lesson.

### subtopic_2 — Cells in Series
**Assistant:** Can I mix a new cell and an old cell in my torch?

**Tutor:** It is better not to, whether they are in series or in parallel, because the old cell drags down the performance and can be damaged. In a series arrangement, as in most torches, the current flows through both cells. The old cell has a lower voltage and a higher internal resistance, so the total voltage is less than it should be and the torch is dimmer. As the old cell becomes completely flat, the new cell keeps forcing current through it, which can make the old cell's voltage reverse, heat it up and cause it to leak corrosive chemicals inside the torch, damaging the contacts. In a parallel arrangement, the stronger new cell drives current into the weaker cell even when the torch is switched off, wasting the new cell's energy and possibly causing leakage. Manufacturers therefore advise replacing all the cells in a device at the same time, with cells of the same type and brand, and never mixing rechargeable and non-rechargeable cells. Mixing an alkaline cell with an older zinc-carbon cell causes similar problems, because they have different internal properties. If you find leaked white or blue crusty deposits on the contacts, clean them carefully with a cotton bud dipped in vinegar or lemon juice, which neutralises the alkaline residue, an application of the acid-base chemistry from Term 2, and dry it before inserting new cells.

### subtopic_3 — Cells in Parallel
**Assistant:** Why might a voltmeter show 1,5 V on a cell that will not light a bulb?

**Tutor:** Because a voltmeter draws almost no current, so it measures the cell's voltage when the cell is barely working, and a nearly flat cell can still show a reasonable voltage under those conditions. A good voltmeter has a very high resistance, so only a tiny current flows through it, and the cell does not have to work hard. Every cell also has some internal resistance, the resistance of its own chemicals and electrodes. In a fresh cell, this internal resistance is small, so when the cell delivers a normal current to a bulb, only a little of its voltage is lost inside it. As a cell is used up, its internal resistance increases greatly. When you connect it to a bulb and it tries to deliver a normal current, much of its voltage is used up driving the current through its own internal resistance, and the voltage left for the bulb falls sharply, perhaps to well under a volt, so the bulb is dim or does not light. So the open-circuit reading on a voltmeter can be misleading. A better test is to measure the voltage across the cell while it is connected to a load, such as a bulb or a resistor, which is what many battery testers do. You will study internal resistance properly in Grade 12. It is also why a car battery that seems fine on a meter may fail to start the car on a cold morning, when the starter motor demands a very large current.

### subtopic_4 — Comparing Arrangements and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do solar backup batteries use series and parallel together?

**Tutor:** They connect groups of cells in series to reach the voltage the system needs, and then connect those groups in parallel to increase how much energy can be stored. Suppose a home backup system needs a 48 volt battery. A common lithium iron phosphate cell gives about 3,2 volts, so 15 cells connected in series give about 15 × 3,2 = 48 volts. That string of cells can store a certain amount of energy. To store more, so that the system can run the lights, fridge, Wi-Fi router and television through a long period of load shedding, a second identical string is connected in parallel with the first, which keeps the voltage at 48 volts but doubles the amount of charge, and so the energy, that can be supplied. Lead-acid systems work the same way, with 2 volt cells grouped into 12 volt batteries, and four 12 volt batteries in series giving 48 volts. An electronic battery management system monitors each cell's voltage and temperature to keep them balanced and to prevent overcharging or deep discharging, which would damage them. An inverter then converts the battery's direct current into the 230 volt alternating current used by household appliances. Solar panels follow the same logic: panels are wired in series strings to raise the voltage and the strings in parallel to increase the current. Electric cars contain thousands of cells arranged in this way, often several hundred volts in total.
