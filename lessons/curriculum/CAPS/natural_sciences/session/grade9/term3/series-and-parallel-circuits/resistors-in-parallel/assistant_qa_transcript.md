### subtopic_1 — Voltage in a Parallel Circuit
**Assistant:** How can adding a resistor make the total resistance smaller?

**Tutor:** Because in parallel the new resistor does not add an obstacle in the existing path; it adds a completely new path alongside it. Resistance measures how hard it is for current to flow through the circuit as a whole from one terminal of the battery to the other. With one resistor, all the charge must squeeze through that one path. With a second resistor in parallel, the charge has two paths to choose from, so more charge can flow each second for the same voltage, and the circuit as a whole offers less opposition. The supermarket picture captures this: each till processes customers at the same rate, but two open tills serve twice as many customers per minute as one, so the queue moves through more easily overall. Similarly, a road with two lanes carries more traffic than a road with one. The calculation for identical resistors is simple: two identical resistors in parallel have half the resistance of one, three have a third, and so on. So two 10 ohm resistors give 5 ohms and three 30 ohm resistors give 10 ohms. For different resistors there is a formula you will learn in Grade 10, based on adding the reciprocals of the resistances. Whatever the values, the result is always smaller than the smallest resistor in the group, because even the easiest single path is made easier still by adding another path beside it.

### subtopic_2 — Current Divides in Parallel
**Assistant:** Why does the battery run down faster with bulbs in parallel?

**Tutor:** Because the battery has to supply a larger total current, which means it delivers more energy every second, so its chemicals are used up faster. Each bulb in parallel has the full battery voltage across it and draws the same current it would on its own. With two identical bulbs, the battery supplies twice the current; with three, three times. Since every unit of charge leaving the battery carries the same energy, set by the voltage, delivering more charge per second means delivering more energy per second. Two bulbs glowing at full brightness use twice the energy per second of one bulb, and the battery's store of chemical energy runs out in about half the time. Compare a series circuit: there the bulbs are dim and the total current is smaller than for one bulb, so the battery lasts longer but gives less light. There is no free lunch: bright light from several bulbs costs energy. This is the same reason that a household's electricity bill rises with the number of appliances switched on at once. At home, the supply comes from the national grid rather than a battery, so it does not run down, but the cost and the total current do rise. You will calculate the cost of running appliances later this term, using the power each one uses and the time it is switched on.

### subtopic_3 — Adding Resistors in Parallel
**Assistant:** What happens if a wire with no resistance is connected across a bulb in parallel?

**Tutor:** That creates a short circuit, and it can be dangerous. A short circuit is a path with almost no resistance connected across the source. Because the parallel branches all have the same voltage, and current takes paths in inverse proportion to their resistance, almost all the current takes the zero-resistance wire instead of passing through the bulb. The bulb goes out, because almost no current flows through it. More importantly, the total resistance of the circuit falls almost to zero, so the battery tries to push an enormous current through the wire. The wire and the battery heat up rapidly; a thin wire may glow, melt its insulation and start a fire, and a battery can be damaged, leak or, for lithium batteries, catch fire. That is why you must never connect the terminals of a battery directly with a wire, never put a coin or keys in a pocket with loose batteries, and never connect an ammeter across a component, because an ammeter has very low resistance and acts almost like a short circuit. In household wiring, short circuits happen when damaged insulation lets the live and neutral wires touch, and the huge current that results is what makes a circuit breaker trip or a fuse blow almost instantly, cutting off the supply before a fire can start.

### subtopic_4 — Comparing Series and Parallel and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Are any circuits in a house wired in series?

**Tutor:** Yes, a few parts are, although every appliance and light is connected in parallel with the supply. The switches are the clearest example. Each light switch is connected in series with the light it controls, on the live wire, so that opening the switch breaks the path to that light alone, while the light itself is on a parallel branch of the lighting circuit. Each fuse or circuit breaker is in series with the circuit it protects, so that when it opens, it cuts off every outlet on that circuit. The main switch on the distribution board is in series with everything in the house. The earth leakage unit, which you will study in the next topic, is also connected in series with all the circuits it protects. Inside appliances, there are more series parts: a geyser's thermostat and its safety cut-out are in series with its heating element, a kettle's on-off switch and its thermostat are in series with the element, and the two-way switches at the top and bottom of a staircase are arranged so that either one can control the same light. So a house uses both kinds of connection: parallel to give every appliance the full 230 volts and independent operation, and series to place control and protection devices in the path of the current they need to control.
