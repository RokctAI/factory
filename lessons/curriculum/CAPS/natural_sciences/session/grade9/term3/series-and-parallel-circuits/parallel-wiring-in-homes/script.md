# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains how household circuits are wired in parallel, why that arrangement is used, how the current drawn by an appliance depends on its resistance when the voltage is fixed, and how the total current in a household circuit builds up as appliances are switched on. The anchor facts are that the South African mains supply is 230 volts alternating current at 50 hertz; that each appliance, connected in parallel, receives the full 230 volts; that a 2000 watt kettle has a resistance of about 26 ohms and draws about 8,7 amperes; and that the currents of appliances on the same circuit add, so that a kettle, a toaster and a microwave together draw about 8,7 + 3,5 + 5,2 = 17,4 amperes. The governing principle is that for the same voltage, a smaller resistance draws a larger current, and that the parallel branches of a household circuit add their currents.

## Subtopic: Why Homes Are Wired in Parallel

Electricity reaches a home from the national grid through a cable to the meter, often a prepaid meter, and then to the distribution board, the box containing the main switch, the earth leakage unit and the circuit breakers. From the distribution board, separate circuits run to different parts of the house: lighting circuits, plug circuits, and dedicated circuits for heavy appliances such as the stove and the geyser.

Within each circuit, every light and every plug socket is connected in parallel between the live and neutral wires. This arrangement is chosen for three reasons, which follow directly from the rules of parallel circuits.

First, every appliance receives the full mains voltage of 230 volts, which is the voltage it is designed for. A kettle, a television and a lamp each get 230 volts, whatever else is switched on. If they were in series, the voltage would be divided and none would work properly.

Second, each appliance can be switched on and off independently. Each light has its own switch in series with it on its branch, and each plug socket has its own switch.

Third, if one appliance fails or a bulb blows, the others keep working, because their own branches are still complete.

The South African mains supply is alternating current, AC, which reverses direction 50 times every second, at a frequency of 50 hertz. A battery supplies direct current, DC, which flows in one direction only. The rules of series and parallel circuits that you have learnt apply to both.

Each circuit leaving the distribution board consists of three wires inside the walls and ceiling: the live wire, which carries the current to the appliances at 230 volts; the neutral wire, which completes the circuit back to the supply; and the earth wire, a safety wire connected to the ground. Every light fitting and socket on that circuit is connected across the live and neutral wires, which is what makes them parallel branches. You will study the role of each wire, and the 3-pin plug that connects to them, in the next topic.

A circuit diagram of a lighting circuit shows the live and neutral wires as two long parallel lines from the distribution board, with each light and its switch drawn on a rung between them. The switch is always placed in the live wire, so that when it is off, the light fitting is disconnected from the live supply and is safer to work on.

The prepaid meter measures the energy used in kilowatt-hours and deducts it from the credit bought. When the credit runs out, a switch inside the meter opens, cutting off the whole house, which is itself a switch in series with every circuit.

## Subtopic: Current Depends on Resistance for the Same Voltage

Since every appliance in a home has the same voltage across it, 230 volts, the current each one draws depends only on its resistance. The smaller the resistance, the larger the current; the larger the resistance, the smaller the current. This relationship, current equals voltage divided by resistance, is Ohm's law, which you will use fully in Grade 10.

Heating appliances have heating elements with fairly low resistances, so they draw large currents. A 2000 watt kettle has an element of about 26 ohms and draws about 230 ÷ 26,45 = 8,7 amperes. A 2000 watt heater is similar. A geyser element of 3000 watts draws about 13 amperes. An 800 watt toaster draws about 3,5 amperes.

Appliances that use little energy have much higher resistances and draw small currents. A 10 watt LED bulb draws about 0,04 amperes. A phone charger draws a small fraction of an ampere. A television might draw about half an ampere.

So the appliances that produce heat, such as kettles, geysers, stoves, heaters, irons, hair dryers and toasters, draw the largest currents, and they are the ones that most often cause overloads and most affect the electricity bill.

The current an appliance draws, together with its power rating in watts, is printed on a rating plate or label on the appliance. You will use power ratings to calculate the cost of electricity later this term.

A table of typical currents at 230 volts helps with estimates: stove plate about 8 amperes; geyser about 13; kettle about 8,7; heater 2000 watts about 8,7; iron about 4 to 10; microwave about 5; toaster about 3,5; fridge about 1 when running; television about 0,5; laptop charger about 0,3; LED bulb about 0,04. The heating appliances stand out immediately.

The same principle explains why an old filament bulb and an LED bulb giving the same light draw very different currents. A 60 watt filament bulb draws about 0,26 amperes, while an LED bulb giving the same light draws about 0,04, because the LED has a much higher effective resistance and wastes far less energy as heat.

## Subtopic: Adding Up the Current

Each appliance switched on in a circuit adds another parallel branch, so the total current in the circuit's cable is the sum of the currents of all the appliances that are on.

Example: a kitchen plug circuit protected by a 16 ampere circuit breaker. The kettle draws 8,7 amperes. Switch on the toaster as well, adding 3,5 amperes: total 12,2 amperes, within the limit. Now switch on the microwave, adding 5,2 amperes: total 8,7 + 3,5 + 5,2 = 17,4 amperes, more than the 16 ampere rating, so the breaker trips and switches off the whole circuit.

If there were no breaker, the cable would carry a current larger than it was designed for. The cable has a small resistance, and a larger current heats it more, so the insulation could melt and a fire could start. This is called overloading a circuit.

Multiplugs and adaptors create a special risk. Plugging a heater, a kettle and an iron into one double adaptor or multiplug on a single wall socket sends all their current through that one socket and the short cable behind it, which may overheat even if the circuit breaker does not trip quickly.

That is why stoves and geysers have their own dedicated circuits with thick cables and their own breakers: they draw large currents for long periods.

South African homes usually have several separate circuits so that no single circuit carries too much. A typical small house might have one or two lighting circuits protected by 10 ampere breakers, two or more plug circuits protected by 16 or 20 ampere breakers, a stove circuit with a larger breaker and a geyser circuit with its own breaker. Spreading high-power appliances across different circuits, for example plugging the heater into a socket in another room's circuit, avoids overloading any one of them. Wiring must follow the national wiring code, SANS 10142, and an electrical certificate of compliance is required when a house is sold.

## Subtopic: Planning Safe Use and the Error Museum

Good habits follow from the physics. Do not run several heating appliances at once on the same circuit. Do not overload multiplugs with high-power appliances. Use extension cords that are rated for the current they will carry, and unroll cable drums fully so that the coiled cable does not overheat. When a breaker trips, switch off or unplug some appliances before resetting it; if it trips again with little switched on, there may be a fault, and a qualified electrician should be called.

During load shedding, when power returns, many appliances that were left on start at the same moment, drawing a large total current. Switching off heavy appliances during an outage and turning them on one at a time when power returns reduces this surge.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: appliances at home are in series; they are in parallel. Two: each appliance gets a share of the 230 volts; each gets the full 230 volts. Three: a higher resistance draws more current; at the same voltage, a lower resistance draws more current. Four: switching on more appliances reduces the total current; it increases it. Five: a multiplug is safe for any number of appliances; the total current through it can overheat it.

Layout for a household circuit answer: state that the appliances are in parallel with 230 volts across each; compare currents using resistance; add the currents of appliances that are on; compare the total with the breaker rating; and state the consequence.

Saving electricity also reduces current. Switching off appliances that are not in use, using LED bulbs, boiling only the water needed, drying washing outdoors rather than in a tumble dryer, and putting the geyser on a timer all reduce the total current drawn, lower the bill and reduce the load on the grid at peak times.

## Subtopic: Branches from the Board

Electricity comes into your home through the meter to the distribution board, the box with the row of switches called circuit breakers. From there, wires run out to the lights, the plugs, the stove and the geyser.

Every light and plug is connected in parallel. That gives three big benefits. Every appliance gets the full 230 volts it was made for. You can switch each one on and off by itself. And when one bulb blows, everything else keeps going.

Our mains electricity is called alternating current, or AC: it swings back and forth 50 times a second. A battery gives direct current, DC, which flows one way only. But the series and parallel rules work the same for both.

Inside the walls, each circuit has three wires: live, neutral and earth. Each light and socket is connected between live and neutral, which makes them parallel branches. The light switch always goes in the live wire, so a switched-off light is safer to work on.

## Subtopic: Thirsty Appliances

Every appliance at home gets the same 230 volts, so how much current it draws depends on its resistance. Low resistance means a big current. High resistance means a small current.

The thirstiest appliances are the ones that make heat: the kettle, geyser, stove, heater, iron and toaster. Their elements have low resistance. A kettle element of about 26 ohms gulps nearly 9 amperes. A geyser takes about 13.

The sippers have high resistance. An LED bulb takes only about 0,04 amperes. A phone charger takes less than one ampere.

Look on the label or rating plate of an appliance and you will see how many watts it uses, which tells you how thirsty it is.

That is also why heating appliances make your electricity bill go up so fast, and why turning down the geyser or boiling only the water you need saves money.

Compare two bulbs giving the same light: an old 60 watt filament bulb draws about a quarter of an ampere, but an LED bulb draws about 0,04. The LED wastes far less as heat.

A quick rule: if an appliance gets hot on purpose, it is thirsty. If it only gives light, sound or pictures, it usually sips.

## Subtopic: Adding Up to a Trip

Every appliance you switch on is another branch, and their currents add up in the wires.

Kitchen example: the circuit breaker allows 16 amperes. Kettle on: 8,7. Add the toaster: 12,2, still fine. Add the microwave: 17,4. Click. The breaker trips and the whole kitchen circuit goes dead.

That click is protecting you. Too much current makes the wires hot, and hot wires can melt their plastic coating and start a fire.

Watch out for multiplugs. A heater, kettle and iron all plugged into one adaptor push all their current through one socket, which can overheat.

When power comes back after load shedding, everything that was left on starts at once. Switch heavy appliances off during the outage and turn them on one by one later.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Homes are wired in parallel, so every appliance gets the full 230 volts, works independently and keeps going if another fails. At the same voltage, lower resistance means bigger current, so heating appliances draw the most. Currents of appliances that are on add up, and too much current overloads a circuit, so breakers trip. Do not overload multiplugs. Grade 10 to 12 Physical Sciences calculates these values with Ohm's law and power.

That is why houses have several circuits: lights on one or two, plugs on others, the stove and geyser on their own. Spread heavy appliances across different circuits so no single one carries too much.
