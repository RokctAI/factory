# Part 1 — Expert

This session compares alternative energy sources for generating electricity in South Africa, explains how the nuclear power station at Koeberg works, and describes how nuclear waste is disposed of. The anchor facts are that renewable sources such as solar, wind and hydroelectric energy are replenished naturally while fossil fuels and uranium are non-renewable; that solar panels change light directly into electricity, while wind and water turn turbines connected to generators; that Koeberg, near Cape Town, is the only nuclear power station on the African continent; that nuclear fission of uranium releases heat that makes steam to drive turbines; and that radioactive waste is stored and isolated, with low- and intermediate-level waste sent to Vaalputs in the Northern Cape and spent fuel kept at Koeberg. Every idea here builds on the previous lesson on coal power stations.

## Subtopic: Renewable and Non-renewable Sources

An energy source is renewable if it is replaced naturally at least as fast as we use it. Sunlight, wind, flowing water, waves and tides, and heat from the Earth are renewable. A source is non-renewable if it was formed over very long periods, or not at all on human timescales, so that once it is used it is gone. Coal, oil, natural gas and uranium are non-renewable.

South Africa has excellent renewable resources. The Northern Cape is one of the sunniest places on Earth, with clear skies for most of the year. The coastlines of the Eastern and Western Cape have strong, reliable winds. The Orange River provides flowing water for hydroelectric power.

Since about 2011 South Africa has added many solar and wind farms through a government programme in which independent power producers build renewable power stations and sell electricity to the grid. Many homes, schools, farms and businesses have also installed rooftop solar panels, especially during periods of load shedding.

Other sources are possible too. Biomass power stations burn plant waste such as bagasse, the fibre left after sugar cane is crushed, and some sugar mills in KwaZulu-Natal generate their own electricity this way. Landfill gas, which is mainly methane from rotting rubbish, is captured at some municipal dumps and used to run generators. Wave and tidal power are being studied but are not yet used on a large scale in South Africa. Natural gas power stations can be started very quickly, so they are useful for meeting short peaks in demand, but gas is a fossil fuel that releases carbon dioxide.

A useful way to compare sources is to ask three questions. Does it run out? Does it release carbon dioxide or other pollution while generating? Is it available whenever we need it, or only when the weather allows? Coal fails the first two tests but passes the third. Solar and wind pass the first two but not the third. Nuclear passes the second and third, but uranium is non-renewable and the waste is a problem. No source scores perfectly, which is why choices about energy involve trade-offs between cost, reliability, safety and the environment.

## Subtopic: Solar, Wind and Water

Solar photovoltaic panels change light energy directly into electrical energy. Each panel contains cells made of silicon, a semiconductor. When light falls on a cell, it gives energy to electrons in the silicon, which then flow as a direct current. An inverter changes this direct current into alternating current for use in homes and on the grid. There are no moving parts and no fuel. Solar panels produce electricity only while the sun shines, so they need batteries or other sources to supply power at night.

Concentrated solar power works differently. Large fields of mirrors focus sunlight onto a receiver, heating a fluid that is used to make steam and turn a turbine, as in a coal station. Some of these stations in the Northern Cape, near Upington and Pofadder, store heat in tanks of molten salt so they can continue to generate electricity for some hours after sunset.

Wind turbines have large blades that are pushed by moving air. The kinetic energy of the wind turns the blades, which turn a generator in the housing at the top of the tower. Wind farms near Jeffreys Bay in the Eastern Cape and Darling and Caledon in the Western Cape feed the grid. Wind turbines generate electricity only when the wind blows within a suitable range of speeds.

Hydroelectric power stations use water stored behind a dam. Water flows down through pipes, gaining kinetic energy as it falls, and turns turbines connected to generators. The energy transfer is gravitational potential energy → kinetic energy → electrical energy. South Africa's main hydroelectric stations are at the Gariep and Vanderkloof dams on the Orange River. South Africa also has pumped-storage schemes such as Drakensberg, Palmiet and Ingula: when demand is low, electricity is used to pump water to an upper dam, and at peak times the water flows back down through turbines to generate electricity quickly.

Advantages of these renewable sources are that they release no carbon dioxide or sulfur dioxide while they run, use no fuel, and never run out. Disadvantages are that solar and wind are variable, depending on weather and time of day; they need large areas of land; and hydroelectric power is limited in a dry country with few large rivers.

Because no single source is perfect, electricity planners talk about an energy mix. A mix combines sources that run steadily, such as nuclear and coal, with variable renewables, and with sources that can be switched on quickly, such as pumped storage, gas turbines and large batteries. When the sun sets and solar output falls just as people come home and switch on stoves and kettles, the quick sources fill the gap. Spreading wind farms across different regions also helps, because the wind is unlikely to drop everywhere at once.

## Subtopic: Nuclear Power at Koeberg

Koeberg Nuclear Power Station is on the coast about 30 kilometres north of Cape Town. It began supplying electricity in the 1980s and is the only nuclear power station in Africa. It has two units, each able to produce about 970 megawatts, so together 2 × 970 = 1 940 megawatts, a large share of the Western Cape's electricity.

The fuel is uranium, formed into small ceramic pellets stacked inside long metal rods. Uranium atoms have very large, unstable nuclei. In a process called nuclear fission, a uranium nucleus absorbs a neutron and splits into two smaller nuclei, releasing a large amount of energy and two or three more neutrons. These neutrons can split more uranium nuclei, producing a chain reaction. Control rods made of materials that absorb neutrons are moved in and out of the reactor core to keep the chain reaction steady and to shut it down when needed.

The energy released by fission heats water that is kept under high pressure in the reactor. This hot water passes through a steam generator, where it heats a separate supply of water and turns it into steam. From here the station works just like a coal station: the steam turns turbines, the turbines turn generators, and electricity is sent to the grid. Koeberg uses cold seawater from the Atlantic Ocean to condense the steam.

The energy transfer is nuclear potential energy → thermal energy → kinetic energy → electrical energy.

Advantages of nuclear power are that a small mass of fuel releases an enormous amount of energy, that the station runs day and night regardless of weather, and that it releases no carbon dioxide while operating. Disadvantages are that it is very expensive and slow to build, that it produces radioactive waste, and that an accident could release radioactive material, as happened at Chernobyl in 1986 and Fukushima in 2011. Koeberg is built with thick concrete containment buildings and many safety systems, and it is regulated by the National Nuclear Regulator.

Koeberg was originally designed to run for 40 years. After a long safety review, the regulator has approved extending the life of its units by a further 20 years, so it is expected to keep supplying electricity well into the 2040s. Extending an existing station is far cheaper than building a new one, but it requires replacing major parts, such as the steam generators, and showing that the reactor vessel and safety systems remain sound.

## Subtopic: Nuclear Waste and the Error Museum

Used nuclear fuel and materials that have been in contact with it are radioactive: they give off radiation that can damage living cells and cause cancer. Some radioactive substances lose their radioactivity in days, but others remain dangerous for thousands of years, so the waste must be isolated from people and the environment.

Low- and intermediate-level waste, such as contaminated clothing, tools, filters and resins, is sealed in steel drums or concrete containers and transported to the Vaalputs national radioactive waste disposal facility in the dry, sparsely populated Namaqualand region of the Northern Cape. There it is buried in engineered trenches, where low rainfall and deep, stable ground help keep it isolated.

High-level waste is the spent fuel itself. When fuel rods are removed from the reactor they are very hot and highly radioactive. At Koeberg they are first stored under water in deep pools inside the station, where the water cools them and blocks the radiation. Older spent fuel is moved into thick, sealed steel casks on the site. South Africa does not yet have a permanent deep underground store for high-level waste; finding a safe long-term solution is a challenge for every country with nuclear power.

Radiation can be blocked by dense materials. Water, thick concrete and lead all absorb radiation, which is why fuel pools are several metres deep and why casks have very thick walls. Workers at Koeberg wear badges called dosimeters that measure the radiation they receive, so that their exposure can be kept well within safe limits.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: nuclear power stations burn uranium; there is no burning, the energy comes from splitting nuclei. Two: the steam from a nuclear power station is radioactive smoke; the water that makes steam for the turbines is kept separate from the reactor water. Three: renewable energy means energy that is free; it means a source that is naturally replaced, though the equipment still costs money. Four: solar panels work only in hot weather; they need light, not heat, and in fact work slightly better when cool. Five: nuclear waste can simply be buried anywhere; it needs specially chosen and engineered sites.

## Subtopic: Sun, Wind and Water

Some energy sources run out, and some do not. Coal, oil, gas and uranium will run out. Sunlight, wind and flowing water keep coming back. Those are renewable.

Solar panels turn light straight into electricity, with no moving parts. The Northern Cape is perfect for them, because the sun shines almost every day.

Wind turbines are giant propellers. Wind pushes the blades, the blades spin a generator, and out comes electricity. You see them near Jeffreys Bay and along the Western Cape coast.

Hydroelectric stations let water from a dam rush down through turbines. The Gariep Dam on the Orange River has one.

Pumped storage is like a giant water battery. When electricity is spare at night, water is pumped up into a high dam. When everyone needs power in the evening, the water rushes down again through turbines.

The catch? The sun sets, the wind drops, and South Africa is a dry country with few big rivers. So these sources need help from batteries, stored water or other power stations.

## Subtopic: Splitting Atoms

Koeberg, near Cape Town, is Africa's only nuclear power station.

Instead of burning coal, it splits atoms of uranium. When a uranium atom splits, it releases a huge amount of heat and a few tiny particles called neutrons. Those neutrons split more atoms, and so on: a chain reaction. Control rods soak up spare neutrons to keep it steady.

The heat boils water, the steam spins turbines, and the turbines spin generators, just like a coal station. Cold seawater from the Atlantic cools the steam.

The good side: a tiny amount of fuel gives a huge amount of energy, it runs day and night, and it releases no carbon dioxide while it runs.

The bad side: it is very expensive to build, an accident could be serious, and it leaves dangerous waste.

Think of the chain reaction like a row of falling dominoes that branches out: one knocks over two, two knock over four. Control rods are like removing dominoes so it never runs away.

## Subtopic: Keeping the Waste Safe

Nuclear waste gives off radiation that can harm living cells. Some of it stays dangerous for thousands of years, so it must be kept far away from people and water.

Less dangerous waste, like used gloves and tools, is sealed in drums and buried at Vaalputs, a remote site in the dry Northern Cape.

The used fuel rods are the most dangerous. At Koeberg they cool down in deep pools of water, which also block the radiation, and later go into thick steel casks on site. A permanent home for them is still being planned.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Renewable sources such as sun, wind and water are naturally replaced; coal and uranium are not. Solar panels change light into electricity; wind and water turn turbines and generators. Koeberg splits uranium atoms in a chain reaction to make steam, with no carbon dioxide while running, but it produces radioactive waste. Low-level waste goes to Vaalputs; spent fuel is stored at Koeberg. In Grade 10 to 12 Physical Science you will study atoms, nuclei and radioactivity in more depth.
