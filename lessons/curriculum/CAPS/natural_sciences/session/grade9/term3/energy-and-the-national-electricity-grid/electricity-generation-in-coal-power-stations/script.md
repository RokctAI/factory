# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains how a coal-fired power station generates electricity, the energy transfers involved, why the process is far from perfectly efficient, and the environmental effects of coal power in South Africa. The anchor facts are that coal stores chemical potential energy; that burning coal in a boiler releases thermal energy that turns water into high-pressure steam; that steam turns a turbine, transferring kinetic energy; that the turbine turns a generator, in which a rotating magnet and coils of wire produce electricity; that the steam is condensed and the water reused; and that most of South Africa's electricity still comes from coal, which releases carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide and ash. Every idea here builds on the earlier work on energy transfer, conservation of energy and the electric circuit.

## Subtopic: From Coal to Steam

Coal is a fossil fuel formed over millions of years from the remains of plants that were buried and compressed. It stores chemical potential energy in the bonds between its carbon and hydrogen atoms. South Africa has large coal reserves, mainly in Mpumalanga, and for this reason most of its power stations were built on or near the coalfields, so coal can be delivered by conveyor belt directly from the mine.

South Africa's coal-fired stations have long supplied the large majority of the country's electricity, roughly four fifths of it in recent years, although the share is slowly falling as solar and wind power are added. The stations have names many South Africans know from the news, such as Matimba and Medupi near Lephalale in Limpopo, and Kendal, Kusile, Duvha, Matla and Majuba in Mpumalanga. A large station burns many tens of thousands of tonnes of coal every day, so a reliable supply from a nearby mine is essential.

At the power station the coal is first ground into a fine powder in mills. Powdered coal burns quickly and completely, because it has a very large surface area in contact with air. The powder is blown into a huge furnace in the boiler, where it burns at a very high temperature. This is a combustion reaction: carbon reacts with oxygen to form carbon dioxide, and energy is released.

C + O₂ → CO₂

The walls of the boiler are lined with thousands of metres of steel tubes filled with water. The thermal energy from the burning coal is transferred to the water, which boils and becomes steam. The steam is then heated further and kept under very high pressure, so it carries a large amount of energy and can push hard on whatever it meets.

Before the water enters the boiler it is carefully purified, because dissolved salts would form a hard scale inside the tubes, just as scale forms inside a kettle in areas with hard water. Scale is a poor conductor of heat, so it would waste energy and could cause the tubes to overheat and burst. The hot waste gases from the furnace are also used to preheat the incoming water and air before they leave through the chimney, which recovers some energy that would otherwise be lost.

The energy transfer so far is: chemical potential energy in coal → thermal energy in the flames and hot gases → thermal energy in high-pressure steam.

## Subtopic: Turbine and Generator

The high-pressure steam is led through pipes to a turbine. A turbine is a long shaft fitted with many rows of angled blades, a little like a set of very large fans on one axle. As steam rushes past the blades it pushes them, and the shaft spins very fast, at about 3 000 revolutions per minute in South African power stations, which matches the 50 hertz frequency of the mains supply.

The steam usually passes through several turbines on the same shaft: a high-pressure turbine first, then intermediate-pressure and low-pressure turbines. The blades in each stage are larger than the last, because the steam expands and its pressure falls as it gives up energy. Using several stages extracts as much energy from the steam as possible. The thermal energy of the steam is transferred to kinetic energy of the spinning turbine. As the steam does this work it expands and cools.

The turbine shaft is connected directly to a generator. A generator works on the principle you met when studying electromagnetism: when a magnet moves relative to a coil of wire, or a coil moves in a magnetic field, a voltage is produced in the coil, and if the coil is part of a complete circuit, a current flows. In a power-station generator, a large spinning electromagnet, called the rotor, turns inside fixed coils of copper wire, called the stator. The changing magnetic field through the coils produces a large voltage and an alternating current.

The rotor is an electromagnet rather than a permanent magnet, because a very strong magnetic field is needed and an electromagnet's strength can be controlled. A small direct current is fed into the rotor's coils to magnetise it. The generator produces electricity at a voltage of many thousands of volts, which is then increased much further by a step-up transformer before it is sent into the national grid. You will see why in the lesson on the grid.

The energy transfer here is: kinetic energy of the turbine → kinetic energy of the rotor → electrical energy in the stator coils.

A single large generating unit can produce hundreds of megawatts. The newest coal power stations in South Africa, Medupi in Limpopo and Kusile in Mpumalanga, each have six units and are among the largest coal power stations in the world, with a capacity of close to 4 800 megawatts each.

## Subtopic: Cooling, Efficiency and the Environment

After passing through the turbine, the steam has lost much of its energy, but it is still warm. It flows into a condenser, where it is cooled and turned back into water so that it can be pumped back to the boiler and used again. The cooling water that takes heat away from the condenser is itself cooled in the large curved cooling towers you see at many power stations. In a wet cooling tower, warm water is sprayed inside the tower, some of it evaporates, and the cloud rising from the top is water vapour condensing, not smoke. Because South Africa is a water-scarce country, Medupi and Kusile use dry cooling, which uses far less water.

No power station converts all the chemical energy in its coal into electrical energy. Energy is conserved, but much of it is transferred to the surroundings as heat: in the hot gases leaving the chimney, in the cooling water, and through friction and heating in the machines. A typical coal power station has an efficiency of only about one third. If 300 megajoules of chemical energy are released from coal and the efficiency is one third, then 300 MJ ÷ 3 = 100 MJ becomes electrical energy, and the other 200 MJ is transferred to the surroundings as heat. We can write efficiency as useful energy out divided by total energy in, so 100 ÷ 300 = 1/3, or about 33 percent.

Burning coal also affects the environment and people's health. Carbon dioxide is a greenhouse gas that contributes to global warming and climate change, and South Africa's coal-based electricity makes it one of the larger carbon dioxide emitters per person. Coal contains sulfur, which forms sulfur dioxide when it burns; sulfur dioxide dissolves in rain to form acid rain, and you met this reaction when studying non-metal oxides. Fine particles of fly ash and soot are trapped by filters and precipitators, but some escape and harm people's lungs; the Highveld area of Mpumalanga, where many power stations are clustered, has some of the poorest air quality in the country. Mining coal scars the land and can pollute rivers with acid mine drainage, and the ash left over must be stored in large ash dumps.

## Subtopic: The Whole Chain and the Error Museum

Put the stages together as a flow diagram: coal is mined and transported; it is ground and burned in the boiler, releasing thermal energy; water becomes high-pressure steam; the steam turns the turbine; the turbine turns the generator; the generator produces electricity, which goes to the grid through transformers; the steam is condensed and the water returns to the boiler. The energy chain is chemical potential → thermal → kinetic → electrical, with heat transferred to the surroundings at every stage.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: power stations make energy; they transfer energy from one form to another, they do not create it. Two: the clouds from cooling towers are smoke; they are water vapour condensing into droplets, while the waste gases leave through the tall chimney. Three: all the energy in the coal becomes electricity; only about a third does, and the rest is transferred to the surroundings as heat. Four: the steam itself flows along the wires to our homes; the steam stays in a closed loop in the power station, and only electrical energy is transmitted. Five: the generator works because of the heat; it works because a magnet rotates inside coils of wire, and the heat only provides the steam that turns it.

Notice that every exhibit is corrected by a principle you already know: the law of conservation of energy, the difference between a gas and condensed droplets, the idea of useful and wasted energy, and electromagnetic induction. When a question asks you to explain a power station, use those principles rather than describing the machines only.

Layout for a power-station answer: name each part in order, state what happens at each part, give the energy transfer at each step, and mention where energy is transferred to the surroundings.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through fire and steam, the spinning magnet, and what goes up the chimney: plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Fire and Steam

Think of a power station as a giant kettle connected to a giant bicycle dynamo.

It starts with coal. Coal is made from ancient plants, and it stores chemical energy. South Africa digs most of its coal in Mpumalanga, and the power stations are built right next to the mines.

The coal is crushed into powder as fine as flour. Powder burns fast and fully, because so much of it touches the air.

The powder burns in a huge furnace called the boiler. The walls are covered with pipes full of water. The heat boils the water into steam, and the steam is kept under great pressure, like the steam squeezed inside a pressure cooker, only far stronger.

So far: chemical energy in coal becomes heat, and heat is stored in the steam.

Why so much pressure? High-pressure steam is packed with energy and pushes very hard. A kettle lid only rattles, but this steam is strong enough to spin machines that weigh hundreds of tonnes.

## Subtopic: The Spinning Magnet

The steam rushes into a turbine: a long axle with rows and rows of blades. Steam pushes the blades like wind pushing a pinwheel, and the axle spins about 3 000 times a minute.

The turbine is joined to the generator. Inside the generator a huge magnet spins inside coils of copper wire. Whenever a magnet moves near a coil, electricity is pushed through the wire. Spin it fast and keep it spinning, and you get a steady supply.

A bicycle dynamo that powers a bike lamp does the same thing on a tiny scale: the wheel spins a little magnet next to a coil.

So the chain is: coal's chemical energy, then heat, then the movement of the turbine, then electrical energy.

Medupi and Kusile are two of the biggest coal power stations in the world. Each can produce close to 4 800 megawatts.

To picture that, one megawatt is a million watts, the power of about 500 kettles boiling at once. So one of these stations could, in principle, run millions of kettles at the same time.

Keep the spinning magnet in mind and try this part's questions: the turbine, the generator and the energy chain.

## Subtopic: What Goes Up the Chimney

After the turbine, the steam is cooled back into water and sent round again. The big curved towers help with cooling. The white clouds above them are water vapour, not smoke.

Here is a surprise: only about one third of the coal's energy becomes electricity. If burning the coal releases 300 units of energy, about 100 become electricity and about 200 escape as heat. The energy is not destroyed; it just goes somewhere we cannot use it.

Where does it go? Mostly into the cooling water and the air, and out of the chimney in hot gases. That is why power stations need so much cooling.

The tall chimneys release the waste gases. Carbon dioxide adds to global warming. Sulfur dioxide makes acid rain. Tiny ash particles harm people's lungs, which is why the air in parts of Mpumalanga is so polluted. Coal mines also scar the land and can poison rivers.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Coal stores chemical energy. Burning it in a boiler turns water into high-pressure steam. Steam spins a turbine, and the turbine spins a generator, where a magnet turning in coils produces electricity. The steam is condensed and reused. Only about a third of the coal's energy becomes electrical energy; the rest is transferred to the surroundings as heat. Coal power releases carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide and ash.
