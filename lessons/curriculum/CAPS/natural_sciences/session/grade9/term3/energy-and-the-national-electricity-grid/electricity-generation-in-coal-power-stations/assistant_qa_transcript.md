### subtopic_1 — From Coal to Steam
**Assistant:** Why can't we just make power stations 100 percent efficient?

**Tutor:** Because some of the energy must always be transferred to the surroundings when heat is turned into motion, and this is a basic law of nature rather than a problem of poor engineering. A heat engine such as a steam turbine works by letting energy flow from something very hot, the steam, to something cooler, the condenser. Only part of that energy flow can be turned into useful work; the rest must be passed on to the cooler surroundings, otherwise the steam could not be condensed and the cycle could not continue. The hotter the steam and the colder the condenser, the larger the fraction that can be turned into work, which is why modern power stations use very high temperatures and pressures. Medupi and Kusile use supercritical boilers, which run at higher temperatures and pressures than older stations and are a little more efficient. There are also extra losses from friction in the moving parts, heat escaping in the chimney gases, and the electricity the power station itself uses for its mills, fans and pumps. You will study these ideas more formally in later grades. The useful lesson for now is that some energy transfer to the surroundings is always unavoidable, so saving electricity at home also saves far more coal than it seems.

### subtopic_2 — Turbine and Generator
**Assistant:** Why is the frequency of our electricity 50 hertz, and what does the turbine speed have to do with it?

**Tutor:** The frequency tells you how many times each second the alternating current changes direction and back again, and in South Africa it is 50 times per second, or 50 hertz. In a generator, each time the spinning magnet turns once, the current in the coils goes through one complete cycle for a simple two-pole magnet. To get 50 cycles per second, the magnet must therefore spin 50 times per second, and 50 times 60 seconds gives 3 000 revolutions per minute. That is why the turbines and generators in South African coal power stations spin at about 3 000 revolutions per minute. All the generators connected to the national grid must spin in step with one another, so that their alternating currents rise and fall together. If the demand for electricity suddenly becomes larger than the supply, the generators are slowed down slightly and the frequency drops below 50 hertz. Grid operators watch the frequency closely, and if it falls too far they must reduce demand quickly, which is one reason load shedding is sometimes needed to protect the system.

### subtopic_3 — Cooling, Efficiency and the Environment
**Assistant:** If coal power is so polluting, why does South Africa still use so much of it?

**Tutor:** Mainly because of history, cost and the size of the coal reserves, though the situation is changing. South Africa has large, easily mined coal deposits, especially in Mpumalanga, and for most of the twentieth century coal was the cheapest way to produce large amounts of reliable electricity. Eskom built its power stations next to the coal mines, and whole towns, industries and many thousands of jobs grew up around them. A coal station can run day and night regardless of the weather, which made it the backbone of the grid. Replacing it takes time and huge investment. Today the country is adding solar and wind power, and older coal stations are gradually being closed as they reach the end of their lives. The aim, often called a just energy transition, is to reduce carbon dioxide emissions and air pollution while protecting the jobs and communities that depend on coal, for example by retraining workers and building new industries in Mpumalanga. You will study the alternative energy sources in the next lesson.

### subtopic_4 — The Whole Chain and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What happens to all the ash left after the coal is burned?

**Tutor:** South African coal has a fairly high ash content, so a large coal power station produces many tonnes of ash every hour, and handling it safely is a big task. There are two kinds. Bottom ash is the heavier material that falls to the bottom of the boiler. Fly ash is the very fine powder carried upward with the hot gases. Before the gases reach the chimney, they pass through filters called fabric filters or electrostatic precipitators, which trap most of the fly ash so it does not pollute the air. The collected ash is mixed with water or transported dry and placed on large ash dumps next to the power station, which must be managed so that dust does not blow away and rainwater does not carry chemicals into rivers or groundwater. Some fly ash is put to good use: it can be mixed into cement and concrete, where it makes the concrete stronger and reduces the amount of cement needed, and it can be used in bricks and road building. Finding more uses for ash is an important way to reduce the environmental impact of coal power.
