# Part 1 — Expert

This session describes the national electricity grid as a system: generation, transmission and distribution; explains the role of step-up and step-down transformers and why electricity is transmitted at high voltage; identifies where energy is lost; and explains what happens when the grid is overloaded. The anchor facts are that South Africa's grid is operated mainly by Eskom; that generators produce electricity at many thousands of volts, which transformers step up to transmission voltages of 275 000 to 400 000 volts and on some lines 765 000 volts; that the same power can be carried by a smaller current at a higher voltage, which greatly reduces heating losses in the lines; that substations and local transformers step the voltage down to 230 volts for homes; and that when demand exceeds supply the grid can become unstable, so demand must be reduced, for example by load shedding.

## Subtopic: Generation, Transmission and Distribution

A grid is a network that connects many sources of electricity to many users, so that electricity generated anywhere can be used anywhere on the network. South Africa's national grid links coal power stations in Mpumalanga and Limpopo, Koeberg near Cape Town, hydroelectric and pumped-storage stations, and solar and wind farms in the Northern, Eastern and Western Cape. The grid is also connected to neighbouring countries through the Southern African Power Pool, so electricity can be bought and sold across borders.

The grid has three parts.

Generation: power stations produce electricity.

Transmission: high-voltage lines on tall steel pylons carry electricity over long distances from the power stations to major substations near cities and industrial areas. Eskom's transmission network has tens of thousands of kilometres of lines.

Distribution: lower-voltage lines, on smaller poles or underground, carry electricity from substations to homes, schools, shops, farms and factories. In many areas distribution is done by the municipality, which buys electricity from Eskom and sells it to residents.

A national control centre monitors the whole grid around the clock, deciding which power stations should run and how much each should produce, so that supply matches demand from second to second.

Thinking of the grid as a system helps explain why a problem in one place can affect people far away. If a large power station suddenly trips off, the other stations must quickly produce more to make up the difference, and if they cannot, the whole system is affected. In the same way, a fault on a major transmission line can cut supply to a whole region even though the power stations are working. A system is only as reliable as its weakest important link, which is why grid operators build in spare capacity and alternative routes for power to flow.

## Subtopic: Transformers and High-voltage Transmission

A transformer is a device that changes the voltage of an alternating current. It consists of two coils of insulated wire wound on an iron core. An alternating current in the first coil creates a changing magnetic field in the core, which produces a voltage in the second coil. If the second coil has more turns than the first, the voltage is stepped up; if it has fewer turns, the voltage is stepped down. Transformers have no moving parts and work only with alternating current, which is one important reason the mains supply is alternating current.

A simple rule links the turns and the voltages: the ratio of the voltages equals the ratio of the numbers of turns. For example, if the second coil has ten times as many turns as the first, the output voltage is about ten times the input voltage. Energy is conserved, so when the voltage is stepped up, the current is stepped down by roughly the same factor; a transformer does not give something for nothing. Large grid transformers are very efficient, but they still warm up, and they are cooled by oil that circulates through radiator fins on their sides.

At the power station, the generators produce electricity at a voltage of many thousands of volts. Step-up transformers raise this to a transmission voltage, usually 400 000 volts or 275 000 volts in South Africa, and 765 000 volts on some very long lines.

Why use such high voltages? The power carried by a line equals the voltage multiplied by the current, P = V × I. For a fixed amount of power, a higher voltage means a smaller current. Consider 100 megawatts, or 100 000 000 watts. At 400 000 volts the current is 100 000 000 ÷ 400 000 = 250 amperes. At only 20 000 volts it would be 100 000 000 ÷ 20 000 = 5 000 amperes, twenty times larger.

The wires in transmission lines have resistance, and current through a resistance heats the wire, transferring energy to the surroundings. The larger the current, the more heating, and the heating increases very rapidly as current rises: twenty times the current gives about four hundred times the heating loss. A smaller current also means thinner, lighter and cheaper cables can be used. So transmitting at a high voltage and a small current saves a great deal of energy and money.

Near towns, the voltage is stepped down in stages at substations, for example to 132 000, 88 000 or 66 000 volts, and then to 11 000 or 22 000 volts for local distribution lines. Finally, a transformer on a pole or in a green mini-substation steps it down to 230 volts for homes. High voltages are far too dangerous to use in homes, which is why the final step-down is essential.

You can see these stages around you. Huge steel lattice pylons carry the transmission lines across the countryside. Fenced substations with rows of transformers and switches stand on the outskirts of towns. Concrete or wooden poles with smaller transformers, or green metal mini-substations on pavements, supply each group of homes. Every one of these carries a warning sign, because even the lowest of these distribution voltages can kill.

## Subtopic: Energy Losses and Overload

Even with high-voltage transmission, not all of the electrical energy generated reaches users. Energy is lost as heat in the transmission and distribution lines, in the transformers, which warm up as they work, and in the equipment at substations. In addition, some electricity is lost through theft and illegal connections, which you studied earlier. Long lines lose more energy than short ones, which is one reason power stations are built where they can serve large centres of demand.

The grid must balance supply and demand at every moment, because electricity cannot easily be stored in large amounts. Demand rises and falls during the day: there is a morning peak when people wake up and switch on kettles, geysers and heaters, and a bigger evening peak between about 5 p.m. and 9 p.m., when people cook, bath and watch television. Winter demand is higher than summer demand because of heating.

If demand becomes larger than the electricity being generated, the generators are slowed by the extra load and the frequency of the supply falls below 50 hertz. If this is not corrected quickly, power stations can trip off to protect themselves, which makes the shortage worse, and in the worst case the whole grid could collapse into a national blackout that could take days to restore.

To prevent this, the control centre first uses fast reserves such as pumped-storage stations and gas turbines, and asks large users to cut demand. If that is not enough, it uses load shedding: switching off the supply to different areas in turn, according to a published schedule, so that demand falls to match supply. Load shedding stages describe how much demand must be cut; each stage represents about 1 000 megawatts, so stage 4 means about 4 × 1 000 = 4 000 megawatts must be removed from the grid. Load shedding is a controlled way to protect the grid; it is not caused by a single fault in your street.

Overload can also happen locally. A substation or local transformer has a maximum safe load; if too many homes draw a large current at once, especially through illegal connections, the transformer overheats and can fail. This is different from load shedding: it is a local equipment failure.

Load reduction, which you met in the lesson on illegal connections, is different again: it is applied by Eskom in specific areas where local networks are overloaded, often because of illegal connections, to protect the equipment in those areas.

## Subtopic: Saving Electricity and the Error Museum

Because supply and demand must balance, every household can help. Switching off geysers during the evening peak, using a timer for the geyser, switching off lights and appliances not in use, boiling only as much water as needed, and using energy-efficient LED lights all reduce peak demand. Less demand at peak times means less risk of load shedding and less coal burned.

Large users can help even more. Mines, smelters and factories sometimes agree to reduce their demand at short notice in return for a lower tariff, which gives the control centre a quick way to cut demand without switching off homes. Smart meters and time-of-use tariffs, where electricity costs more during the peaks, encourage everyone to move flexible uses such as washing machines and pool pumps to quieter times of the day.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: transmission lines use high voltage so electricity travels faster; high voltage is used to reduce the current and therefore the heating losses. Two: transformers make energy; they change voltage and current, and some energy is always lost as heat in them. Three: electricity in pylon lines is 230 volts like in homes; it is hundreds of thousands of volts. Four: load shedding is caused by a broken line in your area; it is a planned cut to balance national supply and demand. Five: transformers work with direct current from batteries; they need alternating current to produce a changing magnetic field.

## Subtopic: The Long Journey

Your electricity probably started hundreds of kilometres away, perhaps at a coal power station in Mpumalanga.

The national grid is like a road network for electricity. Big highways, the tall pylons, carry it across the country. Smaller roads, the poles in your street, take it to each home.

The grid links all kinds of power stations, coal, nuclear, water, sun and wind, so electricity made anywhere can be used anywhere. It even connects to our neighbouring countries.

One big advantage of a grid is sharing. If one power station breaks down, others can make up the difference, and electricity can be sent to wherever it is needed.

Three parts: generation makes it, transmission carries it far, and distribution delivers it to your door.

And someone is always watching: a national control centre keeps track, day and night, of how much is being made and how much is being used.

## Subtopic: The Voltage Ladder

Here is the puzzle. Wires heat up when current flows through them, and heat is wasted energy. Long wires waste a lot.

The trick is to carry the electricity at a very high voltage. Power is voltage times current. Make the voltage huge and the current becomes small, and a small current wastes much less energy as heat.

Example: 100 million watts at 400 000 volts needs only 250 amperes. At 20 000 volts it would need 5 000 amperes, twenty times more current and about four hundred times more heating loss.

Transformers change the voltage. They are two coils of wire around an iron core. A step-up transformer at the power station raises the voltage to around 400 000 volts. Step-down transformers at substations bring it down in stages. The last one, on a pole or in a green box in your street, brings it to 230 volts.

Transformers need alternating current to work. That is one big reason our plugs give alternating current.

Keep the voltage ladder in mind and try this part's questions: stepping up, stepping down and why.

## Subtopic: Keeping the Balance

The grid must make exactly as much electricity as people use, every second.

The busiest time is the evening, roughly 5 to 9 p.m., when everyone cooks, baths and switches on heaters. Winter is busiest of all.

If people use more than is being made, the generators slow down slightly. If nothing is done, power stations can trip off one after another, and the whole country could go dark.

So, when there is not enough, areas are switched off in turns: load shedding. Each stage cuts about 1 000 megawatts. It is planned, to protect the grid.

You can help by switching off the geyser and unneeded lights in the evening peak.

Imagine a seesaw that must stay perfectly level. Electricity being made sits on one side, electricity being used sits on the other. Add weight to the using side and the seesaw tips. Either more must be made quickly, or some using must stop.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The grid has generation, transmission and distribution. Transformers step the voltage up to hundreds of thousands of volts for transmission, so the current is small and less energy is lost as heat, then step it down to 230 volts for homes. Energy is still lost in lines and transformers. When demand exceeds supply, the frequency falls and load shedding protects the grid.
