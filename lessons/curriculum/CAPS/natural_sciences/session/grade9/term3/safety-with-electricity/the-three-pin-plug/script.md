# Part 1 — Expert

This session describes the three wires in a household cable and the 3-pin plug, their colour codes and functions, explains how the earth wire protects a person when an appliance develops a fault, why switches and protective devices are placed in the live wire, how a plug is correctly wired, and why double-insulated appliances can safely use two pins. The anchor facts are that the live wire is brown, or red in older wiring, and is at 230 volts; the neutral wire is blue, or black in older wiring, and is close to 0 volts; the earth wire is green and yellow, or green in older wiring, and connects metal casings to the ground; and that the earth pin is the longest pin. The governing principle is that the live wire delivers the energy, the neutral wire completes the circuit, and the earth wire provides a low-resistance path to the ground for fault currents, so that metal parts a person might touch can never stay live.

## Subtopic: Live, Neutral and Earth

A household cable usually contains three insulated wires, each with a colour that shows its function. The colours were standardised internationally; older South African installations use different colours, which you may still find.

Why does the colour code matter so much? An electrician opening a plug, a distribution board or a light fitting must know at a glance which wire is dangerous. If someone wired a plug using the wrong colours, the next person who works on it could assume that a wire is safe when it is in fact live. That is why the code is part of the national wiring rules, and why mixing the old and new colours in one installation needs great care. When in doubt, a qualified electrician tests the wire rather than trusting its colour.

The live wire, brown, or red in older wiring, carries the current from the supply to the appliance. Its voltage alternates, swinging between positive and negative values about 50 times every second, with an effective value of 230 volts. It is the dangerous wire: touching it while standing on the ground, or touching anything connected to it, lets current flow through your body to earth.

The neutral wire, blue, or black in older wiring, completes the circuit, carrying the current back from the appliance to the supply. It is connected to the earth at the substation or distribution board, so its voltage stays close to 0 volts. In normal use the current in the neutral wire equals the current in the live wire, which is what the earth leakage unit checks.

The earth wire, green and yellow stripes, or plain green in older wiring, is a safety wire. It does not normally carry any current. It connects the metal casing of an appliance through the plug and socket to an earth electrode, a metal rod driven into the ground at the property, so that the casing is held at the same voltage as the ground.

Because the voltage of the neutral wire is close to that of the earth, a person touching only the neutral wire would normally not get a shock, but this must never be relied on: a fault or wrong wiring can make the neutral wire live. All wires should be treated as dangerous.

## Subtopic: How the Earth Wire Protects You

Many appliances have metal casings or metal parts a person can touch: kettles, stoves, washing machines, fridges, toasters, irons and heaters. Inside, the live wire runs close to these metal parts, separated only by insulation.

Insulation does not last for ever. Heat from heating elements, vibration in a washing machine, rodents chewing cables and simple age all cause plastic and rubber to crack. Water that gets inside a kettle base or a geyser element can also create a path between the live wire and the metal. These faults are often invisible from the outside, so the appliance looks normal. The earth wire is a protection that works even when nobody knows a fault has happened.

Suppose the insulation inside a washing machine is damaged and the live wire touches the metal casing. Without an earth wire, the whole casing would be at 230 volts, and it would stay live, looking perfectly normal, until someone touched it. The current would then flow through that person's body to the ground, giving a possibly fatal shock.

With an earth wire connected to the casing, as soon as the live wire touches the casing, current flows through the earth wire to the ground. The earth wire has a very low resistance, so a large current flows. That large current trips the circuit breaker or blows the fuse almost immediately, and the earth leakage unit, detecting that current is leaving the circuit by the earth path instead of returning on the neutral wire, also switches off. The faulty appliance is disconnected before anyone touches it, and if someone does touch it, most of the current takes the low-resistance earth wire rather than the much higher resistance of their body.

This is why the earth wire must never be cut off or disconnected, why plugs with broken earth pins must be replaced, and why adaptors that do not connect the earth must not be used with metal-cased appliances.

## Subtopic: Switches, Plugs and Double Insulation

Switches, fuses and circuit breakers must always be placed in the live wire. If a switch were placed in the neutral wire, switching it off would stop the current, but the appliance would still be connected to the live supply. Its internal parts would stay at 230 volts, and anyone changing a bulb or touching the inside would still be in danger. With the switch in the live wire, switching off disconnects the appliance from the live supply completely. The same reasoning applies to fuses and breakers: if a fuse in the neutral wire blew, the appliance would stop working but remain live.

Double-insulated appliances are designed differently. Hair dryers, cellphone chargers, many drills and most small kitchen appliances have plastic casings and an extra layer of insulation around every live part, so no metal that a person can touch could become live. These appliances often have only two wires and a two-pin plug, and they carry a symbol of one square inside another. They do not need an earth wire, because there is no exposed metal for a fault current to flow through.

South African 3-pin plugs follow the national standards SANS 164-1, the older plug with three large round pins, and SANS 164-2, the newer plug with three smaller round pins. In both, the earth pin is longer than the live and neutral pins. When the plug is pushed into a socket, the earth pin connects first, and in many sockets it opens safety shutters that cover the live and neutral holes. When the plug is pulled out, the earth pin disconnects last. So the appliance is earthed whenever it is connected to the live supply.

Correctly wiring a plug: strip only enough outer sheath so that the coloured wires reach their terminals with the sheath held firmly by the cord grip; connect each coloured wire to its correct terminal, as marked on the plug; make sure there are no loose strands of copper; tighten the screws firmly; and fit the cover. The cord grip stops the wires being pulled out of the terminals if the cable is tugged. Modern appliances are sold with factory-fitted moulded plugs, which are safer than plugs fitted by hand.

Some appliances need no earth wire. Double-insulated appliances, such as hair dryers, phone chargers, many drills and televisions, have plastic casings or two separate layers of insulation, so that no fault can make an outer part live. They are marked with the double-insulation symbol, a square inside a square, and use two-pin plugs.

## Subtopic: Checking Plugs and the Error Museum

Simple checks prevent many accidents: look for cracked or burnt plugs, scorch marks on sockets, loose or bent pins, frayed cords and cords where the coloured wires are visible at the plug. Do not overload sockets with adaptors. Pull a plug out by gripping the plug, not by yanking the cord. Keep plugs and sockets away from water, and never handle them with wet hands.

Many South African homes use adaptors, because older appliances may have the round-pin plug while newer sockets accept the SANS 164-2 plug with its thinner pins, or the other way round. A good adaptor is safe, but a cheap one that is loose, cracked or overloaded with several heavy appliances can overheat. Signs of trouble include a warm or discoloured plug, a burning smell and a crackling sound. Switch off at the wall, unplug carefully and have the item replaced.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: the earth wire carries the current back from the appliance; that is the neutral wire, while the earth wire carries current only in a fault. Two: the switch can go in either the live or the neutral wire; it must go in the live wire. Three: the brown wire is neutral; brown is live, blue is neutral, green and yellow is earth. Four: a two-pin appliance is unsafe; double-insulated appliances are designed for two pins. Five: all pins are the same length; the earth pin is longest.

Layout for a plug question: name each wire with its colour; state its function; for a fault, trace the path of the current through the earth wire and state which device switches off; and explain why the switch and fuse are in the live wire.

## Subtopic: Three Wires, Three Jobs

Cut open an appliance cable and you will find three coloured wires.

Think of them as a team where each member has one job. The live wire brings the push of the supply to the appliance. The neutral wire gives the current a path back to complete the circuit. The earth wire normally carries no current at all, it just waits, ready to act only if something goes wrong. Learn the colours as a pair of words: brown for live, blue for neutral, and green and yellow stripes for earth.

Brown is live. It brings the electricity in at 230 volts. It is the dangerous one. In old wiring it is red.

Blue is neutral. It takes the current back to complete the loop. It sits close to 0 volts. In old wiring it is black.

Green and yellow stripes is earth. It is the safety wire. Normally it carries nothing at all. In old wiring it is plain green.

A memory trick: brown is live, like the bark of a living tree; blue is neutral and calm, like the sky; green and yellow is earth, like grass in the ground.

Even though neutral is close to 0 volts, never touch any bare wire. A fault can make any of them dangerous.

## Subtopic: The Escape Route

Picture a kettle with a metal body. Inside, the live wire is close to the metal, kept apart only by insulation. Now the insulation cracks and the live wire touches the metal body.

Without an earth wire, the metal body is now at 230 volts and simply waits. The kettle may still boil water perfectly, so nobody notices. The danger comes when someone touches it while also touching a tap, a wet floor or another earthed object. Then the current has a path through that person's body to the ground. This is exactly how many shocks happen in kitchens and bathrooms, where water and metal are everywhere.

No earth wire: the whole kettle is now at 230 volts, but it looks normal. The next person to touch it becomes the path to the ground. That can kill.

With an earth wire: the moment the live wire touches the metal, a big current rushes down the earth wire, which is an easy escape route to the ground. That big current trips the breaker and the earth leakage unit, and the power switches off, before anyone touches it.

So the earth wire is an escape route for dangerous current, and it must never be cut, broken or bypassed.

## Subtopic: The Longest Pin

Look at a 3-pin plug. One pin is longer: that is the earth pin. It goes in first and comes out last, so the appliance is always earthed while it is connected. In many sockets it also pushes open little shutters that cover the other two holes.

Those shutters are there to protect curious children who might push a hairpin or a piece of wire into a socket hole. With the shutters closed, the live and neutral contacts cannot be reached. Only when the longer earth pin enters do the shutters slide open. So a single design feature, a longer pin, does two jobs at once: it earths the appliance first, and it keeps little fingers and objects away from the live contacts.

Switches always go in the brown live wire. If the switch were in the neutral, switching off would stop the current but leave the inside of the appliance still connected to 230 volts. Fuses and breakers go in the live wire for the same reason.

Some appliances have only two pins, like hair dryers and phone chargers. They are double insulated: wrapped in plastic or two layers of insulation, so the outside can never become live. Look for the symbol of a square inside a square.

Check your plugs: no cracks, no burn marks, no coloured wires showing. Pull on the plug, not the cord. Keep them dry.
