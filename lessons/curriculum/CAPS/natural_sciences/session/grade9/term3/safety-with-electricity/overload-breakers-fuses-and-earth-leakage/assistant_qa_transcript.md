### subtopic_1 — Overloads and Short Circuits
**Assistant:** Why does an electric shock hurt or kill, when the current is so small?

**Tutor:** Because the human body runs on tiny electrical signals, and an outside current interferes with them. Your nerves carry messages as small electrical impulses, and your heart beats because a regular electrical signal spreads through the heart muscle, making it contract in a coordinated rhythm. A current from outside, flowing through the body, swamps these signals. A current of about 1 milliampere can be felt as a tingle. Around 10 to 20 milliamperes, the muscles contract so strongly that a person may be unable to let go of a live wire, which keeps them in contact. Around 50 milliamperes or more flowing through the chest can disrupt the heart's rhythm so that it quivers uselessly instead of pumping, which is called ventricular fibrillation, and without rapid treatment it is fatal. Larger currents also cause severe burns where the current enters and leaves the body, and internal burns along its path. The danger depends on the size of the current, the path it takes through the body, and how long it flows, which is why the earth leakage unit's speed matters so much. The body's resistance is lower when the skin is wet, so the same mains voltage drives a much larger current through wet skin, which is why electricity and water are such a deadly combination in bathrooms, kitchens and outdoors.

### subtopic_2 — Fuses and Circuit Breakers
**Assistant:** Why does my earth leakage unit trip during heavy rain?

**Tutor:** Usually because water has found its way into part of the electrical installation and created a leakage path to earth, and the unit is doing exactly what it is designed to do. Rainwater containing dissolved salts conducts electricity. If it gets into an outdoor socket, an outside light fitting, a garden pump, a gate motor, a junction box, or a cable joint that is not properly sealed, some current can flow from the live parts through the water to the earth instead of returning on the neutral wire. As soon as this leakage reaches about 30 milliamperes, the unit trips. Damp in walls and roof spaces can have the same effect. Old or cracked cable insulation, which may be fine when dry, can leak when wet. To find the cause, switch off the circuit breakers one at a time and reset the earth leakage unit after each: the circuit whose breaker allows the unit to stay on when it is off is the one with the fault. Outdoor fittings should be weatherproof, rated for outdoor use, and checked by a registered electrician. Removing or bypassing the earth leakage unit because it trips in rain is dangerous, because the same leakage path could pass through a person who touches a wet outdoor light or a pool pump.

### subtopic_3 — The Earth Leakage Unit
**Assistant:** Do South African plugs have fuses inside them like British plugs?

**Tutor:** Generally no, which is why protection in South African homes depends on the circuit breakers and earth leakage unit in the distribution board. British plugs contain a small cartridge fuse because the British wiring system often uses ring circuits that can carry a large current to each socket, so each plug needs its own smaller fuse to protect the appliance's thin flexible cord. South African homes use radial circuits, each protected at the distribution board by a breaker sized for the cable, so standard plugs do not contain fuses. Some adaptors, multiplugs and appliances do include their own fuse or small breaker for extra protection, and many appliances have internal fuses that protect their circuits. South Africa has two standard plug types. The older one, with three large round pins, is specified in the national standard SANS 164-1. The newer one, with three smaller round pins, is specified in SANS 164-2 and is designed so that its sockets also accept the slim two-pin plugs used on many small appliances. New installations are moving towards the newer sockets. In both, the earth pin is longest, so that it connects first and disconnects last. You will look at the wiring of the three pins, live, neutral and earth, in the next lesson.

### subtopic_4 — What to Do When Power Trips and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What should I do if someone is being electrocuted?

**Tutor:** Protect yourself first, because if you touch them while they are still in contact with the live supply, the current can flow through you as well. Do not touch the person with your bare hands. Switch off the power immediately at the main switch on the distribution board, or pull out the plug by its insulated body if you can do so safely. If you cannot switch off the supply, stand on something dry and insulating, such as a dry wooden board, a rubber mat or a thick pile of newspapers, and use a dry wooden or plastic object, such as a broom handle or a wooden chair, to push the person or the cable away from each other. Never use anything metal or wet. Never approach a person in contact with a fallen overhead power line or a substation; keep everyone at least 10 metres away and call the electricity supplier and emergency services, because high-voltage current can jump through the ground and through the air. Once the person is free of the current, call for emergency medical help, such as 10177 for an ambulance or 112 from a cellphone. If they are not breathing normally, someone trained should begin cardiopulmonary resuscitation, CPR, until help arrives. Even a person who seems fine after a shock should be checked by a doctor, because internal burns and heart rhythm problems may not show at first.
