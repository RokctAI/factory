### subtopic_1 — Live, Neutral and Earth
**Assistant:** If neutral is near 0 volts, why do electricians still treat it as dangerous?

**Tutor:** Because several faults can make the neutral wire carry a dangerous voltage, and an electrician cannot know by looking whether it is safe. In normal operation, the neutral wire is connected to earth at the supply side, so its voltage is close to zero, perhaps a few volts because of the current flowing through its own resistance. But if the neutral connection between a house and the supply breaks somewhere, a fault called a broken or lost neutral, then the neutral wire inside the house can rise towards the live voltage, because current from the live wire through any appliance that is switched on has nowhere to go but back along the neutral. Touching the neutral then completes the path through your body. Mistakes in wiring, such as live and neutral swapped at a socket or in an appliance, also make the supposedly neutral wire live. In addition, neutral wires on shared circuits can carry current from other appliances, so disconnecting a neutral while other things are running can produce a shock or a spark. For all these reasons, electricians follow a strict routine: switch off and lock the circuit breaker, test with a reliable voltage tester that the circuit is dead, and only then work on it. Only qualified, registered electricians should work on household wiring in South Africa; it must comply with the national wiring code and be certified.

### subtopic_2 — How the Earth Wire Protects You
**Assistant:** How does the earth wire actually connect to the ground?

**Tutor:** Through a chain of connections from the appliance to a metal electrode buried in the soil. Inside the appliance, the earth wire is screwed firmly to the metal casing. It runs through the cable to the earth pin of the plug, then through the socket to the earth wire in the house wiring, which runs back to an earth bar in the distribution board. The earth bar is connected by a thick copper conductor to an earth electrode, usually a copper-coated steel rod driven a metre or more into the ground near the house, or to a buried strip or to the earthed metal structure of the building. Moist soil is a reasonable conductor, so current can flow from the electrode into the ground and back to the supply transformer, whose neutral is also connected to earth. The resistance of this earth path must be low enough that a fault produces a large enough current to trip the protection quickly, so electricians measure it when they test a new installation. In very dry or rocky soils, such as in parts of the Karoo or the Northern Cape, a single rod may not give a low enough resistance, and longer rods, several rods or special treatments are used. Old houses sometimes relied on metal water pipes as the earth, but modern plastic pipes cannot be used, so a proper earth electrode is required.

### subtopic_3 — Switches, Plugs and Double Insulation
**Assistant:** Why do some plugs have a fuse even though the distribution board has breakers?

**Tutor:** To protect the appliance and its thin cord, which the breaker at the distribution board cannot do on its own. A circuit breaker for a plug circuit is rated to protect the fixed wiring in the walls, often at 16 or 20 amperes. The flexible cord of a small appliance, such as a lamp or a radio, is much thinner and may be designed for only a few amperes. If that cord developed a fault that drew, say, 12 amperes, the breaker would not trip, but the thin cord could overheat. A small fuse in the plug or adaptor, rated just above the appliance's normal current, cuts the supply before the cord overheats. That is why British plugs, which connect to ring circuits rated at 32 amperes, always contain a fuse. In South Africa, standard plugs usually do not contain a fuse, because circuits are radial and appliances are expected to be designed with cords that can withstand the circuit breaker rating, or with their own internal fuses. Many multiplugs and adaptors sold in South Africa include a fuse or a small resettable breaker to protect the adaptor itself from overload. Inside many appliances, such as microwaves, televisions and chargers, there are internal fuses that protect delicate components. The principle is the same everywhere: a fuse or breaker should be matched to the smallest conductor it is protecting.

### subtopic_4 — Checking Plugs and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why are bathrooms so strict about plugs and switches?

**Tutor:** Because water and wet skin greatly increase the risk of a fatal shock, so bathrooms have special rules for where and how electrical equipment may be installed. Dry skin has a fairly high resistance, often thousands of ohms, which limits the current that a given voltage can drive through the body. Wet skin, and a body standing in water or touching a metal tap or bath connected to the ground, has a much lower resistance, so the same 230 volts can drive a far larger, more dangerous current. Water can also get into sockets and appliances and create paths between live parts and the user. For these reasons, South African wiring regulations divide bathrooms into zones around baths and showers, with restrictions on what equipment may be placed in each. Ordinary plug sockets are not allowed close to a bath or shower; only special shaver sockets, which contain an isolating transformer, are allowed near basins. Light switches are often outside the bathroom or are pull-cord switches with an insulating cord. Light fittings and heaters must be designed for damp areas, and all circuits must be protected by an earth leakage unit. Never use a hair dryer, heater, radio or phone charger connected to the mains near a bath or shower, never handle switches with wet hands, and never let an appliance fall into water.
