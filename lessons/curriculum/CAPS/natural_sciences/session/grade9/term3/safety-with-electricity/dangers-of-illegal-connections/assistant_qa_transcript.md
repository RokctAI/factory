### subtopic_1 — What Counts as an Illegal Connection
**Assistant:** If birds sit on power lines without getting shocked, why is a cable dangerous to a person?

**Tutor:** A bird sitting on one line touches only that one wire, so there is no path for current through its body: both feet are at the same voltage, and current only flows when there is a difference in voltage across something. A person is in a very different position. When you stand on the ground and touch a live cable, your hand is at the voltage of the live wire and your feet are at the voltage of the earth, which is close to zero. There is now a large voltage difference across your body, and current flows from your hand, through your chest, to your feet and into the ground. That is a complete circuit. Birds are electrocuted too when they bridge two wires, or a wire and an earthed pole, which is why large birds such as vultures and storks are sometimes killed on power lines in South Africa. Eskom fits bird guards and changes pole designs to reduce this. For a person, the lesson is that any contact with a live wire while touching the ground, a fence or a wall can be fatal.

### subtopic_2 — Dangers to People
**Assistant:** Why do some illegal cables feel warm when nothing seems to be switched on?

**Tutor:** Because many households are probably using electricity through the same thin cable at the same time, even if you cannot see it. An illegal cable often supplies several homes, each with lights, a stove, a heater or a kettle, and all of those currents pass through the one cable. A thin cable has a higher resistance than a thick one, and when a current passes through resistance, electrical energy is changed into heat. The larger the current, the greater the heating, and the heating increases very quickly as the current rises. A warm cable is a sign that it is carrying more current than it can handle safely. If it gets hot enough, its plastic softens, melts and burns, and the bare wires may touch each other, causing a short circuit with sparks. Because there is no breaker to switch it off, the cable can keep burning. This is one of the main reasons illegal connections start so many fires, especially in winter evenings when heating is used. Never touch a warm or damaged cable; report it. A legal circuit would protect you here, because its breaker would trip long before the cable became dangerously hot.

### subtopic_3 — Dangers to the Network and the Community
**Assistant:** Is it still dangerous if the illegal cable is buried in the ground?

**Tutor:** Yes, and in some ways it is more dangerous, because people cannot see it. Illegal cables are often buried just a few centimetres below the surface, with no protective duct and no warning tape. Soil moves, rain washes it away, and the cable can be exposed or damaged by spades, vehicles and people walking over it. Ordinary cable insulation is not designed to be buried without protection, so it cracks and lets moisture reach the copper. Wet soil conducts electricity, so the ground around a damaged buried cable can become live, especially after rain, and a person or animal standing on it may receive a shock. Proper underground cables are designed for burial, are laid at a set depth in ducts or with protective covers, and are connected through protection devices that switch off when a fault occurs. A buried illegal cable has none of this. If you see a cable sticking out of the ground, or the ground near a cable is sparking or smoking, keep away and report it.

### subtopic_4 — Staying Safe, Reporting and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What happens when someone reports an illegal connection to Eskom or the municipality?

**Tutor:** The report is usually logged and passed to a team that checks the area, and if illegal connections are confirmed, the team disconnects and removes them. Both Eskom and municipalities have call centres, and many have apps, websites or social media channels for reporting faults, illegal connections, cable theft and damaged equipment. You should give the exact location, such as the street, landmarks or the number of a pole or mini-substation if it is marked. In an emergency, such as a person being electrocuted, a fallen line or a burning transformer, call emergency services as well. Removing illegal connections can make an area safer immediately, and in some places it is followed by programmes that install legal, metered supplies for households. Some people worry about being seen as reporting on neighbours. It helps to remember that the main aim is to stop electrocutions and fires, and that many reports can be made without giving your name. Adults at home or at school can help you make a report. Even one report can lead to a dangerous cable being removed before anyone is hurt, and when a community sees illegal cables being cleared, it is often the first step towards a safe, legal supply for every home in the area.
