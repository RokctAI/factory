# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains what causes excessive current in a household circuit, the overload and the short circuit, why excessive current is dangerous, and how the three protective devices work: the fuse, the circuit breaker and the earth leakage unit. The anchor facts are that a fuse contains a thin wire that melts when the current exceeds its rating; that a circuit breaker is an automatic switch that opens when the current exceeds its rating and can be reset; that common breaker ratings in a home are 10, 16 and 20 amperes; and that an earth leakage unit trips when it detects a difference of about 30 milliamperes between the live and neutral currents. The governing principle is that fuses and circuit breakers protect the wiring and appliances from too much current, while the earth leakage unit protects people from electric shock.

## Subtopic: Overloads and Short Circuits

Every cable in a house is designed to carry up to a certain current safely. If the current is larger, the cable's resistance turns more electrical energy into heat, and the cable can overheat. The heating increases very rapidly with current: doubling the current makes the heating four times greater. Overheated cables can melt their plastic insulation, damage sockets and plugs, and set fire to nearby materials in roofs and walls.

There are two main ways the current becomes too large.

An overload happens when too many appliances, or appliances with too high a power, are connected to one circuit at the same time. Since appliances are in parallel, their currents add. For example, a heater drawing 8,7 amperes, a kettle drawing 8,7 amperes and an iron drawing 4 amperes on a 16 ampere circuit draw 8,7 + 8,7 + 4 = 21,4 amperes, which is more than the circuit is designed for. Overloads often come from multiplugs and double adaptors that put many appliances on one socket.

A short circuit happens when the live wire touches the neutral wire, or touches the earth, directly, without passing through an appliance. The path then has almost no resistance, so an enormous current flows, perhaps hundreds of amperes, and cables heat up almost instantly with sparks and flashes. Short circuits are caused by damaged insulation, for example on a cable that has been crushed, chewed by rats, pinched under furniture or worn through, by water getting into sockets or appliances, and by loose or badly made connections.

Signs that a circuit may be overloaded or faulty include plugs or sockets that feel warm, a smell of burning plastic, scorch marks around a socket, flickering lights, and breakers that trip often.

Damaged appliance cords are a common cause of faults. Cords that are bent sharply at the plug, run under carpets, trapped in doors or wrapped tightly around hot appliances such as irons can crack inside, exposing the wires. A cord with exposed wires, tape repairs or a cracked plug should be replaced, not used. Appliances that have been dropped in water or have water inside must not be used until they have been checked.

## Subtopic: Fuses and Circuit Breakers

Fuses and circuit breakers are both safety devices connected in series in the live wire of a circuit, so that all the current to the circuit passes through them. Their job is to cut off the current when it becomes too large, before the cables overheat.

A fuse contains a short, thin piece of wire, often inside a glass or ceramic cartridge, designed to melt when the current through it exceeds its rating. The thin wire has a relatively high resistance and a low melting point, so it heats up faster than the circuit's cables. When it melts, it breaks the circuit, and the current stops. Fuses are rated in amperes, for example 3, 5, 10 or 13 amperes, and the rating chosen must be a little above the normal current of the circuit or appliance. A blown fuse must be replaced with a new fuse of the same rating, after the fault has been found and fixed. Fitting a higher-rated fuse, or replacing a fuse with ordinary wire or foil, is extremely dangerous, because the circuit is then unprotected. Fuses are found in many appliances, in cars, in some plugs, and in older distribution boards.

A circuit breaker is an automatic switch that does the same job as a fuse but does not need to be replaced. Inside, a bimetallic strip heated by the current bends and trips the switch when there is a sustained overload, and an electromagnet pulls the switch open instantly when a very large current flows in a short circuit. Once the fault has been fixed, the breaker is reset by pushing its switch back on. Modern distribution boards have a row of circuit breakers, one for each circuit, typically rated at 10 amperes for lighting circuits, 16 or 20 amperes for plug circuits, and higher for stoves and geysers.

Advantages of breakers over fuses: they react quickly, can be reset easily, do not need spare parts and cannot be wrongly replaced with a higher rating by an untrained person.

A simple classroom demonstration shows a fuse at work. A single strand of fine steel wool or very thin fuse wire is connected between two terminals in a low-voltage circuit with a few cells and a bulb. When a short wire is connected across the bulb, creating a short circuit, the current rises sharply, the thin strand glows and melts, and the circuit is broken. This is done only with low-voltage cells and under the teacher's supervision, never with mains electricity.

## Subtopic: The Earth Leakage Unit

Fuses and circuit breakers protect the wiring, but they do not reliably protect people. The current that can kill a person is far smaller than the current needed to trip a breaker: a current of about 50 milliamperes, which is 0,05 amperes, flowing through the chest can stop the heart, while a 16 ampere breaker would not react to it at all.

The earth leakage unit, also called a residual current device, is designed to protect people. It is placed in the distribution board, in series with the live and neutral wires of the circuits it protects. In normal operation, all the current that flows out along the live wire returns along the neutral wire, so the two currents are equal. The earth leakage unit continuously compares them. If some current is leaking out of the circuit by another path, for example through a person who touches a live wire or a faulty appliance, and down through their body to the ground, then the returning neutral current is smaller than the live current. When this difference reaches about 30 milliamperes, the unit switches off the supply in about 30 milliseconds, quickly enough to prevent a fatal shock in most cases.

Earth leakage units also detect current leaking through damp insulation or through water in a faulty appliance, and they help prevent fires caused by such leaks. South African wiring regulations require earth leakage protection on household plug circuits. Each unit has a test button, which deliberately creates a small leak to check that it trips; it should be pressed every few months.

The earth leakage unit and the earth wire work as a team. If an appliance with a metal casing develops a fault in which the live wire touches the casing, the earth wire carries the current safely to the ground, and the earth leakage unit detects that current leaving the circuit and switches off, even before anyone touches the appliance. Without an earth wire, the casing would sit at 230 volts until someone touched it, and the leakage would then pass through that person, though the earth leakage unit would still switch off very quickly. ## Subtopic: What to Do When Power Trips and the Error Museum

When a breaker or the earth leakage unit trips, follow a safe routine. Switch off and unplug appliances on that circuit. Reset the breaker or earth leakage unit. Plug appliances back in one at a time; if the unit trips again when a particular appliance is plugged in, that appliance is faulty and should be repaired or replaced. If it trips again with nothing plugged in, there is a fault in the fixed wiring, and only a registered electrician should investigate.

Never try to bypass a protective device. Holding a breaker on, taping it in the on position, bridging a fuse with wire, or removing an earth leakage unit because it trips often removes the very protection that prevents fires and deaths.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: a fuse is there to protect people from shock; fuses and breakers protect the wiring, while the earth leakage unit protects people. Two: replace a blown fuse with a thicker one so it will not blow again; use the same rating and fix the fault. Three: a breaker that trips is faulty; it is usually doing its job. Four: an earth leakage unit trips at the same current as a breaker; it trips at about 30 milliamperes of leakage. Five: fuses go in the neutral wire; protective devices go in the live wire.

Layout for a protection answer: name the device, state where it is connected, explain how it detects the problem, state what it does, and say what it protects.

## Subtopic: Too Much Current

Wires in your walls can safely carry only so much current. Push more through and they get hot. Very hot wires melt their plastic coating, and that can start a fire inside a wall or ceiling.

Two things cause too much current.

Overload: too many appliances on one circuit, especially heaters, kettles and irons. Their currents add up. A heater, a kettle and an iron together can pull more than 21 amperes on a circuit made for 16.

Short circuit: the live wire touches the neutral wire directly, through damaged insulation, a crushed cable, a rat bite or water in a socket. With almost no resistance in the way, a huge current rushes through, with sparks and a bang.

Warning signs: a warm plug or socket, a burning plastic smell, scorch marks, flickering lights or breakers that keep tripping.

Check your cords. A cracked plug, a cord with tape around it or bare wire showing must be replaced, not used. Never run cords under carpets or trap them in doors, where they get crushed.

## Subtopic: The Melting Link and the Clicking Switch

To stop wires overheating, every circuit has a guard on the live wire.

The old guard is the fuse: a thin wire that melts on purpose when too much current flows. Once it melts, the circuit is broken and safe. But then you must fix the fault and fit a new fuse of exactly the same rating. Never use a thicker fuse or a piece of wire or foil, because then nothing protects the circuit.

The modern guard is the circuit breaker: an automatic switch. A small overload heats a strip inside until it bends and clicks the switch off. A short circuit makes a little electromagnet yank it off instantly. Once you fix the problem, you just push the switch back on. No spare parts needed.

Lights usually have 10 ampere breakers, plugs 16 or 20, and the stove and geyser get bigger ones of their own.

A teacher can show a fuse melting safely with a few torch cells: a thin strand of steel wool in the circuit glows and snaps when a wire is put across the bulb. Never try this with mains electricity.

## Subtopic: The People Protector

Here is a shock: a current of only about 50 thousandths of an ampere through your chest can stop your heart. A 16 ampere breaker would not even notice that. So breakers protect wires, not people.

The earth leakage unit protects people. It keeps checking that the current going out on the live wire equals the current coming back on the neutral wire. If some current goes missing, maybe through a person touching a faulty appliance and down to the ground, the two numbers do not match. As soon as about 30 thousandths of an ampere is missing, it switches everything off in a fraction of a second.

It has a test button. Press it every few months to make sure it trips.

When something trips, unplug things, reset, and plug back in one at a time to find the faulty appliance. If it trips with nothing plugged in, call an electrician. Never tape a switch on or remove a safety device.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Overloads and short circuits make currents too large, which overheat wires and cause fires. Fuses melt and breakers trip to protect the wiring; both go in the live wire, and fuses must be replaced with the same rating. The earth leakage unit protects people by switching off when about 30 milliamperes leaks away. Never bypass protection. Grade 10 to 12 Physical Sciences builds on electrical safety.

The earth wire helps it. If a fault makes the metal case of a kettle or a washing machine live, the earth wire carries the current to the ground straight away, the earth leakage unit notices it missing and switches off, often before anyone even touches the appliance.
