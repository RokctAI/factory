# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains what an illegal electrical connection is, why it is dangerous to the people who make it and to everyone around it, how it damages the electricity network and the community, and how to stay safe and report it. The anchor facts are that an illegal connection is any connection to the mains supply made without the permission of the supplier, which in South Africa is Eskom or the local municipality; that mains electricity is supplied at 230 volts in homes and at much higher voltages on overhead distribution lines; that illegal connections have none of the safety features of a proper installation, so faults are not switched off; that they cause electrocutions, fires and damaged transformers; and that they are against the law. Every idea here builds on the earlier lessons on the household circuit, the three-pin plug and protective devices.

## Subtopic: What Counts as an Illegal Connection

An illegal connection is any connection to the electricity network that has not been approved and installed by the supplier or by a registered electrician working within the rules. Common examples in South Africa include the following.

A cable thrown or hooked over an overhead power line, sometimes called a "spider web" connection when many cables run from one pole to many homes.

A cable run from a house or a mini-substation that has a legal supply to neighbouring houses or shacks, often buried just under the soil or lying across paths and streets.

A meter that has been bypassed or tampered with, so that electricity flows into the house without being recorded or paid for.

A connection that is reconnected by someone other than the supplier after the supply was cut off.

People who make these connections are often called izinyoka, a word meaning snakes, because the cables snake along the ground and through fences. Some connections are made by people who cannot afford electricity; others are sold by criminals who charge households for an illegal supply. In both cases the cable used is often too thin for the current it must carry, joined by twisting bare wires together and wrapping them in plastic bags or not covering them at all.

It is important to separate an illegal connection from an informal settlement. Many informal settlements are being electrified legally, with proper meters and protection. The danger comes from the way an illegal connection is made, not from where people live.

Compare this with a legal supply. A legal connection starts at a properly rated cable from the supplier, passes through a meter, and enters a distribution board containing a main switch, circuit breakers for each circuit and an earth leakage unit. Every socket has an earth wire connected to an earth electrode in the ground. The cables are the right thickness for the current, the joins are made inside sealed boxes, and the whole installation must be tested and certified. An illegal connection skips every one of these steps.

## Subtopic: Dangers to People

The first danger is electrocution. A proper cable has insulation designed for the voltage and is protected inside walls, conduits or underground ducts. An illegal cable is often thin, cracked or bare at the joints, and it lies on the ground, hangs low from fences or runs through puddles. Anyone who touches a bare part while standing on the ground becomes part of a circuit from the live wire to earth. At 230 volts, the current through the body can be far more than the roughly 50 milliamperes that can stop the heart.

Children are most at risk. They play in the open spaces where cables lie, pick up wires out of curiosity, or touch a metal fence or a shack's corrugated iron wall that has become live because an illegal cable is touching it. Every year in South Africa, children and adults die after touching illegal connections, and many more suffer severe burns.

The people who make the connections are also at risk. Overhead lines on poles may carry 11 000 volts or more, not 230 volts. Climbing a pole or throwing a wire over such a line can kill instantly, and electricity at high voltage can arc across a gap through the air, so a person does not even need to touch the line.

Many people who make illegal connections use tools such as long metal poles or wet ropes, which conduct electricity. Others work at night or in the rain, when visibility is poor and water on the skin lowers the body's resistance, making a larger current flow. Survivors of high-voltage shocks often suffer deep burns where the current entered and left the body, and damage to muscles, nerves and the heart.

The second danger is fire. With no circuit breaker or fuse, nothing switches the supply off when a cable is overloaded or short-circuited. A thin cable carrying a large current overheats, its plastic melts and burns, and sparks from twisted joints can ignite wood, plastic sheeting, paper and blankets. In closely packed informal settlements, a fire that starts in one shack can spread to hundreds of homes in minutes.

The third danger is that there is no earth leakage protection. Recall that an earth leakage unit switches the supply off in a fraction of a second when about 30 milliamperes leaks to earth. An illegal connection has no such device, so a person who touches a live wire may stay in the circuit until somebody pulls them away or the cable burns through.

## Subtopic: Dangers to the Network and the Community

Illegal connections also damage the network that everyone depends on.

A transformer near a community is designed to supply a certain number of households with a certain total power. Illegal connections add many extra loads that the transformer was never designed for. Suppose 20 extra homes each run a 2 000 watt heater on a winter evening: 20 × 2 000 W = 40 000 W, an extra 40 kilowatts on a transformer that may already be working near its limit. The transformer overheats, its insulating oil can catch fire, and it may explode or burn out. Replacing it costs a great deal of money and can take weeks, leaving legal customers without electricity as well.

Overloading also makes the voltage drop, so lights dim and appliances work poorly, and it trips the supply for whole areas. In some areas with very high losses from illegal connections and unpaid bills, Eskom applies load reduction, switching off the supply for several hours at peak times to protect its equipment. This affects every household in the area, including those who pay.

Illegal connections are a form of electricity theft. The cost of electricity that is used but not paid for is carried by the supplier and, in the end, by paying customers and taxpayers through higher tariffs. Municipalities lose income that they need to maintain the network and provide other services.

There is also a wider national cost. When demand on the grid is higher than expected because of unmetered use, it adds to the pressure on power stations, and the supplier cannot plan properly because it does not know how much electricity is really being used in an area. Money that could be spent on extending legal electricity to more households is spent instead on repairing damaged equipment.

Linked to this is cable theft, where copper cable is stolen from the network to be sold as scrap metal. It is a different crime from an illegal connection, but it also causes outages, puts thieves at risk of electrocution, and is very costly to repair.

Finally, making or using an illegal connection, or tampering with a meter, is against the law. People can face criminal charges and fines, and the supplier can disconnect the supply and remove the cables.

## Subtopic: Staying Safe, Reporting and the Error Museum

The safe routine is simple. Never touch a cable lying on the ground, hanging from a fence or running across a path, even if it looks dead; treat every cable as live. Keep away from fallen overhead lines and stay at least ten metres away; warn others and keep children back. Never climb poles, pylons or substation fences, and never enter a substation. Do not touch a person who is being electrocuted; switch off the supply if it is safe to do so, or push the person away with a dry, non-conducting object such as a wooden broom handle. Report illegal connections, fallen lines and damaged equipment to Eskom or the municipality, and phone emergency services in an emergency.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: a cable on the ground is safe because it is not moving or sparking; it can be live and deadly. Two: an illegal connection is just like a normal supply but free; it has no breakers, no earth leakage unit and no earth wire. Three: only the person who makes the connection is in danger; children, neighbours and passers-by are often the victims. Four: illegal connections hurt only Eskom; paying customers lose power through damaged transformers and load reduction, and pay more. Five: you must touch an overhead line to get a shock; high voltages can arc through the air.

Notice how each exhibit is corrected by a fact from earlier lessons: the missing earth leakage unit, the role of the earth wire, the heating effect of a large current in a thin cable, and the very high voltage of overhead lines. When a test question asks you to explain a danger, use these physical reasons rather than simply saying that something is illegal or bad.

Layout for an illegal-connections answer: state what the connection lacks, name the danger to people, name the danger to the network, and say what a safe person should do.

## Subtopic: Wires on the Ground

An illegal connection is electricity taken without permission: a cable hooked onto an overhead line, a wire run from a neighbour, or a meter that has been bypassed.

The cables are usually thin and badly joined. The joins are often just twisted bare copper. They lie in the sand, across paths and through puddles.

Here is the problem. A proper house circuit has three guards: breakers, an earth leakage unit and an earth wire. An illegal cable has none of them. If something goes wrong, nothing switches off.

So when a child picks up a wire, or touches a fence that a cable is leaning against, the current flows through the child to the ground and keeps flowing. Remember that about 50 thousandths of an ampere through the chest can stop the heart, and 230 volts can push far more than that through a body.

Overhead lines are even worse. They can carry 11 000 volts or more, and electricity at that voltage can jump through the air to someone who only comes close.

Water makes it worse. Wet skin lets more current through, and a puddle around a cable can carry current to bare feet. That is why the risk is highest after rain.

Then there is fire. A thin cable carrying too much current gets red hot. In a crowded settlement, a fire can jump from shack to shack in minutes.

## Subtopic: Who Pays for Stolen Power

Many people think stolen electricity hurts only Eskom. It hurts everyone, including the families using the illegal connection.

Each area has a transformer built for a certain number of homes. Add many illegal connections and it must work far beyond its design. Picture 20 extra homes each running a 2 000 watt heater: that is 40 000 watts more. The transformer overheats and can burn out or explode.

Then the whole area goes dark, including the homes that pay. A new transformer costs a lot and can take weeks to arrive.

Where losses are very high, Eskom may switch areas off for hours at busy times, called load reduction, to protect the equipment.

And the money that is lost? It is made up through higher prices for paying customers and less money for municipal services.

Think about a clinic, a school or a water pump in the area. When the transformer fails, they lose power too, so illegal connections can stop vaccines being kept cold or water being pumped.

Cable theft is the cousin of this problem: copper is stolen and sold as scrap, causing blackouts and costly repairs.

Making an illegal connection or tampering with a meter is a crime. It can lead to fines, charges and the power being cut off.

## Subtopic: Staying Safe and Speaking Up

Here is your personal safety list. Learn it, and teach it to younger brothers, sisters and cousins, because they are the ones most likely to pick up a loose wire while playing.

Treat every loose cable as live. Do not touch it, step on it or move it with a stick.

Stay well away from fallen overhead lines, at least ten metres, and keep younger children away too.

Never climb a pole, pylon or substation fence.

If someone is being shocked, do not grab them. Switch off the power if you safely can, or use something dry and wooden, like a broom handle, to push them free. Then call for help.

Report illegal connections, fallen lines and damaged boxes to Eskom or the municipality. Reporting is not about getting neighbours into trouble; it is about stopping a child from dying.

Pocket summary of the lesson. An illegal connection is a connection to the mains without the supplier's permission. It has no breakers, no earth leakage unit and no earth wire, so faults are not switched off. It causes electrocutions, especially of children, and fires. It overloads transformers, causing outages and load reduction, and paying customers carry the cost. It is against the law. Treat loose cables as live, keep away and report them.
