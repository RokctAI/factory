### subtopic_1 — Comparing Energy Use of Appliances
**Assistant:** How can I find out how much electricity each appliance in my home really uses?

**Tutor:** There are a few practical ways, from simple estimates to direct measurement. The simplest is an estimate from the label: find the power rating on each appliance, estimate how many hours a day it is used, and multiply kilowatts by hours, as in the audit table. This works well for appliances that use their full power whenever they are on, such as kettles, lights and televisions. For appliances controlled by thermostats, like fridges, geysers and ovens, the running time is harder to judge. A second method uses the meter. Switch off everything except one appliance, read the meter, let the appliance run for a measured time, and read it again; the difference is the energy that appliance used. Many prepaid meters also display the present rate of use in kilowatts, so you can watch the number jump when an appliance switches on. A third method is a plug-in energy monitor, a small device placed between the plug and the wall socket that measures and displays power and energy used over time. Some smart meters and solar systems come with an app that shows energy use through the day. Comparing several days helps you see patterns and test whether your savings are working.

### subtopic_2 — Where the Energy Goes in a Home
**Assistant:** Why should I wrap my geyser if it is already insulated?

**Tutor:** Because many geysers, especially older ones, have only a thin layer of insulation, and a geyser loses heat all day and night, every day of the year. Heat flows from a hot object to its cooler surroundings, and the larger the temperature difference, the faster the heat flows. A geyser kept at 60 °C in a roof space that can be close to freezing on a winter night loses heat continuously, and the element must switch on regularly to replace it, even when no one is using hot water. These standing losses can add up to 1 to 3 kWh a day. A geyser blanket is a thick layer of insulating material, usually fibreglass with a foil or plastic cover, wrapped around the geyser. Insulating materials contain many tiny pockets of trapped air, which is a poor conductor of heat. Insulating the first few metres of hot-water pipe leaving the geyser also helps, because those pipes lose heat too. Modern geysers sold in South Africa must meet higher insulation standards, so the gain is smaller, but for an older geyser a blanket is cheap, quick to fit and pays for itself within a few months. Always switch off the geyser at the distribution board first and keep the blanket clear of the thermostat and electrical cover.

### subtopic_3 — Solar Water Heating and Other Alternatives
**Assistant:** Do solar water heaters work in winter or on cloudy days?

**Tutor:** Yes, but less well, which is why most systems have a backup. A solar collector absorbs radiant energy from the Sun, and on a clear winter day there is still plenty of sunlight in most of South Africa, especially on the Highveld, where winter skies are often cloudless. However, winter days are shorter, the Sun is lower in the sky and the air is colder, so the collector loses more heat and the water does not get as hot. Evacuated-tube collectors handle this better than flat-plate ones, because the vacuum between the glass layers stops most heat escaping by conduction and convection. On cloudy or rainy days, much less radiant energy reaches the collector. To make sure the household always has hot water, the storage tank usually has an electric element or a connection to a gas heater, controlled by a thermostat and often a timer. The trick is to set the timer so that the backup only switches on late in the afternoon if the sun has not heated the water enough, giving the Sun the chance to do the work first. In very cold areas, systems must also be protected from frost, which can freeze and burst the pipes in the collector at night.

### subtopic_4 — Efficiency, Payback and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** If solar panels are so good, why doesn't everyone have them?

**Tutor:** Mainly because of the upfront cost, though there are other practical reasons too. A solar photovoltaic system with enough panels, an inverter and batteries to run a home through the evening can cost tens of thousands of rands, which many households cannot afford, even though the system saves money over time. Renters usually cannot install systems on homes they do not own. Some roofs face the wrong direction, are shaded by trees or buildings, or are not strong enough. Batteries are expensive and wear out after some years, and without them most panels only help during daylight. Installations must be done by qualified people and, in many municipalities, registered so that they are safe and do not feed electricity back into the grid when technicians are working on lines. Despite these challenges, the number of rooftop solar systems in South Africa grew very rapidly during the years of severe load shedding, and prices have fallen a lot. Cheaper options, such as a solar water heater, a small solar light kit or a single panel and battery to charge phones and run lights, help households that cannot afford a full system. Government programmes have also installed solar water heaters in many low-cost homes.
