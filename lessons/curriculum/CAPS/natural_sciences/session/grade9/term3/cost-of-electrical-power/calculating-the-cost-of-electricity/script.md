# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains how the cost of electricity is calculated. It introduces the kilowatt-hour as the unit of electrical energy sold to consumers, shows how to calculate the energy used by an appliance from its power rating and the time it is used, and how to calculate the cost from the energy and the price per unit. The anchor facts are that electricity is paid for according to the energy used, not the power; that one kilowatt-hour is the energy transferred by a 1 kW appliance in one hour, equal to 3 600 000 joules; that energy in kilowatt-hours equals power in kilowatts multiplied by time in hours; that cost equals energy in kilowatt-hours multiplied by the price of one kilowatt-hour; and that a kilowatt-hour is the same as one unit on a prepaid meter. In the examples a price of R2,50 per kilowatt-hour is used; actual prices depend on the supplier, the area and the tariff, and they change every year.

## Subtopic: The Kilowatt-hour

In the previous lesson you learnt that power is the rate at which energy is transferred, and that energy = power × time. If power is in watts and time in seconds, energy comes out in joules. But a joule is a very small amount of energy. A 2 kW kettle boiling for one hour transfers 2 000 × 3 600 = 7 200 000 joules. A household uses hundreds of millions of joules every month, which would make bills full of enormous numbers.

So electricity suppliers use a much larger unit of energy, the kilowatt-hour, symbol kWh. One kilowatt-hour is the energy transferred by an appliance with a power of 1 kilowatt working for 1 hour.

1 kWh = 1 000 W × 3 600 s = 3 600 000 J

The kilowatt-hour is a unit of energy, even though its name contains the word kilowatt. This is a common source of confusion. A kilowatt measures power, how fast energy is used; a kilowatt-hour measures energy, how much has been used altogether.

To find the energy in kilowatt-hours, use the power in kilowatts and the time in hours:

energy (kWh) = power (kW) × time (h)

For example, a 2 kW heater used for 3 hours uses 2 × 3 = 6 kWh. A 0,5 kW drill used for 2 hours uses 0,5 × 2 = 1 kWh. A 100 W light used for 10 hours: first change 100 W to 0,1 kW, then 0,1 × 10 = 1 kWh. So a 100 W light left on for 10 hours uses the same energy as a 1 kW heater used for 1 hour.

On an electricity meter, one kilowatt-hour is called one unit. Prepaid meters in South Africa count down your remaining units as you use electricity, and conventional meters count up the units used so the supplier can send a bill.

A conventional meter can be used to measure energy directly. If the reading is 45 210 kWh on the first day of the month and 45 690 kWh on the last day, the household used 45 690 − 45 210 = 480 kWh during that month. Dividing by 30 days gives an average of 16 kWh a day. By reading the meter every day for a week, a family can see which days use the most electricity and connect that to what they did.

## Subtopic: Calculating the Cost

To calculate the cost of using an appliance, follow three steps.

Step 1: change the power rating to kilowatts and the time to hours.
Step 2: calculate the energy used: energy (kWh) = power (kW) × time (h).
Step 3: calculate the cost: cost = energy (kWh) × price per kWh.

These can be combined into one formula: cost = power rating (kW) × hours used × price per kWh.

Example 1. A 2 kW kettle is used for a total of half an hour each day. The price is R2,50 per kWh. Energy per day = 2 × 0,5 = 1 kWh. Cost per day = 1 × R2,50 = R2,50. Over a 30-day month the kettle uses 30 kWh and costs 30 × R2,50 = R75.

Example 2. A 3 kW geyser element is on for 3 hours a day. Energy per day = 3 × 3 = 9 kWh. Energy per month = 9 × 30 = 270 kWh. Cost per month = 270 × R2,50 = R675. This is why the geyser is often the largest single item on a household electricity bill.

Example 3. A 100 W television is watched for 5 hours. Power = 100 ÷ 1 000 = 0,1 kW. Energy = 0,1 × 5 = 0,5 kWh. Cost = 0,5 × R2,50 = R1,25.

Example 4. A 1 200 W microwave oven is used for 15 minutes. Power = 1,2 kW. Time = 15 ÷ 60 = 0,25 hours. Energy = 1,2 × 0,25 = 0,3 kWh. Cost = 0,3 × R2,50 = R0,75, which is 75 cents.

Example 5. A stove plate of 1,5 kW is used for 40 minutes each evening for a 30-day month. Time per day = 40 ÷ 60 hours, so the energy per day = 1,5 × 40 ÷ 60 = 1 kWh. Energy per month = 30 kWh, and the cost is 30 × R2,50 = R75. Cooking with a lid on the pot, matching the pot to the size of the plate and switching the plate off a few minutes early, using the heat stored in the plate, all reduce this.

It is often useful to set the work out in a table with columns for the appliance, its power in kW, the hours used per day, the kWh per day and the cost per day. Adding up the last column gives the daily cost for the whole household, and multiplying by 30 gives a monthly estimate.

Notice the two conversions that catch people out: watts must be changed into kilowatts by dividing by 1 000, and minutes must be changed into hours by dividing by 60.

Always check whether an answer is sensible. A single appliance costing thousands of rands a day, or a geyser costing a few cents a month, signals a slip in a conversion.

## Subtopic: Meters, Units and Tariffs

Most South African homes have either a prepaid meter or a conventional, also called credit, meter. With a prepaid meter, you buy electricity in advance at a shop, a bank, a cellphone app or an online service, and receive a 20-digit token that you type into the meter. The meter adds the units to its balance and then counts them down as electricity is used. When the balance reaches zero the meter switches off the supply until more units are loaded. With a conventional meter, the meter records the total number of units used, and the supplier reads it, or estimates it, and sends a bill at the end of the month.

The price of a unit is called the tariff. Tariffs are set by Eskom and by municipalities, and must be approved by the National Energy Regulator of South Africa, NERSA. They usually increase every year. Many households pay an inclining block tariff, where the first block of units each month is cheaper and units above that cost more. This helps protect households that use only a little electricity. Some households also receive a number of free basic electricity units each month, often 50 kWh, which helps poor households pay for lighting, cooking and basic needs.

You can work backwards from the amount spent. If R200 is spent at R2,50 per kWh, the number of units bought is 200 ÷ 2,50 = 80 kWh. If a 2 kW heater is then used for 4 hours a day, it uses 8 kWh a day, so the 80 units would last 80 ÷ 8 = 10 days for the heater alone.

Standby power also costs money. Suppose a household has a decoder, a television, a microwave oven clock and several chargers that together use 20 W on standby for 20 hours a day. That is 0,02 × 20 = 0,4 kWh a day, 12 kWh a month, or R30 a month at R2,50 per kWh, spent on appliances that nobody is using.

Value-added tax, VAT, is usually included in the price per unit. Some suppliers also add a fixed monthly service or network charge, which must be paid no matter how many units are used.

## Subtopic: Comparing Costs and the Error Museum

Calculations let us compare choices. A 60 W filament bulb and a 9 W LED bulb give about the same light. If each is used for 6 hours a day for 30 days, that is 180 hours. The filament bulb uses 0,06 × 180 = 10,8 kWh, costing 10,8 × R2,50 = R27. The LED's power is 9 ÷ 1 000 kW, so it uses 9 × 180 ÷ 1 000 = 1,62 kWh, costing 1,62 × R2,50 = R4,05. Changing one bulb saves R22,95 a month, and a house with ten bulbs could save over R200 a month.

Similarly, reducing the geyser's running time from 3 hours to 2 hours a day, with a timer or by lowering the thermostat setting, saves 3 kWh a day, which is 90 kWh or R225 a month at R2,50 per kWh.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: we pay for watts; we pay for energy in kilowatt-hours. Two: a kilowatt-hour is a unit of power; it is a unit of energy. Three: a 100 W appliance used for 2 hours uses 200 kWh; you must first change 100 W to 0,1 kW, giving 0,2 kWh. Four: 30 minutes is 0,3 hours; it is 0,5 hours. Five: the appliance with the highest power always costs the most; the cost depends on both power and time, so a low-power appliance used for many hours can cost more than a high-power one used briefly.

Layout for a cost answer: write the power in kW, the time in hours, energy = power × time in kWh, and cost = energy × price, with the units at every step.

## Subtopic: Counting Units

When you buy electricity, you are buying energy. Not volts, not amperes, not watts.

Energy in joules gives huge numbers, so electricity is sold in a bigger unit: the kilowatt-hour, written kWh. On a prepaid meter, one kilowatt-hour is called one unit.

One unit is the energy a 1 kilowatt appliance uses in 1 hour.

So a 2 kilowatt heater used for 1 hour uses 2 units. Used for 3 hours, it uses 6 units.

A 100 watt light is a tenth of a kilowatt. Left on for 10 hours, it uses 1 unit too.

The rule is simple: units equal kilowatts times hours.

A meter is like a taxi meter for energy. The longer appliances run, and the bigger they are, the faster the numbers move.

Be careful with the name. A kilowatt is how fast energy is used. A kilowatt-hour is how much energy was used. They sound alike, but they measure different things.

## Subtopic: The Three-step Cost

Here is the recipe for any appliance.

Step 1: power in kilowatts and time in hours.
Step 2: units = kilowatts times hours.
Step 3: cost = units times the price of one unit.

Try it with a kettle. It is 2 kilowatts and is used half an hour a day. That is 1 unit a day. At R2,50 a unit, that is R2,50 a day, and R75 for a month.

Now the geyser: 3 kilowatts for 3 hours a day. That is 9 units a day, 270 units a month, and R675. Ouch.

Watch the two traps. Watts must become kilowatts: divide by 1 000. Minutes must become hours: divide by 60. So 15 minutes is a quarter of an hour, 0,25 hours.

You can also do a whole house. Make a list of appliances, work out the units for each one, add them up and multiply by the price. That shows where the money really goes.

And remember, if you know how much money was spent, you can divide by the price to find the units bought. R200 at R2,50 a unit buys 80 units.

## Subtopic: Saving Money at Home

Now the fun part: saving money.

Swap a 60 watt bulb for a 9 watt LED. Over a month of 6 hours a day, the old bulb costs R27 and the LED about R4. That is almost R23 saved from one bulb.

Cut the geyser by one hour a day with a timer. That saves 90 units a month, about R225.

Boil only the water you need. Switch off lights in empty rooms. Switch the TV and chargers off at the wall.

Even appliances on standby add up. A few chargers, a decoder and a microwave clock left on can quietly cost about R30 a month.

The biggest savings come from the appliances that heat: the geyser, the stove, heaters and kettles.

Pocket summary of the lesson. We pay for energy, counted in kilowatt-hours, also called units. Units equal power in kilowatts times time in hours. Cost equals units times the price per unit. Change watts to kilowatts and minutes to hours first. Cost depends on both power and time.
