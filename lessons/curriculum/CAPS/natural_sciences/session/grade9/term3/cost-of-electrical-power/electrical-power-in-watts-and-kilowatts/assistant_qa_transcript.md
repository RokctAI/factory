### subtopic_1 — What Electrical Power Means
**Assistant:** If power is energy per second, why do we pay for kilowatt-hours instead of watts?

**Tutor:** Because the electricity company charges you for the total amount of energy you use, not for how fast you use it, and the kilowatt-hour is a convenient unit of energy. Power tells you the rate, like the speed of a car; energy is the total, like the distance the car has travelled. To get the total energy you multiply the power by the time it is used. If energy were measured in joules, the numbers on a bill would be enormous, because a single 2 kW kettle running for one hour transfers 2 000 × 3 600 = 7 200 000 joules. So instead we multiply power in kilowatts by time in hours, and call the unit a kilowatt-hour. One kilowatt-hour is the energy used by a 1 kW appliance running for one hour, which is 3 600 000 joules. A prepaid meter or a monthly bill counts these units. A powerful appliance used for a short time can cost less than a weak one left on all day, which is exactly why the bill is based on energy and not on power. You will calculate these costs in the next lesson.

### subtopic_2 — Watts and Kilowatts
**Assistant:** Why do some appliance labels say 220 to 240 volts instead of just 230?

**Tutor:** Because the mains voltage is not perfectly steady, and appliances must work safely across the whole range they are likely to meet. South Africa's standard supply voltage is 230 volts, but the actual voltage at your plug can vary by a few per cent above or below that, depending on how far you are from the transformer, how many people are using electricity at the time and the condition of the local network. Older standards in South Africa used 220 volts and some countries use 240 volts, so manufacturers often design one model to work anywhere in that range and sell it in many countries. The power rating on the label is given for the normal working voltage. If the voltage is a little lower than normal, a heating appliance draws a little less current and gives slightly less power, so a kettle may take a bit longer to boil. If the voltage is far outside the range, appliances can be damaged, which is why a label also tells you never to plug a 230 volt appliance into a much higher supply, and why appliances from countries that use about 110 volts may need a special transformer here.

### subtopic_3 — Power Ratings of Appliances
**Assistant:** Why does a fridge use a lot of energy if its power rating is only about 150 watts?

**Tutor:** Because a fridge is switched on all day and all night, every day of the year, so even a small power adds up to a large amount of energy. The fridge's motor and compressor do not run all the time: a thermostat switches them on when the inside warms up and off when it is cold enough. On average, a fridge's compressor might run for about a third to a half of the time. Even so, a fridge running 8 to 12 hours each day uses roughly as much energy as a kettle boiled for an hour or so each day, and over a year it can be one of the bigger users in a home. How much it uses depends a lot on how it is treated. Opening the door often or leaving it open lets warm air in, putting hot food inside makes the compressor work harder, and a fridge placed next to a stove or in direct sunlight has to work more. Rubber door seals that no longer close tightly also let cold air escape. Modern fridges with good insulation and an efficient compressor carry an energy-efficiency label, with an A rating or better meaning lower energy use.

### subtopic_4 — Calculating Power and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Can an appliance use more power than its label says?

**Tutor:** Yes, sometimes, for short times or when something is wrong. The power rating is the power used under normal working conditions at the correct voltage. Appliances with electric motors, such as fridges, washing machines, air conditioners and power tools, draw a much larger current for a fraction of a second when the motor first starts, because a motor at rest offers very little opposition to the current. This starting surge can be several times the normal current, which is why a fridge switching on can make the lights flicker briefly. A faulty appliance can also draw more current than normal, for example if its insulation breaks down or a motor is jammed and cannot turn. In that case the wiring heats up, and the circuit breaker or fuse should switch off the supply. On the other hand, many appliances use less than their rated power for much of the time: a heater on a low setting, an oven that has reached its temperature and switches its element on and off, or a television showing a dark scene. The label tells you the maximum normal power, which is what you need to plan safe circuits.
