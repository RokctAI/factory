# Part 1 — Expert

This session compares the energy consumption of different household appliances, identifies which appliances use the most energy, and describes alternatives that reduce electricity use, especially solar water heating. The anchor facts are that the energy an appliance uses depends on both its power rating and the time it is used, energy (kWh) = power (kW) × time (h); that appliances which heat, especially the electric geyser, usually use the largest share of a household's electricity; that a household energy audit lists each appliance's daily use to find where energy goes; that a solar water heater uses energy from the Sun to heat water and can supply most of a household's hot water, greatly reducing electricity use; and that other alternatives include heat pumps, gas for cooking and heating, solar panels, insulation, energy-efficient appliances and retained-heat cookers. In the examples a price of R2,50 per kilowatt-hour is used; actual prices vary.

## Subtopic: Comparing Energy Use of Appliances

In the previous lessons you learnt that the energy used by an appliance, in kilowatt-hours, is its power in kilowatts multiplied by the hours it is used. This means a high power rating alone does not make an appliance a big user of energy. A kettle has a high power, but it is used only for short periods. A fridge has a low power, but it runs for many hours every day.

A household energy audit is a table that lists the appliances in a home and works out how much energy each uses in a typical day. Here is an example for one household.

Kettle: 2 kW used for 0,5 hours gives 1 kWh.
Geyser: 3 kW element on for 3 hours gives 9 kWh.
Stove: 2 kW used for 1 hour gives 2 kWh.
Fridge: 0,15 kW running for 10 hours gives 1,5 kWh.
Television: 0,1 kW used for 5 hours gives 0,5 kWh.
Five LED lights: 5 × 9 W = 45 W, which is 0,045 kW, used for 5 hours gives 0,225 kWh.

Adding the six daily amounts gives a total for the day of 14,225 kWh. Over a 30-day month that is about 427 kWh, costing about R1 067 at R2,50 per kWh.

The geyser alone uses 9 of the 14,225 kWh, which is about 63 per cent, nearly two thirds of the household's electricity. The stove is second, followed by the fridge and the kettle. The five lights together use less than a quarter of a kilowatt-hour, only about 1,6 per cent of the total.

The exact figures differ from home to home, but the pattern is very common: water heating, cooking and space heating use most of the energy, while lighting and electronics use relatively little, especially with LED lights.

An audit can also be done with a family. Each person records when appliances are switched on and off for a few days, and the times are added up. This often brings surprises, such as a heater left on in an empty room, a geyser element that runs far longer than expected, or a second fridge in the garage that is nearly empty but runs all day.

Two appliances with the same power can therefore have very different energy use, and two appliances with very different power can use the same energy. A 1 kW iron used for 2 hours and a 2 kW kettle used for 1 hour both use 2 kWh. Comparisons must always include time.

## Subtopic: Where the Energy Goes in a Home

Heating is energy-hungry because water and air need a lot of energy to warm them. Water in particular has a very high capacity to store heat: it takes about 4 200 joules to warm just one kilogram of water by one degree Celsius. A 150 litre geyser holds about 150 kilograms of water. Heating it from 15 °C to 60 °C, a rise of 45 degrees, needs about 4 200 × 150 × 45 = 28 350 000 joules, which is about 7,9 kWh. That is the energy for one full tank of hot water.

A geyser also loses heat all day through its walls and pipes. These standing losses can be 1 to 3 kWh a day, depending on the age of the geyser, its insulation and where it is installed. An old geyser in a cold roof space loses heat much faster than a modern geyser wrapped in a geyser blanket.

In winter, space heaters add a great deal. A 2 kW bar heater used for 4 hours each evening uses 8 kWh a day, almost as much as the geyser.

Cooking is the next big user. Stoves, ovens and kettles all have high power ratings. Using a lid, cooking several things at once, using a microwave oven for small amounts, and only boiling as much water as needed all reduce cooking energy.

Fridges and freezers run every day of the year. Lighting and electronics, such as televisions, chargers and Wi-Fi routers, use smaller amounts, though standby power adds up over time.

Air conditioners are increasingly common in hot parts of the country, such as KwaZulu-Natal, Limpopo and the Northern Cape. A small air conditioner may use about 1 kW while its compressor runs, so several hours of cooling on a hot afternoon can add several kilowatt-hours a day. Keeping curtains closed during the hottest hours, shading windows and insulating ceilings all reduce the need for cooling as well as heating.

Understanding where the energy goes helps a family decide which changes will save the most. Replacing every bulb in the house might save R50 a month, but reducing the geyser's use by a third could save several times that.

## Subtopic: Solar Water Heating and Other Alternatives

South Africa is one of the sunniest countries in the world, with most areas receiving well over 2 500 hours of sunshine a year. A solar water heater uses this free energy from the Sun to heat water.

A solar water heater has a collector on the roof, facing north in the Southern Hemisphere so that it receives the most sunlight. There are two main types of collector. A flat-plate collector is a box with a glass cover and a black metal plate and pipes inside; the black surface absorbs radiant energy from the Sun and the glass traps the heat, like a car parked in the sun. An evacuated-tube collector is a row of glass tubes with a vacuum between two layers of glass, which reduces heat loss, so it works well even on cold days.

Water heated in the collector is stored in an insulated tank. In a thermosiphon system the tank is above the collector, and the warm water rises naturally into the tank while cooler water sinks to the collector, with no pump. Most systems have an electric element as a backup for cloudy days. In sunny parts of South Africa, a solar water heater can supply around 60 to 80 per cent of a household's water-heating energy. In the audit example, saving 70 per cent of the geyser's 270 kWh a month saves 189 kWh, which is R472,50 a month at R2,50 per kWh.

Other alternatives include the following. A heat pump moves heat from the outside air into the water, like a fridge working in reverse, and uses about a third of the electricity of an ordinary element. Liquefied petroleum gas, LPG, can be used for cooking, heating and instant water heaters. Solar photovoltaic panels change sunlight directly into electricity, often with batteries for use at night. Insulating ceilings and geysers keeps heat where it is wanted. A hotbox or retained-heat cooker, an insulated container into which a boiling pot is placed, lets food finish cooking for hours using the heat already stored in it, with no extra energy.

Not every alternative suits every home. A family must think about the cost, the space available, the direction the roof faces, how much hot water they use, and whether gas can be delivered and stored safely. Government and municipal programmes have installed hundreds of thousands of low-pressure solar water heaters on houses in low-income areas, bringing hot water to homes that previously heated water on the stove.

## Subtopic: Efficiency, Payback and the Error Museum

Alternatives usually cost money to buy and install, so it is useful to work out the payback time: the time it takes for the savings to cover the cost. If a solar water heater costs R15 000 installed and saves R472,50 a month, the payback time is 15 000 ÷ 472,50, which is about 32 months, or under three years. After that, the savings are like free money for the rest of its life, which can be 15 to 20 years.

Energy labels help when buying new appliances. In South Africa, fridges, washing machines, ovens and other appliances carry an energy-efficiency label with ratings from A, or A+ and higher, which use the least energy, down to G, which use the most. The label also gives the expected energy use, often in kilowatt-hours per year. An efficient fridge may cost more to buy but less to run.

Using less electricity also helps the environment. Most of South Africa's electricity comes from burning coal, which releases carbon dioxide and other pollutants. Every kilowatt-hour saved means less coal burned and less pressure on the national grid at peak times.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: the appliance with the highest power uses the most energy; energy depends on power and time, so the fridge, running many hours, can use more than the kettle. Two: lights are the biggest users in a home; in most homes water heating and cooking use far more. Three: a solar water heater makes electricity; it heats water directly using radiant energy from the Sun. Four: a solar collector should face south in South Africa; it should face north. Five: alternatives always save money immediately; they must first pay back their cost.

Layout for an audit answer: list each appliance with its power in kW, hours used and kWh per day, add up the total, and identify the largest user and its share of the total.

## Subtopic: The Energy Detective

Your job: find out which appliance in your home uses the most energy.

The clue is this: energy depends on two things, how powerful an appliance is, and how long it is on. Units equal kilowatts times hours.

So a kettle is powerful but only on for a short time. A fridge is weak but on for many hours. Sometimes the fridge wins.

Make a list. Kettle: 1 unit a day. Stove: 2 units. Fridge: 1,5 units. TV: half a unit. Five LED lights: less than a quarter of a unit. And the geyser? 9 units a day.

Add them up and you get about 14 units a day. The geyser is about two thirds of all of it.

So the biggest user in most homes is the geyser, and the next biggest are the stove and heaters. Lights and electronics are small.

## Subtopic: Sunshine on the Roof

Heating water takes a lot of energy. Filling a geyser with hot water once takes about 8 units.

But South Africa has loads of sunshine, and sunshine is free.

A solar water heater sits on the roof, facing north to catch the most sun. Its panel is black, because black absorbs the most sunlight, and it has glass on top to trap the heat. Some use glass tubes with a vacuum inside, which keeps the heat in even on cold days.

The hot water rises into an insulated tank. On cloudy days, a backup element helps out.

In a sunny place, it can do about 70 per cent of the water heating. For the family in our example, that saves almost R475 a month.

Other good ideas: a heat pump, which uses about a third of the electricity; gas for cooking; solar panels that make electricity; and a hotbox, which keeps a pot cooking for hours with no electricity at all.

Why north? In South Africa the Sun moves across the northern part of the sky, so a panel facing north catches the most sunlight all day. A panel facing south would spend much of the day in its own shadow.

## Subtopic: Smart Choices

Good choices save money, but some cost money first.

A solar water heater might cost R15 000. Saving about R472 a month, it pays for itself in under three years, and then keeps saving for many more.

When buying a fridge or washing machine, check the energy label. A means efficient; G means it uses a lot.

Saving electricity also means less coal is burned, less pollution, and less chance of load shedding.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Energy used equals power times time. In most homes the geyser uses the most, followed by cooking and heating. A solar water heater uses the Sun to heat water and can cut water-heating electricity by most of the way. Heat pumps, gas, solar panels, insulation and hotboxes are other alternatives. Payback time is the cost divided by the savings. In Grade 10 to 12 you will study energy transfer and the efficiency of devices in more detail.

Remember the detective's rule as you choose: start with the biggest user. Fixing the geyser first saves far more than fixing the smallest things.
