# Part 1 — Expert

This session defines electrical power as the rate at which electrical energy is transferred, introduces the watt and the kilowatt as units of power, shows how to convert between them, and compares the power ratings of common household appliances. The anchor facts are that power is energy transferred per second; that one watt is one joule of energy transferred every second; that one kilowatt equals 1 000 watts; that the power of an electrical appliance can be found by multiplying the voltage by the current, P = V × I; that the power rating on an appliance label tells us how much power it uses when working normally; and that appliances which produce heat, such as kettles, stoves, heaters and geysers, have much higher power ratings than appliances which produce light or sound or charge batteries.

## Subtopic: What Electrical Power Means

In earlier lessons you learnt that energy is the ability to do work or cause change, and that energy is measured in joules. When a cell drives a current through a circuit, electrical energy is transferred from the cell to the components in the circuit, where it is changed into other forms. A bulb transfers electrical energy to light and heat, a motor transfers it to movement and sound, and a heating element transfers it to heat.

Two appliances may transfer the same total amount of energy, but one may do it much faster than the other. Power is the word scientists use for how fast energy is transferred. Power is the energy transferred divided by the time taken:

power = energy ÷ time

The unit of power is the watt, named after the Scottish engineer James Watt, who improved the steam engine. One watt is a transfer of one joule of energy every second. So a 60 watt bulb transfers 60 joules of electrical energy every second, and in one minute, which is 60 seconds, it transfers 60 × 60 = 3 600 joules.

For electrical appliances there is a second useful way to find the power. The power depends on both the voltage across the appliance and the current through it:

power = voltage × current, or P = V × I

Power is in watts when the voltage is in volts and the current is in amperes. For example, a torch bulb that runs on 3 volts and draws a current of 0,5 amperes has a power of 3 × 0,5 = 1,5 watts. A kettle connected to the 230 volt mains that draws about 8,7 amperes has a power of about 230 × 8,7 = 2 001 watts, close to 2 000 watts.

The two formulas describe the same thing from two directions. The first, power = energy ÷ time, works for any energy transfer at all: a learner running up stairs, a car engine, or the Sun. The second, P = V × I, is special to electricity, because voltage tells us how much energy each unit of charge carries and current tells us how much charge flows past each second. Multiply them and you get the energy delivered each second.

Notice also that power can be rearranged to find energy: energy = power × time. A 1 000 W microwave oven running for 2 minutes, which is 120 seconds, transfers 1 000 × 120 = 120 000 joules. This idea, that the energy used depends on both power and time, is the key to working out the cost of electricity in the next lesson.

This formula explains why mains appliances can be so powerful: the mains voltage of 230 volts is much larger than the few volts of a battery, and mains appliances can draw several amperes of current.

## Subtopic: Watts and Kilowatts

The watt is quite a small unit. Many household appliances use hundreds or thousands of watts, so we often use a larger unit, the kilowatt. The prefix kilo means one thousand, just as one kilometre is 1 000 metres and one kilogram is 1 000 grams. The symbol for watt is W and the symbol for kilowatt is kW.

1 kilowatt = 1 000 watts

To change watts into kilowatts, divide by 1 000. A 2 000 W kettle has a power of 2 000 ÷ 1 000 = 2 kW. A 1 500 W heater has a power of 1 500 ÷ 1 000 = 1,5 kW. A 100 W television has a power of 100 ÷ 1 000 = 0,1 kW. A 9 W LED light has a power of only nine thousandths of a kilowatt, 0,009 kW.

To change kilowatts into watts, multiply by 1 000. A 3 kW geyser element has a power of 3 × 1 000 = 3 000 W. A 0,8 kW microwave oven has a power of 0,8 × 1 000 = 800 W.

A quick way to do these conversions is to move the decimal comma three places: to the left when changing watts into kilowatts, and to the right when changing kilowatts into watts.

Even larger units are used for power stations. One megawatt, MW, is one million watts, or 1 000 kilowatts. A large coal power station such as Kusile or Medupi has six generating units of about 800 MW each, so its total capacity is about 4 800 MW. This is enough to supply more than a million homes at once.

On appliance labels you will find the power rating, sometimes followed by the voltage and the frequency, for example 220 to 240 V, 50 Hz, 2 000 W. The power rating is the power the appliance uses when it is working normally on the correct voltage. Some appliances, such as fridges and air conditioners, switch on and off automatically, so they do not use their full power all the time.

## Subtopic: Power Ratings of Appliances

When the power ratings of common appliances are compared, a clear pattern appears. The table below gives typical values; the exact numbers vary from model to model.

LED light bulb: about 5 to 10 W.
Cellphone charger: about 5 to 20 W.
Television: about 50 to 150 W.
Fridge: about 100 to 200 W while the motor runs.
Microwave oven: about 800 to 1 200 W.
Hair dryer: about 1 000 to 2 000 W.
Iron: about 1 000 to 2 400 W.
Kettle: about 2 000 to 3 000 W.
Geyser element: about 2 000 to 4 000 W, commonly 3 000 W.
Four-plate stove with oven: up to about 10 000 W with everything on.

The appliances with the highest power ratings are those whose main job is to produce heat: kettles, irons, heaters, stoves, ovens and geysers. They contain heating elements, which are resistors made of a special wire such as nichrome that becomes very hot when a large current passes through it. Heating water or air needs a lot of energy, and to do it quickly the appliance must transfer energy at a high rate.

Appliances that produce light, sound or movement, or that charge batteries, usually have much lower power ratings. An LED bulb of about 9 W gives about as much light as an old filament bulb of 60 W, because the LED changes a much larger fraction of its energy into light and wastes much less as heat.

The current an appliance draws from the 230 volt mains can be found by rearranging P = V × I to give I = P ÷ V. A 2 300 W kettle draws 2 300 ÷ 230 = 10 amperes. A 23 W light draws only 23 ÷ 230 = 0,1 amperes. This is why heating appliances are connected to plugs and circuits that can safely carry large currents, and why plugging several heating appliances into one multiplug can overload it.

Some appliances also use a little power when they seem to be off. Televisions, decoders, microwave ovens with clocks and chargers left in the wall often use a few watts on standby. A few watts sounds tiny, but it is used for every hour of every day, so it is worth switching appliances off at the wall when they are not in use.

## Subtopic: Calculating Power and the Error Museum

Here is a set of worked examples using what you have learnt.

Example 1. A heater transfers 90 000 joules of energy in 60 seconds. What is its power? Power = energy ÷ time = 90 000 ÷ 60 = 1 500 W, which is 1,5 kW.

Example 2. A toaster connected to 230 V draws a current of 4 A. What is its power? P = V × I = 230 × 4 = 920 W, or 0,92 kW.

Example 3. A 1 200 W microwave oven runs for 30 seconds. How much energy does it transfer? Energy = power × time = 1 200 × 30 = 36 000 joules.

Example 4. Arrange these in order of power from smallest to largest: 2,2 kW kettle, 850 W microwave, 0,06 kW fan, 1 800 W iron. First change everything to watts: 2 200 W, 850 W, 60 W and 1 800 W. The order is fan, microwave, iron, kettle.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: power and energy are the same thing; power is the rate at which energy is transferred, so a low-power appliance used for a long time can transfer more energy than a high-power appliance used briefly. Two: 1 kW is 100 W; it is 1 000 W. Three: a bright light must have a high power rating; an LED can be very bright with a low power rating because it wastes little energy as heat. Four: the power rating tells you how long an appliance can run; it tells you how fast it transfers energy. Five: to change watts to kilowatts, multiply by 1 000; you divide by 1 000.

Layout for a power answer: write the formula, substitute the values with units, calculate, and give the answer with the correct unit, W or kW.

## Subtopic: How Fast Energy Moves

Think of two learners filling the same bucket of water. One uses a cup, the other uses a hosepipe. In the end both buckets hold the same amount of water, but the hosepipe fills it much faster.

Power is like that. It does not tell you how much energy is used altogether. It tells you how fast the energy is used.

Energy is measured in joules. Power is measured in watts. One watt means one joule every second.

A 60 watt bulb uses 60 joules every second. A 2 000 watt kettle uses 2 000 joules every second. So the kettle uses energy much, much faster than the bulb.

There is a handy formula for anything that plugs in or uses a battery: power equals voltage times current. Big voltage and big current means big power.

Here is a quick check on the idea. A heater left on low for a whole afternoon can use more energy in total than a kettle boiled once, even though the kettle has the bigger power. Power is the speed; time matters too.

So a kettle on 230 volts with about 8,7 amperes flowing through it has a power of about 2 000 watts.

## Subtopic: Watts, Kilowatts and Labels

Watts are small, so we often count them in thousands. One thousand watts is one kilowatt, written kW, just as one thousand metres is one kilometre.

To change watts into kilowatts, divide by 1 000. A 2 000 watt kettle is 2 kilowatts. A 1 500 watt heater is 1,5 kilowatts.

To change kilowatts into watts, multiply by 1 000. A 3 kilowatt geyser is 3 000 watts.

Now turn over a kettle, a hair dryer or a phone charger and find the small label. You will see something like 230 V, 50 Hz, 2 000 W. The W number is the power rating: how fast it uses energy when it is working.

Power stations are counted in megawatts: millions of watts. One big coal power station can make about 4 800 megawatts.

## Subtopic: Big Users and Small Users

Line up the appliances in your home from smallest to biggest power, and you see a pattern.

Small users: LED lights, phone chargers, radios and televisions. They use from a few watts up to about 150 watts.

Big users: kettles, irons, hair dryers, heaters, stoves and the geyser. They use thousands of watts.

What do the big users have in common? They all make heat. Heating things needs a lot of energy, and they do it fast.

An LED bulb is a clever small user. A 9 watt LED gives about the same light as an old 60 watt bulb, because it wastes much less energy as heat.

Big power also means big current. A 2 300 watt kettle on 230 volts pulls 10 amperes, which is why you should not plug several heaters into one multiplug.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Power is how fast energy is transferred. One watt is one joule per second, and one kilowatt is 1 000 watts. Power equals voltage times current. Divide by 1 000 to change watts into kilowatts. Heating appliances have the highest power ratings. In the next lesson you will use power ratings to work out how much electricity costs.
