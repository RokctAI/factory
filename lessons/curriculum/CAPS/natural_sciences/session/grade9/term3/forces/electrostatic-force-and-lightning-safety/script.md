# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains electric charge, how objects become charged by rubbing, the law of electrostatic attraction and repulsion, why charged objects attract neutral ones, how charge is conducted or held, and how the same principles produce lightning, together with the rules for safety during thunderstorms. The anchor facts are that rubbing transfers electrons, so the object that gains electrons becomes negative and the object that loses them becomes positive; that like charges repel and unlike charges attract; that a polythene rod rubbed with a woollen cloth becomes negative while an acrylic rod rubbed with a cloth becomes positive; and that lightning is a giant discharge between charged regions of a thundercloud and the ground. The governing principle is that charges exert a field force on each other, and that charge separated by rubbing or by storm clouds will flow back, sometimes violently, when a path becomes available.

## Subtopic: Charge and Charging by Rubbing

All matter is made of atoms, and atoms contain charged particles. The nucleus at the centre contains protons, which carry a positive charge, and the nucleus is surrounded by electrons, which carry a negative charge. A normal atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so the charges balance and the atom is neutral, with no overall charge. Most objects are neutral for the same reason.

Electrons on the outer parts of atoms can be removed fairly easily, but protons are locked in the nucleus. So when an object becomes charged, it is always because electrons have moved onto it or off it. An object that gains electrons has more negative than positive charge and becomes negatively charged. An object that loses electrons has more positive than negative charge and becomes positively charged.

Charging by friction, or by rubbing, happens when two different insulating materials are rubbed together. The rubbing brings their surfaces into close contact, and electrons transfer from one material to the other, because different materials hold their electrons with different strengths. The two objects end up with equal and opposite charges: whatever one gains, the other loses. Charge is neither created nor destroyed, only moved.

Standard laboratory examples: a polythene rod rubbed with a woollen cloth gains electrons and becomes negative, while the cloth becomes positive. An acrylic or perspex rod rubbed with a cloth loses electrons and becomes positive. A balloon rubbed on dry hair gains electrons and becomes negative, while the hair becomes positive; each hair, now positively charged, is repelled by the others, which is why the hair stands up.

Charging works best in dry weather. In humid air, a thin film of moisture on surfaces lets charge leak away, so static effects are weaker in a humid Durban summer than in a dry Highveld winter.

A simple instrument detects charge: the electroscope. In its common form, a metal rod passes through an insulating stopper into a glass jar, with a metal disc at the top and two thin strips of gold leaf or aluminium foil hanging from the bottom. When a charged object touches the disc, charge spreads down the rod to both leaves. Since they now carry like charges, they repel and spread apart. The bigger the charge, the wider they open. Touching the disc with a finger earths it, and the leaves collapse.

## Subtopic: Attraction, Repulsion and Conductors

The law of electrostatics: like charges repel; unlike charges attract. Two negatively charged balloons hung side by side push apart. Two positively charged acrylic rods push apart. A negatively charged polythene rod and a positively charged acrylic rod pull together.

The electrostatic force is a field force. It acts across a gap, without contact, through the electric field around every charged object. It is stronger when the charges are larger and when the objects are closer together, and it weakens rapidly with distance.

Charged objects also attract neutral objects. A charged comb or balloon picks up small pieces of tissue paper, bends a thin stream of water from a tap, and sticks to a neutral wall. This happens because the charged object pulls on the opposite charges in the neutral object and pushes away the like charges. In the paper, the electrons shift slightly, so the side nearest a positive rod becomes slightly negative and the far side slightly positive. The attracted side is closer than the repelled side, so the attraction wins. This is called induced charge. As with magnets, attraction alone does not prove an object is charged; repulsion is the only sure test.

Materials differ in how charge moves through them. Conductors allow charge to flow easily through them: metals such as copper, aluminium and iron, as well as graphite, the human body, the ground and tap water, all conduct. Insulators do not allow charge to flow easily: plastic, rubber, glass, dry wood, wool, silk and dry air are insulators. Only insulators can be charged by rubbing and hold the charge in one place; a metal rod held in bare fingers loses any charge at once, because it flows through the body into the ground.

Connecting a charged object to the ground through a conductor lets the charge flow away, making it neutral. This is called earthing. Fuel tankers are earthed while they unload petrol, and aircraft are earthed while refuelled, so that a static spark cannot ignite the fuel vapour.

## Subtopic: Lightning and Safety During Storms

A spark is a sudden flow of charge through the air between objects with a large difference in charge. You may feel a small shock and see a tiny spark after walking across a nylon carpet and touching a metal door handle. Lightning is the same thing on a gigantic scale.

Inside a tall thundercloud, a cumulonimbus, strong updraughts carry water droplets and tiny ice crystals upwards, while heavier hailstones and soft hail fall. As these particles collide, charge is transferred between them. The lighter, positively charged ice crystals are carried to the top of the cloud, and the heavier, negatively charged particles collect near the base. The large negative charge at the base of the cloud induces a positive charge on the ground below, especially on tall objects. When the difference in charge becomes great enough, the air breaks down and a huge discharge occurs: lightning. Most lightning flashes stay inside clouds or go between clouds; the dangerous ones strike the ground.

A lightning bolt carries an enormous current for a fraction of a second and heats the air in its path to about 30 000 degrees Celsius, several times hotter than the surface of the Sun. The air expands explosively, producing the shock wave we hear as thunder. Light travels almost instantly, but sound travels about 340 metres per second, roughly one kilometre every three seconds, so counting the seconds between flash and thunder and dividing by three gives the distance to the strike in kilometres.

South Africa, especially the Highveld of Gauteng, Mpumalanga, the Free State and the interior of KwaZulu-Natal and the Eastern Cape, has a very high rate of lightning in summer, and lightning kills people and livestock every year. Rural people working or herding in open fields, and people sheltering under isolated trees, are most at risk.

Lightning safety rules: When thunder roars, go indoors: if you can hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck. Shelter in a substantial building or in a car with a solid metal roof and the windows closed, not in an open shelter, tent or under a tree. Stay away from open fields, hilltops, tall isolated trees, poles, metal fences and water, including swimming pools, dams and the sea. Do not use corded telephones, plugged-in computers or taps and showers during a storm, as lightning can travel along wires and pipes. Unplug sensitive appliances before the storm arrives. If you are caught outside with no shelter, avoid being the tallest object, keep away from others, and crouch low with your feet together. Wait about 30 minutes after the last thunder before going outside again.

Static charge has everyday uses as well as dangers. Photocopiers and laser printers use a charged drum that attracts toner powder only where the image should be. Factory chimneys use electrostatic precipitators, in which smoke particles are given a charge and then attracted to plates of the opposite charge, removing ash before it reaches the air; many coal power stations use them. Spray painting of cars uses charged paint droplets that are attracted to the earthed car body, so less paint is wasted.

## Subtopic: Protecting Buildings and the Error Museum

Buildings are protected by lightning conductors: a pointed metal rod fixed above the highest point of the building, connected by a thick copper or aluminium strip to a metal plate or rod buried in the ground. If lightning strikes, the current flows through the conductor safely into the ground instead of through the building. Tall structures such as church spires, office towers, the Sentech tower in Johannesburg and electricity pylons all have lightning protection, and power lines have special earth wires along their tops. Rural homesteads with thatched roofs can be fitted with separate lightning protection masts.

A car with a metal body protects its occupants because the current flows around the outside of the metal shell and into the ground, not because of the rubber tyres.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: rubbing creates charge; it transfers electrons, and the total charge stays the same. Two: protons move when objects are charged; only electrons move. Three: a charged object attracting paper proves the paper is charged; neutral objects are attracted by induced charge, and only repulsion proves charge. Four: rubber tyres protect you in a car; the metal body carries the current around you. Five: lightning never strikes the same place twice; tall objects are struck repeatedly, which is why they need conductors.

Layout for an electrostatics answer: state which materials were rubbed; say which way electrons moved; state the resulting charge on each object; apply like repel, unlike attract; and for safety questions, give the rule and the reason behind it.

## Subtopic: The Balloon Trick

Rub a balloon on your hair, then hold it near your head. Your hair lifts up to meet it. What is going on?

Everything is made of atoms, and atoms contain tiny charged particles: positive protons stuck in the middle, and negative electrons around the outside. Normally the numbers match, so things have no overall charge.

When you rub the balloon on your hair, some electrons get scraped off your hair and onto the balloon. Now the balloon has extra electrons, so it is negative. Your hair has lost electrons, so it is positive. Opposites attract, so the hair reaches for the balloon. And each hair is now positive like its neighbours, so they push each other apart and stand up.

Only electrons move. The protons stay locked in place. And no charge is made; it is just moved from one thing to the other.

This works best on dry days. On a humid day, the dampness lets the charge leak away.

## Subtopic: Stick or Push

The rule is just like magnets. Like charges push apart. Unlike charges pull together. Two rubbed balloons on strings swing away from each other.

A charged balloon will also stick to a wall that has no charge at all. And a charged comb picks up tiny bits of paper and even bends a thin stream of water from a tap. That is because the charged object pulls the opposite charges in the paper or water a little closer, and the attraction wins.

Some materials let charge flow through them: metals, the ground, water and even your body. These are conductors. Others hold the charge where it is: plastic, rubber, glass and wool. These are insulators. That is why you rub a plastic ruler, not a metal spoon, to get static.

When a charged thing is connected to the ground with a conductor, the charge drains away. That is called earthing. Petrol tankers are earthed when they unload, so that a spark cannot light the fuel.

## Subtopic: The Giant Spark

Walk across a carpet, touch a metal door handle and zap: a tiny spark. Lightning is the same thing, made enormous.

Inside a towering thundercloud, ice and water particles crash into each other and swap charge. The top of the cloud becomes positive and the bottom becomes very negative. That pulls positive charge up into the ground below. When the difference gets big enough, the charge leaps through the air: lightning. It heats the air to about 30 000 degrees, hotter than the surface of the Sun, and the air bursts outwards with a bang: thunder.

Count the seconds between the flash and the thunder and divide by three. That is roughly how many kilometres away it struck.

The Highveld gets some of the most lightning in the world. Stay safe. When thunder roars, go indoors. A proper building or a car with a metal roof is safe; a tree, a tent or an open field is not. Keep away from water and metal fences, and do not use corded phones or take a shower in a storm. If you are stuck outside, crouch low with your feet together, away from tall things. Wait half an hour after the last thunder before going out.

Conductors let charge flow; insulators hold it; earthing drains it. Lightning is a giant spark from a thundercloud, and thunder is the air exploding. When thunder roars, go indoors.
