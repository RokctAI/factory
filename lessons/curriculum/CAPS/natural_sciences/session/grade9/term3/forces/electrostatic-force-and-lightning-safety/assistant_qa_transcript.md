### subtopic_1 — Charge and Charging by Rubbing
**Assistant:** How do I know which material gains electrons when two things are rubbed?

**Tutor:** Scientists have tested many pairs of materials and arranged them in a list called the triboelectric series, which ranks materials by how strongly they hold on to electrons. When two materials are rubbed together, the one lower in the series, which holds electrons more strongly, takes electrons from the one higher up, and becomes negative. A simplified version, from materials that tend to lose electrons to those that tend to gain them, runs roughly: human skin and hair, glass, wool, silk, paper, cotton, wood, rubber, polythene, PVC and Teflon. So hair rubbed with a rubber balloon loses electrons to the balloon; wool rubbed with polythene loses electrons to the polythene; and glass or acrylic rubbed with silk loses electrons to the silk. The further apart two materials are in the series, the larger the charge produced. The series is not perfectly reliable, because results depend on the cleanliness of the surfaces, the humidity and how hard the materials are rubbed, and the reasons behind it are still being researched. For examinations you are usually told the result, for example that the rod became negative, and you must explain it correctly in terms of electron transfer: the negative object gained electrons and the positive object lost the same number. Never say that the positive object gained protons, because protons do not move in this process.

### subtopic_2 — Attraction, Repulsion and Conductors
**Assistant:** Why is a car safe in lightning if it is not because of the tyres?

**Tutor:** Because the metal body of the car conducts the lightning current around the outside of the passenger compartment and into the ground, keeping the people inside out of its path. This is an example of a principle discovered by Michael Faraday in the 1830s: when charge flows onto a closed metal shell, it stays on the outer surface, and there is no electric field inside. A metal shell that protects its contents in this way is called a Faraday cage. A lightning bolt has already crossed several kilometres of air, which is an excellent insulator, so a few centimetres of rubber tyre would make no difference at all. In fact, the current usually jumps from the car body to the ground across or around the tyres, sometimes damaging them. Cars with fibreglass or soft fabric tops, and open vehicles such as motorbikes, bicycles, tractors and golf carts, do not give this protection. If you are in a car during a storm, keep the windows closed, avoid touching metal parts connected to the body, and stay inside until the storm has passed. Aeroplanes are also struck by lightning quite often, on average roughly once a year for each aircraft in commercial service, and their metal or specially protected skins conduct the current around the passengers in the same way, which is why passengers are usually unharmed.

### subtopic_3 — Lightning and Safety During Storms
**Assistant:** Is it true that lightning never strikes the same place twice?

**Tutor:** No, it is a myth, and believing it can be dangerous. Lightning tends to strike the tallest, most pointed or best-connected object in an area, because the electric field is strongest near such objects, and those same features make it likely to be struck again in the next storm. The Empire State Building in New York is struck around twenty times a year on average. Tall towers, mountain peaks, church spires and isolated trees in South Africa are also struck many times. A single lightning flash often consists of several strokes, typically three or four, along the same channel within less than a second, which is why lightning sometimes appears to flicker. For the same reasons, a person who sheltered under a tree that was struck in a previous storm is not safer under it now. The myth may have started because the chance of any particular ordinary spot on open ground being struck is small. The safe response is not to rely on luck but to follow the rules: go into a solid building or metal-roofed car when you hear thunder, avoid being the tallest object, and protect buildings with properly installed lightning conductors. In parts of rural South Africa, schools and homesteads have been fitted with lightning protection systems for exactly this reason, as part of efforts to reduce the number of people killed each year.

### subtopic_4 — Protecting Buildings and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why do I sometimes get a shock from a trolley or a car door?

**Tutor:** Because your body has built up a charge by friction, and when you touch a metal object, the charge jumps across the last tiny gap as a spark. As you walk across a carpet in rubber-soled shoes, or slide across a car seat made of synthetic fabric, electrons are transferred between your clothes or shoes and the surface, and your body, which is a conductor, ends up with a small net charge. The rubber soles and the dry air are insulators, so the charge cannot easily leak away into the ground. When your fingertip approaches a metal door handle, trolley or car door that is connected to the ground or is much larger than you, the charge rushes across the gap in a tiny spark, which you feel as a sharp prick and may see in the dark. The voltage can be several thousand volts, but the amount of charge is tiny, so it is not dangerous to a healthy person, only surprising. These shocks are more common in dry weather, such as Highveld winters, because humid air lets charge leak away gradually. You can reduce them by touching the metal with a key or a coin first, so the spark jumps from the key instead of your skin, by holding the metal frame of the car door as you get out, or by wearing shoes with leather soles. Electronics factories go further, earthing workers with wrist straps so that static sparks do not damage delicate components.
