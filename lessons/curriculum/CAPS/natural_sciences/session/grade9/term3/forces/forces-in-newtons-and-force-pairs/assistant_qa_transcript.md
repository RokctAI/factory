### subtopic_1 — What a Force Is and What It Does
**Assistant:** If forces in a pair are equal, why does the cannon not fly back as fast as the cannonball?

**Tutor:** Because the same size of force produces a much smaller change in motion on a much heavier object. When the cannon fires, the cannon pushes the ball forwards and the ball pushes the cannon backwards, with forces exactly equal in size, acting for exactly the same short time. The cannonball has a small mass, perhaps a few kilograms, so that force gives it a very large speed. The cannon has a mass of hundreds or thousands of kilograms, so the same force gives it only a small backward speed, which is the recoil. The heavier an object is, the harder it is to change its motion; this resistance to change is called inertia, and mass is its measure. The same idea explains the apple and the Earth. The apple pulls the Earth upwards with the same force that the Earth pulls the apple down, but the Earth's mass is so enormous that its movement is far too small to measure. It also explains why a small child and an adult on ice skates, pushing apart, end up with the child moving away much faster. The relationship between force, mass and the change in motion is Newton's second law, which you will use in Grade 11, where a force divided by a mass gives the acceleration. The third law tells you that the forces are equal; the second law tells you what each force does to its own object.

### subtopic_2 — Measuring Forces in Newtons
**Assistant:** Why do we use newtons and not kilograms for force?

**Tutor:** Because mass and force are different quantities, and mixing them up causes errors in every later calculation. Mass, measured in kilograms, is the amount of matter in an object, and it is the same wherever the object is: a 1 kilogram bag of sugar has a mass of 1 kilogram on Earth, on the Moon or floating in a space station. Force, measured in newtons, is a push or a pull, and it depends on the situation. The force with which the Earth pulls that bag of sugar, its weight, is about 9,8 newtons on Earth, but only about 1,6 newtons on the Moon, because the Moon's gravity is weaker, and nearly zero in deep space. A force can also be any push or pull, not just a weight: the force of your hand on a door, of wind on a sail, of a magnet on a nail. These have nothing to do with mass, so they need their own unit. In everyday speech people say things like this bag weighs 2 kilograms, and shop scales show kilograms because they are designed for use on Earth, where weight and mass are always in the same proportion. In science you must keep them separate: kilograms for mass, newtons for force. The lesson on gravitational force will show how to convert, multiplying the mass in kilograms by about 9,8 newtons per kilogram to get the weight in newtons.

### subtopic_3 — Representing Forces and Forces Acting in Pairs
**Assistant:** How can a rocket push on anything in empty space?

**Tutor:** It pushes on its own exhaust, which it carries with it as fuel. A rocket engine burns fuel with an oxidiser, both stored in the rocket, and the reaction produces an enormous amount of very hot gas. The shape of the engine forces this gas out of the nozzle at the back at extremely high speed, several kilometres per second. To accelerate the gas backwards so violently, the rocket must exert a huge force on it. By the push-back rule, the gas exerts an equal and opposite force on the rocket, pushing it forwards. Air plays no part in this; if anything, air gets in the way by causing drag, which is why rockets work even better in the vacuum of space. A simple model is a balloon released without being tied: the stretched rubber squeezes air out of the neck, the air pushes back on the balloon, and the balloon shoots off in the opposite direction. Another is a person on a skateboard throwing heavy balls backwards, who rolls forwards a little with each throw. People once believed that rockets could not work in space because there was nothing to push against, and a famous newspaper editorial in 1920 mocked the rocket pioneer Robert Goddard for saying they could. The newspaper published a correction in 1969, the day after Apollo 11 was launched towards the Moon.

### subtopic_4 — Analysing Forces and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Does the table really push up on a book? It does not seem to do anything.

**Tutor:** Yes, it really does, and you can find evidence for it even though the table looks still. When the book is placed on the table, the book pushes down on the surface and squashes it very slightly. The particles in the table are pushed a tiny bit closer together, and like compressed springs they push back. The table surface bends until its upward push exactly balances the book's weight, and then everything stays still. With a solid wooden table, the bending is far too small to see, but with a softer support it becomes obvious. Put the book on a mattress, a sponge or a thin plank supported only at its ends, and you can see the surface sag until it pushes back enough. Remove the book and the surface springs back. A trampoline is an extreme example. This upward push from a surface is called the normal force or the reaction force, and it always acts at right angles to the surface. It adjusts to whatever is needed, up to the point where the surface breaks. If you stack too many books on a flimsy shelf, the shelf bends further and further, and if the required force is more than the shelf can provide, it cracks and collapses. So the table is not passive; it is actively supporting the book, and it is a force you will draw in every force diagram in Grades 10 to 12.
