# Part 1 — Expert

This session describes magnets and magnetic materials, the poles of a magnet and the rule for attraction and repulsion, the magnetic field and how to map it, and the Earth's magnetic field, including how a compass works and why the field matters to life. The anchor facts are that the magnetic materials are iron, nickel, cobalt and alloys such as steel; that like poles repel and unlike poles attract; that magnetic field lines run from the north pole to the south pole outside a magnet and are closest where the field is strongest; and that the Earth behaves as if it had a giant bar magnet inside it, with the south pole of that magnet near the geographic North Pole. The governing principle is that magnets exert a field force on other magnets and on magnetic materials, through a magnetic field that surrounds every magnet.

## Subtopic: Magnets, Magnetic Materials and Poles

A magnet is an object that produces a magnetic field and attracts certain materials. Magnets come in many shapes: bar magnets, horseshoe magnets, ring magnets, flat fridge magnets and the powerful neodymium magnets in earphones and electric motors. Natural magnets are found as the mineral magnetite, called lodestone, which was the first magnetic material known to people.

Magnetic materials are those attracted by a magnet and that can be made into magnets. The common magnetic metals are iron, nickel and cobalt, and alloys containing them, especially steel. Most other materials, including aluminium, copper, gold, silver, brass, wood, plastic, glass, paper and rubber, are non-magnetic: a magnet does not attract them. This is why a magnet will pick up steel paper clips and nails but not aluminium cooldrink cans or copper coins. Scrap yards use this property: large electromagnets lift steel scrap and separate it from aluminium and other metals.

Every magnet has two poles, regions where its force is strongest, called the north-seeking pole, N, and the south-seeking pole, S. If a bar magnet is hung freely by a thread at its middle, it turns until its north-seeking pole points roughly towards the Earth's geographic north, which is how the poles got their names. Iron filings sprinkled over a bar magnet cluster thickly at the two ends, showing where the poles are, and hardly stick to the middle.

Poles always come in pairs. If a bar magnet is cut in half, each half becomes a complete magnet with its own north and south pole. No one has ever found an isolated north or south pole.

Magnetic materials can be magnetised. Stroke an iron or steel needle many times in the same direction with one pole of a magnet, lifting the magnet clear at the end of each stroke, and the needle becomes a weak magnet. Steel keeps its magnetism for a long time and is used for permanent magnets; soft iron magnetises easily but loses its magnetism as soon as the magnet is removed, which makes it ideal for the cores of electromagnets. Magnets can be weakened by heating, by hammering or dropping them, and by storing them carelessly, so bar magnets are stored in pairs with unlike poles side by side, joined by small iron pieces called keepers.

## Subtopic: Attraction, Repulsion and Magnetic Fields

The law of magnetic poles: like poles repel; unlike poles attract. Bring the north pole of one magnet near the north pole of another, and they push each other apart. The same happens with two south poles. Bring a north pole near a south pole, and they pull together.

A magnet attracts a magnetic material such as an iron nail by either pole, because the magnet induces magnetism in the nail: the end of the nail nearest a north pole becomes a south pole, so they attract. This is why attraction alone does not prove that an object is a magnet. Repulsion is the only sure test: if an object repels a known pole of a magnet, it must itself be a magnet.

The size of the magnetic force increases as the magnets come closer together and is greatest at the poles. The force acts across a gap, and through non-magnetic materials such as paper, wood, plastic, glass and water. A magnet can move a paper clip through a desk or pick up pins through the side of a plastic container.

The magnetic field is the region around a magnet where its magnetic force acts. It can be mapped in two ways. Sprinkle iron filings on a sheet of paper or glass placed over a magnet and tap the paper gently: the filings line up along curved lines from pole to pole. Or place a small plotting compass at many points around the magnet and mark the direction its needle points at each point, then join the marks into lines.

The lines are called magnetic field lines. They show the direction a compass needle would point. By convention they go out of the north pole and into the south pole outside the magnet. They never cross. Where they are close together, the field is strong; where they spread out, it is weak. Between two unlike poles facing each other, the lines go straight across from one to the other; between two like poles, the lines push away from each other and there is a neutral point between them where the field is zero.

A simple investigation compares the strength of magnets: count how many paper clips each magnet can hold in a chain from one pole, or measure the greatest distance at which each magnet can make a paper clip move. In a fair test, the paper clips are identical and the surface is the same.

## Subtopic: The Earth's Magnetic Field

The Earth behaves as though a giant bar magnet were buried at its centre. The field is actually produced by electric currents in the molten iron and nickel of the Earth's outer core, as it slowly churns, which is why it is called a geodynamo.

A compass is a small magnet, a needle, mounted so that it can turn freely. Its north-seeking pole is attracted towards the Earth's magnetic pole in the far north. Since unlike poles attract, the magnetic pole near the geographic North Pole must actually be a south magnetic pole, and the one near the geographic South Pole must be a north magnetic pole. The Earth's field lines therefore run out of the Antarctic region and into the Arctic region.

The magnetic poles are not exactly at the geographic poles, the points on the Earth's axis of rotation. The magnetic poles wander slowly, moving tens of kilometres a year. As a result, a compass points to magnetic north, which can differ from true north by several degrees or more. Navigators, pilots and hikers correct for this difference, called the magnetic declination; in South Africa the compass points between about 15 and 30 degrees west of true north, depending on the place.

The Earth's magnetic field matters to life. It extends tens of thousands of kilometres into space, forming the magnetosphere, which deflects most of the stream of fast charged particles from the Sun, called the solar wind. Without it, these particles would gradually strip away the atmosphere and harm living things. Some particles are funnelled towards the polar regions, where they collide with gases in the upper atmosphere and make them glow, producing the aurora, the shimmering southern and northern lights. Many animals, including migrating birds, sea turtles, whales and some bacteria, sense the Earth's field and use it to navigate.

South Africa has a special role in studying the field. The South African National Space Agency runs a magnetic observatory at Hermanus in the Western Cape, which has recorded the Earth's field continuously since the 1940s. Southern Africa lies near the South Atlantic Anomaly, a large region where the Earth's field is unusually weak, and satellites passing over it receive more radiation.

## Subtopic: Uses of Magnets and the Error Museum

Magnets are everywhere in technology. Fridge doors close with magnetic strips. Electric motors in fans, washing machines, cars and drills use magnets and electromagnets. Generators in power stations, including Koeberg and the coal stations of Mpumalanga, use magnets to make electricity, as you will see later this term. Loudspeakers and earphones use magnets to vibrate a cone that makes sound. Hospitals use very strong magnets in MRI scanners to image soft tissues. Recycling plants use magnets to sort steel cans from aluminium cans. Mines use magnetic separators to concentrate iron ore. An electromagnet, a coil of wire wound around an iron core, becomes a magnet only when an electric current flows, so it can be switched on and off, which makes it useful in cranes, electric bells and door locks.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: magnets attract all metals; only iron, nickel, cobalt and their alloys are magnetic, not aluminium, copper or gold. Two: like poles attract; like poles repel. Three: if something is attracted to a magnet, it must be a magnet; magnetic materials are attracted too, so only repulsion proves it is a magnet. Four: cutting a magnet separates the north and south poles; each piece becomes a complete magnet. Five: the Earth's magnetic north pole is a north magnetic pole; it attracts the north pole of a compass, so it is a south magnetic pole.

Layout for a magnetic field drawing: draw the magnet with N and S marked; draw several smooth lines from N to S, never crossing; add arrows pointing away from N and towards S; draw the lines closer together near the poles.

## Subtopic: The Fussy Pull

Magnets are fussy. They only pull on a few materials: iron, nickel, cobalt and steel, which is mostly iron. They ignore aluminium, copper, gold, plastic, wood, glass and paper. A magnet will grab a steel paper clip but not an aluminium cooldrink can. Recycling plants use exactly this to pick out the steel cans.

Every magnet has two ends where it is strongest, called poles: a north pole and a south pole. Sprinkle iron filings on a bar magnet and they pile up at the two ends.

Here is a puzzle. Snap a magnet in half. Do you get a north piece and a south piece? No. You get two smaller magnets, each with its own north and south. Poles always come in pairs.

The very first magnets people knew were special rocks called lodestones, a type of iron ore.

Look after a magnet and it stays strong. Dropping it, banging it or heating it in a fire makes it weaker, because the tiny magnetic regions inside get jumbled up.

## Subtopic: Push and Pull

The magnet rule is short: like poles push apart, unlike poles pull together. North and north: push. South and south: push. North and south: pull.

Try it with two magnets. Turn one around and feel the push change to a pull. You can even make a magnet float above another one by pushing like poles together.

A magnet pulls on an iron nail with either end, because it turns the nail into a temporary magnet. So if something is pulled by a magnet, it might just be iron. If something is pushed away, it must be a magnet. Pushing is the real test.

The magnet's pull reaches across a gap, through paper, plastic, wood and even water. The space around it where it works is called its magnetic field. Sprinkle iron filings on paper over a magnet and you see the field: curved lines from one pole to the other, crowded together near the poles where the pull is strongest.

You can make your own magnet. Stroke a steel needle about thirty times in one direction with the end of a magnet. Now the needle picks up tiny pins. Float it on a piece of cork in a bowl of water and it turns to point north. You have made a compass.

## Subtopic: The Planet That Is a Magnet

The whole Earth is a magnet. Deep inside, hot liquid iron swirls around, and that makes a huge magnetic field, as if a giant bar magnet were buried inside.

A compass is just a tiny magnet on a pin. Its north end swings round to point north, which is how hikers, sailors and pilots find their way. A compass actually points to magnetic north, which is a little different from true north, so map readers make a small correction.

The Earth's magnetic field is also a shield. It stretches far into space and pushes away most of the dangerous particles that stream out of the Sun. Some of them sneak in near the poles and make the sky glow green and red: the southern and northern lights. Birds, turtles and whales can sense the field and use it like a built-in map.

South Africa watches the field closely. The observatory at Hermanus has measured it every day for more than 80 years.

Like poles repel and unlike poles attract; only repulsion proves something is a magnet. The magnetic field runs from north to south and is strongest at the poles. The Earth is a giant magnet that turns compasses, guides animals and shields us from the Sun's particles. Grade 10 to 12 Physical Sciences builds on magnetism and electromagnetism.
