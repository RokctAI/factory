# Part 1 — Expert

This session describes three contact forces, friction, tension and compression, explains what each one is and what affects its size, and shows where each is useful or a problem in daily life and in engineering. The anchor facts are that friction acts parallel to the surfaces in contact and opposes their sliding past each other; that it increases with rougher surfaces and with a greater push between the surfaces, and decreases with lubrication; that tension is the pulling force along a stretched rope, cable or chain; and that compression is the squashing force along a pillar or support. The governing principle is that contact forces arise only where objects touch, from the push and pull between the particles of their surfaces.

## Subtopic: Friction

Contact forces act only when two objects are touching. When the contact ends, the force disappears.

Friction is the contact force that acts between two surfaces when they slide, or try to slide, across each other. It always acts parallel to the surfaces and in the direction that opposes the sliding. Push a heavy box to the right across the floor, and friction from the floor acts on the box towards the left.

Where does friction come from? Even surfaces that look smooth are rough at a microscopic scale, with tiny bumps and hollows. When two surfaces are pressed together, the bumps catch on each other and the particles at the points of contact attract one another, so a force is needed to slide one surface over the other.

The size of friction depends on two main factors. First, the nature of the surfaces: rough surfaces such as sandpaper, tar or rubber on concrete give more friction than smooth surfaces such as ice, polished tiles or glass. Second, how hard the surfaces are pressed together: a heavy box is harder to slide than a light one made of the same material, because it presses down harder. Friction is reduced by lubricants such as oil, grease and water, which separate the surfaces with a slippery layer; by rolling instead of sliding, using wheels, rollers or ball bearings; and by making surfaces smoother.

Friction always turns some of the energy of motion into heat, which is why rubbing your hands together warms them, why brakes get hot, and why a match lights when struck.

Friction can be investigated with a spring balance. Attach a wooden block to the balance and pull it slowly and steadily across different surfaces, such as a desk, carpet, sandpaper and a sheet of plastic, reading the force needed to keep it sliding. In a fair test, the same block is pulled at the same steady speed each time, so the surface is the only variable changed. Adding masses on top of the block shows the effect of pressing harder: the reading rises as the load increases. Placing the block on round pencils used as rollers shows how much rolling reduces friction.

Friction even acts when nothing moves. A book resting on a sloping desk does not slide because friction pushes up the slope on it, just enough to stop it sliding. Tilt the desk more and, at some angle, the friction can no longer hold it and the book slides.

## Subtopic: Friction: Useful and a Nuisance

Friction is essential to everyday life. Without it, you could not walk, because your shoes would slip; cars and taxis could not start, steer or stop, because tyres would spin; nails and screws would fall out of wood; knots would come undone; you could not hold a pen; and a ladder leaning against a wall would slide down.

Useful friction is often increased on purpose. Shoe soles and car tyres have treads, patterned grooves that grip the ground and channel water away on wet roads; worn, bald tyres are illegal in South Africa because they cannot grip safely in the rain. Brakes press brake pads against discs or drums, and the friction slows the wheel. Sand or gravel is spread on slippery surfaces. Sports players use grips: cricketers' bats and tennis rackets have rubber or leather handles, and rugby players may wear gloves.

Friction is also a nuisance. It wears away surfaces: tyres, shoe soles, brake pads and engine parts all wear out. It wastes energy as heat, which is why machines need more fuel or electricity than they would without it, and why moving parts can overheat. It makes it harder to move heavy loads.

Unwanted friction is reduced in several ways. Engines are lubricated with oil. Bicycle chains and door hinges are oiled. Wheels and ball bearings replace sliding with rolling. Cars, trains and aircraft are given smooth, streamlined shapes to reduce air resistance, which is a form of friction from air, and boats are shaped to reduce water resistance. A skydiver uses air resistance deliberately: an open parachute has a large area and a great deal of air resistance, so it slows the fall to a safe landing speed.

Road safety in South Africa depends heavily on friction. Stopping distance has two parts: the distance travelled while the driver reacts, and the braking distance while friction between tyres and road slows the vehicle. The braking distance increases sharply with speed and on wet or gravel roads, which is why speed limits drop in towns and why drivers are urged to slow down in rain. Worn brake pads and bald tyres both reduce the friction available and lengthen stopping distances.

## Subtopic: Tension and Compression

Tension is the pulling force in a rope, string, cable, chain or wire when it is stretched by forces pulling on both ends. The tension acts along the length of the rope and pulls inwards on whatever is attached at each end. In tug of war, each team pulls on the rope, and the rope pulls on each team. A bucket hanging from a rope in a well is held up by the tension in the rope. The cables of a lift, the strings of a guitar and the steel cables of a suspension or cable-stayed bridge are all in tension. The Nelson Mandela Bridge in Johannesburg is a cable-stayed bridge: steel cables in tension run from tall pylons to the deck and hold it up.

Ropes and cables can only pull; they cannot push. Push the end of a rope and it simply bends. That is why materials used in tension, such as steel cable, nylon rope and wire, must be strong when stretched.

Compression is the pushing or squashing force in an object that is pressed from both ends. It acts along the length of the object and pushes outwards on whatever is at each end. The legs of a table are in compression, squeezed between the tabletop and the floor. So are the pillars of a building, the walls that hold up a roof, the arch of a stone bridge and the bones of your legs when you stand.

Materials used in compression, such as concrete, stone and brick, must be strong when squashed. Many materials are strong in one way but weak in the other. Concrete is very strong in compression but cracks easily in tension, so engineers place steel bars inside it, called reinforced concrete, because steel is strong in tension. A long, thin rod in compression can buckle, bending sideways suddenly, so pillars are made thick enough.

A simple test shows the difference. Take a strip of paper: pull its ends and it resists strongly, because paper is fairly strong in tension; push its ends together and it crumples at once, because a thin strip cannot carry compression. Roll the same paper into a tube and it can carry a surprising compression load, because the shape stops it buckling. Engineers use shape as well as material to handle forces, which is why pillars are thick, beams are shaped like an I, and bridges use arches and triangles.

## Subtopic: Contact Forces in Structures and the Error Museum

Engineers analyse every part of a structure to decide whether it is in tension or compression and choose suitable materials and shapes. In a simple roof truss, the triangle of beams that supports a roof, some beams are squashed and others stretched. In a bicycle wheel, the spokes are in tension, pulling the rim towards the hub. In a camping tent, the poles are in compression and the guy ropes are in tension. In a suspension bridge, the main cables and vertical hangers are in tension, while the towers are in compression.

Triangles are used in many structures, such as electricity pylons, cranes and roof trusses, because a triangle cannot change shape without one of its sides changing length, so it stays rigid when its members carry tension and compression.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: friction always acts in the direction of motion; it opposes sliding. Two: friction is always bad; we could not walk, write or brake without it. Three: a rope can push; ropes can only pull, so they carry tension only. Four: oil increases friction; lubricants reduce friction. Five: heavier objects have less friction; they press harder on the surface and have more.

Layout for a contact force answer: name the force; state its direction, for friction opposite to the sliding, for tension along the rope and pulling, for compression along the support and pushing; give the cause; and say how it is useful or a problem and how it is increased or reduced.

A good habit when analysing any structure is to ask, for each part: if this part were removed, would the rest move apart or fall together? If the pieces would fly apart, the part was holding them together and is in tension. If they would collapse inwards, it was holding them apart and is in compression.

## Subtopic: The Grip Force

Contact forces only work when things touch. Friction is the most famous one.

Friction is the grip between two surfaces. It always works against sliding. Push a heavy box across the floor and friction drags back on it.

Why? Even a surface that looks smooth is bumpy under a microscope. When two surfaces press together, the tiny bumps catch on each other.

More friction comes from rougher surfaces and from pressing harder. Rubber on tar grips well. Socks on polished tiles slip. A heavy box is harder to slide than a light one.

Less friction comes from oil or grease, from wheels and rollers instead of sliding, and from smooth surfaces.

And friction makes heat. Rub your hands together on a cold winter morning and feel them warm up. Brakes get hot for the same reason.

Test it yourself with a spring balance. Pull a block across a desk, then across carpet, then across sandpaper, and read how hard you have to pull. Put a heavy book on the block and pull again. The numbers go up with rougher surfaces and heavier loads. Put round pencils under the block and the number drops right down.

## Subtopic: Friend or Enemy?

Friction is a friend. Without it you could not walk; you would slip like on ice. Taxis could not stop. You could not hold your pen. Shoes and tyres have deep treads to grip better, and bald tyres are dangerous and illegal. Brakes work by rubbing pads against the wheel.

Friction is also an enemy. It wears out shoes, tyres and engine parts. It wastes energy as heat, so machines use more fuel. So we fight it with oil in engines, grease on bike chains, ball bearings in wheels and smooth, sleek shapes for cars and aeroplanes, which cut down air resistance.

Air resistance is friction from the air. A parachute uses it on purpose: the big canopy catches lots of air and slows the skydiver down.

On the road, friction is your safety. When it rains, the tyres grip less, so a car needs a longer distance to stop. That is why drivers should slow down and leave a bigger gap in wet weather.

## Subtopic: Ropes That Pull, Pillars That Push

Tension is the pull inside a stretched rope, cable or chain. In tug of war the rope is in tension. A bucket hanging in a well hangs on tension. Guitar strings, lift cables and the big steel cables of the Nelson Mandela Bridge in Johannesburg are all in tension. A rope can only pull. Try pushing with a rope and it just flops.

Compression is the squash inside something being pressed from both ends. Table legs, building pillars, brick walls and your leg bones when you stand are all in compression.

Some materials are good at one and bad at the other. Concrete is great at being squashed but cracks when stretched, so builders put steel bars inside it to handle the stretching. That is reinforced concrete.

Look at a tent. The poles push, in compression. The guy ropes pull, in tension. Together they hold it up.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Contact forces act only when objects touch. Friction opposes sliding; it is bigger for rough surfaces and harder pressing, and smaller with oil, wheels and smooth surfaces. It is useful for grip and braking, and a nuisance because of wear and heat. Tension pulls along ropes and cables. Compression pushes along pillars and legs. Engineers choose materials for each. Grade 10 to 12 Physical Sciences calculates these forces exactly.

Try it with paper. Pull a strip of paper and it holds strongly. Push the ends together and it crumples. Now roll it into a tube and stand it up. It can hold up a book. The shape stops it buckling.
