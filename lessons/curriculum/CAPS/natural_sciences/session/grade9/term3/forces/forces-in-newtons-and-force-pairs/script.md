# Part 1 — Expert

This session defines a force, describes what forces can do, introduces the newton as the unit of force and the spring balance as the instrument that measures it, shows how to represent forces with arrows, and explains the principle that forces always act in pairs. The anchor facts are that a 100 gram apple has a weight of about 1 newton, that a force is represented by an arrow whose length is proportional to its size, and that when object A exerts a force on object B, B exerts an equal and opposite force on A. The governing principle is that a force is an interaction between two objects, so forces never occur singly: they always come in equal and opposite pairs acting on different objects.

## Subtopic: What a Force Is and What It Does

A force is a push, a pull or a twist exerted by one object on another. Every force has a source, the object that exerts it, and a receiver, the object it acts on. A foot kicks a ball; the Earth pulls a falling mango; a magnet pulls a paper clip; a rope pulls a bucket out of a well.

A twist is a turning effect, produced when forces act to rotate an object, such as turning a tap, a doorknob, a screwdriver or the lid of a jar.

Forces can do several things to an object. A force can make a stationary object start to move, as when a soccer player kicks a ball from rest. It can make a moving object speed up, as when a cyclist pedals harder. It can slow a moving object down or stop it, as when brakes are applied. It can change the direction of a moving object, as when a cricket bat deflects a ball or a netball player catches and redirects a pass. And it can change the shape of an object, stretching, squashing, bending or twisting it, as when you squeeze a stress ball or stretch an elastic band.

An important point: a moving object does not need a force to keep it moving at a steady speed in a straight line. A force is needed to change its motion. On Earth, moving objects slow down because of friction and air resistance, which are forces acting against the motion. In space, far from other objects, a probe keeps travelling at the same speed for years with its engines switched off.

Forces are invisible. We cannot see a force itself, only its effects: a change in motion or a change in shape.

Forces are often grouped into two families, which the next lessons explore. Contact forces act only when objects touch: friction, the push of a surface, tension in a rope and compression in a pillar. Field forces, also called non-contact forces, act across a gap: gravitational force, magnetic force and electrostatic force. A falling mango is pulled by the Earth without touching it; a fridge magnet pulls on a steel door before it touches.

The questions for this section are with you now: what counts as a force, the five effects of forces and the source and receiver of each.

## Subtopic: Measuring Forces in Newtons

The unit of force is the newton, symbol N, named after the English scientist Isaac Newton, who described the laws of motion in 1687. One newton is a fairly small force. A medium-sized apple with a mass of about 100 grams, held still in your hand, pushes down on it with a force of about 1 newton, because the Earth pulls each kilogram with a force of about 9,8 newtons, and 0,1 × 9,8 = 0,98 newtons. Larger forces are measured in kilonewtons: 1 kN = 1000 N.

Some everyday forces for scale: the pull of the Earth on a 1 kilogram bag of sugar is about 9,8 newtons; on a Grade 9 learner with a mass of 50 kilograms, about 50 × 9,8 = 490 newtons; a firm handshake might be 100 to 200 newtons; the thrust of a large passenger jet engine is several hundred kilonewtons.

Forces are measured with a spring balance, also called a newton meter or force meter. It contains a spring attached to a hook, with a scale marked in newtons. When a force pulls on the hook, the spring stretches, and the amount of stretch is proportional to the force, within the spring's limit. Doubling the force doubles the stretch. To use it: check that the pointer reads zero before use and adjust if necessary; choose a balance whose range suits the force, for example 0 to 10 N for light objects; hold it vertically for weights, or in line with the pull for horizontal forces; and read the scale at eye level to avoid parallax error.

Kitchen and bathroom scales show mass in kilograms, not force in newtons, even though they actually respond to the downward force on them. You will see the difference between mass and weight in the lesson on field forces.

A simple class investigation shows the link between force and stretch. Hang a spring from a clamp, measure its length, then add 1 newton weights one at a time and measure the length after each. The extension, the increase in length, goes up by the same amount for each newton added, so a graph of extension against force is a straight line through the origin. If too much force is added, the spring is stretched beyond its limit and no longer returns to its original length, which is why a spring balance must not be overloaded.

## Subtopic: Representing Forces and Forces Acting in Pairs

Because a force has both a size and a direction, it is drawn as an arrow. The arrow starts at the point where the force acts on the object, points in the direction of the force, and has a length proportional to the size of the force, using a stated scale such as 1 cm represents 10 N. Each arrow is labelled with the name or symbol of the force and its size. A force diagram of a book resting on a table shows a downward arrow for the Earth's pull, the weight, and an upward arrow of equal length for the push of the table.

Now the key principle. A force is always an interaction between two objects, so forces always occur in pairs. If object A exerts a force on object B, then object B exerts a force on object A that is equal in size and opposite in direction. This is Newton's third law of motion, often stated as: for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction.

The two forces of a pair always act on different objects. That is why they do not cancel each other out. When you push a wall with 50 newtons, the wall pushes back on you with 50 newtons. When you stand on the floor, you push down on the floor and the floor pushes up on you. When the Earth pulls an apple down with 1 newton, the apple pulls the Earth up with 1 newton; the Earth does not noticeably move because its mass is enormous.

Examples. Walking: your foot pushes backwards on the ground, and the ground pushes forwards on your foot, so you move forward; on slippery ice or a polished floor, you cannot push back well, so walking is hard. Swimming: the swimmer pushes water backwards, and the water pushes the swimmer forwards. Rowing a boat: the oar pushes water backwards; the water pushes the oar and boat forwards. A rocket: it pushes hot exhaust gases downwards at enormous speed, and the gases push the rocket upwards. Jumping off a small boat onto a jetty: you push the boat backwards as you jump forwards, which is why the boat moves away and you may fall short.

The questions for this section are waiting: drawing force arrows to scale, identifying force pairs and explaining walking and swimming.

## Subtopic: Analysing Forces and the Error Museum

To identify a force pair, use the formula: A pushes or pulls on B; therefore B pushes or pulls on A, with the same size, in the opposite direction, and of the same type. If the first force is a contact push, the partner is a contact push; if it is a gravitational pull, the partner is a gravitational pull.

Worked example: a car tyre pushes backwards on the road with 2000 newtons. The partner force is the road pushing forwards on the tyre with 2000 newtons. This forward push from the road is what drives the car forward. On a wet or gravel road, the tyre cannot grip, the road cannot push back as strongly, and the wheels spin.

A common examination trap is to name the weight of a book on a table and the upward push of the table as a force pair. They are equal and opposite, but they both act on the same object, the book, and they are different types, one gravitational and one contact. The true partner of the book's weight is the book's upward pull on the Earth. The partner of the table's push on the book is the book's downward push on the table.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: a force is needed to keep something moving at a steady speed; a force is needed only to change motion, and on Earth we keep pushing to overcome friction. Two: force pairs cancel out; they act on different objects, so they do not cancel. Three: the bigger object exerts the bigger force; the forces in a pair are always equal, though the lighter object accelerates more. Four: a kilogram is a unit of force; force is measured in newtons, mass in kilograms. Five: a rocket needs air to push against; it pushes on its own exhaust gases, which is why rockets work in space.

Layout for a force pair answer: name the first force with its source and receiver, then state the partner force with its source, receiver, size and direction, and say why the two do not cancel.

The questions for this section are with you now: naming force pairs, avoiding the book-on-table trap and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through pushes and pulls, the size of a newton, and the push-back rule — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Pushes, Pulls and Twists

A force is simply a push, a pull or a twist. You push a trolley at the shop. You pull a door open. You twist the lid off a jar of peanut butter. Something always does the pushing or pulling, and something always gets pushed or pulled.

What can a force do? Quite a lot. It can start something moving, like kicking a ball that was standing still. It can make something go faster, like pedalling harder downhill. It can slow something down or stop it, like squeezing the brakes on a bike. It can change the direction, like a goalkeeper punching the ball away. And it can change the shape, like squashing a cooldrink can or stretching an elastic band.

You cannot see a force. You only see what it does.

Here is a surprise. If you roll a ball on a perfectly smooth surface, it would just keep rolling for ever without any push. On a real floor it slows down because friction is pushing against it. Out in space, with nothing to rub against, a spacecraft can coast for years with its engines off.

Keep pushes, pulls and twists in mind and try the questions for this part: spotting forces and what they do.

## Subtopic: How Big Is a Newton?

We measure forces in newtons, named after the scientist Isaac Newton. The short form is a capital N.

How big is one newton? Hold a small apple in your hand. The push you feel on your palm is about one newton. A one-kilogram bag of sugar pushes down with about ten newtons. A Grade 9 learner standing on the floor pushes down with a few hundred newtons.

To measure a force, use a spring balance, also called a newton meter. It is a spring inside a tube with a hook at the bottom and a scale marked in newtons. Hang something on the hook, or pull on it, and the spring stretches. The bigger the force, the more it stretches, and the pointer shows the number.

Tips for using it: check it reads zero first, pick one that suits the size of the force, keep it straight, and read the scale with your eye level with the pointer.

On paper, we draw a force as an arrow. The arrow points the way the force acts, and a longer arrow means a bigger force.

Hold the newton in mind and try this part's questions: the unit, the spring balance and the arrows.

## Subtopic: The Push-Back Rule

Here is the big idea: forces always come in pairs. Whenever you push on something, it pushes back on you, just as hard, in the opposite direction.

Push against a wall and you feel the wall pushing back on your hands. Lean on it too hard on roller skates and you roll backwards, because the wall's push moves you.

That push-back is how you move around. When you walk, your foot pushes the ground backwards, and the ground pushes you forwards. On ice, your foot slips, the ground cannot push back, and you go nowhere. A swimmer pushes water backwards and the water pushes the swimmer forwards. A rocket blasts hot gas downwards and the gas pushes the rocket up. It does not need air to push on, so rockets work in space.

Why do the two forces not cancel? Because they act on different things. Your push is on the wall; the wall's push is on you.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A force is a push, pull or twist from one object on another. It can start, stop, speed up, slow down, turn or reshape things. Forces are measured in newtons with a spring balance, and an apple weighs about one newton. Draw forces as arrows. Forces come in equal and opposite pairs acting on different objects. Grade 10 to 12 Physical Sciences builds all of mechanics on these ideas.

Try it with a friend on skateboards or on a smooth floor in socks. Stand facing each other and push hands. You both roll apart, even if only one of you pushes, because the push-back is automatic. If one of you is much heavier, the lighter one moves away faster, but the pushes are equal.

Hold the push-back rule in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: walking, swimming, rockets and why the pairs do not cancel.
