# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces field forces, describes the gravitational force between objects, distinguishes clearly between mass and weight, shows how to calculate weight from mass using the gravitational field strength, and explains free fall and apparent weightlessness. The anchor facts are that the gravitational field strength at the Earth's surface is about 9,8 newtons per kilogram, often rounded to 10 for quick estimates; that weight equals mass multiplied by gravitational field strength, so a 60 kilogram learner weighs about 588 newtons on Earth and about 96 newtons on the Moon, where the field strength is about 1,6 newtons per kilogram; and that mass, in kilograms, is the same everywhere. The governing principle is that every mass attracts every other mass with a gravitational force that grows with their masses and weakens with distance, and that weight is the gravitational force on an object, while mass is the amount of matter it contains.

## Subtopic: Field Forces and Gravitational Attraction

Field forces, also called non-contact forces, act between objects that are not touching. The space around an object in which it exerts such a force is called its field. There are three field forces at this level: gravitational force, magnetic force and electrostatic force.

Gravitational force is the attraction between any two objects that have mass. It is always attractive: gravity pulls, it never pushes. Every object attracts every other object. You attract the person sitting next to you, and they attract you, but the force is far too small to notice because both masses are small. The force becomes large only when at least one of the objects has an enormous mass, such as the Earth, the Moon or the Sun.

The size of the gravitational force depends on two things. It is larger when the masses are larger: the Earth, with a mass about 81 times that of the Moon, pulls much more strongly than the Moon does. It is smaller when the objects are further apart: the pull weakens rapidly with distance, so a satellite high above the Earth feels a weaker pull than an object at the surface.

Gravity holds the universe together. It keeps your feet on the ground, makes rain fall and rivers flow downhill, holds the atmosphere around the Earth, keeps the Moon in orbit around the Earth and the Earth in orbit around the Sun, and causes the ocean tides through the pull of the Moon and Sun on the oceans. In Term 4 you will see that gravity also pulls clouds of gas together to form stars.

Like every force, gravitational forces come in pairs. The Earth pulls you down with your weight; you pull the Earth up with an equal force.

The idea of a field is useful for picturing these forces. A field can be drawn as lines showing the direction of the force on an object placed at each point. The Earth's gravitational field lines all point inwards towards the centre of the Earth, which is why down means a different direction in Cape Town and in London, yet everywhere down points towards the centre of the planet. Where the lines are closer together the field is stronger, so they spread out and weaken further from the Earth.

The questions for this section are with you now: what a field force is, what gravity depends on and examples of its effects.

## Subtopic: Mass and Weight

Mass is the amount of matter in an object. It is measured in kilograms, kg, or grams, g, using a balance that compares the object with standard masses. Mass does not depend on where the object is: a 2 kilogram bag of maize meal has a mass of 2 kilograms in Johannesburg, on top of Kilimanjaro, on the Moon or floating in space.

Weight is the gravitational force that a planet or moon exerts on an object. Because it is a force, it is measured in newtons, N, using a spring balance. Weight acts downwards, towards the centre of the planet. Weight does depend on where the object is: it is smaller on the Moon, where gravity is weaker, and nearly zero in deep space far from any planet.

The connection between them is the gravitational field strength, symbol g, which is the gravitational force on each kilogram of mass. At the Earth's surface, g is about 9,8 newtons per kilogram. On the Moon it is about 1,6 newtons per kilogram, roughly one sixth of the Earth's value; on Mars about 3,7; on Jupiter about 24,8.

The relationship is: weight = mass × gravitational field strength, or W = m × g.

Worked examples on Earth, using g = 9,8 newtons per kilogram: a 2 kilogram bag of maize meal weighs 2 × 9,8 = 19,6 newtons. A 50 kilogram learner weighs 50 × 9,8 = 490 newtons. A 1000 kilogram car weighs 1000 × 9,8 = 9800 newtons. Working backwards, an object that weighs 588 newtons on Earth has a mass of 588 ÷ 9,8 = 60 kilograms.

On the Moon, with g = 1,6 newtons per kilogram, a 60 kilogram astronaut weighs 60 × 1,6 = 96 newtons, compared with 60 × 9,8 = 588 newtons on Earth. The mass is unchanged; only the weight differs.

In daily life, people say I weigh 60 kilograms. In science, that is your mass; your weight is about 588 newtons.

A table of comparisons is worth learning. Mass: quantity of matter; unit kilogram; measured with a balance; same everywhere; has no direction. Weight: gravitational force; unit newton; measured with a spring balance; changes with g; acts downwards, towards the centre of the planet.

A class investigation shows the relationship. Hang masses of 100, 200, 300, 400 and 500 grams on a spring balance and record the reading for each. The readings are about 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 newtons, and a graph of weight against mass in kilograms is a straight line through the origin whose slope is close to 9,8 newtons per kilogram, which is the value of g.

The questions for this section are ready: distinguishing mass from weight, their units and instruments, and calculating weight from mass.

## Subtopic: Falling, Free Fall and Weightlessness

When an object is dropped, gravity pulls it downwards and it speeds up as it falls. Without air resistance, all objects speed up at exactly the same rate, whatever their mass. A heavy object has a larger weight, but it also has more mass to set moving, and the two effects cancel exactly. This was famously demonstrated on the Moon in 1971, when the Apollo 15 astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a falcon feather at the same moment; with no air, they hit the ground together.

On Earth, air resistance makes light, wide objects fall more slowly. A sheet of paper flutters down, while the same sheet crumpled into a ball falls quickly, because crumpling reduces air resistance without changing the weight. A coin and a feather in a tube with the air pumped out fall side by side.

An object falling with only gravity acting on it is in free fall. Astronauts in the International Space Station, about 400 kilometres above the Earth, are not beyond the reach of gravity; the Earth's pull there is still about 90 percent of its value at the surface. They float because they and the station are in free fall together, continuously falling around the Earth while moving sideways so fast, about 28 000 kilometres per hour, that they keep missing it. With nothing pushing up on them, they feel weightless, an effect called apparent weightlessness. The same feeling occurs briefly when a lift accelerates sharply downwards or on a roller coaster as it crests a hill.

Engineers and doctors also deal with the effects of long periods in free fall. Without the constant load of weight on the body, astronauts' muscles and bones weaken, so they exercise for about two hours a day on special machines. Their body fluids shift towards the head, giving them puffy faces. Back on Earth, they need time to readjust to their full weight.

The questions for this section are waiting: why objects fall together without air, the effect of air resistance and why astronauts float.

## Subtopic: Mass and Weight Problems and the Error Museum

Problems often give values on different worlds. A rover has a mass of 900 kilograms. On Earth its weight is 900 × 9,8 = 8820 newtons; on Mars, 900 × 3,7 = 3330 newtons; its mass is 900 kilograms on both. A rock that weighs 16 newtons on the Moon has a mass of 16 ÷ 1,6 = 10 kilograms, so on Earth it weighs 10 × 9,8 = 98 newtons.

Instruments matter. A beam or electronic balance calibrated for mass compares masses and would give the same reading on the Moon if it were a true balance with standard masses. A spring balance measures force, so the same spring balance stretches less on the Moon.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: mass and weight are the same thing; mass is matter in kilograms, weight is a force in newtons. Two: astronauts float because there is no gravity in space; gravity at the space station is still about 90 percent of that at the surface, and they are in free fall. Three: heavier objects always fall faster; without air, all objects fall at the same rate. Four: your mass is smaller on the Moon; your weight is smaller, your mass is the same. Five: weight is measured in kilograms; it is measured in newtons.

Layout for a weight calculation: write the formula W = m × g; substitute the mass in kilograms and the value of g with units; calculate; and give the answer in newtons. When converting grams, divide by 1000 first: 500 grams is 0,5 kilograms.

The questions for this section are with you now: weight on different worlds, finding mass from weight and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through the invisible pull, kilograms and newtons, and falling together — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Invisible Pull

Some forces work without touching. They reach across empty space. Gravity is the biggest of these.

Everything with mass pulls on everything else with mass. You pull on your desk, your desk pulls on you. But those pulls are tiny, far too small to feel. Gravity only gets strong when something is enormous, like a planet.

The Earth's pull keeps your feet on the ground, makes a dropped cup fall, makes rivers flow downhill and stops the air from floating off into space. The Earth's pull keeps the Moon going round us, and the Sun's pull keeps the Earth going round the Sun. The Moon's pull even lifts the sea a little, giving us tides at the beach.

Two rules: bigger masses pull harder, and things further apart pull more weakly.

And gravity only ever pulls. It never pushes.

Picture arrows all over the Earth pointing to its centre. That is the Earth's gravity field. Wherever you stand, on any side of the planet, down means towards the middle, which is why people in Australia do not fall off.

Keep the invisible pull in mind and try the questions for this part: what gravity does and what makes it stronger.

## Subtopic: Kilograms and Newtons

Here are two words people mix up all the time: mass and weight.

Mass is how much stuff you are made of. It is measured in kilograms. It never changes when you travel. A 60 kilogram learner is 60 kilograms in Durban, on the Moon or floating in space.

Weight is how hard a planet pulls on you. It is a force, so it is measured in newtons. It changes depending on where you are.

On Earth, every kilogram is pulled with about 9,8 newtons, nearly 10. So multiply your mass by 9,8 to get your weight. A 60 kilogram learner weighs about 588 newtons. A 2 kilogram bag of maize meal weighs about 19,6 newtons.

On the Moon, gravity is about one sixth as strong, so each kilogram is pulled with only about 1,6 newtons. The same 60 kilogram learner weighs about 96 newtons there. Same body, same mass, much lighter weight. You could jump about six times higher.

So when someone says I weigh 60 kilograms, a scientist hears: my mass is 60 kilograms.

Try it yourself with a spring balance. Hang a 100 gram mass: about 1 newton. Two of them: about 2 newtons. Five: about 5 newtons. Each 100 grams adds about one newton, because each kilogram adds about 9,8.

Hold kilograms and newtons in mind and try this part's questions: which is which, and multiplying by 9,8.

## Subtopic: Falling Together

Drop a heavy book and a light pencil at the same time from the same height. They land together. Surprised? Without much air in the way, everything falls at the same rate.

So why does a feather float down slowly? Air resistance. The feather is light and wide, so the air holds it back. On the Moon, where there is no air, an astronaut once dropped a hammer and a feather together, and they hit the ground at the same moment.

Try it with paper: a flat sheet drifts down, but crumple it into a ball and it drops fast. Same weight, less air resistance.

And floating astronauts? There is still plenty of gravity up at the space station. The astronauts float because they and their station are falling together around the Earth, moving sideways so fast that they never hit it. Nothing pushes up on them, so they feel weightless.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Gravity is a field force; every mass attracts every other mass, more strongly for bigger masses and weaker with distance. Mass is the amount of matter, in kilograms, and never changes. Weight is the pull of gravity, in newtons: weight equals mass times 9,8 on Earth and times 1,6 on the Moon. Without air, everything falls at the same rate. Astronauts float because they are in free fall. Grade 10 to 12 Physical Sciences builds on gravitation.

Living in free fall is strange. Astronauts' muscles and bones get weaker with no weight to carry, so they exercise every day on special machines. When they come home, it takes a while to get used to being heavy again.

Hold falling together in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: the hammer and the feather, crumpled paper and floating astronauts.
