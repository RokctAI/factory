### subtopic_1 — Friction
**Assistant:** Why is it harder to start pushing a heavy box than to keep it sliding?

**Tutor:** Because friction is usually larger before sliding starts than while it is happening. When the box is resting on the floor, its surface and the floor's surface have had time to settle into each other: the tiny bumps sit deep in each other's hollows, and the particles at the points of contact are close together and attract each other strongly. To get the box moving, you must push hard enough to break all those contacts at once. This larger, starting friction is called static friction. Once the box is sliding, the bumps do not have time to settle so deeply, so the friction is a bit smaller; this is called kinetic or sliding friction. That is why you often have to give a heavy box, a stuck drawer or a stalled car a big shove to get it going, and then it becomes easier to keep moving. Static friction also adjusts to what is needed: if you push gently on a heavy fridge, friction pushes back with exactly the same force and nothing moves, and only when your push exceeds the maximum static friction does it start to slide. The same principle matters for car brakes. A rolling tyre grips the road with static friction, which is larger, so brakes stop a car fastest if the wheels keep turning just short of skidding. Anti-lock braking systems, ABS, are designed to prevent the wheels from locking and sliding.

### subtopic_2 — Friction: Useful and a Nuisance
**Assistant:** How does water on the road make a car skid?

**Tutor:** Water acts as a lubricant between the tyre and the road, reducing friction. On a dry road, the rubber of the tyre presses directly against the rough surface of the tar, and the friction is large. When the road is wet, a thin film of water separates parts of the tyre from the road, so less rubber touches the tar and the grip falls, often by a third or more. The first rain after a long dry spell is especially dangerous, because oil and rubber dust that have built up on the road mix with the water to form a slippery film. If a car drives fast through standing water, the tyre may not be able to push the water out of the way quickly enough, and it rides up on a layer of water, losing contact with the road almost completely. This is called aquaplaning, and the driver suddenly cannot steer or brake. Tyre treads are designed to prevent this: the grooves give the water channels to escape through, so the rubber blocks between them can still touch the road. That is why South African law requires a minimum tread depth of 1 millimetre across the tyre, and why many experts advise replacing tyres well before that. Drivers should slow down in the rain and leave a longer following distance, because stopping distances on a wet road can be much longer than on a dry one.

### subtopic_3 — Tension and Compression
**Assistant:** How do engineers know if a bridge cable will hold?

**Tutor:** They test the material, calculate the forces and then add a large safety margin. First, samples of the steel wire or cable are tested in a machine that pulls on them with steadily increasing force until they stretch permanently and then break. This shows how much tension each wire can carry. Next, engineers calculate the largest tension each cable will have to carry in use, including the weight of the bridge deck itself, the heaviest possible traffic, strong winds and, in some regions, earthquakes, using the physics you will learn in Grades 10 to 12 and at university. They then design each cable so that it can carry several times that maximum force. This multiple is called the factor of safety, and for important structures such as bridges and lift cables it is often between about two and five or more, depending on the regulations. Cables are made of many thin wires twisted or bundled together, so that if one wire is damaged the others still carry the load. During the life of the bridge, the cables are regularly inspected for corrosion and damage, protected with coatings, and sometimes fitted with sensors that monitor their tension. The same thinking, about tension, compression, materials and safety factors, goes into lifts, cranes, cable cars like the one on Table Mountain, and even the ropes used by rock climbers.

### subtopic_4 — Contact Forces in Structures and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why does a parachute slow you down but not stop you completely?

**Tutor:** Because air resistance only grows until it balances your weight, and then you fall at a steady speed. When a skydiver first jumps, the only large force is weight, so they speed up. As their speed increases, they push through more air every second, and air resistance increases. Eventually, air resistance becomes equal to their weight. The forces on the skydiver are then balanced, so they stop speeding up and fall at a constant speed, called terminal velocity. With the body spread out, terminal velocity is roughly 200 kilometres per hour. When the parachute opens, its huge area suddenly creates far more air resistance than the weight, so the skydiver slows down sharply. As they slow, air resistance decreases, until once again it equals their weight, at a much lower terminal velocity of roughly 15 to 25 kilometres per hour, which is safe for landing. Air resistance can never become larger than the weight at a steady speed, because if it did, the skydiver would slow down and the air resistance would drop again. So a parachute does not stop you; it makes your steady falling speed slow enough that your legs can absorb the landing, much like jumping off a low wall. The same balance of forces explains why a feather falls slowly and why raindrops do not hit you at dangerous speeds.
