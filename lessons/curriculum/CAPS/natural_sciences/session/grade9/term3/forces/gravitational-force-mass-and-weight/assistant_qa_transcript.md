### subtopic_1 — Field Forces and Gravitational Attraction
**Assistant:** If gravity pulls a heavy object harder, why does it not fall faster?

**Tutor:** Because a heavy object also needs a proportionally larger force to get it moving, and the two effects cancel exactly. Gravity pulls on each kilogram with the same force, about 9,8 newtons on Earth. A 10 kilogram rock has ten times the weight of a 1 kilogram rock, so it is pulled ten times as hard. But it also has ten times as much mass, and mass is a measure of how hard it is to change an object's motion, its inertia. Ten times the pull acting on ten times the mass produces exactly the same increase in speed each second. That is why, without air resistance, every object falls with the same acceleration, about 9,8 metres per second faster every second near the Earth's surface. The ancient Greek philosopher Aristotle taught that heavy objects fall faster, and people believed this for nearly two thousand years, partly because everyday experience with feathers and leaves seemed to support it. Galileo Galilei, in the late 1500s and early 1600s, argued with clever thought experiments and with experiments using balls rolling down slopes that the speed of falling does not depend on the weight, and that air resistance explains the differences we see. The Apollo 15 hammer and feather demonstration in 1971 showed it beautifully on the Moon, and in Grade 10 you will measure the acceleration due to gravity yourself.

### subtopic_2 — Mass and Weight
**Assistant:** Is g exactly 9,8 everywhere on Earth?

**Tutor:** No, it varies slightly from place to place, by up to about half a percent, but 9,8 newtons per kilogram is a good average value for calculations. There are several reasons for the variation. The Earth is not a perfect sphere; it bulges slightly at the equator, so a person at the equator is further from the Earth's centre than a person at the poles and feels slightly weaker gravity. The Earth also spins, and at the equator part of the gravitational pull is used to keep you moving in a circle with the spinning Earth, which reduces your apparent weight a little more. Together these give g about 9,78 at the equator and about 9,83 at the poles. Height matters too: on top of a high mountain you are further from the centre, so g is very slightly smaller. Local rocks matter: dense ore bodies beneath the surface increase g very slightly, which is why geologists use sensitive instruments called gravimeters to search for mineral deposits, a method used in South African mining exploration. For school work, you may be told to use 9,8 or, for quick estimates, 10 newtons per kilogram; always use the value given in the question. Note that these variations affect weight, not mass. A balance that compares masses gives the same reading anywhere, while a spring balance would show a tiny difference between Cape Town and the North Pole.

### subtopic_3 — Falling, Free Fall and Weightlessness
**Assistant:** Do bathroom scales measure mass or weight?

**Tutor:** They actually measure a force, your weight, but they display the result as a mass in kilograms. Inside a typical bathroom scale is either a spring or an electronic strain gauge that bends very slightly when you stand on it. The amount of bending depends on how hard you push down, which is your weight in newtons. The manufacturer has calibrated the scale on Earth by assuming g is about 9,8 newtons per kilogram, so it divides the force by 9,8 and shows the answer in kilograms. This works perfectly well on Earth. If you took the same scale to the Moon and stood on it, the spring would bend only about one sixth as much, and the display would show about 10 kilograms for a 60 kilogram person, which would be wrong, because your mass has not changed. A true balance, such as a beam balance that compares your mass with standard masses on the other side, would give the correct mass on the Moon, because gravity pulls on both sides equally and the comparison is unaffected. You can also see the scale measuring force if you press down on it with your hands or push up on a table while standing on it: the reading changes even though your mass has not. And if you stand on a bathroom scale in a lift, the reading increases as the lift starts going up and decreases as it starts coming down.

### subtopic_4 — Mass and Weight Problems and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why does the Moon not fall onto the Earth if gravity pulls it?

**Tutor:** It is falling towards the Earth all the time, but it is also moving sideways so fast that it keeps missing. The Moon travels along its orbit at about 1 kilometre per second. If there were no gravity, it would carry on in a straight line and drift off into space. The Earth's gravitational pull bends its path continually towards the Earth, and the result is a curved path that goes round and round: an orbit. Isaac Newton explained this with a famous thought experiment. Imagine a cannon on a very high mountain firing a ball horizontally. A slow ball curves down and lands nearby. A faster ball lands further away. If the ball were fired fast enough, the ground would curve away beneath it as fast as it falls, and it would never land: it would orbit the Earth. The Moon, the International Space Station and every satellite, including those that provide weather forecasts, satellite television and GPS positions, are doing exactly this. The space station needs about 28 000 kilometres per hour at its height of about 400 kilometres. The further away an orbit is, the weaker the pull and the slower the orbital speed needed. In the same way, the Earth is falling around the Sun once a year. Newton realised that the force making an apple fall and the force keeping the Moon in orbit are the same force, gravity, which was one of the great insights in the history of science.
