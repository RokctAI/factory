### subtopic_1 — Magnets, Magnetic Materials and Poles
**Assistant:** Why are only iron, nickel and cobalt magnetic, and not other metals?

**Tutor:** Because of the way the electrons in their atoms are arranged. Every electron behaves like a tiny magnet. In most atoms, the electrons are paired so that their tiny magnetic effects cancel out, and the atom as a whole is not magnetic. In atoms of iron, nickel and cobalt, several electrons are unpaired, so each atom acts as a small magnet. That alone is not enough, since many atoms have unpaired electrons. What makes these three metals special is that neighbouring atoms interact in a way that makes their tiny magnets line up in the same direction, forming small regions called magnetic domains, each containing billions of aligned atoms. In an unmagnetised iron nail, the domains point in random directions and cancel out. When a magnet comes near, the domains that point along its field grow and others turn, so the nail becomes a temporary magnet and is attracted. In a permanent magnet, the domains stay aligned. Heating a magnet strongly, or hammering it, scrambles the domains and weakens it. Above a certain temperature, called the Curie temperature, about 770 degrees Celsius for iron, the alignment is lost completely. A few other metals and alloys, such as gadolinium and some special alloys, are also magnetic, and modern neodymium magnets combine iron with the rare earth element neodymium and boron to make the strongest permanent magnets available.

### subtopic_2 — Attraction, Repulsion and Magnetic Fields
**Assistant:** Does the Earth's magnetic field ever flip around?

**Tutor:** Yes. Over the Earth's history, the magnetic field has reversed many times, so that the magnetic north and south poles swapped places. Evidence comes from volcanic rocks. When lava cools, tiny grains of magnetic minerals inside it line up with the Earth's field at that moment and are then locked in place as the rock solidifies, preserving a record of the field's direction. Rocks of different ages show the field pointing in opposite directions. On the ocean floor, where new crust forms at mid-ocean ridges and spreads outwards, the rocks show stripes of normal and reversed magnetism that match on both sides of the ridge, which was important evidence for plate tectonics. Reversals happen irregularly, on average every few hundred thousand years, and the last full reversal was about 780 000 years ago. A reversal takes perhaps a thousand to several thousand years, during which the field becomes weaker and more complicated. The field has weakened by several percent over the last two centuries, and the South Atlantic Anomaly over southern Africa is one region where it is especially weak, which scientists at Hermanus and elsewhere monitor closely. However, this does not mean a reversal is about to happen, and there is no evidence that past reversals caused mass extinctions, although a weaker field would let more radiation reach satellites and high-flying aircraft.

### subtopic_3 — The Earth's Magnetic Field
**Assistant:** How can a magnet's force pass through my hand or a table?

**Tutor:** Because magnetic force is a field force, which does not need contact or a medium to travel through, and most materials, including your hand, wood, plastic and glass, do not block it. The magnetic field extends through the space around the magnet, and it passes through non-magnetic materials almost unchanged, because they do not contain aligned domains that would distort it. That is why you can move a paper clip on top of a desk with a magnet underneath, and why fridge magnets hold paper notes to the door. Magnetic fields even pass through the human body, which is how MRI scanners in hospitals work: an extremely strong magnetic field passes harmlessly through the patient, and radio signals from atoms in the body are used to build detailed images of organs and soft tissues. The force does get weaker with distance, so a thick table reduces the effect simply by increasing the gap, not by blocking it. Magnetic materials behave differently. A sheet of iron placed between a magnet and a paper clip draws the field lines into itself and can shield the clip from much of the force. Engineers use this idea to protect sensitive electronic equipment with casings of special magnetic alloys. Field forces like this one, and gravity, which also passes through everything, puzzled scientists for centuries, and the idea of a field was developed in the 1800s to explain them.

### subtopic_4 — Uses of Magnets and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why does the compass in my phone sometimes need calibrating?

**Tutor:** Because the phone's tiny magnetic sensor is easily confused by other magnetic fields near it, and calibrating teaches it to subtract them. A phone does not contain a needle; it has an electronic magnetometer, a chip that measures the strength and direction of the magnetic field in three directions. The Earth's field at the surface is quite weak, roughly a hundred times weaker than a fridge magnet's field close up. The phone itself contains magnets and magnetic materials, in its speaker, its vibration motor and the steel parts of its case, and nearby objects such as a steel desk, a car body, a laptop or a magnetic phone cover add their own fields. These can be much stronger than the Earth's field at the sensor and can shift the direction the compass shows. When the phone asks you to move it in a figure of eight, it is measuring the field in many orientations. Fields from the phone's own parts move with the phone, while the Earth's field stays fixed in space, so the software can separate them and correct the reading. It is a good habit to calibrate the compass away from large steel objects. A traditional needle compass suffers from the same problem: hold it near a steel fence or a car and it will point wrongly. Navigators on steel ships correct their compasses for the ship's own magnetism.
