# Part 1 — Expert

This session differentiates plant cells from animal cells by the three structures found only in plant cells: the cell wall, the large permanent vacuole and the chloroplast. The anchor specimens are the onion epidermis cell stained with iodine and the cheek cell stained with methylene blue, the two slides of the Grade 9 practical. The governing principle is that plants are stationary and make their own food, so their cells carry structures for support, water storage and photosynthesis that an animal cell, which moves and eats, does not need.

## Subtopic: The Cell Wall

The cell wall is a rigid outer layer that surrounds the cell membrane of every plant cell. It is made of cellulose, a tough carbohydrate built from long chains of glucose molecules, the same material that gives paper and cotton their strength. The wall lies outside the membrane, so a plant cell has both: the cell wall outermost, the cell membrane just inside it, pressed against the wall by the contents of the cell.

The function of the wall is support and shape. A plant has no skeleton, so each cell contributes its own rigidity, and millions of boxed cells glued together by their walls hold a leaf flat and a stem upright. Under the microscope this is why onion cells appear as neat rectangular bricks in rows, while cheek cells, which have only a flexible membrane, are irregular and rounded. The wall also protects the cell from bursting when it takes in water, which becomes important in the next section.

The wall is fully permeable. Unlike the selectively permeable membrane, the cellulose wall has gaps through which water and dissolved substances pass freely, so it does not control what enters the cell; the membrane just inside it still does that job. Learners who write that the cell wall controls what enters and leaves a plant cell have given the membrane's function to the wall, and that is a common lost mark. Wall: support, shape, protection, fully permeable. Membrane: control of entry and exit, selectively permeable.

Animal cells have no cell wall at all. The cheek cell is bounded only by its membrane, which is why it can change shape, why white blood cells can squeeze between other cells, and why an animal cell placed in pure water swells and bursts while a plant cell merely becomes firm. The absence of a wall is as important a fact as its presence, and the comparison table marks both columns.

The questions for this section are with you now: cellulose, support and shape, fully permeable against selectively permeable, and the brick-like appearance of onion cells.

## Subtopic: The Large Permanent Vacuole

A vacuole is a fluid-filled space in the cytoplasm surrounded by a membrane. Animal cells may have several small, temporary vacuoles that store food or water briefly. A mature plant cell has one large permanent vacuole that fills most of the cell, often 80 percent or more of its volume, and pushes the cytoplasm and the nucleus outward into a thin layer against the wall. That is why the nucleus of an onion cell is seen near the edge of the cell rather than in the centre.

The vacuole is filled with cell sap, a solution of water with dissolved sugars, salts, pigments and waste products. It has two functions. It stores these substances, and it keeps the cell firm. When the vacuole is full of water it presses the cytoplasm against the cell wall and the cell becomes tight and rigid, a state called turgid. A plant whose cells are all turgid stands upright with its leaves spread flat to the light.

When a plant loses water faster than its roots can replace it, the vacuoles shrink, the cytoplasm pulls away from the wall and the cells go soft, a state called flaccid. Millions of flaccid cells can no longer support the leaf, and the plant wilts. A lettuce left in the sun droops for exactly this reason, and it stiffens again in a bowl of water because the vacuoles refill. The wall prevents the cell bursting when it refills; the vacuole provides the pressure that gives support. The two structures work together.

The pigments dissolved in cell sap give many flowers and fruits their colour: the red of a beetroot and the purple of a red cabbage are vacuole pigments, which is why red cabbage water works as a natural pH indicator in Term 2. The comparison for marking: animal cells have small temporary vacuoles or none, plant cells have one large permanent vacuole filled with cell sap that stores substances and supports the cell.

Take this section's questions now: cell sap, turgid and flaccid, the wilting lettuce, and the position of the plant cell nucleus.

## Subtopic: Chloroplasts and Photosynthesis

Chloroplasts are the green organelles of plant cells. They are oval bodies, larger than mitochondria at about 5 μm long, and they contain the green pigment chlorophyll, which absorbs the energy of sunlight. Chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light strongly and reflects green, which is why leaves look green to us. Chloroplasts are the site of photosynthesis, the process by which a plant makes its own food.

The word equation for photosynthesis is carbon dioxide plus water gives glucose plus oxygen, with sunlight as the energy source and chlorophyll as the pigment that captures it. Carbon dioxide enters the leaf from the air through pores, water arrives from the roots, and the glucose made is used by the plant for energy or stored as starch. The oxygen released is the oxygen every animal breathes. Photosynthesis is the reverse of the respiration equation from last lesson, and the pair will be developed into a full topic in Grade 11 Life Sciences.

Chloroplasts are found only in the green parts of a plant. A leaf cell is packed with them; a root cell, which lives in the dark, has none; and the onion epidermis used in the practical has none either, because it is the inner, white, underground part of the plant. This is the single most important trap in the topic: the onion cell on the slide is a plant cell, with a wall and a large vacuole, but it shows no chloroplasts, and a learner who draws green dots in an onion cell has invented a structure that is not there. A moss leaf or a thin slice of pondweed is used when chloroplasts must be seen.

No animal cell contains chloroplasts, because animals obtain their food by eating. Both plant and animal cells, however, contain mitochondria, because every living cell needs to release energy from glucose. Plant cells therefore carry out both processes: photosynthesis in the chloroplasts by day, making glucose, and respiration in the mitochondria at all times, releasing energy from it. The statement that plants photosynthesise instead of respiring is false and is tested.

This section's questions are available now: chlorophyll and the colour of leaves, the word equation, onion cells without chloroplasts, and mitochondria in both cell types.

## Subtopic: The Comparison Table and the Error Museum

The examiner's preferred answer format for this topic is a table with two columns and a clear row for each feature. Shared structures: cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus and mitochondria appear in both. Plant-only structures: cell wall of cellulose, one large permanent vacuole with cell sap, chloroplasts in green parts. Shape: plant cells are regular and box-like because of the wall; animal cells are irregular and rounded. Nucleus position: at the edge of a plant cell, pushed there by the vacuole; near the centre of an animal cell. Vacuoles: large and permanent in plants; small and temporary or absent in animals.

Set the table out with the feature in the first column, plant cell in the second and animal cell in the third, and write a word in every box. A blank box earns nothing; the word none or absent in the animal column for cell wall is worth a mark. For a question that asks you to identify a cell from a drawing, look for the wall and the vacuole first, because the chloroplasts may be absent even in a plant cell.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: the cell wall controls what enters the cell, when that is the membrane's job and the wall is fully permeable. Two: plant cells have no mitochondria, when every living cell respires. Three: all plant cells have chloroplasts, when roots and onion bulbs have none. Four: animal cells have no cell membrane, when the membrane is the one boundary every cell has and it is the wall that animals lack. Five: drawing a plant cell as a circle, when the wall makes it a rectangle or hexagon.

Why the comparison matters beyond the table: it explains the lives of the organisms. A plant cannot run from drought or hunt for food, so its cells are built for standing still: a wall for support, a vacuole for water storage and pressure, chloroplasts for making food on the spot. An animal moves and eats, so its cells stay flexible and carry no food-making machinery. In Grade 10 Life Sciences the same logic is extended to tissues: xylem and phloem in plants, muscle and nerve in animals, each built from cells specialised in the ways this lesson has begun to describe.

The questions for this section are ready: the three-column table, identifying a cell from its drawing, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same three differences again, through a brick house and a tent, a water balloon, and a set of green solar panels — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Brick House and a Tent

Think of a plant cell as a small brick house and an animal cell as a tent. Both have a door that controls who comes in, which is the cell membrane. Both have a floor where the living happens, the cytoplasm, and an office with the instructions, the nucleus, and a generator, the mitochondria. Those four are the same in both. The difference is what is around the outside.

The brick house has brick walls. That is the cell wall, made of cellulose, the stuff of paper and cotton, strong and stiff. The walls hold the house up and give it its square shape, and when thousands of houses are glued together in rows they hold up a whole leaf or stem. Look at onion cells under the microscope and you see exactly that: rectangular bricks in neat rows. The tent, the animal cell, has only its canvas, the membrane. It is flexible, it changes shape, and it is round or irregular. Cheek cells on a slide look like squashed fried eggs for this reason.

Two things to get straight about the bricks. First, the plant cell has the wall AND the membrane; the wall is outside, the membrane is just inside it, like the brick wall and the inner door. Second, the brick wall is full of gaps; water and dissolved things go straight through it. It does not choose who comes in. The door chooses. In test words: the cell wall is fully permeable and gives support and shape; the cell membrane is selectively permeable and controls what enters and leaves.

And the tent has no bricks at all. If you are asked whether an animal cell has a cell wall, the answer is a confident no, and writing none in that box of the table is a mark.

Keep the house and the tent in mind and try the questions for this part: what the bricks are made of, what they do, and what they do not do.

## Subtopic: The Water Balloon Inside

Inside the brick house there is a big water balloon that fills most of the room. That is the large permanent vacuole, and the water in it, with sugar and salts and sometimes colour dissolved in it, is called cell sap. The balloon is so big that it squashes everything else, the cytoplasm and the nucleus, out to the edges against the walls. That is why the nucleus of an onion cell sits at the side of the cell, not in the middle like a cheek cell's nucleus does.

When the balloon is full, it presses hard on the walls and the whole house is stiff and firm. The science word is turgid. A plant made of millions of turgid cells stands up straight and holds its leaves out flat. Now let the plant dry out. The balloons lose water and shrink, they stop pressing on the walls, and each cell goes soft; the word is flaccid. Millions of soft cells cannot hold a leaf up, and the plant wilts. You have seen it: a lettuce left out on the counter goes limp, and if you put it in a bowl of cold water for half an hour it crisps up again as the balloons refill.

The balloon and the bricks work as a team. The balloon pushes out to make the cell firm; the bricks stop the cell from bursting when the balloon is full. An animal cell has no bricks, so it has no big balloon either, only a few small temporary ones, because a big balloon in a tent would burst it. In the table: plant cell, one large permanent vacuole with cell sap, for storage and support; animal cell, small temporary vacuoles or none.

A bonus you can taste. The red of beetroot and the purple of red cabbage are colours dissolved in the cell sap of their vacuoles, and in Term 2 you will use red cabbage water to test acids and bases.

Hold the water balloon and try this part's questions: cell sap, turgid and flaccid, the limp lettuce, and where the nucleus sits.

## Subtopic: The Green Solar Panels

The last difference is the one everyone remembers: plant cells can be green. On the roof of the brick house sit green solar panels, the chloroplasts. They are filled with a green chemical called chlorophyll that soaks up sunlight, and they use that energy to make food. The recipe is carbon dioxide from the air plus water from the roots gives glucose plus oxygen, and it only runs when the sun shines. That is photosynthesis. The glucose feeds the plant, and the oxygen that comes off is the oxygen you are breathing right now.

Two traps. First trap: not every room in the plant has solar panels. Only the green parts do, the leaves and green stems. Roots live in the dark, so they have none. An onion bulb grows underground and is white, so onion cells have no chloroplasts, even though they are plant cells with walls and big vacuoles. If you draw green dots in your onion cell, you have drawn something that is not there. Second trap: the solar panels do not replace the generator. Plant cells still have mitochondria, because every living cell has to release energy from glucose, day and night. Plants photosynthesise AND respire; animals only respire.

Animal cells have no solar panels because animals get their food by eating. That is really the whole story of the three differences. A plant stands still in one place for its whole life, so its cells have bricks to hold it up, a water balloon to keep it firm and store water, and solar panels to make food on the spot. An animal walks around and eats, so its cells stay flexible and skip all three.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Same in both: membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, mitochondria. Plant only: cell wall of cellulose for support and shape, fully permeable; one large permanent vacuole of cell sap for storage and firmness; chloroplasts with chlorophyll for photosynthesis, in green parts only. Plant cells are box-shaped with the nucleus at the edge; animal cells are rounded with the nucleus in the middle. Set it out as a three-column table and fill every box.

Hold the bricks, the balloon and the solar panels, and take the final questions of the lesson: the three plant-only structures, the onion trap, and the table.
