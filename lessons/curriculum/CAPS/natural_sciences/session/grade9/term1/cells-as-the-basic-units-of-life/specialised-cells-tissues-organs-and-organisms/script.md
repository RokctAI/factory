# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers two linked ideas: that cells are adapted to specific functions, and that in a multicellular organism cells are organised into tissues, organs and systems. The anchor examples are the red blood cell, the muscle cell, the nerve cell and the root hair cell for specialisation, and the chain muscle cell, cardiac muscle tissue, heart, circulatory system, human being for organisation. The governing principle is division of labour: when a cell does one job it can be shaped to do that job well, and a body of specialised cells must then be organised so the jobs are coordinated.

## Subtopic: Unicellular and Multicellular Organisms

A unicellular organism consists of a single cell, and that one cell carries out every life process: it takes in food, releases energy, gets rid of waste, responds to its surroundings, moves and reproduces. Bacteria are unicellular, and so are yeast, Amoeba and Paramecium, the protists found in pond water. An Amoeba is a shapeless blob of cytoplasm up to half a millimetre across that flows around its food and engulfs it. A Paramecium is slipper-shaped and covered in tiny hairs called cilia that beat to drive it along. Each is a complete organism in one cell.

A multicellular organism is made of many cells, from a few hundred in the simplest animals to roughly 30 trillion in an adult human. The cells are not all alike. In a multicellular body there is division of labour: different cells take on different jobs, and each becomes specialised for its job. A red blood cell carries oxygen and nothing else; a muscle cell contracts and nothing else. Neither could survive alone, because each depends on the others for the tasks it has given up. The reward is that each job is done far better than a single all-purpose cell could do it.

Why can an organism not simply be one enormous cell? Because a cell depends on its surface. Everything the cell needs enters through its membrane, and everything it discards leaves through the membrane. As a cell grows larger, its volume, which demands supplies, increases faster than its surface area, which delivers them, and beyond a certain size the surface cannot keep up. Large organisms therefore must be built of many small cells, each with enough membrane for its own needs. This surface-area-to-volume argument returns in the lungs and the small intestine later this term.

Multicellular organisms gain in another way: cells can be replaced. A unicellular organism that is damaged dies. In your body, skin cells, blood cells and the lining of the gut are being replaced continually, and a wound heals because new cells are produced. The cost of multicellularity is coordination; a body of trillions of specialised cells must be organised so that they work together, and that organisation is the subject of the second half of this part.

The questions for this section are with you now: unicellular against multicellular, division of labour, and why cells stay small.

## Subtopic: Cells Adapted to Their Functions

A specialised cell has a structure that matches its function, and the examiner tests the match. The red blood cell is a flattened disc, dented on both sides, about 7,5 μm across. It has no nucleus, so almost its whole volume is available for haemoglobin, the red protein that binds oxygen; its dented shape gives a large surface area for oxygen to diffuse across and lets it bend through the narrowest capillaries. It lives about 120 days and is replaced from the bone marrow.

The muscle cell is long and thin, packed with protein fibres that can slide past one another to shorten the cell, and crowded with mitochondria to release the energy that contraction demands. Many muscle cells lying side by side and contracting together move a bone. The nerve cell has a cell body with branching extensions and a single long fibre, the axon, that can be over a metre long in the nerve running from your spine to your foot; its length lets it carry electrical messages quickly across the body without handing them from cell to cell. The sperm cell has a tail for swimming and a collar of mitochondria behind the head to power it, and the egg cell is large because it stores food for the early embryo.

Plant cells specialise too. The root hair cell has a long thin extension that pushes between soil particles, greatly increasing the surface area through which water and minerals are absorbed. The palisade cell near the upper surface of a leaf is tall and packed with chloroplasts to catch the most light. Guard cells in pairs open and close the pores of the leaf. Xylem cells are hollow and have lost their contents to form continuous pipes that carry water up the stem.

The pattern in every case is the same, and the answer format follows it: name the feature, then state the function it serves, joined by which allows or so that. The red blood cell has no nucleus, which allows more room for haemoglobin. The root hair cell has a long extension, which increases the surface area for absorption. The muscle cell has many mitochondria, so that enough energy is released for contraction. Two clauses, two marks.

Take this section's questions now: four animal cells and two plant cells, each feature matched to its function.

## Subtopic: Cells, Tissues, Organs and Systems

Specialised cells of the same kind, grouped together and doing the same job, form a tissue. Muscle cells together form muscle tissue; nerve cells form nervous tissue; the flat cells lining your cheek form epithelial tissue; bone cells and the hard material around them form bone tissue. A tissue is defined by the sameness of its cells and the shared function. In plants, xylem is a tissue that carries water, and phloem is a tissue that carries dissolved sugar.

Different tissues working together form an organ. The heart is an organ made of cardiac muscle tissue that contracts, nervous tissue that times the contraction, connective tissue that holds it together and epithelial tissue that lines its chambers. The stomach, the lungs, the kidneys, the brain and the skin are organs. In plants the leaf is an organ, built from epidermal tissue, palisade tissue, xylem and phloem. An organ is not simply a big tissue; it is several different tissues combined to do a job none could do alone.

Organs that work together to perform a major body function form a system. The heart and the blood vessels form the circulatory system; the mouth, stomach and intestines the digestive system; the lungs and airways the respiratory system; the kidneys and bladder the excretory system; the brain, spinal cord and nerves the nervous system; the bones and muscles the musculoskeletal system. The systems together, each depending on the others, make up the organism.

The ladder, written in order, is cell, tissue, organ, system, organism, and it is read in both directions in tests. Upward: a muscle cell is part of cardiac muscle tissue, which is part of the heart, which is part of the circulatory system, which is part of the human being. Downward: the human being has a digestive system containing the organ called the stomach, whose wall contains muscle tissue made of muscle cells. Every body-system lesson this term, from the musculoskeletal system next week to the digestive system in week nine, is an application of this ladder.

This section's questions are available now: the definitions of tissue, organ and system, the heart as an organ, and the ladder in both directions.

## Subtopic: Stem Cells and the Error Museum

Not every cell in the body is specialised. A stem cell is an unspecialised cell that can divide repeatedly and whose offspring can develop into specialised cells of different kinds. The cells of a very early embryo are stem cells that can become any cell type at all, which is how a single fertilised egg produces trillions of cells of over 200 kinds. In an adult, stem cells remain in a few places: in the bone marrow, where they produce red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets throughout life; in the skin; and in the lining of the gut. Blood from the umbilical cord at birth is rich in stem cells and is sometimes stored.

Stem cells matter medically because they can replace cells that are lost. A bone marrow transplant gives a patient with leukaemia a new supply of blood-forming stem cells from a matched donor, and the South African Bone Marrow Registry exists to find those matches. CAPS requires only this much: what a stem cell is, that it is unspecialised, that it can become specialised cells, and that it is found in embryos and bone marrow. The mechanisms and the ethics belong to later grades.

One idea worth stating precisely now because it prevents a persistent misconception: every specialised cell of your body still contains the complete set of 46 chromosomes. A muscle cell and a nerve cell have identical DNA. They differ because different genes are switched on in each, not because their DNA has changed. Specialisation is a matter of which instructions are used, not which are present.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: an organ is a large tissue; in fact an organ is several different tissues combined. Two: a tissue is a group of different cells; a tissue is a group of similar cells with a shared function. Three: the red blood cell has a nucleus like other cells; the mature human red blood cell has none. Four: specialised cells have lost DNA; they have the same DNA with different genes active. Five: a unicellular organism is a cell of a larger body; it is a complete independent organism.

Layout for a specialisation question: feature, then function, joined by which allows. Layout for an organisation question: name the level, give the definition, give the example. A tissue is a group of similar cells performing the same function, for example muscle tissue. Five levels, five definitions, five examples, and the full marks follow.

The questions for this section are ready: stem cells in embryos and bone marrow, same DNA with different genes active, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through a one-person spaza shop and a big company, a toolbox, and a house built from bricks — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A One-Person Shop and a Big Company

Imagine a spaza shop run by one person. She orders the stock, serves the customers, sweeps the floor, counts the money and locks up at night. One person, every job. That is a unicellular organism: a bacterium, a yeast cell, an Amoeba in a pond. One cell that eats, moves, gets rid of waste and makes copies of itself, with nobody to help.

Now imagine a big supermarket chain. There is a person who only drives the delivery truck, a person who only works the till, a person who only does the accounts, a security guard who only watches the door. Nobody does everything, and nobody could run the whole business alone, but because each person does one job all day they are very good at it. That is a multicellular organism, and the big-company idea is called division of labour. Your body has about 30 trillion workers, and almost every one has a single job.

Why not just one giant cell instead of trillions of small ones? Because a cell lives through its skin. Food and oxygen come in through the membrane, waste goes out through it, and if you make the cell bigger the inside grows faster than the outside. A giant cell would starve in the middle. So big bodies are built from many small cells, each with enough membrane to feed itself. Keep that idea; it comes back when we look at the lungs and the gut, which are folded up to give more surface.

One more advantage of the company: if one worker leaves, you hire another. Your skin cells, your blood cells and the lining of your stomach are being replaced all the time, and a cut heals because new cells grow. A one-person shop that gets sick simply closes.

Keep the shop and the company in mind and try the questions for this part: one cell doing everything, many cells sharing the jobs, and why cells stay small.

## Subtopic: Shaped for the Job

Open a toolbox. A hammer, a screwdriver and a saw are all tools, but each is shaped for its own job, and you can guess the job from the shape. Specialised cells are the same, and in a test you look at the shape and say what it is for.

The red blood cell is a flat disc dented in the middle, like a sweet that has been sucked thin. No nucleus, so the whole inside is packed with haemoglobin, the red stuff that grabs oxygen. Flat and dented means more surface for oxygen to cross, and bendy enough to squeeze through the thinnest blood vessels. The muscle cell is long and thin like a rubber band, full of fibres that slide to shorten it, and crammed with mitochondria, the generators, because pulling takes energy. The nerve cell is a wire: a body with branches and one long cable, up to a metre long, so a message runs from your spine to your toe without being passed along.

Plants have their tools too. The root hair cell is a cell with a long finger sticking out into the soil, like a sponge with a long tip, to touch more soil and soak up more water. The palisade cell in a leaf is tall and stuffed with chloroplasts, standing upright near the top of the leaf like a solar panel on a pole, to catch the most light.

The way to write it is always the same sentence: this cell has this feature, which allows it to do this job. Red blood cell, no nucleus, which allows more room for haemoglobin. Root hair cell, long extension, which gives more surface area to absorb water. Feature, then job. Say the feature without the job and you earn half; say both and you earn all of it.

Hold the toolbox and try this part's questions: red blood cell, muscle cell, nerve cell, root hair cell, and the feature-then-job sentence.

## Subtopic: From Brick to Building

Now the organising. Think of a house. One brick is a cell. Lots of the same bricks laid together make a wall; that is a tissue, a group of the same kind of cells doing the same job, like muscle tissue or nervous tissue. Different parts, walls plus a floor plus a roof plus a door, make a room; that is an organ, several different tissues working together, like the heart or the stomach. Several rooms make a house; that is a system, like the circulatory system or the digestive system. And a whole street of houses is the organism, you.

Cell, tissue, organ, system, organism. Say it both ways. Up: a muscle cell is part of heart muscle tissue, which is part of the heart, which is part of the circulatory system, which is part of you. Down: you have a digestive system, which includes the stomach, whose wall is made of muscle tissue, which is made of muscle cells. Every lesson this term, the skeleton, the kidneys, the lungs, the gut, is just one house on this street.

Two bricks that trip people up. An organ is not a big tissue; it is different tissues put together. And a tissue is not a mix of different cells; it is the same cells doing the same job.

Last, the blank bricks. A stem cell is a brick that has not decided what it is yet. It can divide again and again, and its new cells can become blood cells or skin cells or muscle cells. The cells of a very young embryo are all like this, and adults keep some in the bone marrow to make new blood for life. That is why a bone marrow transplant can save someone with leukaemia, and why a registry in South Africa looks for matching donors. And here is the surprising bit: every brick in your body, blank or specialised, carries the same full set of 46 chromosomes. A nerve cell and a muscle cell have identical DNA; they just use different pages of it.

Pocket summary of the lesson. One cell doing everything is unicellular; many cells sharing jobs is multicellular, with division of labour. Each specialised cell has a feature that fits its job, and you write the feature then the job. Cell, tissue, organ, system, organism, like brick, wall, room, house, street. Stem cells are blank bricks that can become any of the others. In Grade 10 Life Sciences each of these levels gets its own chapter.

Hold the brick, the wall, the room and the street, and take the final questions of the lesson: the five levels in order, an example at each, and what a stem cell is.
