### subtopic_1 — The Cell Theory and the Microscope
**Assistant:** Why do we multiply the two lens magnifications instead of adding them?

**Tutor:** Because each lens enlarges the image the previous lens has already made, and enlarging an enlargement compounds. The objective lens, the one close to the slide, forms a first image of the cheek cell that is, say, 40 times larger than the cell. The eyepiece then takes that enlarged image as its object and magnifies it a further 10 times, so the final image is 10 times 40, 400 times the original. If the lenses added, a 10 times eyepiece and a 40 times objective would give only 50 times, and a 60 micrometre cell would appear 3 millimetres wide, barely visible; at 400 times it appears about 24 millimetres wide, the size of a thumbnail, which is what you actually see. The multiplication also explains why switching the objective from 4 to 40 makes such a dramatic jump: the total goes from 40 to 400, a ten-fold leap, and the field of view shrinks by the same factor, which is why the cell you had centred at low power vanishes if it was slightly off-centre. Write the calculation out in the practical, eyepiece times objective equals total, with the times sign on the answer, and the method mark and the answer mark both follow.

### subtopic_2 — The Cell Membrane and the Cytoplasm
**Assistant:** If the membrane is selectively permeable, how does the cell decide what is allowed through?

**Tutor:** The cell does not decide in the sense of choosing; the membrane's structure does the selecting automatically. Small molecules such as oxygen, carbon dioxide and water pass through the membrane by diffusion, moving from where they are more concentrated to where they are less concentrated, with no energy spent by the cell. Oxygen is high in the blood and low inside a respiring cell, so it moves in; carbon dioxide is produced inside, so it is high inside and moves out. Larger or charged particles cannot slip through the fatty layer of the membrane on their own, and they cross only through specific protein channels and carriers embedded in the membrane, each shaped to admit a particular substance, glucose through a glucose carrier and so on. Substances with no matching channel are simply excluded. So the selection is a matter of size, charge and whether a matching protein door exists, which is what the phrase selectively permeable summarises. Grade 9 needs the phrase, the diffusion of oxygen and carbon dioxide, and the idea that the membrane controls what enters and leaves; the detailed membrane model, with its double layer of phospholipids and its proteins, is built in Grade 10 Life Sciences.

### subtopic_3 — The Nucleus and DNA
**Assistant:** Why does the nucleus control the cell if the work happens out in the cytoplasm?

**Tutor:** Because control is about instructions, not about doing the work yourself. Everything a cell does is carried out by proteins: proteins build the structures, proteins act as enzymes that run the chemical reactions, proteins carry substances across the membrane. The instructions for making every one of those proteins are written in the DNA, and the DNA is kept inside the nucleus in the chromosomes, 46 of them in a human body cell. The nucleus sends copies of the relevant instructions out through the pores in its membrane into the cytoplasm, where the ribosomes read them and assemble the proteins. Whoever holds the instruction book controls what gets made, and what gets made determines what the cell can do, so the nucleus controls the cell even though it never leaves its place. The red blood cell proves the point in reverse: it ejects its nucleus to make room for haemoglobin, and having lost its instructions it can never divide or repair itself, so it is worn out and replaced after about 120 days. When an examiner asks why the nucleus is the control centre, the mark is for mentioning the DNA or the instructions; calling it the brain without that reason earns nothing.

### subtopic_4 — Mitochondria and the Organelles
**Assistant:** Does the mitochondrion make energy? My textbook says energy cannot be created.

**Tutor:** Your textbook is right, and so is the careful wording of the memorandum: mitochondria release energy, they do not make it. The energy is already present, stored in the chemical bonds of glucose, which the plant that made the glucose captured from sunlight during photosynthesis. In the mitochondrion, glucose is combined with oxygen in the controlled, step-by-step process of cellular respiration, glucose plus oxygen giving carbon dioxide plus water, and the energy held in the glucose is released and transferred into a carrier molecule the cell can spend on contraction, on building proteins, on moving substances across membranes. Some energy is also released as heat, which is why you are warm. It is the same energy transformation as burning wood, but slow and controlled so the cell is not damaged. The reason this matters beyond the vocabulary is structure matching function: a cell that needs more energy per second needs more sites of release, so a muscle cell is packed with thousands of mitochondria while a skin cell has few. In Grade 10 Life Sciences you will see the folded inner membrane of the mitochondrion and learn that the folds increase the surface area on which the energy-releasing reactions take place.
