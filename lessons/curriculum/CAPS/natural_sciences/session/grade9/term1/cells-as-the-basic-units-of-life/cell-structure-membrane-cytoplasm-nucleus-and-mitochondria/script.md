# Part 1 — Expert

This session establishes the cell as the basic structural and functional unit of all living organisms, and describes the four structures the CAPS Grade 9 syllabus requires: the cell membrane, the cytoplasm, the nucleus containing DNA, and organelles such as the mitochondria. The anchor specimen is the human cheek cell, roughly 60 micrometres across, viewed through a school light microscope at a total magnification of 400 times. The governing idea is that structure matches function: each part of the cell is shaped for the job it does, and an examiner tests both the name and the job together.

## Subtopic: The Cell Theory and the Microscope

The word cell was first used for living structures in 1665, when Robert Hooke looked at thin slices of cork through an early microscope and saw rows of tiny boxes that reminded him of the small rooms, or cells, of a monastery. A few years later Anton van Leeuwenhoek, grinding his own lenses, saw single-celled organisms swimming in pond water. By 1839 the botanist Schleiden and the zoologist Schwann had concluded that every plant and every animal is made of cells, and in 1855 Rudolf Virchow added the final clause: every cell comes from an existing cell. These three statements are the cell theory. All living organisms are made of one or more cells. The cell is the basic unit of structure and function in living things. New cells arise only from existing cells by cell division.

Cells are too small to see with the naked eye, so the light microscope is the essential tool of this topic. Light passes up through the specimen on the glass slide, through the objective lens near the specimen, and then through the eyepiece lens at the top. Each lens magnifies, and the total magnification is the eyepiece magnification multiplied by the objective magnification. A standard school eyepiece is 10 times. With the 4 times objective the total is 10 × 4 = 40 times; with the 10 times objective it is 10 × 10 = 100 times; with the 40 times objective it is 10 × 40 = 400 times. Always state magnification with the multiplication sign, as 400×, never as a percentage.

The unit of cell measurement is the micrometre, symbol μm, one thousandth of a millimetre, so 1 mm = 1 000 μm. A human cheek cell is about 60 μm across, a red blood cell about 7,5 μm, and a typical bacterium 1 to 2 μm. A grain of table salt is about 300 μm, which is why you can see salt and cannot see cells. At 400 times, a 60 μm cheek cell appears as if it were 24 000 μm, that is 24 mm, across, roughly the width of a thumbnail, which is why the 40 times objective is the one that reveals the nucleus clearly.

Two practical details earn marks in the practical task. First, the specimen must be thin enough for light to pass through, which is why a cheek scraping or a single layer of onion skin is used. Second, most cell contents are transparent, so a stain is added: methylene blue colours the nucleus of a cheek cell dark blue, and iodine solution colours the nucleus and cell wall of an onion cell yellow-brown. Without stain the cell is a faint grey outline; with stain the three main structures appear.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three statements of the cell theory, total magnification from two lenses, and the micrometre.

## Subtopic: The Cell Membrane and the Cytoplasm

The cell membrane is the thin, flexible boundary of every cell. It is only about 7 nanometres thick, far too thin to see as anything but a line under the light microscope, yet it is the structure that decides what the cell is made of, because it controls which substances enter and which leave. Oxygen and glucose must come in; carbon dioxide and other wastes must go out; large molecules and most harmful substances are kept out. A membrane that lets some substances through and blocks others is described as selectively permeable, and that two-word phrase is the one the memorandum rewards.

The movement across the membrane that Grade 9 names is diffusion: the net movement of particles from a region where they are more concentrated to a region where they are less concentrated, without the cell spending energy. Oxygen in the blood is more concentrated than oxygen inside a working muscle cell, so oxygen diffuses into the cell; carbon dioxide is produced inside, so it diffuses out. The same principle governs gas exchange in the lungs, which returns in week four of this term, and the structure of the membrane itself, a double layer of fat-like molecules with proteins embedded in it, is built up in Grade 10 Life Sciences.

Inside the membrane is the cytoplasm, a jelly-like substance that is about 70 to 80 percent water, with dissolved salts, sugars, amino acids and proteins. The cytoplasm has two roles. It is the medium in which the organelles are suspended and held in place, and it is the site of many of the chemical reactions that keep the cell alive, because reactions need their reactants dissolved in water to meet. Under the school microscope the cytoplasm appears as the pale, grainy region between the membrane and the nucleus; the graininess is thousands of organelles too small to resolve individually.

A point of precision for marking: the membrane is a structure, the cytoplasm is a substance, and the whole cell is the unit. Learners who write that the cytoplasm "controls the cell" or that the membrane "makes energy" have swapped the jobs, and the examiner marks the pairing of structure and function, not the words alone. Membrane: boundary, selective control of entry and exit. Cytoplasm: water-based site of reactions, support for organelles.

Take this section's questions now: selectively permeable, diffusion in and out, and the two roles of the cytoplasm.

## Subtopic: The Nucleus and DNA

The nucleus is the largest and most visible structure inside an animal cell, a roughly spherical body that takes the stain and appears dark under the microscope. It is surrounded by its own membrane, the nuclear membrane, which has small pores through which messages pass out to the cytoplasm. The nucleus is described as the control centre of the cell because it contains the instructions for everything the cell builds and does.

Those instructions are stored in a chemical called DNA, deoxyribonucleic acid, a very long molecule shaped as a double helix, two strands twisted around each other like a spiral staircase. The structure was worked out in 1953. In the nucleus the DNA is organised into threads called chromosomes, and a human body cell carries 46 chromosomes arranged as 23 pairs, one of each pair inherited from each parent. A section of DNA that carries the instruction for one protein is a gene; humans have about 20 000 genes. Grade 9 needs the chain nucleus, chromosomes, DNA, genes, instructions, and does not need the chemistry of the bases, which is Grade 12 work.

The nucleus controls the cell by sending copies of its instructions into the cytoplasm, where proteins are assembled according to those instructions. Proteins build structures, act as enzymes that speed up reactions, and carry out almost every cell activity, so whoever controls protein-making controls the cell. When a cell divides, the DNA is first copied exactly so that each new cell receives a complete set of 46 chromosomes; the detail of this division, mitosis, is a Grade 10 Life Sciences topic.

Not every cell has a nucleus. Mature human red blood cells lose theirs so that more space is left for haemoglobin, and bacteria have DNA but no nuclear membrane around it. These exceptions appear as higher-order questions and are answered by returning to the function: a red blood cell cannot divide or repair itself because it has lost its instructions, and it lives for only about 120 days as a result.

This section's questions are available now: the control centre, the chain from nucleus to gene, and 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs.

## Subtopic: Mitochondria and the Organelles

An organelle is a small structure inside the cytoplasm with a particular job, in the same way that an organ is a structure inside the body with a particular job. The organelle the CAPS syllabus names is the mitochondrion, plural mitochondria. Mitochondria are sausage-shaped bodies 1 to 2 μm long, with a folded inner membrane, and they are the site of cellular respiration: the controlled release of energy from glucose using oxygen. The word equation is glucose plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide plus water plus energy, and the energy is stored in a carrier molecule the cell can spend. For that reason mitochondria are called the powerhouses of the cell.

The number of mitochondria matches the cell's energy demand, which is the clearest example of structure matching function at this scale. A muscle cell in your thigh contains thousands of mitochondria; a skin cell has far fewer; a sperm cell packs its mitochondria around the base of its tail where the swimming energy is needed. An examiner who asks why a muscle cell has more mitochondria than a skin cell wants the answer that muscle cells do more work and need more energy released from glucose, and that mitochondria are where that release happens.

Other organelles exist and will be studied in Grade 10, but the names are worth meeting once. Ribosomes are tiny granules where proteins are assembled according to instructions from the nucleus. Vacuoles are fluid-filled sacs used for storage; animal cells have small temporary ones, plant cells a single large permanent one, which is next lesson's business. Chloroplasts, the green organelles of photosynthesis, occur in plant cells only and never in animal cells, and confusing them with mitochondria is the most common Grade 9 error on this topic.

The error museum for this lesson has four exhibits. One: calling the nucleus the brain of the cell and stopping there; it controls the cell because it holds the DNA instructions, and that reason is the mark. Two: writing that mitochondria make energy; energy is not made, it is released from glucose, and the memorandum uses the word release. Three: labelling a cell wall on an animal cell; animal cells have only a membrane. Four: stating a magnification without the sign, or multiplying the wrong pair of lenses.

Layout for a cell-structure question: name the structure, state its function in one clause, and if asked, link the two with because. The membrane controls what enters and leaves because it is selectively permeable; the nucleus controls the cell because it contains the DNA; mitochondria supply energy because they release it from glucose during respiration. Three structures, three functions, three reasons, and the full marks follow.

The questions for this section are ready: powerhouses, muscle cells against skin cells, and the four exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same cell again, through a spaza shop, a thumbnail and a pencil drawing — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Cell Is a Spaza Shop

Picture a small spaza shop at the end of your street. It has a door with a security gate, a shop floor where everything happens, a back office where the owner keeps the recipe book and the rules, and a generator humming in the corner for when the power goes. That shop is a cell, and the four parts are the four structures you need.

The security gate is the cell membrane. It is thin, but it decides who comes in and who goes out. Customers with money come in, people who want to cause trouble stay out, and the stock goes out through the same gate. The cell membrane does exactly that with substances: oxygen and food in, carbon dioxide and waste out, harmful things kept outside. The science phrase is selectively permeable, which just means it lets some things through and not others.

The shop floor is the cytoplasm. It is where the business of the cell actually happens, and it is mostly water, like a floor that has just been mopped. All the small machines of the cell float in it and all the chemical reactions happen in it, because chemicals have to be dissolved in water before they can react. Nothing on the shop floor is in charge; it is the busy space where work gets done.

The back office is the nucleus, and the recipe book on the shelf is the DNA. Every instruction for how to run the shop is written in that book, and copies of pages are sent out to the floor so the workers know what to make. Take the book away and the shop cannot make anything new or repair anything broken. That is why we say the nucleus controls the cell: it holds the instructions.

The generator is the mitochondrion. It takes in fuel, glucose, and oxygen, and releases the energy the shop needs to run its fridges and lights. A big busy shop needs more generators; a busy muscle cell has thousands of mitochondria, while a quiet skin cell has few.

Keep the shop in your head and try the questions for this part: gate, floor, office and generator, and which real structure each one stands for.

## Subtopic: Smaller Than a Grain of Salt

How small is a cell? Smaller than anything you have ever seen with your own eyes. A grain of table salt is about 300 micrometres across, and you can just see it. A cheek cell is about 60 micrometres, five times smaller. A micrometre is a thousandth of a millimetre, so one millimetre holds a thousand micrometres end to end, which means about sixteen cheek cells would fit across one millimetre on your ruler.

That is why we need a microscope, and the microscope works by stacking two magnifying glasses. The one you look through, the eyepiece, is usually 10 times. The one near the slide, the objective, is 4, 10 or 40 times. Multiply them for the total: 10 times 4 is 40, 10 times 10 is 100, and 10 times 40 is 400. At 400 times, that 60 micrometre cheek cell looks about 24 millimetres wide, the width of your thumbnail, and now you can see the dark nucleus sitting inside it.

Here is what you actually do in the practical. Scrape the inside of your cheek gently with a clean cotton bud, smear it on a slide, add a drop of methylene blue, and lower a cover slip. The blue is the trick: without it, the cell is see-through and you see almost nothing. With it, the nucleus turns dark blue, the cytoplasm pale blue, and the membrane shows as the thin outline. For a plant cell, peel one thin layer from the inside of an onion and use iodine instead; it turns the nucleus and the cell wall yellow-brown.

Three things to remember so you do not waste the lesson: start on the lowest objective to find the cell, then switch up to 40 times; keep the slide thin so light gets through; and write the magnification on your drawing with the times sign, 400×, never as a word or a percentage.

Hold the grain of salt and the thumbnail and try this part's questions: how many micrometres in a millimetre, multiplying the lenses, and why the blue stain.

## Subtopic: Drawing a Cell for Full Marks

Every Grade 9 practical and most tests ask you to draw and label a cell, and the marks are for following rules, not for art. Rule one: sharp pencil, never pen, so you can correct. Rule two: clear, single, unbroken lines, no sketchy fur and no shading or colouring. Rule three: draw it big, at least half the space you are given. Rule four: label lines drawn with a ruler, touching the structure they name, never crossing each other, with no arrowheads. Rule five: a heading above the drawing that says what it is, and the magnification you used, for example Animal cheek cell, 400×.

Then the three labels every animal cell drawing must carry: cell membrane on the outer line, cytoplasm on the pale inside, nucleus on the dark round body. If the question asks for an organelle, add a small sausage shape in the cytoplasm and label it mitochondrion. Do not draw a cell wall on an animal cell, do not draw a green chloroplast, and do not draw the cell as a perfect circle; a cheek cell is irregular, like a fried egg that has spread.

Now the functions, said the shop way and the test way together. Membrane: the security gate, controls what enters and leaves, selectively permeable. Cytoplasm: the shop floor, jelly-like and mostly water, where the chemical reactions happen. Nucleus: the back office with the DNA recipe book, controls the cell. Mitochondria: the generators, release energy from glucose in respiration. Say the shop word to remember, write the test word to score.

Pocket summary of the lesson. All living things are made of cells, cells are the basic unit of life, and new cells come from old cells. Cells are measured in micrometres, a thousand to a millimetre, and seen at eyepiece times objective magnification. The membrane controls entry and exit, the cytoplasm hosts the reactions, the nucleus holds the DNA and controls the cell, and the mitochondria release energy. Next lesson adds the three extras that only plant cells have, and in Grade 10 Life Sciences this same cell comes back with every organelle named.

Hold the spaza shop, the thumbnail and the pencil rules, and take the final questions of the lesson: the drawing rules, the three essential labels, and matching each structure to its job.
