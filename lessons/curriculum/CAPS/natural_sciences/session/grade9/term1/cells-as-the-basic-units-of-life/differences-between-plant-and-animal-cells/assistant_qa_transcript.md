### subtopic_1 — The Cell Wall
**Assistant:** If the cell wall is full of gaps, why does the plant bother having it?

**Tutor:** Because the gaps are irrelevant to the job the wall does. The wall is not there to filter; the membrane does that. The wall is there for mechanical strength. Cellulose fibres are laid down in criss-crossing layers, like the fibres in a sheet of paper or the threads in cotton cloth, and the result is a box that resists being stretched or crushed. A plant has no bones, so every one of its cells must contribute to holding the organism up, and a stem is in effect millions of tiny cellulose boxes cemented to one another by their walls. The wall also protects the cell from bursting. When the vacuole fills with water it presses outward with considerable pressure, and a membrane alone would rupture, which is exactly what happens to an animal cell dropped into pure water. The wall resists that pressure, and the pressure pushing against the resisting wall is what makes the cell turgid and the plant firm. So the gaps let water and dissolved minerals reach the membrane freely, which the plant wants, while the fibres provide strength, which the plant needs. For marking, keep the two structures apart: wall, fully permeable, support and shape; membrane, selectively permeable, control of what enters and leaves.

### subtopic_2 — The Large Permanent Vacuole
**Assistant:** Why does the lettuce go crisp again in water if the cells were already damaged by wilting?

**Tutor:** Because wilting is not damage; it is a reversible loss of pressure. When a lettuce leaf loses water faster than it is replaced, the large vacuole in each cell shrinks, the cytoplasm pulls inward away from the cell wall, and the cell stops pressing outward on its wall. The cell is flaccid, soft, but alive and intact; the wall, the membrane and the nucleus are all still there. Put the leaf in cold water and water moves back into the cells, because the cell sap inside is a concentrated solution and the water outside is not, so water diffuses in across the membrane. The vacuoles swell, press the cytoplasm back against the walls, and each cell becomes turgid again; millions of turgid cells make a crisp leaf. The process reverses as long as the cells have not actually died. Leave the lettuce wilted for days and the membranes break down, the cell sap leaks out, and then no amount of soaking restores it, which is the brown slimy lettuce at the back of the fridge. The point for the examiner is the pairing of structure and state: a full vacuole gives turgid cells and an upright plant; an emptying vacuole gives flaccid cells and a wilting plant.

### subtopic_3 — Chloroplasts and Photosynthesis
**Assistant:** Why do plant cells still need mitochondria if the chloroplasts already make glucose?

**Tutor:** Because making glucose and getting energy out of glucose are two different processes, and the plant needs both. Photosynthesis in the chloroplasts captures light energy and stores it by building glucose from carbon dioxide and water; the energy is now locked in the chemical bonds of the glucose. To actually use that energy, to grow a new leaf, to move minerals into a root hair, to build proteins, the cell must release it again, and that release is respiration, glucose plus oxygen giving carbon dioxide plus water plus energy, which happens in the mitochondria. The chloroplast is the factory that makes the fuel; the mitochondrion is the engine that burns it. The two also run on different schedules. Photosynthesis needs light, so it stops at night, but a plant is alive at night as much as by day and its cells respire continuously, using glucose made and stored earlier. Root cells, which never see light, have no chloroplasts at all and depend entirely on sugar sent down from the leaves, which they respire in their mitochondria. So the statement that plants photosynthesise instead of respiring is false; every living plant cell respires, and the green ones photosynthesise as well.

### subtopic_4 — The Comparison Table and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** In a test, how do I tell a plant cell from an animal cell if the drawing has no colour?

**Tutor:** Look for the wall and the vacuole, not the chloroplasts. The cell wall shows as a thick, straight-sided outline, so a plant cell appears as a rectangle or a hexagon with a second thin line, the membrane, just inside the thick one, while an animal cell is a single thin, irregular outline. The large vacuole shows as a big empty-looking space filling most of the cell, with the cytoplasm squeezed into a thin band around the edge and the nucleus pushed to one side against the wall; an animal cell has its nucleus near the centre and cytoplasm all around it. Those two features are present in every plant cell, including the onion cell with no chloroplasts, so they are the reliable markers. Chloroplasts, drawn as small ovals in the cytoplasm, confirm a plant cell when present but their absence proves nothing, because root and bulb cells lack them. So the method is: thick wall and edge nucleus means plant; thin irregular outline and central nucleus means animal; chloroplasts are a bonus clue. State two features in your answer, for example it is a plant cell because it has a cell wall and a large central vacuole, and both marks are earned regardless of colour.
