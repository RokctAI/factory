### subtopic_1 — Unicellular and Multicellular Organisms
**Assistant:** Why can an Amoeba survive as one cell but a human cell cannot survive alone?

**Tutor:** Because the Amoeba has kept every life process inside its one cell, while a human cell has given most of them away. An Amoeba feeds by flowing around food particles and digesting them in small vacuoles, exchanges gases across its whole surface, pushes out waste, senses light and chemicals, moves by changing shape, and reproduces by splitting in two. It is small, under a millimetre, so its surface is large enough relative to its volume to supply everything by diffusion, and it lives in water, which brings food and carries away waste. A human red blood cell, by contrast, has specialised so completely for carrying oxygen that it has discarded its nucleus and cannot even repair itself; it depends on the digestive system for glucose, the lungs for oxygen, the kidneys to remove its waste and the bone marrow to replace it. The same is true of a muscle cell or a nerve cell: each is superb at one task and helpless at the rest. That dependence is the price of division of labour and it is also the reason specialised cells must be organised into tissues, organs and systems, so that every job somebody has given up is still done by somebody else. The examiner's phrase for the Amoeba is that a single cell carries out all the life processes; for the human cell, that it is specialised and depends on other cells.

### subtopic_2 — Cells Adapted to Their Functions
**Assistant:** How does the shape of a cell actually help it? Does a round red blood cell carry less oxygen?

**Tutor:** Yes, measurably less, and the reason is surface area. Oxygen enters and leaves the red blood cell by diffusing across its membrane, and the amount that can cross per second depends on how much membrane there is relative to how much haemoglobin sits inside. A sphere has the smallest surface for its volume of any shape; a flattened disc dented on both faces has far more surface for the same volume, so oxygen loads in the lungs and unloads in the muscles much faster. The dented disc is also flexible and can fold to pass through capillaries narrower than itself, which a rigid sphere could not do. Discarding the nucleus adds about a third more space for haemoglobin, so each cell carries more oxygen. There is a genuine disease that proves the point: in sickle cell anaemia the haemoglobin is faulty and the cells become stiff crescents, they carry oxygen poorly, they jam in small vessels and they are destroyed early, which causes pain and anaemia. So shape is not decoration; it is the mechanism. The pattern is the same for every specialised cell: the long root hair increases absorbing surface, the long axon avoids slow hand-offs between cells, the many mitochondria in muscle meet a high energy demand. Always name the feature and then the function it makes possible.

### subtopic_3 — Cells, Tissues, Organs and Systems
**Assistant:** What is the difference between a tissue and an organ? They both seem to be groups of cells.

**Tutor:** The difference is sameness against difference. A tissue is a group of cells of the same kind doing the same job: muscle tissue is muscle cells and nothing else, nervous tissue is nerve cells, and the lining of your cheek is a sheet of similar flat epithelial cells. An organ is built from several different tissues combined so that together they do something no single tissue could. The heart is the clearest example. Cardiac muscle tissue provides the squeeze; nervous tissue sets the rhythm; connective tissue forms the valves and holds the chambers in shape; epithelial tissue lines the inside so blood flows smoothly. Remove any one and the heart fails as a pump. The stomach is the same story: muscle tissue churns, glandular epithelial tissue secretes acid and enzymes, nervous tissue coordinates, connective tissue binds. So the test is to ask whether the cells are all alike. If yes, it is a tissue; if several different tissues are cooperating, it is an organ. Then organs cooperating form a system, the heart and vessels forming the circulatory system, and the systems together are the organism. For marks, give the definition and an example at each level, and avoid the trap of calling an organ a large tissue.

### subtopic_4 — Stem Cells and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** If every cell has the same DNA, what makes a stem cell turn into a muscle cell instead of a nerve cell?

**Tutor:** Which genes it switches on, in response to signals from the cells around it. All 46 chromosomes are present in every cell of your body, with the same roughly 20 000 genes, but no cell uses them all. A muscle cell reads the genes for the contractile proteins and leaves the genes for nerve-signalling proteins silent; a nerve cell does the opposite. A stem cell has not yet committed; its genes are all available and it keeps dividing. As an embryo develops, chemical signals from neighbouring cells tell each stem cell which set of genes to activate, and once a cell has switched on the muscle set and switched off the others, it is specialised and normally stays that way. That is why specialisation does not involve losing DNA, which is the misconception the error museum targets; a muscle cell could in principle be reprogrammed, and researchers have done exactly that in the laboratory. Grade 9 needs only the headline: stem cells are unspecialised, they can divide and become specialised cells, they are found in embryos and in adult bone marrow, and specialised cells keep the full DNA. Bone marrow stem cells are the medically useful ones, because they produce all the blood cells throughout your life and can be transplanted to a patient whose own marrow has failed. The genetics of switching genes on and off is Grade 12 Life Sciences.
