### subtopic_1 — Mechanical Digestion: Teeth, Tongue and Churning
**Assistant:** Why does the stomach not digest itself if it makes acid and a protein-digesting enzyme?

**Tutor:** Because it protects itself in several ways at once, and when that protection fails, it partly does digest itself, which is what an ulcer is. The stomach wall is made largely of protein, and its contents are strongly acidic, about pH 2, with pepsin present to break proteins down, so without defences it would indeed be attacked. The first defence is a thick layer of alkaline mucus secreted by cells of the stomach lining. It coats the whole inner surface and traps a little hydrogencarbonate next to the cells, so that right against the wall the conditions are close to neutral even though the middle of the stomach is very acidic; pepsin cannot work there and acid cannot reach the cells. The second defence is that pepsin is not made in its active form. The stomach cells release an inactive version, which becomes active only when it meets the acid in the stomach cavity, so the enzyme cannot digest the cells that make it. The third defence is rapid repair: the cells of the stomach lining are replaced every few days, so small areas of damage are quickly mended. An ulcer forms when these defences are broken down in one area. Most ulcers are caused by infection with a bacterium that can survive in the stomach mucus and inflames the lining, and others by long-term use of certain painkillers, which reduce the production of mucus. Acid and pepsin then eat into the unprotected wall, causing a burning pain, and sometimes bleeding. Treatment with antibiotics to clear the bacterium and with medicine that reduces acid production allows the lining to heal, a discovery that earned its researchers a Nobel Prize.

### subtopic_2 — Enzymes and Chemical Digestion
**Assistant:** Why does a fever not destroy our enzymes if high temperatures denature them?

**Tutor:** Because a fever raises body temperature only a few degrees, far below the temperatures at which most human enzymes are denatured, although a very high fever is dangerous for related reasons. Normal body temperature is about 37 degrees. A fever is usually 38 to 40 degrees, a deliberate response in which the brain resets the body's thermostat higher, partly because many bacteria and viruses multiply less well at those temperatures and the immune system works more actively. Most human enzymes keep working at these temperatures; their rate may even rise slightly, because molecules move a little faster. Significant denaturing of most enzymes begins only above about 45 degrees, and many are not destroyed until well above 50 or 60. The enzyme experiment in this lesson uses 80 degrees precisely because that is far past the point of damage. A fever above about 41 degrees, however, is a medical emergency. At these temperatures some of the body's proteins, especially in the brain, begin to lose their shape, nerve cells malfunction, and seizures, confusion and organ damage can follow. The same danger exists in heatstroke, when the body overheats from hot conditions and exertion and cannot cool itself, for instance during long outdoor sport in summer without enough water. This is why a very high fever in a child is brought down with cool sponging and medicine and checked at a clinic, and why players drink water and rest in the shade on hot days. So the lesson's rule holds: enzymes have an optimum near 37 degrees, a modest rise is tolerated, and the further the temperature climbs above about 41 degrees, the closer the body comes to denaturing its own proteins.

### subtopic_3 — Digestion Along the Canal: Mouth, Stomach and Small Intestine
**Assistant:** What would happen to digestion if a person's gall bladder were removed?

**Tutor:** Digestion would continue almost normally, because the gall bladder only stores bile; the liver, which makes it, keeps working. In a healthy person the liver produces bile continuously, and between meals most of it flows into the gall bladder, where it is stored and concentrated by the removal of water. When a meal containing fat reaches the duodenum, a hormone signals the gall bladder to contract and squirt a dose of concentrated bile into the duodenum exactly when it is needed. The most common reason for removing the gall bladder is gallstones, hard lumps that form when substances in the concentrated bile crystallise. A stone can block the duct and cause severe pain under the right ribs, especially after a fatty meal, along with nausea and sometimes jaundice, a yellowing of the skin because bile cannot escape. After the operation, bile flows directly from the liver into the duodenum in a slow, steady trickle rather than in a concentrated dose at mealtimes. For most people this is enough to emulsify the fat in an ordinary meal, and they eat normally within weeks. A very large, very fatty meal may then cause some discomfort or diarrhoea, because there is not enough bile at once to emulsify all the fat, so the lipase works more slowly and some fat passes undigested into the large intestine. This case shows clearly the difference between making and storing: the liver is essential to life and digestion, while the gall bladder is a useful reservoir that the body can manage without. It also confirms that bile is not an enzyme: it does not digest fat, it emulsifies it for lipase to digest.

### subtopic_7 — Three Big Molecules, Three Small Ones
**Assistant:** If all three foods end as small molecules, why is protein digestion spread over the stomach and the intestine?

**Tutor:** Because protein molecules are long, varied and tightly folded, and breaking them down efficiently takes several different enzymes working in sequence under different conditions. A protein is a chain of hundreds of amino acids of about twenty different kinds, folded into a compact shape. The first step happens in the stomach. The strong acid unfolds the protein chains, rather as heat unfolds egg white, exposing them to attack, and it activates pepsin, which cuts the long chains at particular points into shorter fragments. This also prepares the food for the next stage and starts the process while the food is held in the stomach for a few hours anyway. In the small intestine, trypsin from the pancreas cuts the fragments at different points, producing still shorter chains. Each protease cuts only between particular kinds of amino acids, so several types are needed to cover all the links. Finally, enzymes on the surfaces of the cells lining the small intestine snip off amino acids one or two at a time until single amino acids remain, which are then absorbed into the blood. This stepwise arrangement is efficient: an enzyme that cuts the middle of chains creates many ends for the end-cutting enzymes to work on, so the whole job is done much faster than any single enzyme could manage. Starch has a simpler structure, a chain of only one kind of unit, glucose, so amylase and maltase can handle it, and fat digestion needs mainly bile and lipase. The stomach stage also matters for protection: the acid kills most bacteria in the food. Grade 11 Life Sciences returns to the structure of proteins and the role of enzymes in detail.
