### subtopic_2 — Energy in Food and Reading a Food Label
**Assistant:** Why does fat give so much more energy per gram than carbohydrate or protein?

**Tutor:** Because fat molecules are made almost entirely of carbon and hydrogen, with very little oxygen, so they are much less burnt already. All three nutrients release energy in respiration when their carbon and hydrogen atoms combine with oxygen to form carbon dioxide and water. A carbohydrate such as glucose already contains a great deal of oxygen in its molecule, about one oxygen atom for every carbon atom, so in a sense it is partly combined with oxygen before respiration starts, and less energy is left to release. A fat molecule consists mainly of long chains of carbon atoms each carrying hydrogen atoms, with oxygen only at one end, so far more of its atoms are available to combine with oxygen, and roughly twice as much energy is released per gram: 37 kJ against 17 kJ. Protein gives about the same as carbohydrate per gram, although the body prefers to use protein for building rather than fuel. This difference explains why the body stores its long-term energy reserves as fat rather than as carbohydrate: storing the same energy as glycogen, which is also held with a lot of water, would make a person several times heavier. A camel's hump, a bear's winter fat and the fat under human skin are all compact energy stores. It also explains why fried foods and foods rich in oil, butter or nuts are so energy-dense, so that a small amount can supply a large share of a day's energy needs. That is useful for someone doing heavy work or recovering from illness, but for an inactive person it makes it easy to take in more energy than is used, which is why the guidelines say to use fats sparingly rather than to avoid them.

### subtopic_3 — A Balanced Diet and the South African Food Guidelines
**Assistant:** Why are vitamins added to maize meal and bread flour by law instead of people just eating better?

**Tutor:** Because fortification reaches everyone at very low cost, including the people least able to change their diets, and it has a long history of success. Most South African households eat maize meal or bread every day, often several times a day, and for many poorer families these staples are a large part of what they eat because they are the cheapest source of energy. Refining maize and wheat removes much of the outer layers of the grain, where many vitamins and minerals are found. A diet based heavily on refined staples can therefore supply enough energy but too little of the micronutrients, and in the past that caused real disease: pellagra, caused by too little of the B vitamin niacin, was common in parts of South Africa among people who lived mainly on maize, and anaemia and vitamin A deficiency remained widespread. Telling everyone to eat a wider variety of foods is important, but it depends on money, access to shops and markets, and knowledge, and it changes slowly. Adding the missing nutrients to the staple foods at the mill costs a few cents per bag and works automatically, whatever a family can afford. Since 2003 South African law has required maize meal and bread flour to be fortified with vitamin A, several B vitamins including folic acid, and iron and zinc. Folic acid has measurably reduced birth defects of the spinal cord. Iodised salt, compulsory since 1995, has sharply reduced goitre. Fortification does not replace a balanced diet, because staples cannot supply protein, fibre, vitamin C and everything else, but it provides a safety net under the whole population.

### subtopic_4 — Malnutrition, Deficiency Diseases and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why does a child with kwashiorkor have a swollen belly if they are not getting enough food?

**Tutor:** Because the swelling is fluid, not fat or food, and it is caused by a shortage of protein in the blood. Blood plasma contains dissolved proteins, made by the liver from the amino acids we absorb, and these proteins help hold water inside the blood vessels. In the capillaries, the pressure of the blood pushes some fluid out into the spaces between the cells, and the plasma proteins then draw most of it back by osmosis. When a child's diet has far too little protein, typically after being weaned from breast milk onto a diet that is nearly all maize porridge or other starch, the liver cannot make enough of these plasma proteins. With too little protein in the blood, less fluid is drawn back, so it accumulates in the tissues, a condition called oedema. The swelling is most obvious in the belly, legs, feet and face, and the child's liver may also enlarge as fat builds up in it. Underneath the swelling, the muscles are wasting, which is why the arms are thin. The child's skin may peel and darken, the hair becomes thin, brittle and pale, the immune system weakens so that infections such as diarrhoea and measles become deadly, and the child is miserable and listless. The name comes from a language of Ghana and is often explained as the sickness of the older child when a new baby arrives, describing the child who loses breast milk too early. Treatment is careful, gradual feeding with protein-rich therapeutic food under medical supervision. Prevention is a diet with enough protein, which does not have to be expensive: beans, lentils, peanuts, eggs and amasi are all good sources.

### subtopic_7 — The Plate That Keeps You Well
**Assistant:** Can a person who is overweight also be malnourished?

**Tutor:** Yes, and in South Africa it is common: malnutrition means an unbalanced diet, and a diet can provide too much energy and too few nutrients at the same time. Many cheap, convenient foods are energy-dense but nutrient-poor: white bread, refined maize meal, sugary drinks, sweets, chips, fried snacks and fatty processed meats provide plenty of kilojoules from starch, sugar and fat, but little protein, fibre, vitamins or minerals, especially when vegetables, fruit, beans and dairy foods are expensive or not easily available. A person who eats mainly these foods can take in more energy than they use, storing the excess as fat and gaining weight, while still being short of iron, vitamin A, calcium or other micronutrients. Surveys in South Africa find this pattern within single families and even single people: a mother who is obese and anaemic, or a child who is stunted, too short for their age from long-term poor nutrition, while also being overweight. Researchers call this the double burden of malnutrition. It happens partly because people's diets have shifted from traditional foods such as legumes, vegetables, sorghum and whole maize towards processed and fast foods, and partly because physical activity has decreased with more sitting, screens and transport. The health effects combine: excess energy raises the risk of type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure and heart disease, while micronutrient shortages cause tiredness, poor immunity and poor school performance. The solution is not simply eating less but eating differently: more vegetables, fruit, beans and whole grains, fewer sugary drinks and fried foods, and more activity, which is exactly what the food-based dietary guidelines recommend.
