### subtopic_1 — Why We Digest: The Processes in Order
**Assistant:** Why can food be swallowed while lying down or even upside down?

**Tutor:** Because food is moved along the oesophagus by muscles, not by gravity. The wall of the oesophagus has two layers of muscle, one running around the tube and one running along it. When you swallow, the circular muscle just behind the bolus contracts, squeezing the tube shut behind the food, while the muscle just ahead of it relaxes, widening the way forward. This wave of contraction and relaxation travels down the tube in a few seconds, pushing the bolus ahead of it, rather like squeezing toothpaste along a tube with your fingers. This is peristalsis, and it continues automatically all the way through the alimentary canal, in the stomach, the small intestine and the large intestine, controlled by nerves in the gut wall itself, without any conscious effort. Gravity helps when you are upright, but it is not needed: astronauts in orbit, where there is no sense of up or down, eat and digest normally. At the bottom of the oesophagus a ring of muscle, a sphincter, opens to let the food into the stomach and then closes to keep the acidic contents from coming back up. When that ring is weak or the stomach is very full, acid can rise into the oesophagus, and that is heartburn, which is why lying down straight after a large meal can make it worse. Peristalsis also explains why fibre matters: the muscles need something with bulk to push against, and a diet low in fibre gives them little to grip, so the contents move slowly and constipation results.

### subtopic_2 — Components: The Alimentary Canal and the Accessory Organs
**Assistant:** Why does blood from the intestines go to the liver first instead of straight to the heart?

**Tutor:** Because the liver acts as a checkpoint that controls and cleans what has been absorbed before it reaches the rest of the body. After a meal, the blood leaving the small intestine carries large amounts of glucose and amino acids, as well as anything harmful that was swallowed, such as alcohol, certain drugs and toxins made by bacteria. If all of that went straight into the general circulation, the glucose level in the blood would swing wildly after every meal and the brain and other organs would be exposed to every toxin. Instead, the hepatic portal vein carries this blood directly to the liver, which processes it. The liver takes up excess glucose and stores it as glycogen, releasing glucose again between meals so that the blood level stays steady, under the control of hormones such as insulin from the pancreas. It breaks down excess amino acids, which the body cannot store, removing the nitrogen-containing part as urea, which travels in the blood to the kidneys and is excreted in urine. It breaks down alcohol and many drugs and poisons into less harmful substances. It stores certain vitamins and iron. Only then does the blood continue, through the hepatic vein, to the heart and the rest of the body. This arrangement explains why the liver is so often damaged by heavy drinking: it receives the highest concentrations of alcohol and does most of the work of breaking it down, and years of heavy drinking replace its working cells with scar tissue, the disease cirrhosis. It also explains why some medicines must be injected rather than swallowed: swallowed, they would be broken down by the liver before reaching their target.

### subtopic_3 — Absorption, Assimilation and Egestion
**Assistant:** How does sugar and salt in water help a child with diarrhoea? Would plain water not be enough?

**Tutor:** Plain water helps a little, but the sugar and salt together make the gut absorb water far more effectively, and they replace the salts the child is losing. In diarrhoea, infection causes the intestine to pour water and salts out into the canal and to absorb less, so the body loses both. A child's body is small and mostly water, so it can become dangerously dehydrated within hours, and the loss of salts upsets the working of the heart, nerves and muscles. The key discovery, one of the most important in modern medicine, is that the cells lining the small intestine have carrier proteins that move glucose and sodium, from salt, into the cell together. Even during most diarrhoeal infections these carriers keep working. When glucose and sodium are carried in, water follows them into the cells and the blood by osmosis. So a drink with the right amounts of sugar and salt is absorbed much better than plain water, and it replaces the lost sodium at the same time. The proportions matter: too much sugar or salt draws water into the gut instead and makes the diarrhoea worse, which is why the recipe is exact: one litre of clean water, eight level teaspoons of sugar and half a level teaspoon of salt, or a ready-made sachet from a clinic or pharmacy. It is given in small sips, often, even if the child vomits, because some is still absorbed. Feeding and breastfeeding should continue, and zinc tablets from the clinic shorten the illness. This simple treatment has saved millions of children's lives worldwide, but a child who is very drowsy, cannot drink, or has blood in the stool needs a clinic urgently.

### subtopic_4 — Health Issues of the Digestive System and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** If humans cannot digest fibre, why are we told to eat lots of it?

**Tutor:** Because fibre does its most important work precisely by not being digested. Fibre is mainly cellulose and similar substances from plant cell walls, found in vegetables, fruit with their skins, beans, lentils, whole-grain bread, brown rice and unrefined maize meal. Human enzymes cannot break the bonds that hold cellulose together, so fibre passes through the stomach and small intestine largely unchanged and reaches the large intestine. There it does several useful things. It holds water and adds bulk, making the faeces soft and large, which gives the muscles of the gut wall something to grip, so peristalsis moves the contents along quickly and regularly; this prevents constipation and reduces straining, which can cause painful swollen veins near the anus. Some kinds of fibre are partly broken down by the trillions of bacteria living in the colon, which use it as their food; in doing so they produce substances that nourish the cells of the colon wall and that appear to protect against inflammation and bowel cancer, and they make some vitamins, such as vitamin K, which we absorb. Fibre also slows the absorption of sugar from a meal, so blood glucose rises more gently, which helps prevent type 2 diabetes, and it makes meals more filling, which helps control weight. Diets low in fibre and high in refined, processed foods and processed meat are linked to constipation, heart disease and cancer of the colon. Many traditional South African foods are excellent sources, such as samp and beans, morogo and other leafy greens, and whole maize meal. The next lesson, on a healthy diet, places fibre alongside the nutrients we do digest.
