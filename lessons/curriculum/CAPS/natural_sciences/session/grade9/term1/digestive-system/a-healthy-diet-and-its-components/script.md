# Part 1 — Expert

This session describes the components of a healthy diet: the nutrients, their functions, sources and food tests, how the energy in food is calculated from a label, what a balanced diet looks like, and the diseases of malnutrition. The anchor numbers are the energy values used on South African food labels, 17 kilojoules per gram of carbohydrate, 17 per gram of protein and 37 per gram of fat, and the fact that water makes up about 60 percent of body mass. The governing principle is that a healthy diet supplies every nutrient in the right amount and supplies energy roughly equal to the energy the body uses.

## Subtopic: The Nutrients and Their Functions

A nutrient is a substance in food that the body needs for energy, growth, repair or the control of its chemical processes. There are seven components of a healthy diet, and each has its functions, its sources and, for the first four, a standard laboratory test.

Carbohydrates are the body's main source of energy. They include sugars, such as glucose in fruit and sucrose in table sugar, and starch, the stored food of plants, found in maize meal, rice, bread, potatoes and samp. Starch is digested to glucose, which cells respire for energy; excess is stored in the liver and muscles as glycogen or converted to fat. The test for starch is iodine solution, which turns from yellow-brown to blue-black. The test for glucose is Benedict's solution, which, when heated with a glucose solution, changes from blue to green, yellow, orange or brick-red depending on how much glucose is present.

Proteins are needed for growth and repair: they build muscle, skin, hair and blood, and they form enzymes, antibodies and haemoglobin. They are made of chains of amino acids. Good sources are beans, lentils, peanuts, soya, eggs, milk, amasi, fish such as pilchards, chicken and meat, and mopane worms. The test for protein is the Biuret test: the solution turns from blue to purple.

Fats and oils, together called lipids, are a concentrated store of energy, giving more than twice as much energy per gram as carbohydrates; they also insulate the body under the skin, cushion organs such as the kidneys, form part of every cell membrane, and carry the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K. Sources include cooking oil, margarine, butter, nuts, avocado, fish and the fat in meat. The test for fat is the grease-spot test: rubbed on brown paper, fat leaves a translucent spot that lets light through.

Vitamins are needed in tiny amounts to keep the body's chemistry working. Vitamin A, from liver, carrots, sweet potato and dark-green leaves such as morogo, keeps the eyes and skin healthy. The B vitamins, from whole grains, beans and meat, help cells release energy from food. Vitamin C, from guavas, oranges and tomatoes, keeps gums and skin healthy and helps wounds heal. Vitamin D, made in the skin in sunlight and found in fish and eggs, helps the body absorb calcium.

Minerals are also needed in small amounts. Calcium, from milk, amasi and small fish eaten with their bones, builds bones and teeth. Iron, from red meat, liver, beans and dark-green vegetables, is needed to make haemoglobin. Iodine, from iodised salt and seafood, is needed by the thyroid gland. Sodium, from salt, is needed by nerves and muscles, though too much raises blood pressure.

Water makes up about 60 percent of body mass; it transports substances in the blood, is the medium of every chemical reaction in cells, and cools the body by sweating. Fibre, the cellulose of plant cell walls, is not digested but adds bulk that keeps food moving through the gut.

The questions for this section are with you now: the function of each nutrient, a good source of each, and the colour changes of the four food tests.

## Subtopic: Energy in Food and Reading a Food Label

The energy in food is measured in kilojoules, kJ. South African food labels show the energy and the mass of each nutrient per 100 grams and per serving. The energy can be calculated from the masses of the three energy-supplying nutrients using standard values: carbohydrate supplies 17 kJ per gram, protein 17 kJ per gram and fat 37 kJ per gram. Vitamins, minerals, water and fibre supply no significant energy.

The method is to multiply the mass of each nutrient by its energy value and add the three products. Take a bowl of cereal with milk containing 30 grams of carbohydrate, 4 grams of protein and 2 grams of fat. Carbohydrate: 30 × 17 = 510 kJ. Protein: 4 × 17 = 68 kJ. Fat: 2 × 37 = 74 kJ. Total: 510 + 68 + 74 = 652 kJ.

Now 100 grams of peanut butter, containing about 20 grams of carbohydrate, 25 grams of protein and 50 grams of fat. Carbohydrate: 20 × 17 = 340 kJ. Protein: 25 × 17 = 425 kJ. Fat: 50 × 37 = 1850 kJ. Total: 340 + 425 + 1850 = 2615 kJ. Notice that fat supplies most of the energy, because each gram of fat carries more than twice the energy of a gram of carbohydrate or protein. That is why fried foods and foods rich in oil are so energy-dense.

How much energy does a person need? It depends on age, sex, body size and, above all, activity. A growing, active teenager needs very roughly 9 000 to 12 000 kJ a day; a person doing heavy physical work needs more, and an inactive adult needs less. The energy is used for the basic work of staying alive, the beating of the heart, breathing, keeping warm, and for every movement on top of that.

Energy balance decides body mass. If the energy taken in equals the energy used, body mass stays steady. If more is taken in than is used, the excess, from any of the three nutrients, is stored as fat and mass increases. If less is taken in than used, stored fat is respired and mass decreases. So no single nutrient makes a person fat; an energy surplus does.

Reading a label also means checking what else is in the food: the amounts of sugar, saturated fat and sodium, and the list of ingredients, which is written in order of mass, largest first. A drink whose first ingredient after water is sugar is mainly sugar.

## Subtopic: A Balanced Diet and the South African Food Guidelines

A balanced diet contains all seven components in the right proportions to meet the body's needs, with energy matching use. No single food provides everything, so the key is variety. South Africa's food-based dietary guidelines turn this into simple advice for everyone over five years old.

Enjoy a variety of foods. Be active. Make starchy foods part of most meals, choosing unrefined, whole-grain types such as brown bread and unsifted maize meal where possible, because they keep more fibre and vitamins. Eat plenty of vegetables and fruit every day, of different colours. Eat dry beans, split peas, lentils and soya regularly, cheap sources of protein and fibre. Have milk, maas or yoghurt every day for calcium. Fish, chicken, lean meat or eggs can be eaten daily. Drink lots of clean, safe water. Use fats sparingly and choose vegetable oils rather than hard fats. Use sugar and foods and drinks high in sugar sparingly. Use salt and foods high in salt sparingly.

A practical way to build a balanced meal is the plate model: about half the plate vegetables and fruit, about a quarter starch, preferably whole-grain, and about a quarter protein, with a little fat, a glass of water and a dairy food during the day. A plate of pap, morogo and pilchards in tomato, or samp and beans with cabbage, is balanced; a plate of chips and a cold drink is not.

South Africa also improves everyone's diet behind the scenes. Since 2003, all maize meal and bread flour sold in the country must be fortified with vitamin A, several B vitamins including folic acid, iron and zinc, to reduce deficiencies across the population. Salt must be iodised, which has greatly reduced goitre. Since 2018 a health levy, often called the sugar tax, is charged on sugary drinks to discourage heavy consumption.

Different people have different needs. Teenagers in the growth spurt need extra protein, calcium and iron; girls who menstruate need more iron; pregnant women need extra folic acid, iron and energy; athletes need more energy and protein; older people need less energy but the same vitamins and minerals.

## Subtopic: Malnutrition, Deficiency Diseases and the Error Museum

Malnutrition means an unbalanced diet: too little of some nutrients, too much of others, or both. It includes undernutrition and overnutrition.

Kwashiorkor is caused by a severe lack of protein, often in young children weaned from breast milk onto a diet that is almost all starch. The child has a swollen belly and legs caused by fluid collecting in the tissues, thin arms, peeling skin and thin, discoloured hair, and is apathetic and prone to infection. Marasmus is caused by a severe lack of energy and protein together, starvation; the child is extremely thin, skin and bone, with a wrinkled face. Stunting, being too short for one's age because of long-term poor nutrition, affects roughly one in four South African children under five and can permanently affect learning.

Deficiency diseases follow from a shortage of a single vitamin or mineral. Too little vitamin A causes night blindness and, in severe cases, permanent blindness, and weakens immunity. Too little vitamin C causes scurvy, with bleeding gums and slow-healing wounds. Too little vitamin D or calcium causes rickets in children, with soft, bent leg bones. Too little of the B vitamin niacin causes pellagra, with rough, darkened skin, diarrhoea and confusion, once common in people living mainly on maize. Too little iron causes anaemia, with tiredness, pale skin and breathlessness. Too little iodine causes goitre, a swelling of the thyroid gland in the neck.

Overnutrition is now as serious a problem in South Africa. Eating more energy than is used, especially from fried food, sugary drinks and refined starch, combined with too little activity, leads to overweight and obesity, which affect about two out of three adult South African women and about one in three men. Obesity raises the risk of type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease, stroke and some cancers. Too much salt raises blood pressure; too much sugar causes tooth decay and contributes to diabetes. A person can be overweight and malnourished at the same time, with plenty of energy but too few vitamins and minerals.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: fat is bad and should be cut out completely; fats are essential for energy storage, cell membranes and fat-soluble vitamins, and should be eaten in moderation. Two: carbohydrates make you fat; an energy surplus from any nutrient is stored as fat. Three: vitamins give you energy; vitamins supply no energy, they help cells release energy from food. Four: a child with a big round belly must be well fed; a swollen belly with thin limbs is a sign of kwashiorkor. Five: protein is only for building muscle; proteins also form enzymes, antibodies and haemoglobin.

Layout for a nutrient question: function, source, deficiency disease. Layout for an energy question: mass times energy value for each nutrient, then the sum, with units. Layout for a diet question: the guideline, the reason and an example meal. The components are then described exactly as the memorandum expects.

## Subtopic: Fuel, Bricks and Spare Parts

Think of your body as a house that is being built while people are living in it. It needs fuel to keep the lights on, bricks to build and repair, and a box of small spare parts to keep everything working.

The fuel is carbohydrates and fats. Carbohydrates are the everyday fuel: pap, rice, bread, potatoes, samp, and sugars like the ones in fruit. Fats are the fuel in the storeroom: oil, margarine, nuts, avocado. A gram of fat holds more than twice the energy of a gram of carbohydrate, which is why the body stores its extra energy as fat. Fats also wrap your organs like bubble wrap, keep you warm, and carry some of the vitamins.

The bricks are proteins. Beans, lentils, peanuts, eggs, milk, amasi, pilchards, chicken, meat, even mopane worms. Your body breaks them into their building blocks, amino acids, and builds its own muscle, skin, hair and blood with them. Proteins are also the workers in the house: enzymes, antibodies, and the haemoglobin that carries oxygen.

The spare parts are vitamins and minerals, needed in tiny amounts, but without them things break. Vitamin A for your eyes, from carrots, sweet potato and morogo. Vitamin C for your gums and healing, from guavas and oranges. Vitamin D, made by your skin in sunlight, for your bones. Calcium for bones and teeth, from milk and amasi. Iron for your blood, from meat, beans and spinach. Iodine for your neck gland, from iodised salt.

And two things that are not exactly food: water, which is about 60 percent of you and carries everything around, and fibre, the stringy plant stuff that you do not digest but that keeps your gut moving.

You can test food for some of these. Iodine solution turns blue-black on starch. Benedict's solution, heated, goes from blue to orange or brick-red with sugar. Biuret goes from blue to purple with protein. And fat leaves a see-through grease spot on brown paper.

## Subtopic: Counting Kilojoules on a Packet

Pick up any packet of food in a South African shop and there is a little table on the back. It tells you how much energy is in the food, in kilojoules, and how many grams of carbohydrate, protein and fat. You can check the energy yourself with three numbers: every gram of carbohydrate gives 17 kilojoules, every gram of protein gives 17, and every gram of fat gives 37.

Try a small packet of chips with 15 grams of carbohydrate, 3 grams of protein and 10 grams of fat. Carbohydrate: 15 × 17 = 255 kJ. Protein: 3 × 17 = 51 kJ. Fat: 10 × 37 = 370 kJ. Add them: 255 + 51 + 370 = 676 kJ. Look where most of the energy came from: the 10 grams of fat gave more than the 15 grams of carbohydrate. Fried snacks are energy-packed for that reason.

What does your body do with the kilojoules? It spends them, like money. Some just to stay alive: heart beating, lungs breathing, brain thinking, body warm. The rest on moving: walking to school, playing soccer, dancing. A growing, active teenager spends very roughly 9 000 to 12 000 kJ a day.

Now the bank balance. Eat about as many kilojoules as you spend, and your weight stays steady. Eat more than you spend, from anything, pap or oil or sugar, and the body banks the extra as fat. Eat less than you spend, and it takes money out of the fat bank. So it is not one food that makes a person gain weight; it is eating more than you spend, day after day.

Two more tricks on the label. The ingredients are listed from the most to the least, so if sugar is near the top, the food is mostly sugar. And check the sugar and salt lines: lots of both is a warning sign.

Hold the packet in mind and try this part's questions: the three numbers, a quick calculation, and what the body does with extra kilojoules.

## Subtopic: The Plate That Keeps You Well

Here is the easiest way to eat well: look at your plate. About half of it vegetables and fruit, in different colours. About a quarter starch, better brown or whole than white. About a quarter protein: beans, eggs, fish, chicken or meat. A little oil. A glass of water. Some milk or amasi during the day. Pap with morogo and pilchards does that. Samp and beans with cabbage does that. Chips with a cold drink does not.

The country helps too. Every bag of maize meal and bread flour has extra vitamins, iron and zinc added by law, and salt has iodine added, so even a simple diet gets some spare parts. Sugary drinks have an extra tax on them to make them less tempting.

What happens when the plate goes wrong? When a small child gets almost only starch and too little protein, the result can be kwashiorkor: a swollen belly and puffy legs from fluid, thin arms, peeling skin and thin, pale hair. That big belly is not a sign of a full child; it is a sign of a sick one. When a child gets too little food of every kind, it is marasmus: skin and bone. Missing one spare part gives its own disease: no vitamin A, night blindness; no vitamin C, bleeding gums, scurvy; no vitamin D or calcium, bent leg bones, rickets; no iron, tired and pale, anaemia; no iodine, a swollen neck, goitre.

And the opposite problem is growing fast: too much fried food, sugar and cold drink, and too little moving, leads to obesity, then diabetes, high blood pressure and heart disease. In South Africa about two out of three adult women are overweight. A person can even be overweight and still short of vitamins.

Food tests: iodine, Benedict's, Biuret, grease spot. Energy: 17 kJ per gram of carbohydrate or protein, 37 per gram of fat; balance in and out. Half a plate vegetables and fruit, a quarter starch, a quarter protein. Too little gives kwashiorkor, marasmus and deficiency diseases; too much gives obesity and diabetes.
