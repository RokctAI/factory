# Part 1 — Expert

This session gives the overview of the human digestive system: the five processes of nutrition in order, the parts of the alimentary canal and the accessory organs with their functions, absorption and egestion, and the health issues of the system. The anchor numbers are an alimentary canal about eight to nine metres long, a small intestine of about six metres with an inner surface of roughly 30 square metres, and a journey of about one to three days from mouth to anus. The governing principle is that food must be broken down into small, soluble molecules before it can be absorbed into the blood and used by cells.

## Subtopic: Why We Digest: The Processes in Order

The food we eat is made largely of large, insoluble molecules: starch in maize meal and bread, proteins in beans, eggs and meat, and fats in oil and butter. These molecules are far too big to pass through the wall of the gut or the membrane of a cell. The digestive system exists to break them down into small, soluble molecules that can be absorbed and carried by the blood to every cell, where they supply energy and the materials for growth and repair.

Nutrition in humans involves five processes, and they are always listed in order. Ingestion is the taking in of food through the mouth. Digestion is the breakdown of large, insoluble food molecules into small, soluble molecules. It has two parts: mechanical digestion, the physical breaking of food into smaller pieces by the teeth and the churning of the stomach, which increases the surface area; and chemical digestion, the breaking of the molecules themselves by enzymes, so that starch becomes glucose, proteins become amino acids and fats become fatty acids and glycerol. Absorption is the movement of these small, soluble molecules through the wall of the small intestine into the blood. Assimilation is the use of the absorbed molecules by the body's cells: glucose is respired for energy in the mitochondria, amino acids are built into new proteins for growth and repair, and fats are used for energy, cell membranes and storage. Egestion is the removal of undigested food, mainly fibre, together with bacteria and dead cells, from the body through the anus as faeces.

A careful distinction is examined every year. Egestion is not excretion. Egestion removes material that was never absorbed and never became part of the body's cells; excretion removes waste products made by the cells' own chemical reactions, such as carbon dioxide from respiration and urea from the breakdown of amino acids. Faeces are egested; urine and carbon dioxide are excreted.

For marking, the five processes in order with one line each: ingestion, food taken into the mouth; digestion, large insoluble molecules broken into small soluble ones, mechanically and chemically; absorption, small molecules pass into the blood in the small intestine; assimilation, cells use the absorbed molecules; egestion, undigested food removed through the anus.

## Subtopic: Components: The Alimentary Canal and the Accessory Organs

The alimentary canal, also called the gut or digestive tract, is a continuous muscular tube from the mouth to the anus, about eight to nine metres long in an adult. Food passes through each of its parts in turn.

The mouth, or buccal cavity, takes in food. The teeth bite and grind it, the tongue mixes it with saliva and rolls it into a ball, the bolus, and pushes it back for swallowing. The pharynx, the throat, is shared with the airway; during swallowing the epiglottis closes the windpipe. The oesophagus, or gullet, is a muscular tube about 25 centimetres long that carries the bolus to the stomach by waves of muscular contraction called peristalsis, which work even if you swallow upside down. The stomach is a muscular, J-shaped bag on the left of the upper abdomen; it stores food for a few hours, churns it with its muscular walls and mixes it with gastric juice containing hydrochloric acid and an enzyme that begins protein digestion. The small intestine, about six metres long, is where most digestion is completed and almost all absorption happens; its first part, the duodenum, receives bile and pancreatic juice, and the long remaining part, the ileum, absorbs. The large intestine, about one and a half metres long and wider, includes the colon, which absorbs water and mineral salts from the remains, and the rectum, which stores faeces until they leave through the anus, a ring of muscle under voluntary control.

The accessory organs are connected to the canal by ducts, but food never passes through them. The salivary glands, three pairs around the mouth, produce about a litre or more of saliva a day, which moistens food and contains an enzyme that starts the digestion of starch. The liver, the largest internal organ, produces bile, which breaks fats into small droplets, among many other jobs. The gall bladder, a small sac under the liver, stores bile and releases it into the duodenum after a meal. The pancreas, a long gland behind the stomach, produces pancreatic juice, containing enzymes for starch, proteins and fats, and releases it into the duodenum.

For marking, the canal in order: mouth, pharynx, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, rectum, anus. The accessory organs with their products: salivary glands, saliva; liver, bile; gall bladder, stores bile; pancreas, pancreatic juice.

## Subtopic: Absorption, Assimilation and Egestion

By the time food reaches the ileum, the enzymes have reduced it to small, soluble molecules: glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol, together with vitamins, mineral salts and water. These must now cross the wall of the small intestine into the blood, and the ileum is adapted to make absorption fast and complete.

It is long, about six metres, giving the food several hours of contact. Its inner surface is folded, and the folds are covered with millions of tiny finger-like projections called villi, about one millimetre long, and the cells on each villus have their own microscopic projections, so that the total absorbing surface is roughly 30 square metres. Each villus has a wall only one cell thick, so the distance for absorption is short. Inside each villus is a dense network of capillaries, which carry away glucose and amino acids, and a lymph vessel, the lacteal, which carries away most of the fats. And the blood is always flowing, keeping the concentration inside the villus low so that absorption continues. These are the same principles you met in the alveoli: large surface area, thin walls and a rich blood supply.

The blood leaving the small intestine does not go straight to the heart. It travels first to the liver along a special vessel, the hepatic portal vein. The liver controls what passes on to the rest of the body: it stores excess glucose as glycogen, releases glucose when the blood level falls, breaks down excess amino acids, forming urea that the kidneys excrete, and removes many poisons, including alcohol. The absorbed molecules then reach every cell through the circulation, and their use by the cells is assimilation: glucose respired for energy, amino acids built into proteins for growth and repair, fats into membranes or stored under the skin.

What remains in the canal is mostly fibre, the cellulose of plant cell walls, which humans cannot digest, together with water, bacteria and dead cells shed from the gut lining. In the colon most of the water and salts are absorbed, about one and a half litres a day, so the remains become semi-solid faeces. Bacteria in the colon break down some of the fibre and produce certain vitamins, including vitamin K. The faeces are stored in the rectum and egested through the anus. Fibre adds bulk that the muscles of the gut can grip, which is why a high-fibre diet keeps food moving and prevents constipation.

## Subtopic: Health Issues of the Digestive System and the Error Museum

Tooth decay is caused by bacteria in the plaque on teeth, which feed on sugar and produce acid that dissolves the enamel; brushing twice a day with fluoride toothpaste and limiting sugary drinks and sweets between meals prevent it. Diarrhoea, the frequent passing of watery faeces, is usually caused by bacteria or viruses in contaminated water or food, such as cholera bacteria or rotavirus. It is dangerous because the body loses water and salts quickly, causing dehydration, which can kill a small child within a day. The treatment is to replace fluid at once with an oral rehydration solution, made from one litre of clean water, eight level teaspoons of sugar and half a level teaspoon of salt, to keep feeding and breastfeeding, and to go to a clinic if the child is drowsy, cannot drink or has blood in the stool. Prevention is clean water, toilets, washing hands with soap and the rotavirus vaccine given to all babies in South Africa.

Food poisoning comes from eating food contaminated with bacteria or their toxins; in 2017 and 2018 South Africa had the largest listeriosis outbreak ever recorded, traced to a processed meat factory, with more than a thousand cases and over 200 deaths. Constipation, infrequent and difficult passing of hard faeces, results mainly from too little fibre, too little water and too little exercise. Heartburn is a burning pain caused by stomach acid rising into the oesophagus. Stomach ulcers are open sores in the lining of the stomach or duodenum, caused mostly by infection with a bacterium and by long-term use of certain painkillers, and they are treated with antibiotics and acid-reducing medicine. Appendicitis is inflammation of the appendix, a small pouch at the start of the large intestine, causing pain that moves to the lower right of the abdomen; it needs urgent surgery before the appendix bursts. Liver disease, including cirrhosis, is caused by heavy drinking of alcohol and by hepatitis viruses. A diet low in fibre and high in processed meat raises the risk of cancer of the colon.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: digestion happens only in the stomach; it begins in the mouth and is completed in the small intestine, where almost all absorption also happens. Two: egestion is the same as excretion; egestion removes undigested food, excretion removes waste made by cells. Three: food passes through the liver and pancreas; they are accessory organs that pour their juices into the duodenum, and food never enters them. Four: stomach ulcers are caused by spicy food or stress; most are caused by a bacterium or by certain painkillers. Five: a child with diarrhoea should stop drinking to stop the diarrhoea; the child must drink more, using oral rehydration solution.

Layout for a disease question: cause, effect, treatment and prevention. The system is then described exactly as the memorandum expects.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same system again, through a meal's nine-metre journey, the helpers on the side, and what happens when the tube goes wrong — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Meal's Nine-Metre Journey

Take one mouthful of pap and beans and follow it. Your body is basically a long tube with you wrapped around it, from your mouth to your anus, about nine metres if it were stretched out, longer than a taxi. Food going through that tube is not yet really inside you; it only gets inside when it crosses the wall of the tube into your blood.

Step one, in the mouth: ingestion. Teeth chop and grind, saliva wets it, the tongue rolls it into a soft ball and pushes it back. Swallow. The ball drops into the oesophagus, a muscular pipe a little shorter than a 30-centimetre ruler, and rings of muscle squeeze behind it in a wave, peristalsis, like squeezing toothpaste along the tube. That is why you could swallow while standing on your head.

Step two, in the stomach, a stretchy muscular bag: it churns the food for a few hours in strong acid, like a washing machine, turning it into a thick soup.

Step three, in the small intestine, six metres of narrow, coiled tube: the real work. Juices from the helpers break the food into tiny pieces so small they dissolve: sugars, building blocks of protein, bits of fat. The inside wall is covered in millions of tiny fingers, the villi, like the pile of a towel, and that is where the tiny pieces cross into the blood. That crossing is absorption.

Step four, everywhere: the blood carries those pieces to every cell, and the cells use them, glucose for energy, protein pieces for building and repairs. That is assimilation.

Step five, in the large intestine: what is left is mostly fibre, the stringy plant stuff you cannot digest. The large intestine soaks up the water from it, so it becomes firmer, stores it at the end, in the rectum, and then it leaves through the anus. That is egestion. Not excretion: excretion is getting rid of waste your cells made, like urea and carbon dioxide. Egestion is getting rid of food you never used at all.

Five steps in order: take it in, break it down, soak it up, use it, push out the rest.

## Subtopic: The Helpers on the Side

Along the tube there are four helpers that never touch the food themselves. They make juices and pour them in through little pipes, like a kitchen passing sauces through a hatch.

The first helpers are the salivary glands, three pairs around your mouth and jaw, making a litre or more of spit every day. Saliva wets the food so you can swallow, and it carries a chemical that starts breaking down starch. Chew a piece of bread for a minute and it starts to taste sweet: that is starch becoming sugar in your mouth.

The second helper is the liver, the biggest organ inside your body, on the right under your ribs. It makes bile, a green liquid that acts like dishwashing liquid on fat, breaking big blobs of oil into tiny droplets so the fat-digesting chemicals can get at them. The liver has a second, huge job too: all the blood from the intestine goes to the liver first, like a checkpoint, where it stores extra sugar, removes poisons like alcohol and turns extra protein into urea for the kidneys.

The third helper is the gall bladder, a little bag under the liver that stores the bile and squirts it into the small intestine after a fatty meal.

The fourth helper is the pancreas, a long, soft gland tucked behind the stomach. It makes pancreatic juice, packed with chemicals that digest starch, protein and fat, and pours it into the start of the small intestine.

So the hatch into the start of the small intestine is a busy place: bile from the liver and gall bladder, juice from the pancreas, all landing on the soup that just came out of the stomach. That is why the start of the small intestine is where most of the breaking down is finished.

## Subtopic: When the Tube Goes Wrong

The tube can go wrong at almost every point, and most of it can be prevented.

At the very start, your teeth. Bacteria living on your teeth eat sugar and make acid, and the acid eats holes in the enamel: tooth decay. Brush twice a day with fluoride toothpaste and cut down on sugary drinks between meals.

Next, a burning feeling behind your chest after a big meal: heartburn, acid from the stomach splashing up into the oesophagus. An open sore in the stomach lining is an ulcer, and most are caused by a germ, not by chilli, and are cured with medicine.

The biggest killer of small children in this list is diarrhoea: runny, watery poo, again and again, usually from germs in dirty water or food. The danger is not the poo, it is the water and salt the body loses. A small child can dry out dangerously in a day. So the rule is the opposite of what people think: give more to drink, not less. The home recipe is one litre of clean water, eight level teaspoons of sugar and half a level teaspoon of salt, sips again and again. Keep breastfeeding. A child who is floppy, will not drink or has blood in the poo goes to the clinic at once. Clean water, a proper toilet, washing hands with soap, and the rotavirus vaccine prevent most of it.

Germs in food cause food poisoning too: South Africa once had the world's biggest listeriosis outbreak, from processed meat, with more than 200 deaths. Too little fibre, water and exercise causes constipation, hard poo that will not move. Sharp pain that moves to the lower right of the belly can be appendicitis, and that is a hospital emergency. Heavy drinking scars the liver.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Five processes: ingestion, digestion, absorption, assimilation, egestion. The canal in order: mouth, pharynx, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, rectum, anus. Helpers: salivary glands, liver making bile, gall bladder storing it, pancreas. Absorption through the villi of the small intestine; water taken back in the large intestine. Egestion is not excretion. Look after your teeth, eat fibre, drink clean water, wash hands, and rehydrate fast. Grade 10 and 11 Life Sciences build on all of this.
