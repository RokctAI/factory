# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains how food is broken down along the alimentary canal: mechanical digestion by the teeth, the tongue, the stomach and bile, and chemical digestion by enzymes, with the conditions each enzyme needs and the place where each acts. The anchor numbers are 32 teeth in an adult, a body temperature of 37 degrees at which human enzymes work best, a stomach pH of about 2, and a small intestine pH of about 7 to 8. The governing principle is that mechanical digestion increases the surface area of food so that enzymes can act faster, while chemical digestion breaks large, insoluble molecules into small, soluble ones that can be absorbed.

## Subtopic: Mechanical Digestion: Teeth, Tongue and Churning

Mechanical digestion is the physical breakdown of food into smaller pieces without changing its chemical nature. A piece of bread chewed into crumbs is still starch; it is simply in smaller pieces. The purpose is to increase the surface area of the food, so that more of it is exposed to enzymes at once, and to make it easier to swallow and move along the canal.

It begins with the teeth. An adult has 32 permanent teeth, replacing the 20 milk teeth of childhood, and they come in four types shaped for different jobs. The 8 incisors at the front are chisel-shaped and cut and bite food. The 4 canines beside them are pointed and tear food. The 8 premolars and 12 molars at the back have broad, ridged surfaces that crush and grind food. Each tooth has a crown above the gum covered with enamel, the hardest substance in the body, over a layer of dentine; at its centre is the pulp cavity with nerves and blood vessels, and the root is held in the jawbone.

The tongue is a muscular organ that moves food between the teeth while chewing, mixes it with saliva and shapes it into a soft ball, the bolus, which it pushes to the back of the mouth for swallowing. Saliva moistens and lubricates the food, which helps both chewing and swallowing.

In the stomach, three layers of muscle running in different directions contract in waves and churn the food, mixing it thoroughly with gastric juice and breaking it into a thick, creamy liquid called chyme. Peristalsis along the whole canal also mixes the contents as it moves them.

Bile, from the liver, carries out a special kind of mechanical digestion on fats. Fat does not mix with water and forms large globules. Bile acts like a detergent, breaking these globules into tiny droplets, a process called emulsification. The fat is chemically unchanged, but its surface area is enormously increased, so that the fat-digesting enzymes can work on it far faster. Bile contains no enzymes.

For marking: mechanical digestion is the physical breakdown of food into smaller pieces, to increase the surface area for enzymes; it is done by the teeth, the tongue, the churning of the stomach and the emulsifying of fats by bile.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four types of teeth and their jobs, the structure of a tooth, and why bile is mechanical, not chemical, digestion.

## Subtopic: Enzymes and Chemical Digestion

Chemical digestion is the breakdown of large, insoluble food molecules into small, soluble molecules by enzymes. An enzyme is a biological catalyst: a protein made by living cells that speeds up a particular chemical reaction without being used up in it. A single enzyme molecule can act on thousands of food molecules one after another, and without enzymes digestion at body temperature would take years.

Enzymes have three properties that are always examined. First, they are specific: each enzyme acts on only one kind of substance, its substrate, because the substrate must fit into a particular region of the enzyme, the active site, the way a key fits only its own lock. An enzyme that digests starch will not touch protein or fat. Second, each enzyme works best at a particular temperature, its optimum. For human enzymes the optimum is about 37 degrees, body temperature. At lower temperatures the reaction slows down because molecules move and collide less often, but the enzyme is not damaged and works again when warmed. At high temperatures, above roughly 45 to 60 degrees, the enzyme's shape is permanently changed and the substrate no longer fits; the enzyme is said to be denatured, and it does not recover on cooling. Third, each enzyme works best at a particular pH, its optimum pH, and stops working in conditions that are too acidic or too alkaline for it.

The digestive enzymes fall into three groups named after their substrates. Carbohydrases digest carbohydrates: amylase breaks starch into a sugar called maltose, and maltase breaks maltose into glucose. Proteases digest proteins into amino acids: pepsin in the stomach and trypsin from the pancreas begin the work, and other proteases in the small intestine finish it. Lipases digest fats and oils into fatty acids and glycerol.

So the three large food molecules become six small ones in three pairs: starch becomes glucose, proteins become amino acids, and fats become fatty acids and glycerol. These small molecules are soluble and small enough to pass through the wall of the small intestine into the blood.

For marking, an enzyme answer has four parts: it is a protein, it is a catalyst that is not used up, it is specific to one substrate, and it has an optimum temperature and pH, being denatured by high temperature.

## Subtopic: Digestion Along the Canal: Mouth, Stomach and Small Intestine

In the mouth, the salivary glands release saliva containing salivary amylase, which begins breaking starch into maltose. The mouth is close to neutral, about pH 7, which suits amylase, but food stays there for only seconds to a minute, so little starch is digested; you can taste it working if you chew bread for a long time and it begins to taste sweet.

In the stomach, the walls secrete gastric juice containing hydrochloric acid, which makes the stomach contents strongly acidic, about pH 2. The acid kills most bacteria swallowed with food and provides the right conditions for pepsin, a protease that begins breaking proteins into shorter chains. Salivary amylase is stopped by the acid, so starch digestion pauses here. A layer of mucus protects the stomach wall from its own acid and enzymes. Food stays in the stomach for about two to four hours, being churned into chyme, and is then released a little at a time into the small intestine through a ring of muscle at the stomach's exit.

In the duodenum, the first part of the small intestine, two secretions arrive through ducts. Bile from the gall bladder neutralises the acid from the stomach and emulsifies fats. Pancreatic juice from the pancreas contains sodium hydrogencarbonate, which helps to neutralise the acid, together with three enzymes: pancreatic amylase, which continues digesting starch to maltose; trypsin, a protease, which continues digesting proteins; and lipase, which digests the emulsified fat droplets into fatty acids and glycerol. The contents become slightly alkaline, about pH 7 to 8, the optimum for these enzymes.

Along the rest of the small intestine, enzymes made by the cells of the intestinal wall complete digestion: maltase turns maltose into glucose, and other proteases turn the short chains into amino acids. By the end of the ileum, the food has been reduced to glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol, and these are absorbed through the villi.

Set out as a table for marking: place, secretion, enzyme, substrate, product, pH. Mouth: saliva, amylase, starch to maltose, about 7. Stomach: gastric juice, pepsin, proteins to shorter chains, about 2, with hydrochloric acid. Duodenum: bile, no enzyme, emulsifies fat and neutralises acid; pancreatic juice, amylase, trypsin and lipase, about 7 to 8. Small intestine wall: maltase and proteases, completing digestion to glucose and amino acids.

This section's questions are available now: why amylase stops in the stomach, the jobs of hydrochloric acid, and what happens in the duodenum.

## Subtopic: Investigating Digestion and the Error Museum

The classic investigation tests the effect of temperature on the digestion of starch by amylase. The question is: how does temperature affect the rate at which amylase digests starch? The hypothesis might be: amylase digests starch fastest at about 37 degrees, more slowly at lower temperatures, and not at all at high temperatures.

The method: place equal volumes of starch solution in test tubes in water baths at several temperatures, for example 0, 20, 37 and 80 degrees, and let them reach temperature. Add an equal volume of amylase solution, or diluted saliva, to each and start a stopwatch. Every minute, remove a drop from each tube onto a spotting tile containing drops of iodine solution. While starch remains, the iodine turns blue-black; when all the starch has been digested, the iodine stays yellow-brown. Record the time taken for the starch to disappear at each temperature.

The variables must be named precisely. The independent variable, the one deliberately changed, is the temperature. The dependent variable, the one measured, is the time taken for the starch to disappear. The controlled variables, kept the same, include the volume and concentration of starch solution, the volume and concentration of amylase, the pH and the interval between tests. A control tube contains starch and water with no amylase, kept at 37 degrees; it stays blue-black throughout, showing that it is the amylase, not the temperature alone, that removes the starch.

A typical class result: at 37 degrees the starch disappears in about two minutes, at 20 degrees in about six minutes, at 0 degrees starch is still present after ten minutes, and at 80 degrees it never disappears. The conclusion: amylase works fastest near body temperature, slowly in the cold, and not at all after being heated to 80 degrees, because the enzyme has been denatured. Testing the tubes with Benedict's solution confirms that sugar has been formed where the starch disappeared.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: bile is an enzyme; bile contains no enzymes, it emulsifies fats and neutralises acid. Two: enzymes are killed by cold; cold only slows them, and high temperature denatures them. Three: chewing is chemical digestion; chewing is mechanical, while the amylase in saliva carries out chemical digestion. Four: the stomach digests starch; the acid stops amylase, and the stomach digests protein. Five: an enzyme is used up in the reaction; it is a catalyst and is used again and again.

Layout for an enzyme question: place, enzyme, substrate, product, conditions. Layout for an investigation question: aim, hypothesis, independent, dependent and controlled variables, control, results, conclusion. Digestion is then described exactly as the memorandum expects.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through scissors, millstones and a washing machine, locks and keys for molecules, and three big molecules that become three small ones — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Scissors, Millstones and a Washing Machine

Think of digestion as a kitchen with two kinds of tools. The first kind just chops things up smaller. That is mechanical digestion. The food is still the same stuff, only in smaller bits, and smaller bits have more outside surface for the second kind of tools to work on.

Your teeth are the first chopping tools. Run your tongue over them. At the front, flat, sharp incisors, like scissors, for biting off a piece of apple. Next to them, pointed canines, like hooks, for tearing tough meat. At the back, wide premolars and molars with bumpy tops, like millstones, for grinding mealies into mush. Adults have 32 of them; children have 20 milk teeth first. The outside of each tooth is enamel, the hardest material in your whole body, but even enamel is dissolved by the acid bacteria make from sugar.

Your tongue is the kitchen helper, pushing food between the teeth, mixing it with spit and rolling it into a soft ball for swallowing.

Your stomach is a washing machine. Its thick walls squeeze in different directions, sloshing the food around with acid juice for a few hours until it becomes a thick, smooth soup.

And bile is the dishwashing liquid. Fat floats in big blobs in watery food, like oil on a pot of soup. Bile breaks the big blobs into thousands of tiny droplets, which gives the fat-digesting chemicals far more surface to attack. Bile itself does not digest anything; it just breaks the blobs up.

So the chopping team is teeth, tongue, stomach churning and bile. They all do the same job: make the pieces smaller so the chemical team can work faster.

## Subtopic: Locks, Keys and Scissors for Molecules

The second kind of tool is chemical: enzymes. An enzyme is a protein your body makes that acts like a tiny pair of scissors for molecules, cutting big food molecules into small ones, over and over, without ever getting used up.

But each pair of scissors only cuts one thing. Think of a lock and a key: the enzyme has a special pocket, and only one kind of food molecule fits it. The enzyme that cuts starch is amylase, and it will not touch protein or fat. The enzymes that cut protein are proteases. The ones that cut fat are lipases. One enzyme, one job.

Enzymes are fussy about temperature. They work best at about 37 degrees, your body temperature. Put them in the cold and they slow right down, like you on a winter morning, but warm them up again and they are fine. Heat them too much, say 80 degrees, and their shape is ruined for good, like an egg white that has been cooked: it can never go back to being runny. We say the enzyme is denatured, and the key no longer fits the lock.

They are fussy about acid too. Amylase from your spit likes a neutral mouth and stops working in the strong acid of the stomach. Pepsin, the protein-cutter in your stomach, loves the acid. The enzymes in the small intestine like it slightly alkaline, which is why bile and pancreatic juice cancel out the stomach acid when the soup arrives.

You can watch amylase work. Mix starch with a little spit in warm water. Every minute put a drop onto iodine. At first the iodine turns blue-black, because starch is there. After a few minutes it stays brown: the starch is gone, cut into sugar. Do the same in ice and it takes much longer; do it in very hot water and it never happens. And a tube with starch and plain water, no spit, stays blue-black the whole time. That one is the control, proving it was the spit that did it.

Hold the locks, keys and scissors in mind and try this part's questions: what an enzyme is, why it only cuts one thing, and what heat and cold do.

## Subtopic: Three Big Molecules, Three Small Ones

Here is the whole story in one sentence: three big food molecules go in, and come out as small ones that can get into your blood.

Starch, from pap, bread and potatoes, is a long chain of sugar beads. Amylase in your spit starts cutting the chain in your mouth. In the stomach the acid stops it. In the small intestine, amylase from the pancreas carries on, and then enzymes on the gut wall finish it off. Starch ends as glucose.

Protein, from beans, eggs and meat, is a long chain of different beads called amino acids. Nothing happens to it in the mouth. In the stomach, pepsin and the acid start cutting it into shorter chains. In the small intestine, trypsin from the pancreas and other enzymes cut it right down to single beads. Protein ends as amino acids.

Fat, from oil and butter, gets nothing in the mouth and almost nothing in the stomach. In the small intestine bile breaks it into droplets, and lipase from the pancreas cuts each droplet into its parts. Fat ends as fatty acids and glycerol.

So: starch to glucose, protein to amino acids, fat to fatty acids and glycerol. Three big, three small. And every one of those small molecules is soluble, small enough to slip through the villi into the blood.

Notice where most of the work happens: the small intestine, the duodenum especially, where the soup from the stomach meets bile and pancreatic juice, the acid is cancelled out, and all three kinds of enzyme get to work at once.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Mechanical digestion makes pieces smaller: teeth, tongue, stomach churning, bile emulsifying fat. Chemical digestion breaks molecules with enzymes, which are specific proteins, work best at 37 degrees and their own pH, slow in cold and are denatured by heat. Mouth: amylase on starch. Stomach: acid at about pH 2 and pepsin on protein. Duodenum: bile, plus pancreatic amylase, trypsin and lipase. Gut wall: finishing enzymes. Ends: glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol. Grade 10 and 11 Life Sciences go deeper into enzymes.

Hold the three big and three small in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: where each food is digested, the end products, and where most digestion happens.
