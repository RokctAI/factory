### subtopic_1 — Components of the Respiratory System
**Assistant:** Why does the trachea need cartilage rings when the oesophagus right next to it has none?

**Tutor:** Because the two tubes carry different things under different conditions, and the design of each matches its job. The oesophagus carries food, and it does so by squeezing: waves of muscle contraction push each swallowed lump down to the stomach, so its walls must be able to collapse completely and bulge open as the food passes. Between swallows it is a flattened, closed tube, and that is fine, because nothing needs to flow through it continuously. The trachea carries air, and air must flow freely every few seconds for your whole life, including while you sleep, bend your neck, swallow or get squeezed in a crowd. A soft tube would be pinched shut the moment pressure fell inside it during inhalation or a neighbour's elbow pressed on your throat. The C-shaped cartilage rings act like the wire in a vacuum-cleaner hose, holding the airway permanently open while leaving it flexible enough to bend. The rings are open at the back, where the trachea lies against the oesophagus, so that a large mouthful passing down the oesophagus can bulge into that gap without the two tubes fighting for space. The same reasoning explains why the bronchi also have cartilage and why the finest bronchioles, which are held open by the elastic pull of the surrounding lung tissue, do not; in asthma it is precisely those muscular, cartilage-free bronchioles that clamp down.

### subtopic_2 — The Mechanism of Breathing
**Assistant:** If the chest gets bigger and pressure drops, what is actually pushing the air into my lungs?

**Tutor:** The atmosphere is. Air at the surface of the Earth is under the weight of the whole column of air above it, a pressure of about one hundred kilopascals, and that pressure pushes in every direction on everything, including into any opening where the pressure is lower. At the end of an exhalation the air inside your lungs is at the same pressure as the room, so nothing moves. When the diaphragm flattens and the ribs lift, the chest cavity enlarges and the elastic lungs are stretched with it, so the same amount of air now occupies a bigger space and its pressure falls, by only a fraction of a percent, but that is enough. The air in the room is now at a slightly higher pressure than the air in your lungs, and higher pressure pushes toward lower pressure, so room air flows down your airway until the two are equal again. You feel it as drawing a breath, but no suction exists; you simply made a low-pressure space and the atmosphere filled it. The bell-jar model shows this cleanly: pulling the rubber sheet down inflates the balloons although nothing touches them. A puncture wound to the chest wall proves it the hard way, because air then enters the chest around the lung instead of into it and the lung collapses. In an answer, name the muscles, say volume increases, say pressure decreases below atmospheric, and then say air moves in; that chain is what the memorandum checks.

### subtopic_3 — Inhaled and Exhaled Air
**Assistant:** Why does exhaled air still have 16 percent oxygen? Why do the lungs not take all of it?

**Tutor:** Because gas exchange works by diffusion, and diffusion stops when the concentrations on both sides are equal, not when one side is empty. Oxygen crosses from the air in the alveolus into the blood only as long as there is more oxygen in the alveolus than in the blood arriving from the body, and that blood is not oxygen-free; it still carries a good deal of oxygen that the tissues did not need to take. So the gradient is modest and the transfer during the half second or so that blood spends in a lung capillary brings the two sides close to balance, which leaves the alveolar air at roughly 14 percent oxygen. Each breath also mixes only about half a litre of fresh air into the two or three litres already sitting in the lungs, so the air you exhale is a blend of fresh and stale, which lifts the figure to about 16 percent. The system is built for reliability rather than for squeezing out every molecule: with 16 percent still in reserve, a person can hold their breath, climb a hill or survive a chest infection with margin to spare, and when demand rises in exercise the body simply breathes faster and deeper rather than extracting more from each breath. The practical consequence you should know is that exhaled air is rich enough in oxygen to keep an unconscious person alive during mouth-to-mouth resuscitation, and the conceptual one is that breathing in and out does not swap oxygen for carbon dioxide wholesale; it shifts the proportions by about five percentage points each.

### subtopic_4 — Health of the Respiratory System and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why do TB patients have to take the pills for six months when a chest infection clears in a week?

**Tutor:** Because the TB bacterium is slow, well protected and expert at hiding, so killing every last one takes time. Most bacteria that cause a chest infection divide every twenty minutes or so, and antibiotics that attack dividing cells wipe them out within days. The TB bacterium divides only about once a day and has a thick, waxy coat that drugs penetrate slowly, so each dose kills only a fraction of the population and the course has to be long. Worse, some of the bacteria sit dormant inside the body's own defensive cells or walled up in scar tissue in the lung, barely dividing at all, and they are reached only by months of steady drug levels. If the patient stops after a few weeks, feeling better because the fast-dividing bacteria are dead, the slow survivors multiply again, and because any bacteria with a slight natural resistance are the ones most likely to have survived, the relapse is often resistant to the drugs that were used. That is how multidrug-resistant TB arises, and it needs up to two years of harsher, more expensive medicines with a far lower cure rate. For these reasons TB is treated with a combination of four antibiotics for the first two months and two for the next four, and clinics in South Africa use supervised treatment, where a nurse or community health worker watches each dose, to make sure the course is finished. The lesson for the examiner is cause, effect and treatment: a bacterium spread by coughing, destruction of lung tissue, and six months of daily antibiotics completed in full.
