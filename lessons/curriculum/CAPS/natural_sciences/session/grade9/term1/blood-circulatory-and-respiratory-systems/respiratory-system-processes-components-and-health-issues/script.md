# Part 1 — Expert

This session describes the components, processes and health issues of the respiratory system: the structures of the airway from nose to alveoli, the mechanism of breathing in and out, the differences between inhaled and exhaled air, and the diseases of the system and their prevention. The anchor numbers are about 15 breaths per minute at rest, each moving about half a litre of air, and an oxygen content that falls from 21 percent in inhaled air to about 16 percent in exhaled air. The governing distinction is that breathing is the movement of air into and out of the lungs, while respiration is the release of energy from glucose in cells; this lesson is about breathing, and the next joins the two.

## Subtopic: Components of the Respiratory System

Air enters through the nose into the nasal cavity, which is lined with hairs and a moist membrane that produces mucus. The hairs and mucus trap dust and microbes, the rich blood supply warms the air to body temperature and the moist surface humidifies it, so that air reaching the delicate lungs is clean, warm and damp. Breathing through the mouth bypasses all three services, which is why mouth-breathing in cold weather makes the throat sore.

From the nasal cavity air passes through the pharynx, the throat, shared with food, and then the larynx, the voice box, where a flap called the epiglottis closes the airway during swallowing so that food goes down the oesophagus and not into the lungs. Below the larynx is the trachea, the windpipe, about 12 centimetres long and held open by C-shaped rings of cartilage that keep it from collapsing when you bend your neck or swallow; you can feel the rings at the front of your throat. The trachea is lined with cells bearing microscopic hairs, cilia, and with mucus-producing cells; the mucus traps particles and the cilia beat upward to carry it to the throat, where it is swallowed. This is the airway's self-cleaning system.

The trachea divides into two bronchi, one to each lung, which divide repeatedly into smaller bronchioles, like the branches of an upside-down tree. The finest bronchioles end in clusters of alveoli, tiny thin-walled air sacs, about 300 million of them in an adult, each surrounded by a net of capillaries. Together they give a surface for gas exchange of about 70 square metres, half a tennis court folded into your chest. The alveoli are the business end of the system and the next lesson studies their adaptations in detail.

The two lungs sit in the chest cavity, protected by the rib cage. Between the ribs lie the intercostal muscles, and beneath the lungs stretches the diaphragm, a dome-shaped sheet of muscle separating the chest from the abdomen. The lungs themselves have no muscle; they are moved by the diaphragm and the rib muscles, which is the subject of the next section. The order of structures for marking: nasal cavity, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, alveoli.

The questions for this section are with you now: the three services of the nose, the cartilage rings and cilia of the trachea, and the airway in order.

## Subtopic: The Mechanism of Breathing

Breathing, also called ventilation, is the movement of air into and out of the lungs, and it works by changing the volume of the chest, because gas flows from where its pressure is higher to where it is lower. The lungs are elastic bags that follow the walls of the chest; make the chest bigger and the lungs expand, lowering the pressure inside them below the pressure of the air outside, so air flows in. Make the chest smaller and the reverse happens.

Inhalation is active. The diaphragm contracts and flattens from its dome shape, pulling the floor of the chest downward. At the same time the external intercostal muscles contract and lift the rib cage upward and outward. Both movements increase the volume of the chest cavity, the pressure inside the lungs falls below atmospheric pressure, and air flows in through the airway until the pressures are equal. A resting breath draws in about half a litre.

Exhalation at rest is passive. The diaphragm relaxes and returns to its dome, the intercostal muscles relax and the ribs fall, the elastic lungs recoil, the volume of the chest decreases, the pressure inside rises above atmospheric pressure and air flows out. In forced exhalation, as when blowing out a candle or exercising hard, a second set of intercostal muscles and the abdominal muscles contract to squeeze the chest smaller still.

The sequence must be written in the right order for marks: muscle action, change in volume, change in pressure, direction of air flow. Inhalation: diaphragm contracts and flattens, intercostals contract and ribs rise, volume increases, pressure decreases, air moves in. Exhalation: diaphragm relaxes and domes, ribs fall, volume decreases, pressure increases, air moves out. The classic model is a bell jar with a rubber sheet for the diaphragm and balloons for the lungs; pull the sheet down and the balloons inflate. Breathing rate at rest is about 12 to 20 breaths per minute, controlled by the medulla, which responds chiefly to the level of carbon dioxide in the blood.

Take this section's questions now: volume and pressure, the diaphragm and intercostals in each phase, and the bell-jar model.

## Subtopic: Inhaled and Exhaled Air

The air you breathe in is about 78 percent nitrogen, 21 percent oxygen, 0,04 percent carbon dioxide, with a variable amount of water vapour and a trace of other gases. The air you breathe out is still about 78 percent nitrogen, because nitrogen is neither used nor made, but its oxygen has fallen to about 16 percent and its carbon dioxide has risen to about 4 percent, a hundred-fold increase. Exhaled air is also saturated with water vapour and is at body temperature, about 37 degrees, which is why your breath mists a cold window and fogs a mirror.

Two points of care. The lungs do not remove all the oxygen; only about a quarter of it, from 21 down to 16 percent, which is why mouth-to-mouth resuscitation works, since the rescuer's exhaled air still carries plenty of oxygen. And the change is a swap, not a disappearance: the oxygen that left the air entered the blood, and the carbon dioxide that appeared came out of the blood, having been produced by respiring cells all over the body.

The standard test for carbon dioxide is lime water, which turns from clear to milky when carbon dioxide bubbles through it. Blow through a straw into one tube of lime water while a pump draws room air through a second, and the tube receiving exhaled air turns milky far sooner. Cobalt chloride paper turns from blue to pink in moisture and shows the extra water vapour; a thermometer shows the warmth. Each test matches one difference, and a practical question asks for the test, the result and the conclusion together.

In exercise the breathing rate and the depth both increase, so that more oxygen is loaded and more carbon dioxide unloaded per minute, and the percentage of carbon dioxide in exhaled air rises further. The breathing rate is measured by counting breaths in one minute at rest and again after exercise, and a comparison of the two is the Grade 9 practical investigation for this topic.

This section's questions are available now: the three percentages that change and the one that does not, the lime water test, and why exhaled air can still revive a person.

## Subtopic: Health of the Respiratory System and the Error Museum

Asthma is a condition in which the muscles of the bronchioles tighten and the lining swells and produces extra mucus, narrowing the airways so that breathing, especially out, becomes wheezy and difficult. Triggers include dust, pollen, cold air, smoke and exercise. An inhaler delivers a drug that relaxes the airway muscles within minutes, and a preventer inhaler reduces the swelling over time; an asthma attack that does not respond is a medical emergency. Bronchitis is inflammation of the bronchi with a cough and mucus; pneumonia is infection of the alveoli, which fill with fluid so that gas exchange fails, and it kills many young children and old people, though vaccines and antibiotics prevent and treat it.

Tuberculosis, TB, is an infection of the lungs by a bacterium spread in droplets when an infected person coughs. It destroys lung tissue, causing a cough lasting weeks, night sweats, weight loss and blood in the sputum, and it is one of the leading causes of death in South Africa, made worse by crowded living and by HIV, which weakens the body's defences. TB is curable with a course of antibiotics taken daily for six months; stopping early allows resistant bacteria to survive, and drug-resistant TB is far harder to treat. The BCG vaccine given at birth gives partial protection.

Smoking damages every part of the system. Tar coats the airways, paralyses the cilia so that mucus and dirt are no longer swept out, and causes the smoker's cough; over years it causes cancer of the lungs, mouth and throat. The hot smoke destroys the walls of alveoli so they merge into fewer, larger sacs with less surface area, the disease emphysema, in which the sufferer is permanently short of breath. Nicotine is the addictive drug and raises heart rate and blood pressure; carbon monoxide binds to haemoglobin and cuts the oxygen the blood can carry. Children in smoking households have more asthma and chest infections. Not starting is the single best decision for the respiratory system, and quitting at any age helps.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: breathing is respiration; breathing moves air, respiration releases energy in cells. Two: the lungs pump themselves; they have no muscle and are moved by the diaphragm and intercostals. Three: exhaled air contains no oxygen; it still holds about 16 percent. Four: we breathe in oxygen and breathe out carbon dioxide; we breathe in and out air, in which the proportions change. Five: air enters the lungs because the chest pushes it; the chest enlarges, pressure falls and air is pushed in by the higher pressure outside.

Layout for a breathing question: muscles, volume, pressure, air flow, in that order. Layout for a disease question: cause, effect on the system, prevention or treatment. Three items each, and the system that keeps you alive between heartbeats is described exactly as the memorandum expects.

The questions for this section are ready: asthma and its inhaler, TB and six months of treatment, the three damages of smoking, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same system again, through an upside-down tree, a balloon in a bottle, and a kitchen window on a cold morning — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: An Upside-Down Tree

Picture a tree, then turn it upside down and put it inside your chest. The trunk is your trachea, the windpipe, running down from your throat. Feel the front of your neck: those ridges are rings of cartilage, like the rings of a vacuum-cleaner hose, keeping the pipe open so it never squashes shut when you turn your head. The trunk splits into two big branches, the bronchi, one into each lung. Each branch splits again and again into thinner and thinner twigs, the bronchioles, and at the very tip of every twig hangs a tiny bunch of grapes. Those grapes are the alveoli, the air sacs, about 300 million of them, and every grape is wrapped in a net of the finest blood vessels. Flatten all the grapes out and they would cover half a tennis court. That is where the air meets the blood.

Before air even reaches the trunk it goes through the nose, and the nose does three jobs. Hairs and sticky mucus catch dust and germs, like a filter. Blood vessels warm the air. The wet lining dampens it. So the air arriving at the grapes is clean, warm and moist. Breathe through your mouth on a winter morning and you skip all three, and your throat knows it.

The tree cleans itself too. The inside of the trunk and branches is coated in mucus and lined with millions of tiny hairs, cilia, that wave upward like a crowd doing a slow wave, pushing the dirty mucus up to the throat where you swallow it without noticing. Smoking paralyses those hairs, and that is what the smoker's cough is: the body trying to do by force what the hairs no longer do.

The tree has one more trick at the top: a trapdoor, the epiglottis, that slams over the windpipe every time you swallow so food goes down the other tube to the stomach. Talk while eating and you may feel what happens when the timing slips.

Keep the tree in mind and try the questions for this part: trunk, branches, twigs and grapes in order, the three jobs of the nose, and the waving hairs.

## Subtopic: The Balloon in the Bottle

The lungs have no muscles of their own. They are two soft, stretchy bags that simply follow the walls of the box they sit in. So how does air get in? By making the box bigger. Here is the model. Take a plastic bottle, cut the bottom off, stretch a rubber sheet across the open bottom, and hang a balloon inside from the neck. The bottle is your chest, the rubber sheet is your diaphragm, the balloon is a lung. Pull the sheet down: the space inside gets bigger, the air inside is spread thinner so its pressure drops, and the higher-pressure air outside pushes in through the neck and the balloon swells. Let the sheet go: it springs back, the space shrinks, the pressure rises, and the air is pushed out.

That is a breath. Breathing in: the diaphragm, the dome of muscle under your lungs, contracts and flattens downward, and the muscles between your ribs pull the rib cage up and out. The chest gets bigger, the pressure inside drops, air flows in. About half a litre, a bottle of cold drink, per quiet breath. Breathing out at rest takes no effort: the diaphragm relaxes back into its dome, the ribs drop, the stretchy lungs shrink, the pressure rises, air flows out. To blow out a candle or empty your lungs hard, extra muscles squeeze the chest even smaller.

Say the four steps in order every time, because the order is the marks: muscles, then volume, then pressure, then air. In: diaphragm contracts and flattens, ribs rise, volume up, pressure down, air in. Out: diaphragm relaxes and domes, ribs fall, volume down, pressure up, air out. Nobody sucks air in; the chest gets bigger and the outside air pushes itself in.

Put your hands on your ribs and breathe deep. You will feel them rise and swing out. Then count your breaths for a minute sitting still, somewhere around 15, and again after running on the spot, and watch the number double; that is your brain stem noticing the extra carbon dioxide in your blood and turning up the pump.

Hold the bottle and the balloon in mind and try this part's questions: the sheet and the balloon, muscles-volume-pressure-air, and why nobody sucks.

## Subtopic: The Window on a Cold Morning

Breathe on a cold window and it fogs. Breathe on a mirror and it mists. Your breath is wet and warm, and it is also different from the air you took in. Air going in is about 78 percent nitrogen, 21 percent oxygen and a tiny 0,04 percent carbon dioxide. Air coming out is still 78 percent nitrogen, because your body does nothing with nitrogen, but the oxygen has dropped to about 16 percent and the carbon dioxide has shot up to about 4 percent, a hundred times more, and it is warm and full of water vapour. The oxygen did not vanish; it went into your blood. The carbon dioxide did not appear from nowhere; it came out of your blood, made by every cell in your body.

Notice that you only use about a quarter of the oxygen you breathe in. There is still 16 percent in the air you breathe out, which is why breathing into someone's mouth in first aid actually helps them. The test for the carbon dioxide is lime water, clear liquid that turns milky when carbon dioxide bubbles through it. Blow through a straw into one tube and watch it cloud over far faster than a tube getting room air.

Now the things that go wrong, and most of them are about the tree getting blocked or damaged. Asthma: the twigs tighten and swell up and fill with mucus, so air whistles and the person fights to breathe out; a puffer relaxes the twigs in minutes. Pneumonia: the grapes fill with fluid and infection, and the air cannot reach the blood. TB: a germ spread by coughing eats away lung tissue; it is one of the biggest killers in South Africa, and it is cured by taking antibiotics every day for six months, every single day, because stopping early breeds a germ the medicine cannot kill. Smoking: tar paralyses the waving hairs and gives you the cough, then cancer; the hot smoke pops the grapes so they merge into big baggy sacs with less surface, emphysema, and the person is out of breath forever; carbon monoxide steals the oxygen seats on your red cells.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Nose cleans, warms and moistens. Trachea with cartilage rings, bronchi, bronchioles, alveoli with capillaries round them. Breathe in: diaphragm flattens, ribs rise, volume up, pressure down, air in; breathe out is the reverse. Oxygen 21 to 16 percent, carbon dioxide 0,04 to 4 percent, nitrogen unchanged, out is warm and wet. Asthma, pneumonia, TB, and everything smoking does. Hold the fogged window and the lime water, and take the final questions of the lesson: the percentages, the tests, and the diseases with their causes.
