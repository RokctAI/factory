Hey — if trachea, bronchioles, alveoli and intercostal muscles felt like a tongue-twister, here is the calm version: an upside-down tree and a balloon in a bottle.

The tree: the nose cleans, warms and moistens the air. Then the trunk, the trachea, held open by cartilage rings and lined with waving cilia that sweep dirt up. Two branches, the bronchi. Thousands of twigs, the bronchioles. Bunches of grapes at the tips, the alveoli, 300 million of them wrapped in capillaries, where the air meets the blood.

The bottle: the lungs are balloons with no muscle. Breathe in: diaphragm contracts and flattens, rib muscles lift the ribs, chest volume up, pressure down, air pushed in. Breathe out: diaphragm relaxes and domes, ribs fall, volume down, pressure up, air out. Say it in that order: muscles, volume, pressure, air.

The window: exhaled air has oxygen down from 21 to 16 percent, carbon dioxide up from 0,04 to 4 percent, nitrogen unchanged, warm and wet. Lime water goes milky with carbon dioxide.

The diseases: asthma narrows the twigs; pneumonia floods the grapes; TB eats the tissue and needs six months of antibiotics; smoking paralyses the cilia and bursts the alveoli.

Open the practice set, tree then bottle. You've got this!
