### subtopic_1 — Breathing, Gas Exchange and Respiration Are Three Things
**Assistant:** If a plant does not breathe, how does it get oxygen for respiration?

**Tutor:** By diffusion straight through its surfaces, which works for a plant because no part of a plant is far from air. Leaves are thin and are riddled with tiny pores called stomata that open into air spaces between the cells, so every leaf cell sits a fraction of a millimetre from air and oxygen simply diffuses in while carbon dioxide diffuses out, exactly as in an alveolus but without any pumping. Stems and roots have similar openings and air spaces, and roots draw oxygen from the air trapped between soil particles, which is why plants in waterlogged soil drown. A plant can manage without breathing for two reasons: its cells respire slowly compared with an animal's, so they need far less oxygen per minute, and its body is spread thin, with a huge surface relative to its volume, so diffusion alone keeps up. An animal cannot do this. A human has a compact body with most cells buried centimetres deep in tissue where no air could ever reach by diffusion, and muscles that need oxygen hundreds of times faster than a leaf, so it must fold a huge exchange surface into the chest, ventilate it with a muscular pump and run a circulation to deliver the oxygen inward. The point for the examiner is that respiration is universal, every living cell does it, while breathing is a solution some animals use to supply it; a plant respires without breathing, a fish breathes with gills, and only the mitochondria are the same in all three.

### subtopic_2 — Gaseous Exchange in the Alveoli
**Assistant:** Why does oxygen move into the blood in the lungs but out of the blood in the muscles? It is the same blood.

**Tutor:** Because diffusion follows the concentration gradient, and the gradient points in opposite directions in the two places. In the alveoli the air is rich in oxygen, about 14 percent after mixing, and the blood arriving through the pulmonary artery is poor in it, having given much of its oxygen to the tissues, so oxygen diffuses from air into blood. In a working muscle the situation reverses: the mitochondria are consuming oxygen constantly, so the fluid around the cells has very little, while the blood arriving through the arteries is loaded, so oxygen diffuses from blood into the cells. Haemoglobin is built to cooperate with both gradients. Where oxygen is plentiful it binds oxygen eagerly, and where oxygen is scarce it lets go readily, so the red cells fill up in the lungs and empty in the tissues without any instruction. Carbon dioxide runs the same logic the other way: high around respiring cells, so it diffuses into the blood; higher in that blood than in fresh alveolar air, so it diffuses out in the lungs. Nothing decides where the gases go; the gradients do, and the two things that keep the gradients steep are breathing, which refreshes the air, and respiration, which consumes oxygen and makes carbon dioxide. A test question that asks why oxygen moves in a particular direction is answered with the two concentrations and the word diffusion, never with the heart or the lungs pushing.

### subtopic_3 — Transport by the Blood
**Assistant:** What exactly is the energy that respiration releases, and where does it go?

**Tutor:** It is chemical energy that was stored in the bonds of the glucose molecule, and it is transferred into a small carrier molecule that the cell spends like cash. Glucose is a stable molecule with a lot of energy locked in the arrangement of its atoms; when it is combined with oxygen the atoms rearrange into carbon dioxide and water, which hold much less energy, and the difference is released. If you burn sugar in a flame all of that difference comes out at once as heat and light. The mitochondrion does the same overall reaction in about thirty small steps, each releasing a little energy, and at several of those steps the energy is used to build a molecule called ATP from its spent form. ATP then diffuses to wherever work is needed and is broken back to its spent form, handing over its packet of energy to a muscle protein that contracts, a pump protein that moves salts across a membrane, or an enzyme that assembles a new protein. A resting person turns over roughly their own body mass in ATP every day, rebuilt and spent many times over. Not all the energy is captured; about 60 percent escapes as heat, which is why you are warm and why exercise makes you hot. That is also why the memorandum insists on release rather than make: energy already present in glucose is moved, in stages, into ATP and then into work and heat. The glucose itself got its energy from sunlight through photosynthesis, so the sprint for the taxi is ultimately powered by the sun via the mealie in your breakfast.

### subtopic_4 — Cellular Respiration and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why does the lactic acid build up only when I sprint and not when I walk?

**Tutor:** Because the two activities sit on opposite sides of the rate at which your body can deliver oxygen. Walking uses oxygen slowly, and the breathing and circulation can supply it faster than the muscles consume it, so every glucose molecule is respired aerobically all the way to carbon dioxide and water, with the maximum energy released and nothing left over; you can walk for hours. A sprint asks the leg muscles for perhaps twenty times more energy per second than walking does, and the supply chain cannot scale that fast: breathing and heart rate take half a minute or more to climb, and even at their maximum they deliver less oxygen than an all-out sprint demands. The muscle cells make up the shortfall by running the first stage of respiration without oxygen, which releases only about a twentieth of the energy per glucose but releases it quickly, and the product of that stage, instead of entering the mitochondria, is converted to lactic acid. The acid accumulates in the muscle and leaks into the blood, interfering with the contraction machinery and causing the burning, heavy feeling that forces you to slow down within a minute or two. A fit person tolerates a faster pace before this happens because their heart pumps more per beat and their muscles hold more mitochondria and capillaries, so the aerobic ceiling is higher. After the sprint the lactic acid is carried to the liver and converted back to glucose using oxygen, which is the oxygen debt that keeps you panting on the taxi seat. So the difference is not the activity but whether oxygen arrives as fast as it is needed.
