# Part 1 — Expert

This session describes the components, processes and health issues of the blood circulatory system: the composition and functions of blood, the structure of the heart and the double circulation, the three types of blood vessel, and the diseases of the system and their prevention. The anchor figures are about 5 litres of blood, a resting heart rate near 70 beats per minute and a healthy blood pressure of about 120 over 80. The governing idea is transport: every substance that moves between one part of the body and another, oxygen, glucose, carbon dioxide, urea, hormones and heat, travels in the blood, and the heart and vessels exist to keep it moving.

## Subtopic: Blood and Its Components

Blood is a liquid tissue with four components. Plasma is the pale yellow liquid, about 55 percent of the volume, mostly water with dissolved glucose, amino acids, salts, proteins, hormones, urea and carbon dioxide; it is the medium in which everything else is carried. Red blood cells, about 45 percent of the volume and some five million in every cubic millimetre, are the dented discs without a nucleus met in the cells topic; they are packed with haemoglobin, which binds oxygen in the lungs to form bright red oxyhaemoglobin and releases it in the tissues, where the blood becomes darker red. They are made in the red bone marrow and last about 120 days.

White blood cells are far fewer, about one for every 700 red cells, and larger, with a nucleus. They defend the body: some engulf and digest bacteria, others produce antibodies, chemicals that mark particular invaders for destruction and give immunity after an infection or a vaccination. Platelets are tiny fragments of cells, without a nucleus, that gather at a cut and trigger clotting, in which a mesh of protein fibres traps cells to form a plug that stops bleeding and hardens into a scab.

The functions of blood follow from its components, and the examiner wants four. Transport: oxygen in red cells; glucose, amino acids, hormones, urea and carbon dioxide in the plasma. Defence: white blood cells against infection. Clotting: platelets sealing wounds. Regulation of temperature: blood carries heat from the muscles and liver to the skin, where it is lost, keeping the body near 37 degrees Celsius. An adult has about 5 litres of blood, roughly 7 percent of body mass, and can safely donate 450 millilitres at a time, which the South African National Blood Service collects for operations, accident victims and mothers who bleed at birth.

For marking, pair each component with its function: plasma transports dissolved substances, red cells carry oxygen, white cells fight infection, platelets clot blood. Four pairs, four marks, and the liquid tissue is complete.

The questions for this section are with you now: the four components, the four functions, and haemoglobin.

## Subtopic: The Heart and the Double Circulation

The heart is a muscular pump about the size of a fist, with a mass near 300 grams, lying between the lungs slightly to the left of centre and protected by the rib cage. Its wall is cardiac muscle, which contracts rhythmically without tiring for a lifetime. It has four chambers: two thin-walled atria at the top that receive blood, and two thick-walled ventricles below that pump it out. Valves between each atrium and ventricle, and at the exit of each ventricle, allow blood to flow in one direction only, and their snapping shut makes the two sounds of the heartbeat.

The circulation is double: blood passes through the heart twice on each complete circuit. The right atrium receives deoxygenated blood from the body through the vena cava and passes it to the right ventricle, which pumps it through the pulmonary artery to the lungs, where it loses carbon dioxide and gains oxygen. The pulmonary vein returns oxygenated blood to the left atrium, which passes it to the left ventricle, which pumps it through the aorta to the whole body. The right side serves the short pulmonary circuit; the left side serves the long systemic circuit, and the wall of the left ventricle is therefore about three times thicker, because it must drive blood as far as the toes.

A septum of muscle divides the right side from the left so that oxygenated and deoxygenated blood never mix, which is the advantage of a double circulation: blood reaching the body is fully loaded with oxygen, and it reaches the body at the high pressure the left ventricle provides, having been re-pumped after the lungs. A fish, with a single circulation, cannot do this.

Each contraction of the ventricles is a heartbeat, and the heart rate at rest is about 60 to 100 beats per minute, typically around 70, rising to 150 or more in hard exercise. At 70 beats per minute the heart beats about 100 000 times a day and moves about 5 litres a minute at rest, so the entire blood volume passes through it roughly once a minute. The order of chambers and vessels is a standard question: vena cava, right atrium, right ventricle, pulmonary artery, lungs, pulmonary vein, left atrium, left ventricle, aorta, body, and back to the vena cava.

## Subtopic: Arteries, Veins and Capillaries

Blood travels in three types of vessel, and each is built for its position in the circuit. Arteries carry blood away from the heart. They receive blood at high pressure with every beat, so they have thick, muscular, elastic walls that stretch and recoil, which is the pulse you feel at the wrist and neck, and they have a relatively narrow lumen. The aorta leaving the left ventricle is about 2,5 centimetres wide, the largest artery; arteries branch into smaller arteries and then arterioles as they approach the tissues.

Capillaries are the smallest vessels, about 8 micrometres wide, so narrow that red blood cells pass through in single file, and their walls are a single layer of cells. They form dense networks in every tissue, and across their thin walls exchange takes place: oxygen and glucose diffuse out to the cells, carbon dioxide and wastes diffuse in. No cell in the body is more than a few cells away from a capillary. The total length of capillaries in an adult is tens of thousands of kilometres.

Veins carry blood back towards the heart. By the time blood has passed through the capillaries its pressure is low and steady, so veins have thinner walls with less muscle and a wider lumen, and they contain valves that stop blood flowing backwards under gravity; the squeezing of the leg muscles during walking pushes venous blood up to the heart, which is why standing still for hours makes ankles swell and why long-distance passengers are told to move their legs. Varicose veins are veins whose valves have failed.

The rule that arteries carry oxygenated blood and veins deoxygenated blood holds for the systemic circuit but has two exceptions that the examiner loves: the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle to the lungs, and the pulmonary vein carries oxygenated blood from the lungs to the left atrium. Define arteries and veins by direction, away from and towards the heart, never by oxygen content, and the exceptions cause no trouble. The comparison for marking: wall thickness, lumen size, pressure, valves, direction and function, set out in a three-column table.

## Subtopic: Health of the Circulatory System and the Error Museum

Blood pressure is the force of the blood against the artery walls, measured in millimetres of mercury as two numbers: the higher reading when the ventricles contract and the lower when they relax. A healthy adult reading is about 120 over 80. Persistently high blood pressure, 140 over 90 or more, is hypertension; it strains the heart and damages the arteries, the kidneys and the brain, usually without symptoms, which is why it is called the silent killer and why clinic visits include a cuff on the arm. Causes include too much salt, excess body mass, lack of exercise, smoking, alcohol and inherited tendency, and it is extremely common among South African adults.

A heart attack occurs when a coronary artery, one of the small arteries supplying the heart muscle itself, is blocked, usually by a clot forming on a fatty deposit of cholesterol in the artery wall, so that part of the heart muscle dies from lack of oxygen. A stroke is the same event in an artery of the brain. The fatty deposits build up over years from a diet high in saturated fat and from smoking, which also narrows arteries and raises blood pressure. Anaemia is a shortage of red blood cells or haemoglobin, most often from too little iron in the diet, and it causes tiredness and breathlessness because the blood carries less oxygen; red meat, liver, beans and dark green leaves supply iron.

Prevention is a matter of choices, and the syllabus expects them listed. Regular exercise strengthens the heart and lowers blood pressure. A diet low in salt and saturated fat and rich in vegetables, fruit and fibre keeps the arteries clear. Not smoking is the single largest avoidable factor. Keeping a healthy body mass and limiting alcohol protect the pressure. Blood donation does no harm to a healthy adult and saves lives: the South African National Blood Service needs about 3 000 donations a day.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: all arteries carry oxygenated blood; the pulmonary artery does not. Two: deoxygenated blood is blue; it is dark red, and veins look blue only through the skin. Three: the heart makes blood; the bone marrow makes blood cells and the heart only pumps. Four: the atria pump blood to the body; the ventricles pump, the atria receive. Five: blood pressure of 120 over 80 means 120 beats per minute; the two numbers are pressures in millimetres of mercury, not a heart rate.

Layout for a circulation question: follow the blood in order, naming each chamber and vessel and stating whether the blood is oxygenated or deoxygenated at that point. Layout for a vessel question: structure, then the function that structure serves, joined by because. The transport system is then described exactly as the memorandum expects.

The questions for this section are ready: reading blood pressure, heart attack and stroke, anaemia and iron, prevention, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same system again, through a delivery fleet, a two-pump engine, and highways, back roads and footpaths — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Delivery Fleet

Think of your blood as a fleet of taxis on a road network, with the heart as the engine that keeps the whole fleet moving. The fleet has four kinds of vehicle. Most of the road is filled with plasma, the yellowish watery liquid, which is the road itself and also the open bakkie carrying anything that dissolves: glucose from your food, salts, hormones, the waste urea on its way to the kidneys, and the carbon dioxide on its way to the lungs.

The red taxis are the red blood cells, millions in every drop, and they carry one passenger: oxygen. Each is a flattened disc with no nucleus so it can be stuffed full of haemoglobin, the red chemical that grabs oxygen in the lungs and lets go of it where cells need it. Loaded with oxygen the blood is bright red; after it has dropped the oxygen off it is dark red. Never blue. The white cells are the security team, far fewer but bigger, hunting down and eating bacteria or making antibodies that tag an invader so it can be destroyed; after an infection or a vaccine the tags are remembered, which is immunity. And the platelets are the puncture repair kit: tiny fragments that rush to a cut, set off clotting, and plug the hole with a mesh that dries into a scab.

Four vehicles, four jobs, and that gives you the four functions of blood for the test. Transport: oxygen in the red cells, everything dissolved in the plasma. Defence: white cells. Clotting: platelets. And a fifth you might forget: the fleet carries heat from your hot muscles to your skin, which is how you stay at 37 degrees.

You have about five litres of this fleet, and you can give away 450 millilitres, about two cups, to the blood service and make it back within weeks. Someone bleeding after a car crash is alive because of a stranger who did.

Keep the fleet in mind and try the questions for this part: plasma, red cells, white cells, platelets, and what each one carries or does.

## Subtopic: A Two-Pump Engine

Make a fist and squeeze it about once a second. That is your heart, about that size, about that rhythm, around 70 times a minute, 100 000 times a day, without ever stopping to rest. It is not one pump but two, side by side, separated by a wall of muscle so the blood on the two sides never mixes. Each pump has a top room that fills, the atrium, and a bottom room with thick walls that squeezes, the ventricle, with one-way doors, the valves, so blood can never slide backwards.

Follow the blood. Dark, used blood from your body arrives at the right top room, drops into the right bottom room, and is squeezed out along the pulmonary artery to the lungs. This is a short trip, just next door. In the lungs it drops off carbon dioxide and picks up oxygen, turns bright red, and comes back along the pulmonary vein to the left top room. It drops into the left bottom room, and that room squeezes it out along the aorta, the biggest pipe in the body, to everywhere: head, arms, gut, legs, toes. That is a long trip, so the left bottom room has walls about three times thicker than the right one.

Right side: used blood to the lungs, short route. Left side: fresh blood to the body, long route. The blood goes through the heart twice on one lap, which is why it is called a double circulation, and the payoff is that the blood heading for your toes has been freshly re-pumped at full pressure after the lungs.

The order is a favourite test question, so say it like a route: vena cava, right atrium, right ventricle, pulmonary artery, lungs, pulmonary vein, left atrium, left ventricle, aorta, body, and back. Say at each stop whether the blood is oxygenated or deoxygenated, and the marks follow.

Hold the two pumps in mind and try this part's questions: top rooms and bottom rooms, the short route and the long route, and why the left wall is thicker.

## Subtopic: Highways, Back Roads and Footpaths

The fleet needs roads, and there are three kinds. Arteries are the highways out of the heart: thick, strong, stretchy walls, because every squeeze of the ventricle slams blood into them at high pressure. Feel the inside of your wrist; that throb is an artery stretching and springing back with each beat, your pulse. Arteries carry blood away from the heart, and in the body that blood is bright red and oxygenated, with one famous exception: the pulmonary artery carries used blood to the lungs. So define an artery by direction, away from the heart, and you are never caught.

Capillaries are the footpaths between the houses. They are so narrow that red cells go through one at a time, and their walls are one cell thick, which is the whole point: oxygen and glucose slip out through the thin wall to the cells, and carbon dioxide and waste slip in. Every cell in your body is within a few cells of a footpath. Veins are the back roads home: by now the pressure is low and steady, so the walls are thinner and floppier, the tube is wider, and inside there are one-way flaps, valves, so blood does not slide back down your legs. Walking squeezes the veins in your calves and pumps blood back up; stand still for hours and your ankles swell.

Now looking after the engine and the roads. Blood pressure is how hard the blood pushes on the artery walls, written as two numbers like 120 over 80: the push when the heart squeezes, and the push when it relaxes. Those are pressures, not beats. If it sits at 140 over 90 or more, that is high blood pressure, which quietly wears out the heart, the arteries, the kidneys and the brain. Too much salt, too much weight, smoking, drink and no exercise push it up. Fatty deposits from a greasy diet and smoking clog the small arteries that feed the heart muscle itself; block one and a piece of the heart dies, a heart attack. Block one in the brain and it is a stroke. Too little iron and you have too few red taxis, anaemia, and you are tired and breathless.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Blood: plasma transports, red cells carry oxygen, white cells defend, platelets clot. The heart is two pumps: right side to the lungs, left side to the body, atria fill, ventricles squeeze, valves stop backflow. Arteries away from the heart with thick walls, capillaries one cell thick for exchange, veins back with valves. Keep it healthy: move, eat less salt and fat, never smoke. In Grade 10 Life Sciences this returns as transport systems in mammals.

Hold the highways, footpaths and back roads, and take the final questions of the lesson: the three vessels compared, 120 over 80, and heart attack against stroke.
