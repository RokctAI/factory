# Part 1 — Expert

This session links breathing, gaseous exchange in the alveoli, transport by the blood and cellular respiration in the mitochondria into a single pathway, and separates the three processes that share the everyday word respiration. The anchor is the chain from a breath of air to a thigh muscle cell during a sprint and back out again. The governing principle is diffusion down a concentration gradient: oxygen moves from where it is more concentrated to where it is less, alveolus to blood and blood to cell, and carbon dioxide moves the opposite way for the same reason, while the mitochondria keep the gradients steep by consuming oxygen and producing carbon dioxide.

## Subtopic: Breathing, Gas Exchange and Respiration Are Three Things

Three different processes are involved in getting energy from the air you breathe, and the examiner tests whether you keep them apart. Breathing, or ventilation, is the physical movement of air into and out of the lungs by the action of the diaphragm and intercostal muscles. Gaseous exchange is the movement of gases across a surface by diffusion: oxygen from the alveoli into the blood and carbon dioxide from the blood into the alveoli in the lungs, and in reverse between the blood and the cells in the tissues. Cellular respiration is a chemical process inside cells, in the mitochondria, in which glucose is broken down using oxygen to release energy, producing carbon dioxide and water.

The three occur in different places and are different kinds of process. Breathing is mechanical and happens in the chest. Gaseous exchange is physical, diffusion, and happens across the alveolar wall and the capillary wall. Respiration is chemical and happens in every living cell of the body, in the heart, the brain, the skin and the toes, not only in the lungs. A fish breathes with gills and a plant does not breathe at all, yet both respire in every cell.

The link between them is the blood. Breathing refreshes the air in the alveoli, so that the oxygen concentration there stays high and the carbon dioxide concentration stays low. Gaseous exchange loads the blood with oxygen and unloads carbon dioxide. The circulatory system carries the blood to the cells, where a second gaseous exchange delivers oxygen into the cell and removes carbon dioxide from it. Respiration in the mitochondria uses the oxygen and makes the carbon dioxide. Four steps, two of them exchanges, with the blood as the vehicle between lungs and cells.

Set out as a table for marking: process, where, type, what happens. Breathing, lungs and chest, mechanical, air in and out. Gaseous exchange, alveoli and tissues, diffusion, oxygen and carbon dioxide swap. Cellular respiration, mitochondria of all cells, chemical, glucose and oxygen give carbon dioxide, water and energy. The commonest lost mark in this topic is writing respiration when breathing is meant.

The questions for this section are with you now: three processes, three places, three types.

## Subtopic: Gaseous Exchange in the Alveoli

Gas exchange in the lungs takes place across two layers, the wall of the alveolus and the wall of the capillary wrapped around it, each a single cell thick, so that the air and the blood are separated by less than a thousandth of a millimetre. Oxygen is more concentrated in the alveolar air than in the blood arriving from the body, so it diffuses into the blood; carbon dioxide is more concentrated in that blood than in the alveolar air, so it diffuses out into the air. No energy is spent; the gradients do the work.

The alveoli are adapted to make this diffusion as fast as possible, and the adaptations are examined as a set. There are about 300 million alveoli, giving a total surface area of about 70 square metres, so that a very large amount of gas can cross at once. The walls are one cell thick, so the distance for diffusion is as short as possible. The inner surface is moist, so that gases dissolve before they cross the membrane. Each alveolus is surrounded by a dense network of capillaries, so that blood is always flowing close to the air, and the blood flow carries oxygenated blood away and brings deoxygenated blood in, keeping the gradient steep. Breathing keeps the gradient steep from the air side by replacing stale alveolar air with fresh.

Every adaptation is a feature paired with a reason. Large surface area, so that more gas can diffuse at the same time. Thin walls, so that the diffusion distance is short. Moist lining, so that gases dissolve. Rich blood supply, so that the concentration gradient is maintained. Write the feature and the reason, because the feature alone earns half.

The same principles explain the design elsewhere. Capillaries in the tissues are also one cell thick and dense, so the exchange with cells is fast. The gills of a fish are feathery for surface area and thin and bloody for the same reasons. The small intestine, which absorbs digested food, uses the identical trick of folding a huge surface into a small space. Whenever you meet an exchange surface in biology, look for surface area, thinness, moisture and blood supply.

Take this section's questions now: the direction of each gas and why, and the four adaptations with their reasons.

## Subtopic: Transport by the Blood

Oxygen that has diffused into the blood in the lungs is carried by haemoglobin in the red blood cells. In the alveolar capillaries, where oxygen is plentiful, haemoglobin binds oxygen to form oxyhaemoglobin, and the blood turns bright red. The pulmonary vein carries this blood to the left side of the heart, and the left ventricle pumps it through the aorta and the arteries to the capillaries of every tissue. There, where oxygen is scarce because the cells are consuming it, oxyhaemoglobin releases its oxygen, which diffuses out through the capillary wall and into the cells, and the blood darkens.

Carbon dioxide travels the opposite way. Produced in the mitochondria, it diffuses out of the cell into the capillary blood, where most of it is carried in the plasma as dissolved hydrogencarbonate ions and some by the red cells. The veins return the blood to the right side of the heart, the right ventricle pumps it through the pulmonary artery to the lungs, and in the alveolar capillaries the carbon dioxide diffuses out into the air to be exhaled. This is why the circulation must be double: the lungs load and unload the gases, and the body uses them, and the heart drives the blood between the two.

Each red blood cell makes the round trip in about a minute at rest. The amount of oxygen the blood can carry depends on how much haemoglobin it has, which is why anaemia, a shortage of haemoglobin, leaves a person breathless, and why carbon monoxide from smoke or a faulty heater is so dangerous: it binds to haemoglobin far more strongly than oxygen does and blocks the seats, so that the blood circulates but carries little oxygen. Training at altitude, where air is thin, stimulates the body to make more red blood cells, which is why some athletes train in the Highveld before competing at the coast.

The complete chain for marking, in order: air into alveoli by breathing; oxygen diffuses into blood and binds to haemoglobin; blood carried via pulmonary vein, heart and arteries to tissues; oxygen released and diffuses into cells; used in mitochondria; carbon dioxide diffuses into blood; carried via veins, heart and pulmonary artery to lungs; diffuses into alveoli; exhaled. Nine links, and every one names a place and a process.

This section's questions are available now: oxyhaemoglobin in the lungs and the tissues, how carbon dioxide travels, and carbon monoxide.

## Subtopic: Cellular Respiration and the Error Museum

Cellular respiration is the controlled release of energy from glucose inside cells, and the form examined in Grade 9 is aerobic respiration, which uses oxygen. The word equation is glucose plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide plus water plus energy. In symbols, C6H12O6 plus 6 O2 gives 6 CO2 plus 6 H2O plus energy, and the equation balances: six carbon atoms, twelve hydrogen atoms and eighteen oxygen atoms on each side. The process happens in the mitochondria, in many small steps so that the energy is released gradually and captured in a carrier molecule the cell can spend, rather than lost as a burst of heat. Some energy is released as heat nonetheless, which is why the body is warm.

Energy is needed by every cell for every activity: contraction in muscle cells, transmission of impulses in nerve cells, building proteins, dividing, moving substances across membranes. That is why every cell respires continuously and why every cell needs a constant supply of glucose and oxygen and constant removal of carbon dioxide, and it is why the systems of the body exist at all: digestion supplies the glucose, breathing and gas exchange supply the oxygen and remove the carbon dioxide, the blood carries them, and the kidneys remove the other wastes.

When oxygen cannot be supplied fast enough, as in the first minute of a sprint, muscle cells switch partly to anaerobic respiration, which breaks glucose down without oxygen into lactic acid and releases far less energy per glucose, about a twentieth. The lactic acid accumulates and causes the burning ache and fatigue of a hard sprint, and after the race it is carried to the liver and broken down using oxygen, which is why breathing stays fast during recovery. Yeast respires anaerobically to produce alcohol and carbon dioxide, which is how bread rises and beer brews; Grade 11 Life Sciences develops both forms of respiration in full.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: respiration is breathing; breathing moves air, respiration releases energy in cells. Two: respiration happens in the lungs; it happens in the mitochondria of every cell, and the lungs only exchange gases. Three: respiration makes energy; energy is released from glucose, not created. Four: oxygen is pumped into the blood; it diffuses down a concentration gradient with no energy spent. Five: plants respire only at night; they respire continuously and photosynthesise in light.

Layout for a chain question: name each step with its place and its process in order from air to cell and back. Layout for an equation question: word equation, then symbols, then where it happens and what the energy is used for. The three processes are then described exactly as the memorandum expects.

The questions for this section are ready: the equation in words and symbols, aerobic against anaerobic, and the five exhibits of the museum.

## Subtopic: Fetching, Swapping, Burning

Three different jobs get tangled up because people use one word, respiration, for all of them. Untangle them with three verbs: fetching, swapping and burning.

Fetching is breathing. The diaphragm and the rib muscles make the chest bigger and smaller, and air is pushed in and out. It is just moving air, like fetching water from the tap in a bucket. It happens in your chest and nowhere else. The proper name is breathing or ventilation.

Swapping is gas exchange. Down in the alveoli, the grapes at the ends of the lung tree, oxygen hops from the air into the blood and carbon dioxide hops from the blood into the air. The hopping is diffusion: a gas spreads from where there is a lot of it to where there is less, with nobody pushing. The same swap happens in reverse out in your muscles and organs, where oxygen hops from the blood into the cells and carbon dioxide hops out of the cells into the blood. Swapping happens in two places: lungs and tissues.

Burning is cellular respiration. Inside the mitochondria of every single cell, glucose from your food is combined with the oxygen the blood delivered, and energy is released, with carbon dioxide and water left over. Glucose plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide plus water plus energy. This is a chemical reaction, a slow, controlled version of burning, and it happens in every cell: your toes respire, your brain respires, a leaf respires, a fish respires. It is not in the lungs at all.

Three jobs, three places, three kinds of thing: moving air in the chest, diffusing gases across thin walls, a chemical reaction in cells. Between the lungs and the cells, the blood is the bucket that carries the oxygen one way and the carbon dioxide the other. Fetch, swap, carry, swap, burn.

Keep the three verbs in mind and try the questions for this part: which one is breathing, which one is in the mitochondria, and which one is diffusion.

## Subtopic: The Wet Grape in a Net

Why are the alveoli built the way they are? Picture one alveolus as a tiny wet grape wrapped in a net of the finest blood vessels. Everything about it is designed to make the swap as fast as possible, and the test wants four features, each with its reason.

First, there are a lot of grapes, about 300 million, and spread flat they would cover half a tennis court. Big surface, so a huge amount of oxygen can cross at the same time. Think of a crowd leaving a stadium: ten gates empty it far faster than one. Second, the grape skin is incredibly thin, one cell, and the blood vessel wall around it is one cell too, so a gas only has to travel a tiny distance, thinner than tissue paper. Short distance, quick crossing. Third, the grape is wet inside, because gases have to dissolve before they can slip through a membrane, like sugar has to dissolve before it can spread through tea. Fourth, the net of blood vessels is dense and the blood keeps moving, so fresh dark blood that is short of oxygen keeps arriving and bright loaded blood keeps leaving, and the difference between air and blood stays big. Breathing does the same on the air side, sweeping out the stale air and bringing fresh.

That difference is the whole engine. Oxygen only hops from air to blood because there is more of it in the air than in the blood. Stop breathing and the alveolar air runs out of oxygen, the difference shrinks, and the hopping stops. Stop the blood moving and the blood near the grape fills up with oxygen, the difference shrinks, and the hopping stops. Keep both going and the swap never pauses.

Write it as feature then reason, every time. Large surface area so more gas crosses at once. Thin walls so the distance is short. Moist lining so gases dissolve. Rich blood supply so the difference stays big. You will meet the same four ideas in fish gills and in the gut.

Hold the wet grape and the net in mind and try this part's questions: four features, four reasons, and what keeps the difference big.

## Subtopic: From Lungs to Leg Muscle and Back

Now the whole trip, told as a sprint for the taxi. You take a deep breath: fetching. In the grapes, oxygen hops into the blood and sticks to haemoglobin in the red cells, which turn bright red: swapping. The blood rides the pulmonary vein to the left side of the heart, gets fired down the aorta, through the arteries, into the thin footpath vessels between the cells of your thigh muscle. There the oxygen is scarce because the muscle is gobbling it, so haemoglobin lets go and oxygen hops into the cells: swapping again. In the mitochondria, glucose plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide plus water plus energy: burning. The muscle pulls and you run.

The carbon dioxide hops out of the cell into the blood, rides the veins to the right side of the heart, is pumped to the lungs, hops out into the grapes and leaves on your next breath out. Nine stops, and the blood is the taxi for the whole middle of the route.

Here is what you feel. Your legs are burning glucose far faster than normal, so the brain stem turns up the fetching and the heart: breathing from 15 to 40 a minute, heart from 70 to 150, so more oxygen gets fetched, swapped and carried every minute. But for the first stretch of the sprint the oxygen still cannot arrive fast enough, and your muscle cells do an emergency version of burning without oxygen that gives a little energy and leaves lactic acid behind. That acid is the burn in your thighs. When you flop into the taxi, you keep panting for a few minutes because the lactic acid has to be carried to the liver and broken down, which needs oxygen too, so the debt gets paid back.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Breathing fetches air into the chest. Gas exchange swaps oxygen and carbon dioxide by diffusion across the wet, thin, huge, bloody alveoli, and again between blood and cells. Blood carries oxygen on haemoglobin and carbon dioxide mostly in the plasma. Cellular respiration burns glucose with oxygen in the mitochondria of every cell: glucose plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide plus water plus energy. Without enough oxygen, muscles make lactic acid instead. Fetch, swap, carry, swap, burn. In Grade 11 Life Sciences both kinds of respiration get a full chapter.

Hold the sprint in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: the nine stops in order, the burn in your legs, and why you keep panting.
