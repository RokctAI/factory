### subtopic_1 — Blood and Its Components
**Assistant:** Why do veins look blue through my skin if deoxygenated blood is dark red?

**Tutor:** Because what you see is light that has travelled into the skin and back out, not the blood itself, and the skin changes the colour on the way. Blood in a vein just below the skin is dark red: haemoglobin that has released its oxygen absorbs light differently from bright red oxyhaemoglobin, but both are red, as anyone who has seen blood drawn from a vein for a test knows, since the tube fills with dark red liquid. Skin and the tissue above the vein scatter light, and red light penetrates deeper into tissue than blue light does. The dark blood in the vein absorbs most of the red light that reaches it, so very little red comes back from that spot, while the blue light scattered from the shallower tissue above returns much as it would anywhere else. Compared with the surrounding skin, the area over the vein is therefore relatively short of red and looks bluish. The effect is strongest in pale skin and faint in dark skin, which is the giveaway that it is an optical effect rather than a property of the blood. Textbook diagrams colour veins blue and arteries red purely as a convention to show oxygen content, and the error-museum exhibit exists because learners take the colouring literally. In an answer, deoxygenated blood is dark red and oxygenated blood is bright red; blue is never correct.

### subtopic_2 — The Heart and the Double Circulation
**Assistant:** Why does the blood need to go through the heart twice? Would one strong pump not do?

**Tutor:** One pump would have to push blood through the lungs and then on through the whole body in one go, and the lungs cannot take it. The capillaries in the lungs are extremely thin-walled, which is what makes gas exchange fast, and they would burst at the pressure needed to drive blood all the way to the feet. So the right ventricle pumps gently, just enough to send blood next door through the lungs and back, and the blood arrives in the left atrium at low pressure. The left ventricle then re-pumps it with a wall about three times thicker, launching it into the aorta hard enough to reach the brain against gravity and the toes against distance. The double circulation thus matches two different pressures to two different jobs. It has a second benefit: because the two sides are divided by the septum, oxygenated blood from the lungs never mixes with deoxygenated blood from the body, so every drop leaving the aorta is fully loaded with oxygen. A fish has a single circulation, heart to gills to body and back, and its body receives blood that has lost pressure in the gills, which is one reason fish are cold-blooded and slow compared with mammals. Birds and mammals, with their high energy demands, all have the four-chambered heart and double circulation, and a baby born with a hole in the septum, which lets the two bloods mix, is breathless and bluish until surgeons close it.

### subtopic_3 — Arteries, Veins and Capillaries
**Assistant:** If arteries carry blood away from the heart at high pressure, why does the blood not simply pool in the capillaries?

**Tutor:** Because the vessels are arranged to keep it moving even as the pressure drops. By the time blood has been forced through the arterioles and spread across the enormous number of capillaries, the pressure that was over a hundred millimetres of mercury in the aorta has fallen to a few millimetres, but it has not fallen to zero, and the capillaries drain into venules and veins that offer very little resistance because they are wide. Blood therefore keeps drifting towards the heart. Three further mechanisms help in the veins. Valves, flaps of tissue that open towards the heart and close against backflow, divide each long leg vein into short sections so that blood is never asked to climb the whole height of the leg at once. Skeletal muscles around the veins squeeze them every time you move, pushing blood up section by section, which is why walking prevents swollen ankles and why soldiers standing rigidly on parade sometimes faint as blood pools in their legs. And breathing lowers the pressure inside the chest each time you inhale, which draws blood from the abdomen into the large veins near the heart. When these mechanisms fail, as when valves stretch and leak, blood does pool and the vein bulges into a varicose vein. So the circulation is a loop driven by the heart but assisted at the low-pressure end by valves, muscles and breathing, and that is why veins are built with valves and thin walls while arteries need neither.

### subtopic_4 — Health of the Circulatory System and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What is actually happening in a heart attack, and why does smoking cause it?

**Tutor:** The heart muscle has its own blood supply, the coronary arteries, which branch off the aorta just above the heart and run across its surface, because the muscle of the heart wall cannot absorb oxygen from the blood inside its chambers; like any muscle it needs capillaries of its own. A heart attack is the blockage of one of these coronary arteries. Over decades, fatty material rich in cholesterol builds up inside the artery wall as plaques, narrowing the vessel; one day a plaque cracks, a clot forms on the crack and blocks the artery completely, and the patch of heart muscle beyond it is starved of oxygen and dies within hours, causing crushing chest pain and, if the damage is large or the rhythm is disturbed, death. Smoking attacks every stage. Chemicals in the smoke damage the lining of the arteries so plaques form faster; nicotine raises blood pressure and heart rate so the heart needs more oxygen; carbon monoxide from the smoke binds to haemoglobin in place of oxygen so the blood carries less; and smoking makes the blood clot more readily, so the final blockage is more likely. A smoker's risk of a heart attack is roughly double a non-smoker's, and it falls steadily after quitting. The same plaque-and-clot process in an artery of the brain is a stroke, and in a leg artery it causes gangrene, so the prevention list is one list: do not smoke, eat less saturated fat and salt, exercise, keep a healthy mass, and have your blood pressure checked.
