# Part 1 — Expert

This session describes the components and processes of the nervous system: the central and peripheral divisions, the three types of neuron, the parts of the brain and their functions, the pathway from stimulus to response, the reflex arc, and the health issues of the system. The anchor example is the hand withdrawn from a hot pot, a reflex completed through the spinal cord in a fraction of a second. The governing idea is that the nervous system gathers information, processes it and coordinates a response, and that every nervous pathway can be written as receptor, sensory neuron, central nervous system, motor neuron, effector.

## Subtopic: Components of the Nervous System

The nervous system has two divisions. The central nervous system consists of the brain and the spinal cord, the processing centres where information is interpreted and decisions are made. The peripheral nervous system consists of the nerves that branch from the brain and spinal cord to every part of the body: 12 pairs of cranial nerves leaving the brain and 31 pairs of spinal nerves leaving the spinal cord. A nerve is a bundle of hundreds or thousands of nerve fibres wrapped together, like a telephone cable containing many wires.

The cells of the nervous system are neurons, the specialised nerve cells met in the cells topic. A neuron has a cell body containing the nucleus, short branching extensions called dendrites that receive messages, and a single long fibre, the axon, that carries the message away; many axons are wrapped in a fatty insulating sheath that speeds the impulse. The message itself is a nerve impulse, a brief electrical signal that travels along the axon at speeds from about 1 metre per second in the slowest fibres to about 120 metres per second in the fastest, so a signal from your toe reaches your brain in under a fiftieth of a second.

There are three types of neuron, named by direction. Sensory neurons carry impulses from receptors in the sense organs and skin towards the central nervous system. Motor neurons carry impulses from the central nervous system out to effectors, the muscles and glands that respond. Interneurons, found within the brain and spinal cord, connect sensory and motor neurons and do the processing. Where one neuron meets the next there is a microscopic gap, a synapse, across which the message is passed chemically.

The whole system is protected because it cannot repair itself well. The brain sits inside the skull and the spinal cord inside the canal of the vertebral column; both are wrapped in three membranes, the meninges, and bathed in fluid that cushions them against knocks. A nerve impulse pathway is always written in the same five-part order: receptor, sensory neuron, central nervous system, motor neuron, effector, and that order is the backbone of every answer in this lesson.

The questions for this section are with you now: central against peripheral, the three types of neuron, and the five-part pathway.

## Subtopic: The Brain and the Spinal Cord

The adult brain has a mass of about 1,4 kilograms and contains roughly 86 billion neurons. Grade 9 names three regions. The cerebrum is the large, folded upper part divided into two hemispheres; it is the seat of conscious thought, memory, learning, language, the senses and voluntary movement. The left hemisphere controls the right side of the body and the right hemisphere the left, because the nerve pathways cross as they leave the brain. The folds increase the surface area of the thin outer layer where most of the processing happens.

The cerebellum lies at the back, below the cerebrum, and coordinates movement, balance and posture; it does not start a movement but makes it smooth and accurate, so that you can walk a straight line or catch a ball. The medulla, part of the brain stem connecting the brain to the spinal cord, controls the involuntary processes that keep you alive without thought: heart rate, breathing rate, blood pressure, swallowing and vomiting. Damage to the cerebrum may cost memory or speech; damage to the cerebellum makes a person clumsy and unsteady; damage to the medulla is usually fatal because breathing stops.

The spinal cord is a cord of nervous tissue about 45 centimetres long running down the canal of the vertebral column from the medulla to the lower back. It has two jobs. It carries impulses between the brain and the body: sensory information travelling up and motor instructions travelling down. It is also a processing centre in its own right for reflexes, which it completes without involving the brain, as the next section describes. A spinal cord injury therefore cuts both services below the injury: the brain neither receives sensation from nor can send movement to the parts beneath it, which is paralysis.

The test question that recurs is to match a function to a region or a region to a function. Thinking, memory, senses and voluntary movement: cerebrum. Balance and coordination: cerebellum. Heart rate and breathing: medulla. Relay to and from the body and reflex centre: spinal cord. Four regions, four jobs, in either direction.

Take this section's questions now: the three brain regions and their functions, the crossing of control, and the two jobs of the spinal cord.

## Subtopic: Stimulus, Response and the Reflex Arc

A stimulus is a change in the environment that the body can detect: light, sound, a chemical, a change in temperature, pressure, pain. Receptors are the structures that detect a stimulus and start a nerve impulse, and they are concentrated in the sense organs: the eyes for light, the ears for sound and also for balance, the nose and tongue for chemicals, and the skin for touch, pressure, temperature and pain. A response is what the body does as a result, produced by an effector, either a muscle contracting or a gland secreting.

Most responses are voluntary and pass through the brain. You see the taxi, the impulse travels along the optic nerve to the cerebrum, you decide to run, the cerebrum sends impulses down the spinal cord and along motor neurons to the leg muscles. The pathway is receptor, sensory neuron, brain, motor neuron, effector, and the decision in the middle takes time, typically a fifth of a second or more for a simple reaction.

A reflex action is different: it is rapid, automatic and does not involve conscious decision, and most protective reflexes are completed in the spinal cord. The hot pot is the standard example. Heat and pain receptors in the fingertip fire; a sensory neuron carries the impulse into the spinal cord; there it passes across a synapse to an interneuron and immediately across another to a motor neuron; the motor neuron carries the impulse out to the muscles of the arm, which contract and pull the hand away. The whole arc, receptor, sensory neuron, spinal cord, motor neuron, effector, is complete in a few hundredths of a second. The brain is informed slightly later, which is why you have already let go when you feel the pain.

The reflex arc is the pathway of a reflex, and it is drawn and labelled in tests: the receptor in the skin, the sensory neuron entering the back of the spinal cord, the interneuron inside the grey matter, the motor neuron leaving the front, the effector muscle. The value of a reflex is speed and protection: the knee jerk that keeps you upright when you stumble, the blink when something flies at your eye, the narrowing of the pupil in bright light, coughing when food touches the airway. Each bypasses the slow decision of the cerebrum because waiting would mean damage.

This section's questions are available now: stimulus, receptor, response and effector, the sense organs, and the reflex arc in order.

## Subtopic: Health of the Nervous System and the Error Museum

Because neurons repair poorly, injury to the nervous system is often permanent, and most of the health issues are about protection. Concussion is a bruising of the brain caused by a blow that throws it against the inside of the skull; it causes confusion, headache and sometimes loss of consciousness, and repeated concussions, as in rugby and boxing, cause lasting damage. Helmets on bicycles and motorbikes and seat belts in cars exist to protect the skull and the brain inside it. Spinal cord injury from a car crash, a dive into shallow water or a fall severs the pathways, and the result is paralysis below the injury, permanent because the cord does not regrow.

Infections and strokes attack the brain from inside. Meningitis is an infection of the meninges, the membranes around the brain and spinal cord, by bacteria or viruses; it causes fever, a stiff neck and a severe headache, can kill within hours, and is prevented in part by childhood vaccination. A stroke occurs when the blood supply to part of the brain is cut off by a clot or a burst vessel, so that the neurons there die within minutes; because control crosses, a stroke in the left cerebrum paralyses the right side of the body and often affects speech. Uncontrolled high blood pressure and smoking are the main avoidable causes. Epilepsy is a condition of recurring seizures caused by uncontrolled electrical activity in the brain and is usually controlled by medication.

Alcohol and other drugs act directly on the nervous system. Alcohol slows the passage of impulses across synapses, so reaction time lengthens, judgement weakens and coordination by the cerebellum deteriorates, which is why a drunk person staggers and why drinking and driving kills on South African roads every weekend. Lack of sleep has a similar effect on reaction time. A simple ruler-drop test shows it: a partner holds a ruler above your open hand and drops it, and the distance it falls before you catch it measures your reaction time, about 20 centimetres for a typical alert teenager, which corresponds to about 0,2 seconds.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: nerves carry blood; nerves carry electrical impulses, and blood is the circulatory system. Two: all reflexes go through the brain; most protective reflexes are completed in the spinal cord. Three: the cerebellum does the thinking; the cerebrum thinks, the cerebellum coordinates. Four: a sensory neuron carries impulses to a muscle; sensory neurons carry impulses towards the central nervous system, motor neurons carry them away. Five: a stroke is a heart problem; it is a loss of blood supply to the brain, though it shares causes with heart disease.

Layout for a pathway question: write the five parts in order and name the specific structure at each: receptor in the skin of the finger, sensory neuron, spinal cord, motor neuron, muscle of the arm. Five parts, five marks, and the fastest system in the body is described exactly as the memorandum wants.

The questions for this section are ready: concussion and spinal injury, meningitis and stroke, alcohol and reaction time, and the five exhibits of the museum.

## Subtopic: The Marshal, the Radios and the Drivers

Back at the taxi rank, there is one person who sees everything and tells everyone what to do: the marshal. The marshal is your central nervous system, the brain and the spinal cord. He stands in the middle, information comes to him, and orders go out from him. Running to every corner of the rank are the radios, the cables carrying messages in and out. Those are your nerves, the peripheral nervous system, bundles of long thin nerve cells reaching every muscle and every patch of skin.

The messages travel along nerve cells as tiny electrical pulses, and they are fast: up to 120 metres a second in the quickest fibres, which is the length of a football pitch in under a second. Messages come in two directions, and the cells are named by the direction. Sensory neurons carry news IN to the marshal, from your eyes, ears, nose, tongue and skin. Motor neurons carry orders OUT from the marshal to the drivers, your muscles, and to the glands. In the middle, inside the brain and spinal cord, short connecting cells pass the message across. Sensory in, motor out.

The marshal has departments. The big folded top part of the brain, the cerebrum, is the thinking office: memory, learning, deciding, seeing and hearing, and every movement you choose to make. The small part tucked at the back, the cerebellum, is the balance and coordination department; it does not decide to kick the ball, it makes sure your kick is smooth and you do not fall over. The stalk at the bottom, the medulla, runs the things you never think about: your heartbeat and your breathing, day and night. The spinal cord is the main cable down your back, carrying messages up and down, and it can also give some orders of its own.

And the marshal is fragile and cannot be replaced, so he is kept in a hard hat, the skull, and the main cable runs inside the bones of the spine, both wrapped in membranes and floating in fluid.

Keep the marshal and the radios in mind and try the questions for this part: central against peripheral, sensory in and motor out, and the three departments.

## Subtopic: The Hot Pot and the Shortcut

You grab a pot handle that is burning hot. Your hand is off it before you even feel the pain. How, if the brain is in charge? Because for emergencies the body has a shortcut that does not go through the brain at all.

Here is the path, in order, and this order is worth marks. The receptors in your fingertip detect the heat; that is the stimulus. A sensory neuron carries the pulse in to the spinal cord. Inside the spinal cord it jumps across to a short connector cell and straight on to a motor neuron. The motor neuron carries the order out to the muscles in your arm, the effector, and they contract: hand off the pot. That is the response. Receptor, sensory neuron, spinal cord, motor neuron, effector. The whole loop is called a reflex arc, and it takes a few hundredths of a second. A message also goes up the cord to the brain, but it arrives after the hand has already moved, which is why the pain seems to come afterwards.

Why have a shortcut? Speed. Going up to the brain, deciding and coming back takes about a fifth of a second at best, and in a fifth of a second a hot pot does real damage. So the spinal cord handles the emergencies itself: pulling your hand from heat, blinking when something flies at your eye, the knee jerk that catches you when you trip, coughing when food goes down the wrong way. All fast, all automatic, none of them decided.

Everything that is not an emergency goes the long way, through the brain. You see the taxi, the message goes to the cerebrum, you decide to run, and orders go down the spinal cord and out to your legs. Same five parts, receptor, sensory neuron, brain, motor neuron, effector, with a decision in the middle. Reflex: spinal cord, no decision, fast. Voluntary: brain, decision, slower.

Hold the hot pot in mind and try this part's questions: the five steps in order, why the spinal cord and not the brain, and three other reflexes.

## Subtopic: Protecting the Marshal

Nerve cells do not grow back well, so a damaged marshal usually stays damaged, and most of what you need to know about nervous system health is about not getting hurt in the first place. A hard knock to the head bounces the brain against the inside of the skull: that is concussion, with confusion and headache, and if it happens again and again, as in boxing or rugby without care, the damage adds up. Helmets on bikes and seat belts in taxis are there to protect the one organ you cannot fix. A crushed or cut spinal cord, from a crash or a dive into a shallow pool, means no messages pass below the injury, so no feeling and no movement there: paralysis, for life.

Then the inside dangers. Meningitis is an infection of the membranes wrapping the brain, with fever, a stiff neck and a crushing headache, and it can kill in hours, so it is an emergency and partly prevented by vaccines. A stroke is when a blood clot or a burst vessel cuts off the blood to a part of the brain and that part dies within minutes; because the left brain runs the right side of the body, a left-side stroke paralyses the right arm and leg and often takes speech. High blood pressure and smoking are the big causes you can avoid.

Then the things you pour into the marshal. Alcohol slows the messages crossing from one nerve cell to the next, so reactions get slower, judgement gets worse and the balance department, the cerebellum, stops doing its job, which is the stagger. That is why drinking and driving fills South African hospitals every weekend. Too little sleep does something similar to your reaction time. Test it yourself: a friend holds a ruler above your open hand and drops it without warning. Catch it. A fresh, alert person catches it after about 20 centimetres, which is about a fifth of a second. Tired, or after alcohol, the ruler falls much further.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The marshal is the central nervous system, brain and spinal cord; the radios are the nerves. Sensory neurons carry messages in, motor neurons carry orders out. Cerebrum thinks, cerebellum balances, medulla keeps you breathing, the spinal cord relays and runs reflexes. Every pathway is receptor, sensory neuron, central nervous system, motor neuron, effector, and a reflex takes the shortcut through the cord. Protect the head and spine, avoid what slows the messages. Grade 12 Life Sciences gives this system a full topic.
