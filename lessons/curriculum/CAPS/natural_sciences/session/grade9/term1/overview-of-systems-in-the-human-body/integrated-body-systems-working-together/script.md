# Part 1 — Expert

This session provides the overview of the systems of the human body that the CAPS Grade 9 syllabus requires before each system is studied on its own: the digestive, circulatory, respiratory, excretory, nervous, musculoskeletal and reproductive systems. The anchor scenario is a learner sprinting for a taxi, and the governing principle is integration: each system supplies something that the other systems and every cell of the body need, so that the failure of one is felt by all. The answer format for integration questions is a chain, system to system to cell, and each link in the chain is a mark.

## Subtopic: The Seven Systems and Their Jobs

A system is a group of organs working together to perform a major function of the body, and Grade 9 names seven. The digestive system, from the mouth through the stomach and intestines, breaks food down into small soluble molecules such as glucose and absorbs them into the blood. The circulatory system, the heart, blood vessels and blood, transports oxygen, nutrients, wastes and heat around the body; the heart pumps about 5 litres of blood every minute at rest. The respiratory system, the airways and lungs, takes oxygen from the air into the blood and removes carbon dioxide from the blood into the air.

The excretory system, chiefly the kidneys, ureters and bladder, filters the blood and removes metabolic wastes such as urea, together with excess water and salts, as urine; the skin and the lungs also excrete. The nervous system, the brain, spinal cord and nerves, receives information from the sense organs, processes it and coordinates the body's responses, controlling the other systems. The musculoskeletal system, the bones, joints and muscles, supports the body, protects the organs and produces movement. The reproductive system produces sex cells and, in females, carries and nourishes the developing baby.

Each system is studied in its own lesson this term, so the goal today is the map rather than the detail: seven names, each paired with a one-clause function. Learners should be able to produce the pairing in either direction, naming the system from the function and the function from the system. Other systems exist, such as the endocrine system of hormone glands and the lymphatic system, and the skin is sometimes counted as an organ system; Grade 9 is examined on the seven.

The systems are not the organism. They are the organism's departments, and the organism lives only because the departments supply one another. The remainder of this part follows that supply.

The questions for this section are with you now: seven systems, seven functions, in both directions.

## Subtopic: Supplying a Cell: Glucose and Oxygen In, Waste Out

Every cell in the body needs glucose and oxygen for cellular respiration in its mitochondria, and every cell produces carbon dioxide and other wastes that must be removed. Supplying one muscle cell in the thigh therefore requires a chain of systems. Glucose begins as starch in a slice of bread. The digestive system digests the starch to glucose and absorbs the glucose through the wall of the small intestine into the blood. Oxygen begins in the air. The respiratory system draws air into the lungs, where oxygen diffuses into the blood in the capillaries of the alveoli.

The circulatory system now carries both. Blood leaving the small intestine and the lungs is pumped by the heart through arteries to the thigh, where capillaries run between the muscle cells. Glucose and oxygen diffuse out of the blood into the cell, and the mitochondria release energy: glucose plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide plus water plus energy. The muscle contracts.

The waste returns along the same road. Carbon dioxide diffuses from the cell into the blood, is carried in veins to the heart, is pumped to the lungs and diffuses into the alveoli to be breathed out; the respiratory system is thus both the supply and the disposal route for gases. Other wastes, chiefly urea produced when excess protein is broken down in the liver, are carried by the blood to the kidneys, where the excretory system filters them out into urine. Excess water and heat leave through the kidneys, the skin and the breath.

Written as a chain for marks: digestive system supplies glucose, respiratory system supplies oxygen, circulatory system transports both to the cell and carries carbon dioxide and urea away, respiratory system removes carbon dioxide, excretory system removes urea. Five links, five marks, and every link names a system and the substance it handles. Omit the circulatory system and the chain breaks, because nothing reaches the cell without transport; this is the most common omission in integration answers.

Take this section's questions now: the chain from bread and air to a muscle cell and back out.

## Subtopic: Sprinting for a Taxi: Systems in Action

The taxi is pulling off and you run. Before a foot moves, the nervous system has acted: the eyes report the taxi, the brain decides, and impulses run down the spinal cord and nerves to the leg muscles. The musculoskeletal system responds; muscles contract, pulling on the bones of the legs at the hip, knee and ankle joints, and you accelerate. Within seconds the muscles are using glucose and oxygen far faster than at rest and producing carbon dioxide just as fast.

The nervous system now adjusts the supply. The heart rate rises from about 70 beats per minute at rest towards 150 or more, and each beat pumps harder, so the circulatory system delivers oxygen and glucose to the legs several times faster. The breathing rate rises from about 15 breaths per minute to 40 or more and each breath is deeper, so the respiratory system loads more oxygen and unloads more carbon dioxide. Blood vessels to the working muscles widen while those to the digestive system narrow; digestion can wait, the sprint cannot.

Heat is produced in the working muscles, and the body temperature must stay close to 37 degrees Celsius. Blood vessels in the skin widen so that heat is carried to the surface and lost, and sweat glands release water that evaporates and cools you. The glucose being used is replaced from stores in the liver, released into the blood under the control of hormones, so the level in the blood stays steady although the muscles are drawing on it heavily.

When you sit down in the taxi, panting, the systems do not stop at once. Breathing and heart rate stay high for a few minutes because the muscles have borrowed: during the sprint the oxygen supply could not keep up fully, and the debt is repaid afterwards. Every change in this scenario is a system adjusting to the needs of another, with the nervous system as the coordinator, and examiners build questions by asking what happened to one system and why. The answer always names the system that changed, the change, and the system or cells whose need it met.

This section's questions are available now: which system acts first, why heart rate and breathing rise together, and how the body stays at 37 degrees.

## Subtopic: Keeping Conditions Steady and the Error Museum

The sprint shows a wider principle. The cells of the body work properly only within narrow limits: a temperature close to 37 degrees Celsius, a steady level of glucose in the blood, a steady amount of water and salt in the body fluids. The systems work together to hold these conditions steady while the outside world and the body's own activity keep changing. The nervous system detects a change and signals the other systems to correct it: too hot, so the skin sweats; too little water, so the kidneys make less urine and the brain produces thirst; too little glucose, so the liver releases some. Grade 10 Life Sciences names this maintenance of a steady internal environment homeostasis and studies it in detail; Grade 9 needs the idea that systems cooperate to keep conditions constant.

The cooperation also means that failure spreads. In asthma the airways narrow, less oxygen reaches the blood, and the muscles tire quickly although nothing is wrong with them. In kidney failure, urea builds up in the blood and poisons cells throughout the body, so a patient must be connected to a dialysis machine to filter the blood artificially. A broken leg, a musculoskeletal injury, immobilises a person and so weakens the heart and lungs through inactivity. In every case a question of the form explain how a problem in system A affects system B is answered by naming what A supplies or removes for B.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: the lungs supply oxygen directly to the muscles; oxygen reaches muscles only through the blood, so the circulatory system is always the middle link. Two: digestion happens only in the stomach; it begins in the mouth and is completed in the small intestine, where absorption also occurs. Three: the heart makes blood; the heart pumps blood, and blood cells are made in the bone marrow. Four: systems work independently; no system supplies its own needs. Five: the excretory system removes faeces; faeces are undigested food leaving the digestive system by egestion, while excretion removes wastes made by cells, such as urea and carbon dioxide.

Layout for an integration question: name each system in the chain, state what it supplies or removes, and connect the links with because or so that. The muscle cell receives oxygen because the respiratory system loads it into the blood and the circulatory system transports it. The chain is the method, and the method is the marks.

The questions for this section are ready: steady temperature, glucose and water, failure spreading between systems, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same body again, through a taxi rank, one breakfast and one breath, and what happens when one department goes on strike — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Body Is a Taxi Rank

Picture a big taxi rank in the morning. For the rank to work, a lot of different people have to do their own jobs at the same time. There is a food stall selling vetkoek, a petrol pump, the taxis themselves running their routes, the rank marshal with the whistle who tells everyone where to go, the drivers and the steel frames of the vehicles, the cleaners who take the rubbish away. Take away any one and the whole rank seizes up. Your body is that rank, and the seven systems are the seven jobs.

The food stall is the digestive system: it turns food into something the body can use, glucose. The petrol pump is the respiratory system: it brings in oxygen and takes away the exhaust, carbon dioxide. The taxis on the roads are the circulatory system: the heart is the engine and the blood is the fleet, delivering glucose and oxygen to every corner and bringing the rubbish back. The marshal is the nervous system: brain, spinal cord and nerves, watching everything and telling every other system what to do. The drivers and the steel frames are the musculoskeletal system: bones to hold the shape, muscles to move it. The cleaners are the excretory system, mainly the kidneys, filtering the blood and sending the waste out as urine. And the reproductive system is the plan for the next generation of the rank.

Seven jobs, seven systems. Learn them as pairs and practise them both ways: hear the job, say the system; hear the system, say the job. Food to glucose, digestive. Oxygen in, carbon dioxide out, respiratory. Delivery fleet, circulatory. Filter and remove waste, excretory. Marshal in charge, nervous. Frame and movement, musculoskeletal. Next generation, reproductive.

The key point of the whole rank is that nobody does their own job alone. The driver needs the petrol, the petrol needs the taxi to get it anywhere, and all of it needs the marshal. That is the word to hold on to: integrated.

Keep the rank in mind and try the questions for this part: which stall is which system, and why no job stands alone.

## Subtopic: Follow One Vetkoek and One Breath

Let us follow a vetkoek and a breath of air all the way to one muscle cell in your leg. The vetkoek goes in your mouth. The digestive system chews it, mixes it with acid in the stomach, and in the small intestine finishes breaking the starch down to glucose, which slips through the gut wall into the blood. Meanwhile you breathe in. The respiratory system pulls air down into the lungs, where oxygen passes through the thin walls of the tiny air sacs into the blood.

Now the taxis take over. The heart pumps the blood, carrying both the glucose and the oxygen, along the arteries down into your leg, into the tiniest vessels running between the muscle cells. Glucose and oxygen hop out of the blood into the cell, and the generators, the mitochondria, do their job: glucose plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide plus water plus energy. The muscle pulls. You walk.

The rubbish has to go back. Carbon dioxide hops from the cell into the blood, rides the veins back to the heart, gets pumped to the lungs and goes out on your next breath. The other main waste, urea, made in the liver when extra protein is broken down, rides the blood to the kidneys, where it is filtered out and leaves as urine. Extra heat and water go out through the skin as sweat.

Count the systems in that one story. Digestive, respiratory, circulatory, circulatory again, respiratory again, excretory. Five links and the blood in the middle of every one. If a question asks how a muscle gets oxygen and you say the lungs give it, you have skipped the taxi; nothing gets anywhere in the body without the blood. Say lungs, blood, muscle, and you have it.

Hold the vetkoek and the breath and try this part's questions: the journey in, the journey out, and the system you must never skip.

## Subtopic: When One Department Goes on Strike

The best way to see that the systems are connected is to break one. Suppose the petrol pump is faulty: a person has asthma and the airways narrow. The respiratory system takes in less oxygen, so the blood carries less, so the muscles cannot release enough energy, and the person is tired and out of breath after a few steps even though their legs are perfectly healthy. The problem started in one system and showed up in another.

Suppose the cleaners strike: the kidneys fail. Urea is still being made, but it is no longer being removed, so it builds up in the blood and reaches every cell in the body, and the person becomes very ill. The treatment, dialysis, is a machine that filters the blood for the kidneys several times a week. Or suppose the frame breaks: a broken leg. The person cannot move for weeks, and because they are not moving, the heart and lungs get weaker too. One strike, three departments affected.

The same connection runs in the good direction. Sprint for a taxi and watch the whole rank speed up together. The marshal, the nervous system, sees the taxi and fires the leg muscles. Within seconds the heart goes from about 70 beats a minute to 150, the breathing from about 15 breaths a minute to 40, because the muscles need more glucose and oxygen and are making more carbon dioxide. Your skin flushes and you sweat, because the muscles are making heat and the body must stay at about 37 degrees. Every system changed because another system needed it to.

That is how you answer a linking question. They say: explain how asthma affects the muscles. You say: the respiratory system takes in less oxygen, the circulatory system carries less to the muscles, the muscle cells release less energy, so the muscles tire. Name the system, say what it supplies, follow the chain.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Seven systems, seven jobs: digestive makes glucose, respiratory swaps the gases, circulatory delivers everything, excretory removes waste, nervous is in charge, musculoskeletal holds and moves, reproductive makes the next generation. Every cell gets its glucose and oxygen through the blood and sends its waste back the same way. Break one system and the others suffer; work one hard and the others speed up. Say the chain.

Hold the rank, the vetkoek and the strike, and take the final questions of the lesson: one failure, its effect on another system, and the chain that explains it.
