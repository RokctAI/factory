# Part 1 — Expert

This session covers the components and processes of the excretory system: what excretion is, the organs that carry it out, the structure of the urinary system, the processes of filtration and reabsorption in the kidney, and the health issues of the system. The anchor numbers are the 180 litres of fluid the kidneys filter from the blood each day and the roughly 1,5 litres of urine that result. The governing idea is that excretion removes wastes made by the body's own cells, and that the kidney does this not by simply discarding fluid but by filtering everything small and then reclaiming what the body still needs.

## Subtopic: Excretion and the Excretory Organs

Excretion is the removal from the body of waste products made by the chemical reactions in cells. The two wastes that matter in Grade 9 are carbon dioxide, produced in every cell by cellular respiration, and urea, produced in the liver when amino acids from excess protein are broken down. Both are toxic if they accumulate: carbon dioxide makes the blood acidic, and urea at high concentration damages cells throughout the body. Excess water and excess salts, taken in beyond the body's needs, are also removed, and so are the breakdown products of some medicines.

Excretion must be kept apart from egestion. Egestion is the removal of undigested food as faeces through the anus; that material passed along the inside of the gut and was never part of the body's cells or blood, so it is not a metabolic waste. Faeces are egested; urea and carbon dioxide are excreted. The confusion is the most common error in the topic.

Three organs excrete. The lungs excrete carbon dioxide and some water vapour in every breath out. The skin excretes water, salts and a little urea in sweat, although its main role in sweating is cooling. The kidneys are the principal excretory organs: they remove urea, excess water and excess salts from the blood and produce urine. Because the kidneys regulate how much water leaves the body, they are also the organs that control the water balance of the body, and that double role, excretion and water balance, is why they receive most of the attention in this lesson.

The liver is sometimes wrongly listed as an excretory organ. It makes urea, which is a vital preparatory step, converting the very toxic ammonia produced from amino acids into the much less toxic urea that the blood can carry safely to the kidneys; but the liver does not remove the urea from the body. Manufacture is the liver; removal is the kidney.

The questions for this section are with you now: the definition of excretion, excretion against egestion, and the three excretory organs with their wastes.

## Subtopic: The Urinary System

The urinary system consists of two kidneys, two ureters, the bladder and the urethra. The kidneys are bean-shaped organs about 11 centimetres long, lying one on each side of the spine at the back of the abdomen, just below the ribs and partly protected by them. Each receives blood from a renal artery branching directly off the aorta, and returns cleaned blood through a renal vein; about a quarter of the blood the heart pumps passes through the kidneys at any moment, which shows how central their work is.

Inside, each kidney contains about a million microscopic filtering units called nephrons, each a tiny tube wrapped around a knot of capillaries. The nephrons do the filtering and reclaiming described in the next section, and the urine they produce drains into a central space in the kidney. From there a ureter, a muscular tube about 25 centimetres long, carries urine down to the bladder by waves of contraction; urine moves this way even when you are lying down, so gravity is not the mechanism.

The bladder is a muscular, stretchy bag that stores urine. It holds about 400 to 500 millilitres comfortably and signals the brain to empty when it is about half full, and a ring of muscle at its outlet, which comes under voluntary control in early childhood, keeps it closed until a convenient moment. The urethra is the tube that carries urine from the bladder out of the body; in males it also carries semen, which is why the male reproductive and urinary systems share this final tube.

Urine itself is about 95 percent water, with about 2 percent urea and the rest dissolved salts and small amounts of other wastes. Its yellow colour comes from a breakdown product of haemoglobin. Normal urine contains no glucose, no protein and no blood cells, and the presence of any of these is a sign of disease, which is why a urine test is one of the first investigations a clinic performs.

## Subtopic: Filtration and Reabsorption

The kidney makes urine in two steps, and both must be described for full marks. The first step is filtration. Blood enters each nephron's knot of capillaries under high pressure, and the pressure forces the small components of the blood out through the thin capillary walls into the nephron tube: water, glucose, salts, urea and other small molecules all pass. Large components stay in the blood: red and white blood cells and the large protein molecules of the plasma are too big for the gaps. The fluid in the tube, the filtrate, is therefore blood plasma without its proteins, and the kidneys produce about 180 litres of it every day.

If all 180 litres left the body you would be dead of dehydration within hours, so the second step is reabsorption. As the filtrate moves along the nephron tube, the cells of the tube wall take back into the blood everything the body still needs. All of the glucose is reabsorbed, so that none appears in normal urine. Most of the water is reabsorbed, about 99 percent. The salts the body needs are reabsorbed and the excess is left behind. Urea is largely not reabsorbed, so its concentration rises as the water is removed around it. What remains at the end of the tube, roughly 1,5 litres a day of water carrying urea and excess salts, is urine.

Reabsorption of water is adjustable, and this is how the kidneys control the body's water balance. On a hot day, or after exercise, you lose water in sweat and the blood becomes more concentrated; the brain detects this and releases a hormone that instructs the kidneys to reabsorb more water, so you produce a small volume of dark, concentrated urine, and you feel thirsty. After drinking a lot on a cool day the opposite happens: less water is reabsorbed and you produce a large volume of pale urine. The colour of urine is therefore a rough indicator of hydration.

The logic of filter-then-reclaim is worth stating explicitly, because learners ask why the kidney does not simply remove the urea directly. Filtration under pressure is fast and unselective; it clears every small molecule at once. Reabsorption is selective; it rescues the valuable ones. Together they remove any small waste, including ones the body has never met before, such as a new medicine, while keeping what matters. Filtration first, reabsorption second, and the words small, large, glucose, water and urea in their proper places.

## Subtopic: Health of the Excretory System and the Error Museum

Kidney stones form when salts dissolved in urine crystallise into hard lumps inside the kidney, usually because the urine is too concentrated for too long. Small stones pass out unnoticed; larger ones block a ureter and cause severe pain until they pass or are broken up by sound waves or removed surgically. The main prevention is drinking enough water, about 1,5 to 2 litres a day in a temperate climate and more in the heat, so that urine stays dilute.

Urinary tract infections occur when bacteria enter the urethra and multiply in the bladder, causing burning, frequent urination and sometimes fever; they are more common in females because the female urethra is shorter, and they are treated with antibiotics. If untreated an infection can travel up the ureters to the kidneys. Good hygiene and drinking enough water, which flushes the system, are the preventive measures.

Kidney failure is the loss of the kidneys' ability to filter. Its two commonest causes in South Africa are uncontrolled diabetes and uncontrolled high blood pressure, both of which damage the delicate capillaries of the nephrons over years; this is why clinics test the urine of diabetic patients for protein, which leaks through damaged filters. When the kidneys fail, urea and water accumulate and the patient dies within weeks unless the blood is cleaned artificially. In dialysis the patient's blood is passed through a machine in which it flows alongside a special fluid, separated by a membrane that lets urea and excess salts and water cross but keeps blood cells and proteins; a session takes about four hours and is repeated three times a week. A kidney transplant from a matched donor is the permanent solution, and a person can live normally with one healthy kidney, which is why living donation is possible.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: the kidneys make urea; the liver makes it and the kidneys remove it. Two: faeces are excreted; they are egested, because they were never part of the body. Three: the bladder filters the blood; the bladder only stores urine, and the kidneys filter. Four: glucose is normally found in urine; it is all reabsorbed, and glucose in urine is a sign of diabetes. Five: the kidneys remove water from the body as waste; water is not a waste, and the kidneys remove only the excess while reabsorbing the rest to keep the balance.

Layout for a kidney question: name the organ, then the two processes in order, filtration of small molecules under pressure and selective reabsorption of glucose, most water and needed salts, and end with the composition of urine. Three sentences, and the system that keeps your blood clean is described exactly as the memorandum expects.

The questions for this section are ready: kidney stones and water, infections, kidney failure with dialysis, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same system again, through a rubbish removal service, a sieve that takes things back, and the colour of your urine on a hot day — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Rubbish Removal Service

Every household makes rubbish just by living in the house, and every cell in your body makes rubbish just by being alive. When a cell burns glucose with oxygen for energy, the smoke is carbon dioxide. When your liver breaks down the extra protein from last night's chicken, the ash is a chemical called urea. Leave the smoke and the ash to pile up and the house becomes unliveable; leave carbon dioxide and urea to pile up in the blood and your cells are poisoned. Getting rid of rubbish that the cells themselves made is called excretion.

One thing to be clear about before anything else. Excretion is not the same as going to the toilet for a number two. Faeces are the leftovers of food that your gut could not digest: fibre, bits of vegetable skin. That food went in your mouth, travelled along the tube of your gut and came out the other end without ever actually getting into your body's cells or blood. Removing it is called egestion. Excretion is about rubbish your cells made. Different word, different process, and a very common test question.

The body has three rubbish exits. The lungs breathe out the smoke, carbon dioxide, with every breath. The skin lets out some water and salt as sweat, though sweating is mostly about cooling you down. And the kidneys, the main rubbish service, take the urea, the extra water and the extra salt out of the blood and send them out as urine. Kidneys, lungs, skin: those are the excretory organs.

One trap: the liver is the factory that makes the urea, turning a very poisonous chemical from protein into a safer one the blood can carry. But the liver does not remove it. Making the rubbish bag is the liver; taking it to the dump is the kidney.

## Subtopic: A Sieve That Takes Things Back

How do the kidneys actually get the urea out of the blood? Not by picking it out one molecule at a time. They use a sieve, and then they go back through what fell through the sieve and reclaim the good stuff. Here is the picture. Imagine emptying your whole school bag onto a table through a sieve to find one lost sweet wrapper. Everything small falls through: pens, coins, the wrapper, your earphones. The big things, the books and the lunchbox, stay on top. Then you pick the pens, coins and earphones back out of the pile and put them in the bag, and what is left on the table is the rubbish.

That is exactly the two steps. Step one, filtration: blood is squeezed under pressure through the walls of tiny blood vessels in the kidney, and everything small comes through, water, glucose, salts and urea, while the big things, blood cells and proteins, stay in the blood. Your kidneys do this to about 180 litres of fluid a day, which is more than a full bathtub. Step two, reabsorption: as that fluid trickles along a tiny tube, the kidney grabs back everything you still need. Every bit of the glucose. Almost all the water, 99 out of every 100 drops. The salt you need. The urea is left behind. What drips out at the end, about one and a half litres a day, is urine: water, urea and extra salt.

Why the roundabout way? Because a sieve catches everything small at once, including rubbish the body has never seen before, like a new medicine, and reclaiming is where the choosing happens. Filter everything, then take back the treasure.

Then the plumbing. Two kidneys, one each side of your spine at the back, below the ribs. Two tubes, the ureters, carrying urine down to the bladder, the stretchy storage bag that holds about two cups before it starts nagging you. One tube out, the urethra. Kidney, ureter, bladder, urethra, in that order.

Hold the sieve and the reclaiming in mind and try this part's questions: what falls through, what is taken back, and the four parts of the plumbing.

## Subtopic: Water In, Water Out

Your urine tells you what your kidneys are doing. On a hot day at school, running around at break, you sweat a lot and lose water. Your blood gets a little too concentrated, your brain notices, and it tells the kidneys to take back even more water from the filtered fluid. You make only a little urine, and it is dark yellow. You also feel thirsty, which is the brain's other move. On a cold day when you have been drinking water all morning, the kidneys let more water go, and you make a lot of pale urine. Pale means well hydrated; dark means drink something. The kidneys are not just a rubbish service; they are the body's water balance.

That balance is also why water matters for kidney health. If urine stays too concentrated for too long, the salts in it can crystallise into kidney stones, hard little lumps that cause terrible pain when they block the tube. Drinking enough, about one and a half to two litres a day and more in the heat, keeps the urine dilute and flushes the system, which also helps prevent infections of the bladder, the burning, frequent urination that bacteria cause and that antibiotics treat.

The serious one is kidney failure, when the sieves are so damaged they stop working. In South Africa the two big causes are diabetes and high blood pressure that are not controlled, both of which wreck the tiny vessels in the kidney over the years. Without working kidneys, urea and water pile up and a person dies in weeks, so they go on dialysis: three times a week, about four hours a time, their blood runs through a machine with an artificial sieve that does the kidneys' job. The real fix is a transplant, and because you can live a full life with one healthy kidney, a living relative can donate one.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Excretion removes rubbish the cells made: carbon dioxide out through the lungs, water and salt through the skin, and urea, extra water and extra salt through the kidneys as urine. Egestion of faeces is different. The kidney filters everything small out of the blood, about 180 litres a day, then takes back all the glucose, 99 percent of the water and the salt it needs, leaving about 1,5 litres of urine. Kidney, ureter, bladder, urethra. Drink water, keep the sieves clean. In Grade 11 Life Sciences the nephron itself gets a full chapter.
