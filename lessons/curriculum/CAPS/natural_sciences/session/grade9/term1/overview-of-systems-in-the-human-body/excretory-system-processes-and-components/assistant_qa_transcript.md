### subtopic_1 — Excretion and the Excretory Organs
**Assistant:** Why does the body bother turning ammonia into urea in the liver instead of just excreting the ammonia?

**Tutor:** Because ammonia is far too poisonous to be carried around in the blood at any useful concentration, and urea is not. When amino acids from excess protein are broken down, the nitrogen-containing part comes off as ammonia, which damages cells, and especially brain cells, at very low concentrations; fish can get away with excreting ammonia because they release it straight into the surrounding water through their gills, where it is instantly diluted away. A land animal has to hold its waste in the blood until it reaches the kidneys, perhaps for an hour, and ammonia at the concentration that would require is lethal. So the liver immediately combines the ammonia with carbon dioxide to make urea, which is thousands of times less toxic and dissolves easily in water. Urea can then circulate safely in the blood at the concentration needed and be filtered out at the kidneys, concentrated in the urine and stored in the bladder for hours without harm. The cost is that the conversion uses energy, but the alternative is death, so every mammal pays it. This is also why liver failure is so dangerous: ammonia accumulates and the patient becomes confused and eventually unconscious. For the examiner, keep the two organs in their roles: the liver makes urea, the kidneys excrete it.

### subtopic_2 — The Urinary System
**Assistant:** If the kidney filters out 180 litres a day, why does the body not just filter less and skip the reabsorption?

**Tutor:** Because filtering a great deal and then reclaiming is the only way to remove every kind of small waste without having to recognise each one. Filtration is a pressure sieve: it is unselective, so it clears urea, excess salts, the breakdown products of medicines and anything else small, including substances the body has never met before, in a single pass. The price is that it also pushes out the water, glucose and useful salts, so a second, selective step must rescue those. Reabsorption works by specific carrier proteins in the tube wall that recognise glucose and the needed salts and pump them back, with water following; anything with no carrier, such as urea, is left to go. If the kidney filtered only a little, it would clear waste too slowly; a build-up of urea between meals would be unavoidable. If it tried to be selective at the filter instead, it would need a recognising mechanism for every possible waste, which is impossible. So the design is filter everything small, fast and blind, then reclaim the known valuables, slowly and precisely. The volume also gives the kidney fine control over water balance, because adjusting how much of that huge filtrate is reabsorbed is what lets it produce anything from half a litre of dark urine on a hot day to several litres of pale urine after drinking heavily.

### subtopic_3 — Filtration and Reabsorption
**Assistant:** Why does glucose in the urine point to diabetes if the kidneys are supposed to reabsorb all of it?

**Tutor:** Because reabsorption has a ceiling, and in untreated diabetes the blood glucose rises above it. The carriers in the nephron wall that pump glucose back into the blood can only work so fast; in a healthy person the amount of glucose arriving in the filtrate is well below that limit, so every molecule is reclaimed and the urine contains none. In diabetes the body cannot move glucose from the blood into the cells properly, either because the pancreas makes too little insulin or because the cells no longer respond to it, so blood glucose climbs to two or three times the normal level. The filtrate then carries more glucose than the carriers can handle, the surplus passes along the tube and appears in the urine. Glucose in the tube also holds water with it, so the diabetic produces large volumes of urine and is constantly thirsty, which were the first signs of the disease recognised by ancient doctors, who tasted the urine. A simple dipstick test on urine at a clinic therefore screens for diabetes in seconds, and a positive result sends the patient for a proper blood glucose test. The further point for this lesson is that years of high glucose damage the delicate filtering capillaries themselves, so uncontrolled diabetes is a leading cause of kidney failure, and diabetics are also tested for protein leaking into the urine as an early warning.

### subtopic_4 — Health of the Excretory System and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How does a dialysis machine know what to take out of the blood and what to leave?

**Tutor:** It does not know anything; it uses the same two physical principles the kidney uses, a membrane with small holes and diffusion down a concentration gradient. The patient's blood is pumped through a bundle of very fine hollow tubes whose walls are a membrane with pores small enough to block blood cells and plasma proteins but large enough to let water, salts, urea and glucose pass, exactly the sieve of the nephron. On the other side of the membrane flows the dialysis fluid, which is made up with the normal healthy concentrations of glucose and the useful salts and with no urea at all. Urea in the blood is high and in the fluid is zero, so urea diffuses out of the blood into the fluid and is carried away. Glucose and the needed salts are at the same concentration on both sides, so there is no net movement and they stay in the blood. Any salt that is too high in the blood diffuses out until it matches the fluid. Excess water is removed by applying a slight pressure difference across the membrane. So the composition of the fluid does the selecting: whatever it contains at normal levels is retained and whatever it lacks is drawn out. Because it is slower than a kidney and only runs for about four hours three times a week, wastes build up between sessions, which is why patients must limit salt, fluid and protein and why a transplant is the preferred long-term treatment.
