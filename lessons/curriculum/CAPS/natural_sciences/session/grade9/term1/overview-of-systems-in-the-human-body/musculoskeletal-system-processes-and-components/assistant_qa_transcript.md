### subtopic_1 — The Skeleton and Its Functions
**Assistant:** If bone is so hard and full of minerals, how can it be alive and heal itself?

**Tutor:** Because the hard mineral is only the material the living cells have built around themselves, in the same way that a coral reef is a hard structure built by soft living animals. Bone tissue is about two thirds calcium phosphate crystals, which give it hardness, laid down on a scaffold of collagen fibres, which give it toughness so it does not shatter like chalk. Embedded throughout this material are living bone cells sitting in tiny chambers, connected to one another and to blood vessels that run through channels in the bone. Those cells are constantly at work: some dissolve old bone and others lay down new, so your entire skeleton is replaced roughly every ten years, and the bone adapts to the loads placed on it, which is why weight-bearing exercise makes it denser and bed rest makes it thinner. When a bone fractures, the blood vessels inside it bleed and a clot forms; cells from the surrounding tissue multiply and first lay down soft cartilage across the gap, then replace that with new bone over about six weeks, and finally remodel it until the repair is almost invisible. A cast works by holding the ends still so that this bridge is not broken as it forms. Bone marrow in the cavities is also living tissue, producing millions of blood cells every second. So bone is a living organ with a hard product, not a dead mineral, and that is why it can grow, strengthen and mend.

### subtopic_2 — Joints and Their Types
**Assistant:** Why can the shoulder move in a circle but the knee only bends? They are both big joints.

**Tutor:** Because the shapes of the bone ends are different, and the shape of the joint fixes the movement it allows. The shoulder is a ball-and-socket joint: the head of the humerus is a smooth ball that sits in a shallow cup on the shoulder blade, so it can swing forward, backward, sideways and rotate, giving the arm the widest range of any joint in the body. The knee is a hinge: the rounded ends of the femur roll on the flat top of the tibia, guided by ligaments that run across and inside the joint, so it bends and straightens in one plane and resists twisting, which is why a footballer who twists a planted knee tears those ligaments. Each design is a trade-off. The shoulder's freedom makes it the most commonly dislocated joint, because a shallow cup cannot hold the ball under a hard blow; the knee's restriction makes it strong enough to carry your whole weight at every step and to lock straight so that standing costs little muscular effort. The same logic places a hinge at the elbow for strong lifting and a separate pivot joint beside it so that the forearm can rotate, which is why the forearm has two bones. When you are asked to classify a joint, picture the movement and match it: one plane means hinge, all directions means ball-and-socket, rotation about an axis means pivot.

### subtopic_3 — Muscles and Antagonistic Pairs
**Assistant:** Why does the biceps not just push the arm back down when it relaxes?

**Tutor:** Because a muscle has only one active action, contraction, and relaxation is the absence of that action, not its reverse. A skeletal muscle is made of long cells packed with two kinds of protein filament that slide past each other when the nerve signal arrives, shortening the cell and so the whole muscle, and that shortening pulls on the tendon and the bone. When the signal stops, the filaments simply stop gripping; the muscle goes slack and can be stretched back to its resting length by something else, but it generates no force of its own in that direction. A relaxed biceps is like a loose rope lying along the arm. So to move the forearm down, another force is required, and the body provides it with the triceps on the other side of the elbow, which contracts and pulls the forearm straight while stretching the slack biceps back to length. That is the whole reason muscles are arranged in antagonistic pairs around every movable joint: flexor and extensor, biceps and triceps, hamstrings and quadriceps. Gravity can help lower the arm slowly, but controlled, fast or upward movement against resistance always needs the opposing muscle. In an answer, name all four parts: the muscle that contracts, the muscle that relaxes, the bone that moves and the joint at which it moves.

### subtopic_4 — Health of the System and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why do I need to worry about osteoporosis as a teenager if it only affects old people?

**Tutor:** Because the bone you will have at seventy is largely decided by the bone you build before you are about twenty-five. Bone mass rises through childhood and the teenage years, peaks in the mid-twenties and then slowly declines for the rest of life, faster in women after menopause because the hormone that protects bone falls away. Someone who reaches the peak with dense, well-mineralised bones can lose mineral for decades and still have enough; someone who reached a low peak crosses the threshold into osteoporosis, porous and brittle bone that fractures from a stumble, much sooner. The teenage years are when the skeleton is most responsive, so three things matter now. Calcium in the diet, from milk, amasi, cheese and leafy greens, is the mineral itself. Vitamin D, which the skin makes in sunlight, is needed for the gut to absorb that calcium; without it the calcium passes straight through, which is why children kept indoors can develop rickets even on a good diet. And weight-bearing exercise, running, jumping, carrying, dancing, loads the bone and signals the bone cells to lay down more mineral where the stress falls. Smoking and heavy fizzy-drink consumption in place of milk work against all three. So osteoporosis is an old person's disease with a teenage cause, and the examiner wants calcium, vitamin D and exercise as the prevention.
