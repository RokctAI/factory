### subtopic_1 — Components of the Nervous System
**Assistant:** How does an electrical impulse travel along a nerve if there is no wire?

**Tutor:** The neuron itself is the wire, but it works by chemistry rather than by a flow of electrons as a copper cable does. The membrane of a resting neuron pumps charged particles, mostly sodium and potassium ions, so that the inside of the cell is slightly negative compared with the outside. When a receptor or another neuron stimulates one point on the membrane strongly enough, tiny channels open there and sodium ions rush in, briefly flipping the charge at that spot. That flip triggers the channels in the next patch of membrane, which flip in turn, and so a wave of charge reversal sweeps along the axon from one end to the other; behind the wave the pumps restore the resting state within a thousandth of a second, ready for the next impulse. The impulse is therefore not electrons moving down the fibre but a self-propagating change in the membrane, a bit like a line of falling dominoes or a flame running along a fuse, and it is all-or-nothing: a stimulus either triggers a full impulse or none. The fatty sheath wrapped around many axons insulates sections so the wave jumps from gap to gap, which is why sheathed fibres reach about 120 metres per second while bare ones manage about 1. At the end of the axon the impulse cannot jump the gap to the next cell, so a chemical is released that crosses the synapse and triggers the next neuron. Grade 9 needs only that neurons carry electrical impulses quickly; the ions and the sheath are Grade 12 Life Sciences.

### subtopic_2 — The Brain and the Spinal Cord
**Assistant:** If the spinal cord can act on its own in a reflex, why do we say the brain controls the body?

**Tutor:** Because the spinal cord's independence is limited to a small set of fixed, protective responses, while everything that involves choice, learning or combining information is the brain's. A reflex arc is hard-wired: the same stimulus produces the same response every time, and the spinal cord cannot decide not to withdraw your hand from a flame. The brain can. If you are carrying a hot plate of food, the cerebrum can send impulses down the cord that override the reflex so that you set the plate down rather than drop it, which is why people manage to hold things that hurt. The brain also receives a copy of every reflex signal a fraction of a second later, registers the pain, learns from it and plans to use a cloth next time; the spinal cord learns nothing. The medulla, which is part of the brain, sets heart rate and breathing continuously, and the cerebellum corrects every movement in progress. So the division is one of speed against judgement: the cord handles a few emergencies instantly and identically, and the brain handles everything else, including whether to let the reflex stand. That is also why spinal injuries are so devastating. Below the break the cord's own reflexes may still work, so a patient's leg can jerk when the knee is tapped, but no sensation reaches the brain and no voluntary command reaches the leg, because the cable between them is cut.

### subtopic_3 — Stimulus, Response and the Reflex Arc
**Assistant:** Why does a stroke on one side of the brain paralyse the other side of the body?

**Tutor:** Because the motor and sensory pathways cross from one side to the other as they pass through the brain stem on their way to and from the spinal cord. The motor neurons that start in the left cerebrum run down through the medulla, cross to the right, and continue down the right side of the spinal cord to the muscles of the right arm and leg; the pathways from the right cerebrum cross to the left. Sensory pathways cross in the same way, so touch on your right hand is registered in your left cerebrum. Nobody knows for certain why evolution arranged it this way, but it is consistent across vertebrates. The consequence is that when a clot or a burst vessel kills a patch of the left cerebrum, the right side of the face, arm and leg lose movement and feeling, and because the main language areas sit in the left hemisphere in most people, speech is often affected too; a right-sided stroke paralyses the left side and more often disturbs spatial awareness instead. The dying neurons cannot be replaced, so recovery depends on neighbouring regions slowly taking over some of the lost work through rehabilitation. The practical message in this lesson is prevention: uncontrolled high blood pressure weakens the vessels and smoking narrows them, and both are common and avoidable, which is why stroke is one of the leading causes of death in South Africa.

### subtopic_4 — Health of the Nervous System and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How does the ruler-drop test actually measure reaction time in seconds?

**Tutor:** It converts a distance into a time using the physics of falling. When your partner lets go, the ruler starts from rest and accelerates downward under gravity at about 9,8 metres per second squared, so the distance it has fallen depends only on how long it has been falling: after a tenth of a second it has dropped about 5 centimetres, after two tenths about 20 centimetres, after three tenths about 44 centimetres. Your reaction time is the interval between seeing the ruler move and closing your fingers, and during that whole interval the ruler is falling, so the mark on the ruler at which you catch it tells you how long you took. Read off the distance, look it up in the table, or square-root it using the falling formula you will meet in Grade 10 Physical Sciences, and you have the time. Twenty centimetres is a good alert teenager, about 0,2 seconds, and that is the time for the full voluntary pathway: light receptors in the eye, sensory neuron to the cerebrum, a decision, motor neurons down to the hand muscles. Try it tired, or after a friend has had a drink, and the catch point moves to 30 or 40 centimetres because alcohol slows the chemical hand-over at every synapse along that path. It is a fair test only if the dropper gives no warning and the catcher's fingers start open at the same mark each time, which is a useful discussion of controlling variables for the practical task.
