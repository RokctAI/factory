# Part 1 — Expert

This session describes the components and processes of the musculoskeletal system: the skeleton and its functions, the joints and their types, the muscles and how they produce movement in antagonistic pairs, and the health of the system. The anchor example is the arm, where the humerus, radius and ulna meet at the hinge joint of the elbow and the biceps and triceps bend and straighten it. The governing principle is that a muscle can only pull, so every movement of a bone needs one muscle to make it and an opposing muscle to reverse it.

## Subtopic: The Skeleton and Its Functions

The adult human skeleton has 206 bones. A baby is born with about 270 separate bones, many of which fuse during childhood, so the number falls as you grow. The skeleton is divided into the axial skeleton, the skull, the vertebral column and the rib cage along the central axis of the body, and the appendicular skeleton, the bones of the limbs with the shoulder girdle and the pelvic girdle that attach them.

The skull protects the brain and houses the sense organs. The vertebral column, or spine, is a stack of 33 vertebrae: 7 in the neck, 12 attached to the ribs, 5 in the lower back, 5 fused to form the sacrum and 4 fused to form the coccyx, with discs of cartilage between the movable ones that cushion each step. The rib cage, 12 pairs of ribs joined to the sternum in front, protects the heart and lungs and moves in breathing. In the arm the humerus joins the radius and ulna at the elbow; in the leg the femur, the longest and strongest bone in the body, joins the tibia and fibula at the knee, with the patella in front. The pelvis supports the organs of the abdomen and transmits the weight of the body to the legs.

The skeleton has five functions, and all five are examined. Support: the bones are a rigid frame that holds the body upright and gives it shape. Movement: bones act as levers that muscles pull on at the joints. Protection: the skull encloses the brain, the rib cage the heart and lungs, the vertebrae the spinal cord. Production of blood cells: red bone marrow, found in the ends of long bones and in flat bones such as the sternum and pelvis, makes red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets. Storage: bone is the body's reserve of calcium and phosphate, released into the blood when needed.

Bone is living tissue. It is about two thirds mineral, mainly calcium phosphate, which gives it hardness, and one third protein fibres, mainly collagen, which give it toughness, and it contains living bone cells with their own blood supply, which is why a broken bone bleeds and heals. Cartilage is a softer, smooth, flexible tissue found at the ends of bones in joints, in the nose and ears, and as the discs between vertebrae.

The questions for this section are with you now: 206 bones, the main named bones, and the five functions of the skeleton.

## Subtopic: Joints and Their Types

A joint is the place where two or more bones meet, and joints are classified by how much movement they allow. Fixed or immovable joints allow none: the bones of the skull are locked together along wavy lines called sutures, so the brain is protected by a rigid case. Slightly movable joints allow a little: the vertebrae are joined by cartilage discs, so each joint moves a few degrees and the whole spine bends and twists. Freely movable joints, also called synovial joints, allow a wide range of movement and are found in the limbs.

A synovial joint has a standard structure. The ends of the bones are covered with smooth cartilage so they slide without grinding. The joint is enclosed in a capsule lined with a membrane that produces synovial fluid, an oily liquid that lubricates the joint and reduces friction, like oil in a bicycle chain. Strong bands of tough tissue called ligaments join bone to bone across the joint and hold it stable, preventing the bones from moving in directions they should not. Ligaments are distinct from tendons, which join muscle to bone; the two are confused constantly and the examiner tests the distinction.

The freely movable joints have types named after the movement they allow. A hinge joint moves in one plane only, bending and straightening like a door: the elbow and knee, and the joints of the fingers. A ball-and-socket joint has the rounded head of one bone sitting in the cup of another and moves in every direction, including rotation: the shoulder and the hip. A pivot joint lets one bone rotate around another: the joint between the first and second vertebrae of the neck that lets you shake your head, and the joint near the elbow that lets you turn your palm up and down. Gliding joints, where flat surfaces slide over one another, occur in the wrist and ankle.

In a question that gives a movement and asks for the joint, match the movement to the type: one-plane bending, hinge; movement in all directions, ball-and-socket; rotation about an axis, pivot. In a question that gives a joint and asks for the type, think of what the joint actually does when you move it. The elbow cannot rotate the forearm by itself; the small pivot joint beside it does that, which is why the forearm has two bones.

Take this section's questions now: fixed, slightly movable and freely movable, the parts of a synovial joint, and hinge against ball-and-socket against pivot.

## Subtopic: Muscles and Antagonistic Pairs

The body has three types of muscle tissue. Skeletal muscle is attached to bones, is under voluntary control and produces movement; it is the muscle of this lesson. Smooth muscle lines the walls of the gut, blood vessels and bladder and works involuntarily, pushing food along or narrowing a vessel. Cardiac muscle is found only in the heart, contracts rhythmically without tiring and is also involuntary. Grade 9 concentrates on skeletal muscle and its relationship to the skeleton.

A skeletal muscle is attached to bones across a joint by tendons, tough cords of inelastic tissue. When the muscle contracts it shortens and thickens, pulling the tendon, which pulls the bone, which moves at the joint. The crucial fact is that a muscle can only pull. It contracts to shorten and then relaxes, but relaxing does not push the bone back; a relaxed muscle simply goes slack. Therefore every movement that can happen in one direction needs a second muscle on the opposite side of the joint to move the bone back. Two muscles arranged this way are an antagonistic pair.

The arm is the standard example. The biceps lies on the front of the upper arm, attached to the shoulder blade above and the radius below. The triceps lies on the back of the upper arm, attached above the shoulder and to the ulna below. To bend the elbow, the biceps contracts and the triceps relaxes, and the forearm is pulled up. To straighten the elbow, the triceps contracts and the biceps relaxes, and the forearm is pulled down. At every moment one muscle of the pair is contracting while the other relaxes; the bending muscle is called the flexor and the straightening muscle the extensor. The hamstrings at the back of the thigh and the quadriceps at the front form the pair that bends and straightens the knee.

The answer format for a movement question therefore has four parts: the muscle that contracts, the muscle that relaxes, the bone that moves and the joint at which it moves. Bending the elbow: biceps contracts, triceps relaxes, radius is pulled up at the hinge joint of the elbow. The contraction is powered by the mitochondria of the muscle cells, which is why they are so numerous there, and the energy comes from respiration of glucose with oxygen delivered by the blood, connecting this system to last lesson's chain.

This section's questions are available now: three muscle types, tendons, muscles only pull, and the biceps and triceps in action.

## Subtopic: Health of the System and the Error Museum

Posture is the way the body is held. Good posture keeps the spine in its natural curves with the weight balanced over the hips and feet; slouching over a desk or a phone, or carrying a heavy school bag on one shoulder for years, strains the muscles and ligaments of the back and can pull the spine out of line. Carry a bag on both shoulders and keep the load to about one tenth of body mass.

Bones can break. A fracture is a crack or break in a bone, caused by a force greater than the bone can bear; it is treated by setting the bone in position and immobilising it in a cast while the bone cells lay down new bone, which takes about six weeks in a healthy young person. A sprain is damage to the ligaments of a joint when it is forced beyond its normal range, as in a twisted ankle; it causes swelling and pain and is treated with rest, ice, compression and elevation. A strain is damage to a muscle or tendon from overstretching. A dislocation is a bone forced out of its joint.

Osteoporosis is a condition in which bones lose mineral and become porous and brittle, so that they fracture easily; it is common in older people, especially women after menopause, and the best protection is laid down in the teenage years: enough calcium in the diet from milk, amasi, cheese and leafy greens, enough vitamin D from sunlight so that calcium can be absorbed, and regular weight-bearing exercise, which stimulates bone to become denser. Rickets is the childhood disease of soft, bowed bones caused by vitamin D deficiency. Arthritis is inflammation of joints; in osteoarthritis the cartilage wears away so the bones grind painfully, and in rheumatoid arthritis the body's own defences attack the joint lining.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: muscles push as well as pull; they only pull, which is why pairs are needed. Two: tendons join bone to bone; tendons join muscle to bone and ligaments join bone to bone. Three: cartilage is a kind of bone; it is a separate, softer tissue. Four: the knee is a ball-and-socket joint; it is a hinge. Five: the biceps contracts to straighten the arm; the biceps bends it and the triceps straightens it.

Layout for a joint question: name the joint type, give the movement it allows and one example. Layout for a movement question: contracting muscle, relaxing muscle, moving bone, joint. Four items in order, four marks, and the system that moves you is described exactly as the memorandum wants.

The questions for this section are ready: posture and the school bag, fractures and sprains, osteoporosis and its prevention, and the five exhibits of the museum.

## Subtopic: Scaffolding and Ropes

Look at a building site. Before the walls go up there is scaffolding: steel poles bolted together into a frame that holds everything up. That is your skeleton, 206 poles called bones. The frame does more than stand there. It holds you up, which is support. It has a hard box at the top around the brain and a cage around the heart and lungs, which is protection. It is what the muscles pull on to move you, which is movement. And two jobs you cannot see: inside the big bones, the red marrow is a factory making blood cells, and the bone itself is a store of calcium that the body can draw on.

Know the main poles. The skull on top. The spine, a stack of 33 small bones with rubbery cushions between them. The rib cage, 12 pairs. In the arm the humerus, then the radius and ulna side by side. In the leg the femur, the biggest bone you own, then the tibia and fibula, with the kneecap in front. The pelvis, the bowl in the middle that your legs hang from.

Now the ropes. Muscles are the ropes tied to the poles, and here is the one idea that unlocks the whole lesson: a rope can only pull. You cannot push with a rope. So to lift your forearm you need a rope on the front of the upper arm to pull it up, and to put it down again you need a second rope on the back to pull it back. The front rope is the biceps, the back rope is the triceps. When one pulls, the other goes slack. Pairs of ropes that work against each other like this are called antagonistic pairs, and every joint that moves has at least one pair.

The ropes are tied to the poles by tough cords called tendons. Feel the big one at the back of your ankle. And where two poles meet, they are strapped together by bands called ligaments, pole to pole. Rope to pole: tendon. Pole to pole: ligament.

Keep the scaffolding and the ropes in mind and try the questions for this part: the five jobs of the frame, the main bones, and why ropes come in pairs.

## Subtopic: Door Hinges and a Gear Lever

Where two poles meet is a joint, and the kind of joint decides how the poles can move. Some joints do not move at all. The bones of your skull are locked together along zigzag seams, like a lid glued shut, so the brain stays safe. Some move just a little: each small bone of the spine shifts a few degrees against its neighbour, but add up 24 of them and you can touch your toes.

The joints that really move are the ones in your arms and legs, and they come in types you can see around the house. A door hinge swings one way and back, nothing else. That is your elbow, your knee and your fingers: hinge joints. Now think of a gear lever in a car, or the joystick on a game controller, a ball sitting in a cup that can swing any direction and spin. That is your shoulder and your hip: ball-and-socket joints. Try it: swing your arm in a circle, then try to swing your lower arm in a circle at the elbow. The shoulder can, the elbow cannot. Last, a pivot, like a key turning in a lock, one bone spinning around another. The joint at the top of your neck that lets you shake your head no is a pivot, and so is the one near your elbow that lets you turn your palm up and down.

Inside every one of the big moving joints is the same kit. The ends of the bones are capped with smooth cartilage so they glide instead of grinding, like the pads on a bicycle brake. The whole joint is wrapped in a bag that makes an oily liquid, synovial fluid, the oil on the bicycle chain, so the joint moves without friction. And the ligaments strap the bones together so they cannot slip out.

The test move: they give you a joint, you name the type by thinking about what it does. One way and back, hinge. Every direction and spin, ball-and-socket. Rotating around an axis, pivot. Knee is a hinge; it is the one everyone gets wrong.

Hold the door hinge and the gear lever and try this part's questions: three amounts of movement, three types of moving joint, and the oil in the chain.

## Subtopic: Looking After the Frame

A bicycle lasts for years if you look after it, and so does your skeleton. Start with how you stand and sit. Slouching over your phone or hanging a heavy school bag off one shoulder pulls the spine out of line year after year, and the muscles of your back ache from holding you up crooked. Sit up, use both straps, and keep the bag under about a tenth of what you weigh.

Then the crashes. If you fall and a bone cracks, that is a fracture; the doctor lines it up and puts on a cast so it cannot move, and the living bone cells knit it together in about six weeks. If you twist your ankle and the ligaments stretch or tear, that is a sprain: swelling and pain, and the fix is rest, ice, compression and elevation. Pull a muscle or its tendon and that is a strain. Pop a bone out of its socket and that is a dislocation.

Then the slow wear. Osteoporosis is when bones lose their calcium and go brittle like old chalk, and it is the reason a grandmother can break a hip from a small fall. The surprising part is that you are building your defence against it right now, as a teenager, because this is when bones are laid down thickest. You need calcium, from milk, amasi, cheese and green leaves; vitamin D from sunlight, so your body can actually use the calcium; and exercise that loads the bones, running, jumping, carrying, which tells the bone to get denser. Arthritis is when a joint wears out or gets inflamed, the cartilage brake pads worn through so the bones grind, and it is painful and common in old age.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The skeleton is scaffolding: 206 bones that support, move, protect, make blood cells and store calcium. Joints are where poles meet: fixed in the skull, slightly movable in the spine, freely movable in the limbs as hinges, ball-and-sockets and pivots, each oiled with synovial fluid and strapped with ligaments. Muscles are ropes that only pull, tied on by tendons, working in antagonistic pairs like the biceps and triceps. Look after it: posture, calcium, sunlight, exercise. Grade 10 Life Sciences picks this frame up again as support systems in animals.
