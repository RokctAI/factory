# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains puberty: what it is, what starts it, which hormones control it and what changes they bring about in boys and in girls, together with the health and hygiene that go with those changes. The anchor numbers are a starting age of roughly 8 to 13 in girls and 9 to 14 in boys, a first period at an average age of about 12 or 13, and a growth spurt that comes about two years earlier in girls than in boys. The governing principle is that a hormone is a chemical messenger, made by a gland, carried in the blood and acting on target organs elsewhere; puberty is what happens when the brain turns up the production of the sex hormones.

## Subtopic: What Puberty Is and What Starts It

Puberty is the stage of development during which a person becomes sexually mature, meaning that the reproductive organs start producing gametes and the body becomes capable of reproduction. It is part of a longer period called adolescence, which includes the emotional and social changes of becoming an adult and stretches through the teenage years. Puberty itself usually takes about three to five years from the first signs to full adult development.

The timing varies a great deal. In girls puberty commonly begins between about 8 and 13 years of age, and in boys between about 9 and 14. Girls on average start about one to two years before boys, which is why many Grade 7 and 8 girls are briefly taller than the boys in their class. Both early and late starts within these ranges are normal; the timing depends on genes, nutrition and general health, and it says nothing about how the person will turn out. A doctor should be consulted only if there are no signs at all by about 13 in girls or 14 in boys, or if signs appear unusually early.

The switch is in the brain. A region at the base of the brain called the hypothalamus begins to send signals to the pituitary gland, a pea-sized gland just below it. The pituitary then releases its own hormones into the blood, and these travel to the gonads, the testes and ovaries, and stimulate them to grow and to begin making gametes and sex hormones. The sex hormones in turn bring about the changes of puberty all over the body. So the chain is brain, pituitary, gonads, sex hormones, body changes. The pituitary is sometimes called the master gland because it controls several other glands in this way.

The changes of puberty are of two kinds. The primary sexual characteristics are the reproductive organs themselves, the testes and penis or the ovaries, uterus and vagina, which are present from birth and mature at puberty. The secondary sexual characteristics are the other features that develop at puberty and distinguish adult males from adult females, such as facial hair or breasts, but which are not themselves part of the reproductive organs.

## Subtopic: Hormones: Testosterone, Oestrogen and Progesterone

A hormone is a chemical messenger produced by a gland and released directly into the blood, which carries it to target organs elsewhere in the body, where it causes a particular response. The glands that make hormones are called endocrine glands, and together they form the endocrine system. Hormones differ from nerve impulses in being slower to act, because they travel in the blood, and longer-lasting in their effects; that suits puberty, which unfolds over years.

Testosterone is the main male sex hormone, made by the testes. At puberty it stimulates the growth of the penis and testes, the start of sperm production, the growth of facial and body hair, the enlargement of the larynx that deepens the voice, and the development of muscle and bone. It also influences behaviour, for example increasing the sex drive.

Oestrogen is the main female sex hormone, made by the ovaries. At puberty it stimulates the development of the breasts, the widening of the hips, the growth of the uterus and vagina and the start of the menstrual cycle, and each month it causes the lining of the uterus to thicken. Progesterone, also made by the ovaries, maintains the thickened lining of the uterus after ovulation and, if pregnancy occurs, keeps it in place throughout the pregnancy. When progesterone levels fall at the end of a cycle without pregnancy, the lining breaks down and menstruation begins. The stages-of-reproduction lesson follows these two hormones through the cycle.

It is worth knowing that both sexes make some of each hormone: males make small amounts of oestrogen and females small amounts of testosterone. It is the large difference in the amounts that produces the different male and female characteristics.

For marking, each hormone needs three facts: where it is made, what kind of messenger it is, and two of its effects. Testosterone: testes; carried in the blood; deeper voice and facial hair. Oestrogen: ovaries; carried in the blood; breast development and thickening of the uterine lining. Progesterone: ovaries; carried in the blood; maintains the uterine lining and supports pregnancy.

## Subtopic: Secondary Sexual Characteristics in Boys and Girls

In boys, under the influence of testosterone, the testes and penis grow, sperm production begins, and the boy may experience wet dreams, the release of semen during sleep, which is a normal sign that the system has started working. Hair grows on the face, in the armpits, in the pubic area and often on the chest, legs and arms. The larynx grows larger and the vocal cords become longer and thicker, so the voice deepens, often cracking unpredictably for some months while the change happens, and the larynx may show at the front of the neck as the Adam's apple. The shoulders broaden, muscle mass increases and the body becomes more muscular. The growth spurt usually comes at about 13 to 15 years.

In girls, under the influence of oestrogen, the breasts develop, usually the first visible sign of puberty, often starting with a small, sometimes tender bud under the nipple. The hips widen as the pelvis broadens, an adaptation for childbirth, and more fat is stored on the hips, thighs and breasts. Hair grows in the armpits and the pubic area. The uterus and ovaries mature, ovulation starts and the first menstrual period, called menarche, arrives, on average at about 12 or 13 years; the first cycles are often irregular for a year or two. The growth spurt usually comes at about 10 to 12 years, earlier than in boys.

In both sexes several changes are shared. There is a rapid growth spurt in height and weight. Sweat glands in the armpits and groin become active, and bacteria on the skin break down this sweat, producing body odour. The oil glands of the skin become more active, and when excess oil and dead skin block a pore, bacteria multiply there and cause pimples, which is acne. And there are emotional changes: stronger and faster-changing moods, a growing need for independence, interest in relationships and sometimes worry about body image. These are normal results of hormonal and brain development during adolescence.

Set out as a table for marking: boys, girls, both. Boys: facial hair, deeper voice, broader shoulders, more muscle, sperm production. Girls: breasts, wider hips, menstruation, ovulation. Both: pubic and underarm hair, growth spurt, active sweat and oil glands, emotional changes.

## Subtopic: Health, Hygiene and the Error Museum

The new body needs new habits. Because sweat glands are more active and bacteria cause body odour, a daily wash or shower with soap, clean underwear and socks every day, and a deodorant or antiperspirant become important. Acne is best managed by washing the face gently twice a day with mild soap and water and not squeezing pimples, which pushes bacteria deeper and can leave scars; severe acne can be treated by a clinic or doctor. Hair in the armpits and pubic area traps sweat, so washing those areas carefully matters.

Menstrual hygiene is part of everyday health for girls. Sanitary pads should be changed several times a day, about every four to six hours or more often on heavy days; tampons must be changed at least every eight hours and never left in for longer. Menstrual cups are a reusable option. Hands should be washed before and after changing, and used products wrapped and placed in a bin, never flushed. Some period pain is common and can be eased by warmth, gentle exercise or simple painkillers; pain that stops daily life, or very heavy bleeding, should be checked at a clinic. Tracking periods on a calendar helps a girl know when to expect the next one.

Other health issues in puberty include body image pressure, which can lead to unhealthy dieting or eating disorders, and the misuse of anabolic steroids, artificial forms of testosterone that some young men take to build muscle; they can damage the liver and heart, shrink the testes and stop natural growth early. Good nutrition matters too, because the growth spurt needs plenty of protein, calcium for bones and, in girls after menarche, iron to replace what is lost in menstruation. Sleep matters more than ever, because growth hormone is released mainly during deep sleep.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: puberty starts at the same age for everybody; it can start anywhere in a wide range, and both early and late are normal. Two: hormones travel along the nerves; hormones are carried in the blood. Three: a cracking voice means the voice box is damaged; the larynx is growing and the vocal cords are lengthening. Four: breasts and facial hair are primary sexual characteristics; they are secondary, while the reproductive organs are primary. Five: acne is caused by dirty skin or by eating chocolate; it is caused by hormones making the oil glands more active, with pores blocked and infected by bacteria.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same changes again, through the body's text messages, two lists of changes, and the same road taken on different timetables — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Body's Text Messages

Your body has two ways of sending messages. The nerves are like phone calls: instant, to one exact place, over in a moment. Hormones are like text messages sent to a whole group: they are chemicals dropped into the blood, they travel everywhere the blood goes, and only the parts of the body set up to read that message respond. They are slower, but the effect lasts. Puberty is a long series of text messages that goes on for years.

Who sends them? It starts in your brain. A small control area at the base of the brain decides it is time and sends a signal to the pituitary gland, a gland the size of a pea hanging just below it. The pituitary sends out its own messages in the blood to the gonads, the testes in boys and the ovaries in girls, and says: wake up, grow, start working. The gonads then send out the main messages of puberty, the sex hormones.

In boys the main message is testosterone, sent from the testes. It says: grow hair on the face, make the voice deeper, build muscle, broaden the shoulders, start making sperm. In girls the main messages are oestrogen and progesterone, sent from the ovaries. Oestrogen says: grow breasts, widen the hips, start the monthly cycle, thicken the lining of the womb. Progesterone says: hold that lining in place, in case a baby arrives.

Both boys and girls actually get a little of each other's hormones too. It is the amounts that are very different, and that is what makes the bodies develop differently.

So the chain is: brain, pituitary, testes or ovaries, sex hormones in the blood, changes all over the body. Brain decides, blood delivers, body changes.

## Subtopic: The Boys' List and the Girls' List

Picture two lists on a fridge. The first list is for boys. The testes and penis grow, and the testes start making sperm; a wet dream, semen released during sleep, is just a sign the factory has started, nothing to worry about. Hair arrives on the face, in the armpits, around the private parts and often on the chest and legs. The voice box grows bigger and the vocal cords get longer and thicker, like swapping a guitar's thin string for a thick one, so the voice drops; for a while it may crack and jump without warning, which is just the voice box growing. The bump at the front of the neck, the Adam's apple, is the bigger voice box. Shoulders broaden and muscles build.

The second list is for girls. Usually the first sign is breasts starting to grow, sometimes a little sore at first. The hips get wider, because the pelvis broadens, ready for one day carrying and delivering a baby, and the body stores a bit more fat on the hips and thighs, which is healthy and normal. Hair grows in the armpits and around the private parts. Then the periods start, the first one usually somewhere around 12 or 13, and for the first year or two they may come at irregular times.

Now the shared list, stuck between the two. Everyone shoots up in a growth spurt, girls at about 10 to 12 and boys at about 13 to 15, which is why girls are often taller for a year or two. Everyone sweats more, and the bacteria on the skin turn that sweat into body odour. Everyone's skin makes more oil, and when oil and dead skin block a pore and bacteria move in, you get a pimple: that is acne, and it is hormones, not dirt or chocolate. And everyone's feelings get bigger and change faster, which is the brain developing too.

One last label: the private organs you were born with are the primary sexual characteristics. Everything on these fridge lists that appears at puberty, the beard, the voice, the breasts, the hips, is a secondary sexual characteristic.

## Subtopic: Same Road, Different Timetables

Everyone in your class is walking the same road through puberty, but on different timetables. One friend might start at 9, another at 14, and both are completely normal. Some people grow tall early and then stop; others are small for years and then shoot up. Comparing yourself to the person next to you tells you nothing, except that you are on different timetables.

The new body needs some new habits, and they are simple. Wash every day with soap, especially the armpits and between the legs, because the new hair traps sweat and the bacteria love it. Clean underwear and socks every day, and a deodorant. Wash your face gently morning and evening, and do not squeeze pimples, because that pushes the germs deeper and can leave marks. For girls: change pads every four to six hours, more often on heavy days, tampons at least every eight hours, wash your hands before and after, wrap used products and bin them, never flush them. A little period pain is common and a hot water bottle helps; pain that keeps you in bed is worth a clinic visit.

Feed the growth spurt properly: protein from beans, eggs, fish or meat, calcium from milk and amasi for growing bones, and for girls, iron from foods like spinach, beans and red meat to replace what periods take. And sleep, because most of your growing actually happens while you sleep.

Two warnings. Pictures online and in adverts are edited, and no real body looks like that, so be kind to your own. And pills or injections that promise big muscles are usually artificial testosterone, steroids, which can damage the heart and liver and even stop you growing.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Puberty is the body becoming able to reproduce, starting roughly 8 to 13 in girls and 9 to 14 in boys. Brain, pituitary, gonads, hormones in the blood. Testosterone from the testes: hair, deep voice, muscles, sperm. Oestrogen and progesterone from the ovaries: breasts, hips, periods, the womb lining. Both: growth spurt, sweat, oil, acne, big feelings. Wash, change, eat well, sleep. In Grade 12 Life Sciences these hormones come back in the endocrine system.
