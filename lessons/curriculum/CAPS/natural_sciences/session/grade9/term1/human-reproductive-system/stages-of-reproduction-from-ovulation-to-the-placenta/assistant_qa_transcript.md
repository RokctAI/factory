### subtopic_1 — The Menstrual Cycle and Ovulation
**Assistant:** Why does the egg survive only about a day when sperm can last up to five?

**Tutor:** Because they are built for different jobs and protected by different conditions. The egg is a very large cell that has stopped part-way through its preparation and is waiting to be completed by fertilisation; it carries a food store but no means of renewing itself, and its outer layers and internal machinery begin to deteriorate within hours of ovulation, so after about 12 to 24 hours it can no longer be fertilised normally and simply breaks down and is absorbed. The sperm's survival depends heavily on where it is. In the acidic vagina most sperm die within hours. But around ovulation the cervix produces a thin, watery, slightly alkaline mucus that protects sperm, guides them through its channels and lets them shelter in tiny folds of the cervix, from which they are released gradually over several days. In these conditions some sperm stay capable of fertilisation for up to about five days. The combination gives a fertile window of about six days in each cycle: the five days before ovulation and the day of ovulation itself. That is why intercourse several days before ovulation can lead to pregnancy, with sperm already waiting in the oviduct when the egg arrives. It is also why calendar counting is unreliable as contraception: nobody can feel ovulation coming in advance, and with irregular cycles, very common in teenagers, it can come several days earlier or later than expected. The practical message is that from a biological point of view unprotected intercourse may lead to pregnancy on almost any day of the cycle, and only reliable contraception prevents it.

### subtopic_2 — Fertilisation and Implantation
**Assistant:** How does the baby get rid of urine and carbon dioxide before it is born?

**Tutor:** Through the placenta, using the mother's organs to finish the job. Every cell of the growing foetus respires, producing carbon dioxide, and breaks down excess amino acids, producing urea, just as your own cells do. These waste products enter the foetus's blood and are carried along the two umbilical arteries to the placenta. There, in the villi, the foetus's blood is separated from the mother's blood by only a thin barrier, and because the concentrations of carbon dioxide and urea are higher on the foetus's side than on the mother's, both diffuse across into the mother's blood. Her circulation carries the carbon dioxide to her lungs, where she breathes it out, and the urea to her kidneys, where it leaves in her urine. This is one reason why a pregnant woman breathes a little more deeply and her kidneys handle a larger load: she is doing the excretion for two. The foetus does have working kidneys from early in pregnancy, and it passes a dilute urine into the amniotic fluid, which is constantly swallowed and renewed; this helps the kidneys and gut develop and keeps the volume of fluid right, but the real removal of urea from the foetus is through the placenta. The lungs of the foetus are filled with fluid and take no part in gas exchange until birth. When the umbilical cord is clamped at birth, carbon dioxide quickly builds up in the baby's blood, and that rise is one of the strongest triggers for the baby's first breath, the moment its own lungs take over from the placenta.

### subtopic_3 — Pregnancy and the Placenta
**Assistant:** Why is drinking alcohol dangerous in pregnancy even if the mother does not feel drunk?

**Tutor:** Because alcohol crosses the placenta freely and the foetus is far more sensitive to it than the mother. Alcohol is a small molecule that dissolves in water and fat, so it passes through the placental barrier as easily as oxygen does, and within minutes the foetus's blood contains roughly the same concentration as the mother's. But the foetus cannot deal with it the way an adult does. An adult's liver breaks alcohol down steadily, while the foetus's liver is immature and processes it very slowly, so the alcohol stays longer in the foetus and keeps being exchanged back and forth. Meanwhile it interferes directly with how cells divide, move and connect in the developing brain, which is growing at an astonishing rate, and with the formation of the face and other organs. The result can be foetal alcohol spectrum disorder: a smaller head and brain, characteristic facial features such as a smooth groove between the nose and lip and a thin upper lip, poor growth, and lifelong problems with learning, memory, attention and behaviour. The damage is permanent and cannot be treated after birth, only managed. There is no amount of alcohol known to be safe, and there is no safe time in pregnancy, because the brain develops throughout all nine months, and damage can happen in the early weeks before a woman even knows she is pregnant. Whether the mother feels drunk depends on her own tolerance, not on the effect on the foetus. That is why the medical advice is clear: no alcohol at all when pregnant or trying to become pregnant. South Africa has some of the highest recorded rates of this disorder in the world, and it is entirely preventable.

### subtopic_4 — Birth, Harmful Substances and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** If the bloods do not mix, how do the mother's antibodies reach the baby?

**Tutor:** They are carried across the placental barrier actively, by a special transport system, rather than by leaking through. Antibodies are large protein molecules made by the mother's white blood cells to fight particular germs, far too large to diffuse through the thin barrier the way oxygen and glucose do. But the cells of the placental villi have receptor proteins that recognise one type of antibody, capture it from the mother's blood, carry it through the cell inside a small membrane bubble and release it on the foetus's side. This transport increases sharply in the last three months of pregnancy, so a baby born at full term arrives with a supply of its mother's antibodies against many of the infections she has had or been vaccinated against, such as measles, while a very premature baby receives fewer and is more vulnerable to infection. This borrowed protection is called passive immunity, because the baby did not make the antibodies itself. It fades over the first months of life as the antibodies are broken down, and by then the baby's own immune system is maturing and the clinic vaccinations, recorded on the Road to Health booklet, are building active immunity. Breastfeeding extends the borrowed protection, because colostrum, the first milk, and later breast milk contain a different type of antibody that coats the baby's gut and protects it against diarrhoeal and other infections. So the mother protects the baby in three ways: the placenta's antibody transport before birth, the antibodies in breast milk after birth, and her own vaccinations, which give her antibodies worth passing on.
