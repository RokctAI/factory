# Part 1 — Expert

This session describes each male and female reproductive organ in detail with its function, compares the structure of the sperm and the egg and links each feature to its function, and sets out how the standard diagrams are labelled. The anchor numbers are a sperm length of about 0,05 millimetres against an egg diameter of about 0,1 millimetres, a scrotum that keeps the testes about two to three degrees below body temperature, and a uterus about the size of a pear that can stretch to hold a full-term baby. The governing principle is that structure suits function: each organ and each gamete is shaped by the job it does.

## Subtopic: The Male Reproductive Organs

The two testes are oval organs about four to five centimetres long, each packed with tightly coiled tubules in which sperm are produced, while cells between the tubules produce testosterone. From puberty onward the testes make sperm continuously, roughly 1 500 every second, more than 100 million a day. The testes hang in the scrotum, a pouch of skin outside the abdomen, because sperm production needs a temperature about two to three degrees below the normal body temperature of 37 degrees. Small muscles in the scrotum pull the testes closer to the body in the cold and let them hang lower in the heat, regulating their temperature.

On the back of each testis lies the epididymis, a single tube about six metres long coiled into a small mass, where sperm are stored and finish maturing over about two weeks, gaining the ability to swim. From each epididymis a sperm duct, also called the vas deferens, runs up into the abdomen, loops over the ureter and behind the bladder, and joins the urethra. Sperm do not swim along the ducts; they are moved by waves of muscular contraction in the duct walls.

Three kinds of glands add fluid to the sperm. The two seminal vesicles, behind the bladder, produce most of the fluid, rich in the sugar fructose that gives the sperm energy. The prostate gland, a walnut-sized gland surrounding the urethra just below the bladder, adds a thin, milky, slightly alkaline fluid that helps neutralise the acidity of the vagina, which would otherwise harm the sperm. Two small Cowper's glands add a little clear fluid that lubricates and cleans the urethra. Sperm plus these fluids is semen; one release is about two to five millilitres.

The urethra carries semen, and at other times urine, through the penis to the outside. The penis contains spongy erectile tissue that fills with blood during sexual arousal, making the penis stiff and erect so that it can be inserted into the vagina to deposit semen near the cervix.

For marking, every male organ with its function: testis, produces sperm and testosterone; scrotum, holds the testes at a lower temperature; epididymis, stores and matures sperm; sperm duct, carries sperm to the urethra; seminal vesicles and prostate, add nourishing and protective fluid to form semen; urethra, carries semen and urine out; penis, deposits semen in the vagina.

The questions for this section are with you now: the temperature of the testes, the job of the epididymis, and the glands that make semen.

## Subtopic: The Female Reproductive Organs

The two ovaries are almond-shaped organs about three to four centimetres long, held in the pelvis on either side of the uterus. Each contains many thousands of immature eggs, each surrounded by a cluster of cells forming a follicle. In each menstrual cycle, under the influence of pituitary hormones, usually one follicle matures into a fluid-filled bubble that bulges on the ovary surface and bursts, releasing its egg. That release is ovulation. The ovaries also produce the hormones oestrogen and progesterone.

The egg is caught by the nearest oviduct, or Fallopian tube, a narrow tube about ten centimetres long whose open, funnel-shaped end is fringed with finger-like projections that sweep over the ovary. The egg cannot move on its own; it is carried along the oviduct by the beating of cilia lining the tube and by gentle muscular contractions of its wall, a journey of about three to four days to the uterus. Fertilisation, if it occurs, normally takes place in the upper third of the oviduct.

The uterus, or womb, is a hollow organ about seven to eight centimetres long, shaped like an upside-down pear, with a very thick wall of smooth muscle and an inner lining, the endometrium, that is rich in blood vessels and glands. The lining thickens each month to receive a fertilised egg and is shed during menstruation if none arrives. During pregnancy the muscular wall stretches enormously to hold the growing baby, and at birth its powerful contractions push the baby out.

The cervix is the narrow lower neck of the uterus, a ring of muscle with a small central canal that produces mucus. It keeps the uterus closed and protected during pregnancy and widens to about ten centimetres to let the baby pass at birth. The vagina is a muscular, elastic tube about eight to ten centimetres long, leading from the cervix to the outside; it receives the penis and semen during intercourse, carries the menstrual flow out, and stretches to form the birth canal. Its slightly acidic environment helps protect against infection. The external organs together form the vulva, including the folds of skin, the labia, that protect the openings of the urethra and the vagina, which are separate in females.

For marking, every female organ with its function: ovary, produces eggs and the hormones oestrogen and progesterone; oviduct, carries the egg to the uterus and is the site of fertilisation; uterus, where the embryo implants and develops; endometrium, the lining that receives the embryo; cervix, closes the uterus and dilates at birth; vagina, receives semen and is the birth canal.

Take this section's questions now: ovulation, how the egg moves along the oviduct, and the functions of the uterus, cervix and vagina.

## Subtopic: The Gametes: Sperm and Egg Compared

The sperm cell is one of the smallest cells in the body, about 0,05 millimetres long, and almost all of that is tail. It has three parts. The head contains the nucleus with 23 chromosomes and is capped by the acrosome, a small sac of enzymes that digests a path through the protective layers around the egg. The middle piece is packed with mitochondria, which release the energy needed for swimming through cellular respiration. The tail, a long whip-like flagellum, lashes from side to side to drive the sperm forward. The sperm carries almost no cytoplasm and no food store; it is built to be light and fast, and it lives only a few days.

The egg cell is the largest cell in the human body, about 0,1 millimetres in diameter, just visible to the naked eye as a speck. It is round and cannot move by itself. It contains a nucleus with 23 chromosomes and a large volume of cytoplasm with a store of nutrients to sustain the first days of development after fertilisation, before the embryo implants. It is surrounded by a jelly-like coat and a layer of follicle cells, which a single sperm must penetrate; once one sperm has entered, the coat hardens to keep others out.

The two gametes are adapted in opposite directions. The sperm is small, numerous and mobile: made in hundreds of millions because the journey is long and most will fail. The egg is large, few and stationary: one per cycle, with the food store the early embryo needs. Both carry exactly half the chromosomes, 23.

One further difference decides the sex of the baby. Among the 23 chromosomes in each gamete is one sex chromosome. Every egg carries an X chromosome. A sperm carries either an X or a Y, in roughly equal numbers. If an X-bearing sperm fertilises the egg the baby is XX, a girl; if a Y-bearing sperm does, the baby is XY, a boy. So it is the father's sperm that determines the sex of the child, a point Grade 12 Life Sciences develops.

Set out as a comparison for marking: size, small against large; number, millions against usually one a cycle; movement, swims with a tail against carried by cilia; food store, almost none against a large supply in the cytoplasm; chromosomes, 23 in both; sex chromosome, X or Y against always X.

This section's questions are available now: the parts of the sperm and their functions, the features of the egg, and which gamete decides the sex.

## Subtopic: Labelling Diagrams and the Error Museum

Diagram questions show the male system from the side and the female system from the front or the side, and the marks go to correct labels with clear label lines. In the side view of the male, trace from the bottom: testis inside the scrotum, epididymis on the back of the testis, sperm duct running up and over the bladder, seminal vesicle behind the bladder, prostate gland around the start of the urethra, the urethra running through the penis. The bladder is drawn above the prostate and is labelled because it is nearby, but it belongs to the urinary system.

In the front view of the female, the uterus sits in the centre like an upside-down pear, the oviducts curve out to each side ending in fringed funnels, the ovaries sit just below the funnels, and below the uterus the narrow cervix opens into the vagina. In the side view, the uterus lies above and behind the bladder, the vagina runs downward and backward behind the urethra, and the rectum lies behind the vagina, so that three separate openings are visible: urethra, vagina and anus.

Label lines must be ruled, must not cross, and must end exactly on the structure. A label pointing near the oviduct but touching the ovary earns nothing. When asked for a function, give the one function examiners expect for that structure, not a vague description of where it is.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: the bladder is a reproductive organ; it is a urinary organ, sharing only the urethra with the male reproductive system. Two: sperm swim all the way from the testes; inside the ducts they are moved by muscular contractions and only swim once they are released. Three: the egg swims to the uterus; it cannot move and is carried by cilia and muscle contractions. Four: the mother decides the sex of the baby; every egg carries X, so the sperm, X or Y, decides. Five: the vagina and the uterus are the same organ; the uterus is the womb where the baby grows, the vagina is the passage to the outside, and the cervix separates them.

Layout for an organ question: name, then the one function. Layout for a gamete question: part, then adaptation, then why it helps. Layout for a diagram: ruled lines, no crossings, the exact structure. The organs are then labelled exactly as the memorandum expects.

The questions for this section are ready: identifying structures from their position, label rules, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same organs again, through the sperm's road, the egg's road, and a tadpole meeting a grain of sand — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Sperm's Road

Follow one sperm from where it is made to where it leaves. It starts in a testis, one of two oval organs in the scrotum, the skin bag that hangs outside the body. Why outside? Because sperm are like milk: they keep best a little cooler than body temperature, about two or three degrees cooler. On a cold day the scrotum pulls the testes up close for warmth; on a hot day it lets them hang down to cool off. Inside the testis, coiled tubes make sperm nonstop, well over a hundred million a day.

The new sperm moves into the epididymis, a tube so long and so tightly coiled that, stretched out, it would be about six metres, folded into a lump the size of a thumbnail on the back of the testis. That is the waiting room. The sperm hang around there for about two weeks, growing up, learning to swim.

Then the sperm duct, the long pipe that runs up out of the scrotum, into the belly and around the back of the bladder. The sperm does not swim here. The pipe squeezes in waves and pushes it along, like toothpaste pushed along a tube.

Now three filling stations add fluid. The seminal vesicles add a sugary drink, fuel for the swim. The prostate gland, the size of a walnut, wrapped around the pipe just under the bladder, adds a milky liquid that cancels out the acid in the vagina, which would otherwise hurt the sperm. Two tiny glands add a slippery drop to clean the way. Sperm plus all that fluid is semen, about a teaspoon each time.

Finally the urethra, the pipe through the penis to the outside. In boys, urine uses the same pipe, but never at the same time, because a muscle at the bladder shuts tight.

So the road is: testis, epididymis, sperm duct, past the glands, urethra, out. Make it, store it, move it, feed it, deliver it.

Keep the road in mind and try the questions for this part: why the testes hang outside, what the waiting room is, and what the prostate adds.

## Subtopic: The Egg's Road

Now follow the egg. It starts in an ovary, one of two almond-sized organs low in the belly, one on each side of the womb. Each ovary is like a tray of seeds: thousands of immature eggs, each wrapped in its own little bubble of cells. Once a cycle, usually one bubble grows big, swells against the surface of the ovary like a blister, and pops. Out comes one egg. That pop is ovulation.

The egg cannot walk or swim, so how does it go anywhere? Right next to the ovary is the open end of an oviduct, shaped like a funnel with soft fingers around its rim, and those fingers sweep over the ovary and catch the egg. Inside, the oviduct is lined with millions of tiny hairs, cilia, waving in one direction like people passing a crowd-surfer over their heads, and the tube gently squeezes too. So the egg is carried along, slowly, about three to four days to reach the womb. If it is going to meet a sperm, it meets it here in the oviduct, near the ovary end.

The womb, the uterus, is the size of a pear, upside down, with walls of thick, strong muscle and a soft, blood-rich lining like a thick carpet. Each month that carpet is laid thicker in case a fertilised egg arrives and needs somewhere to settle. During pregnancy those muscle walls stretch to hold a whole baby, and at birth they squeeze hard to push the baby out.

The bottom of the pear narrows into the cervix, the neck, like the knot at the neck of a balloon: tight and closed during pregnancy, then opening up to about ten centimetres when the baby is born. Below the cervix is the vagina, a stretchy muscular passage to the outside. It receives the sperm, it lets the period blood out, and it stretches to be the birth canal. In girls the urine has its own separate opening, just in front of the vagina.

So the egg's road is: ovary, pop, funnel, oviduct with its waving hairs, uterus. And the way out is cervix, vagina.

Hold the egg's road in mind and try this part's questions: what ovulation is, how the egg moves, and the jobs of the cervix and vagina.

## Subtopic: A Tadpole and a Grain of Sand

Put the two travellers side by side. The sperm looks like a tiny tadpole: a head, a short neck, a long tail. The head holds the nucleus, half a recipe book, 23 chromosomes, and wears a cap full of digging chemicals for getting through the egg's coat. The neck, the middle piece, is stuffed with mitochondria, the little power stations, burning sugar to drive the tail. The tail whips side to side to swim. It carries no packed lunch at all: it is built light and fast, and it only lives a few days. It is about a twentieth of a millimetre long, far too small to see.

The egg is a ball, the biggest cell in your body, about a tenth of a millimetre across, the size of a grain of fine sand, just visible if you look really hard. It has a nucleus with 23 chromosomes too, but it is mostly cytoplasm with a food store, the packed lunch for the first days of the new life. It is wrapped in a jelly coat, and once one sperm gets in, the coat hardens like a door slamming shut. It cannot move at all.

Why so different? Because they have opposite jobs. The sperm's job is the journey, a long swim with huge losses, so the body makes them tiny, fast and in their hundreds of millions. The egg's job is to supply the start of a new life, so the body makes it big, stocked with food, and only one at a time.

And here is the twist. Every egg carries an X chromosome. Each sperm carries either an X or a Y. X sperm plus X egg gives XX, a girl. Y sperm plus X egg gives XY, a boy. So it is the sperm, from the father, that decides whether the baby is a boy or a girl.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Testis makes sperm in a cooler scrotum; epididymis stores; sperm duct moves; seminal vesicles and prostate add fluid to make semen; urethra and penis deliver. Ovary releases an egg; oviduct carries it on cilia and is where fertilisation happens; uterus with its lining is the nursery; cervix is the neck; vagina is the birth canal. Sperm small, many, swimming, mitochondria in the middle piece; egg big, one, still, with a food store. The sperm decides the sex. In Grade 12 Life Sciences these diagrams come back with every label.

Hold the tadpole and the grain of sand in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: the parts of the sperm, the food store in the egg, and who decides the sex.
