### subtopic_1 — Sexual Reproduction and Gametes
**Assistant:** If both parents give 23 chromosomes, why do brothers and sisters look different from each other?

**Tutor:** Because each parent has two versions of every chromosome and every gamete gets a different selection. Your mother has 23 pairs, one chromosome of each pair inherited from her own mother and one from her own father. When she makes an egg, that egg receives one chromosome from each pair, and which one it receives is decided separately for every pair, a little like tossing a coin 23 times. The number of different combinations from 23 coin tosses is two multiplied by itself 23 times, more than eight million, and the same is true of your father's sperm. So any one child is one of over eight million possible eggs meeting one of over eight million possible sperm, which already gives more than 70 trillion combinations, and in reality the chromosomes of each pair also swap pieces with each other before the gametes form, which multiplies the possibilities enormously again. The result is that two children of the same parents share about half their genetic instructions with each other on average, enough to look related, but never the same half twice. The only exception is identical twins, who share all of their instructions because they come from a single zygote that split into two embryos in the first days, while non-identical twins come from two separate eggs fertilised by two separate sperm and are no more alike than other siblings. This variation is the main reason sexual reproduction exists at all, and Grade 12 Life Sciences explains the mechanism, called meiosis, in detail.

### subtopic_2 — Components of the Male and Female Systems
**Assistant:** Why is the urethra shared by urine and semen in males? Does that not cause problems?

**Tutor:** It is shared, but a set of muscles makes sure the two never travel at the same time. The male urethra runs from the bladder, through the prostate gland, and along the length of the penis, and the two sperm ducts join it inside the prostate. At the base of the bladder there is a ring of muscle, a sphincter, which stays closed except during urination. When semen is released, a reflex closes this bladder sphincter tightly, so urine cannot enter the urethra and semen cannot be pushed backwards into the bladder. When a person urinates, the reproductive passages are not active. So the sharing is a matter of economy in the body's plumbing: one exit tube serving two systems at different moments. The female body is arranged differently: the urethra opens separately to the outside, just in front of the vaginal opening, so in females the urinary and reproductive systems have completely separate exits. This difference matters for health in a practical way. Because the female urethra is short and opens close to other openings, bacteria reach the bladder more easily, and bladder infections are much more common in girls and women; drinking plenty of water and wiping from front to back help prevent them. In males an enlarged prostate, which is common after about the age of 50, can squeeze the urethra where it passes through the gland and make urination slow and frequent, which is one reason older men are advised to have the prostate checked, and why prostate problems show up first as urinary symptoms.

### subtopic_3 — The Processes: From Gametes to a Baby
**Assistant:** How long can a sperm or an egg survive, and does that mean pregnancy is possible on any day?

**Tutor:** The two gametes have very different lifespans, and together they set a window of about six days in each cycle when intercourse can lead to pregnancy. An egg released from the ovary survives for only about 12 to 24 hours in the oviduct; if it is not fertilised in that time it breaks down. Sperm, protected by the fluid of the cervix around the time of ovulation, can survive in the female body for up to about five days. Put the two together and pregnancy can result from intercourse during the five days before ovulation and on the day of ovulation itself, because sperm already waiting in the oviduct can fertilise the egg as soon as it arrives. In a regular 28-day cycle ovulation happens around day 14, counted from the first day of the period, but many people, and especially teenagers whose cycles are still settling, have irregular cycles, ovulation can come early or late in any given month, and there is no reliable sign that warns a person beforehand. That is why counting days on a calendar is one of the least reliable methods of preventing pregnancy, and why health workers say that, practically speaking, unprotected intercourse can lead to pregnancy at any point in the cycle. It is also worth knowing that a girl can become pregnant before her first period ever arrives, because her first ovulation happens about two weeks before her first period. The contraception lesson explains the methods that do work reliably, and which of them also protect against infections.

### subtopic_4 — Health Issues of the Reproductive System and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why do young men need to check themselves for testicular cancer if it is rare?

**Tutor:** Because it is rare overall but it is the most common cancer in exactly the age group that would least expect it, and it is one of the most curable cancers when found early, so a simple habit makes a large difference. Testicular cancer is diagnosed mostly in young men between about 15 and 35. It usually starts as a small, hard, painless lump on one testis, or a change in size or feel, and because it is painless it is easy to ignore. Found while it is still confined to the testis, it is cured in well over nine out of ten cases, usually by removing the affected testis, after which the remaining testis normally maintains hormone levels and fertility. Found late, after it has spread, treatment is much harder. The recommended self-examination takes about a minute once a month, after a warm bath or shower when the scrotum is relaxed: roll each testis gently between the thumb and fingers, feeling for any hard lump, swelling or change, and remember that the soft, coiled tube at the back, the epididymis, is a normal structure and not a lump. Anything new or unusual should be shown to a clinic nurse or doctor without delay, and most lumps turn out to be harmless. The same logic of knowing your body applies to girls and breast self-examination, and to the Pap smear later in life: screening finds the problem before it causes symptoms. Embarrassment is the main reason people delay, and clinic staff deal with these checks every day.
