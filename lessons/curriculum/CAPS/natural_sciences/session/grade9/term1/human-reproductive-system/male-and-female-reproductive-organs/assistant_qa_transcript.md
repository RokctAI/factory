### subtopic_1 — The Male Reproductive Organs
**Assistant:** Why does a man make millions of sperm when only one fertilises the egg?

**Tutor:** Because the journey is extremely hostile and almost all the sperm are lost on the way, so huge numbers are needed for one to arrive. A typical release contains tens to hundreds of millions of sperm. The vagina is acidic, which kills many quickly despite the protective alkaline fluid of the prostate. The cervix lets through only the sperm that swim well, and its mucus is thick for most of the cycle, thinning only around ovulation. Many sperm in the uterus are attacked by white blood cells, which treat them as foreign. Half of the survivors go up the oviduct that has no egg in it that month. Of the hundreds of millions released, typically only a few hundred reach the egg. Even then, one sperm cannot get through the egg's protective layers alone: many sperm release enzymes from their acrosomes together to break down the layers of follicle cells and the jelly coat, and then a single sperm fuses with the egg membrane, after which the coat changes so that no other can enter. This is why a low sperm count is a cause of infertility, even though only one sperm is used in the end: if too few start the journey, too few arrive to clear the way. Doctors regard a count below about 15 million per millilitre of semen as low. Smoking, heavy alcohol use, some drugs, obesity and overheating the testes, for example with very tight clothing or hot baths for long periods, can all reduce the count, which is why fertility advice for men includes lifestyle changes as well as medical tests.

### subtopic_2 — The Female Reproductive Organs
**Assistant:** What happens if a fertilised egg implants in the oviduct instead of the uterus?

**Tutor:** That is called an ectopic pregnancy, ectopic meaning out of place, and it is a medical emergency. Normally the fertilised egg is carried down the oviduct by cilia and muscular contractions over several days, dividing as it goes, and implants in the thick lining of the uterus about a week after fertilisation. If the oviduct is damaged or partly blocked, the ball of cells can be held up and implant in the wall of the oviduct itself. The oviduct is a narrow tube with a thin wall and no thick blood-rich lining, so it cannot stretch or support a growing embryo. Within a few weeks the embryo grows too large, and the oviduct can tear and bleed heavily into the abdomen, which can kill the mother if not treated. Warning signs are sharp pain low on one side of the abdomen, bleeding from the vagina, dizziness or shoulder-tip pain, in a person who may have missed a period. The pregnancy cannot continue and must be ended with medicine or an operation, and the damaged oviduct may need to be removed. The main cause of the damage is scarring from earlier infections, especially untreated sexually transmitted infections such as chlamydia and gonorrhoea, which often cause no symptoms but inflame the oviducts. Smoking also raises the risk, because it affects the beating of the cilia. This is one of the strongest practical reasons for using condoms and for getting tested and treated early: an infection that seemed harmless can damage the oviduct and endanger a pregnancy years later.

### subtopic_3 — The Gametes: Sperm and Egg Compared
**Assistant:** Why are the urinary and reproductive systems drawn together if they are different systems?

**Tutor:** Because they share space, a common origin in the embryo and, in males, one tube. Both systems develop from neighbouring tissues in the lower part of the embryo, and the organs end up packed together in the pelvis, so any diagram of one inevitably shows parts of the other. In the male, the urethra serves both systems: it carries urine from the bladder and semen from the sperm ducts, which join it inside the prostate gland. That is why the male diagram always includes the bladder, sitting just above the prostate, with the sperm ducts looping over and behind it. In an exam, the bladder may be labelled on the diagram, but if you are asked which structures belong to the reproductive system, the bladder and the ureters are not among them. In the female, the systems are separate in function and in their openings: the urethra opens just in front of the vagina, with its own short path from the bladder, and the reproductive tract opens through the vagina. Still, the bladder lies directly in front of the uterus, so a side view shows both, and during pregnancy the growing uterus presses on the bladder, which is why pregnant women need to urinate more often. Combined diagrams are therefore useful because they show the real positions, and they explain everyday facts, such as why an enlarged prostate in older men makes urination difficult, since it squeezes the urethra, and why infections can sometimes spread from one system to the other. The skill is to read the diagram and still sort each structure into its correct system.

### subtopic_4 — Labelling Diagrams and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why is a sperm so small but the egg so large, if both carry 23 chromosomes?

**Tutor:** Because, apart from the chromosomes, they have completely different jobs, and their sizes follow those jobs. The sperm's job is to deliver its 23 chromosomes over a long and dangerous journey, a distance of roughly fifteen to twenty centimetres, which is thousands of times its own length. For that, small, streamlined and powered is best: it sheds nearly all its cytoplasm during development, keeps the nucleus tightly packed in the head, the acrosome enzymes for entering the egg, a middle piece of mitochondria to power the tail, and nothing else. Being cheap to make, it can be produced in enormous numbers, which compensates for the huge losses. The egg's job is different. Besides its 23 chromosomes, it must supply everything the new individual needs for its first several days, before it implants in the uterus and can draw on the mother's blood supply. So it contains a large volume of cytoplasm with stored nutrients, as well as nearly all the mitochondria the embryo will start with; the few mitochondria in the sperm's middle piece are usually destroyed after fertilisation, which is why mitochondria are inherited from the mother. The egg's size, about a tenth of a millimetre across, makes it thousands of times larger in volume than the sperm, and being expensive to make, only one is usually released per cycle. The pattern of a small mobile male gamete and a large stationary female gamete with a food store is found in nearly all animals and plants, and it is one of the defining features of sexual reproduction that Grade 12 Life Sciences discusses further.
