# Part 1 — Expert

This session sets out the stages of human reproduction in sequence: the menstrual cycle and ovulation, fertilisation, implantation, pregnancy and the role of the placenta, and birth, together with the harmful substances that can reach a developing baby. The anchor numbers are a cycle of about 28 days with menstruation on days 1 to 5 and ovulation around day 14, an egg that survives about one day, implantation about seven days after fertilisation, and a pregnancy of about 40 weeks counted from the first day of the last period. The governing principle is that each stage prepares for the next and is coordinated by the hormones oestrogen and progesterone, and that the placenta allows exchange between mother and baby by diffusion while keeping their blood separate.

## Subtopic: The Menstrual Cycle and Ovulation

The menstrual cycle is the series of changes in the ovaries and the uterus that repeats about every 28 days from puberty to menopause, preparing the body each month for a possible pregnancy. Day 1 is counted as the first day of bleeding. Normal cycles range from about 21 to 35 days, and the 28-day cycle is the standard model used in questions.

Days 1 to 5 are menstruation. The thick lining of the uterus, the endometrium, built up in the previous cycle, breaks down and leaves the body through the cervix and vagina as menstrual flow, a mixture of blood, tissue and mucus, typically about 30 to 40 millilitres over the whole period.

Days 6 to 13 are the repair and build-up phase. In one ovary a follicle matures, and the follicle cells produce increasing amounts of oestrogen. Oestrogen causes the lining of the uterus to repair and thicken again, growing new blood vessels and glands.

Around day 14 is ovulation. The mature follicle bursts and releases its egg into the oviduct. The egg survives for about 12 to 24 hours, and sperm can survive in the female tract for up to about five days, so the days just before and including ovulation are the fertile period.

Days 15 to 28 are the waiting phase. The empty follicle in the ovary turns into a yellow body, the corpus luteum, which produces progesterone. Progesterone keeps the lining thick, soft and rich in blood vessels, ready to receive an embryo. If no embryo implants, the corpus luteum breaks down towards the end of the cycle, progesterone falls, and without it the lining can no longer be maintained, so it breaks down: menstruation begins and the cycle returns to day 1.

If an embryo does implant, it releases a hormone that keeps the corpus luteum producing progesterone, so the lining is kept and there is no period; that hormone is what a pregnancy test detects. Later the placenta takes over the production of progesterone for the rest of the pregnancy. A missed period is therefore often the first sign of pregnancy.

For marking, the cycle as four phases: menstruation, days 1 to 5, lining shed; build-up, days 6 to 13, oestrogen thickens the lining; ovulation, about day 14, egg released; waiting, days 15 to 28, progesterone maintains the lining. A useful rule for irregular cycles: ovulation happens about 14 days before the next period, not 14 days after the last one.

## Subtopic: Fertilisation and Implantation

Fertilisation is the fusion of the nucleus of a sperm with the nucleus of an egg to form a zygote. It normally happens in the upper part of the oviduct within about a day of ovulation. Of the many millions of sperm released into the vagina, only a few hundred reach the egg. Their acrosomes release enzymes that break down the cells and jelly coat surrounding the egg, one sperm fuses with the egg membrane, and the coat immediately changes so that no other sperm can enter. The sperm's nucleus, with 23 chromosomes, joins the egg's nucleus, with 23, giving the zygote its 46 chromosomes and a new, unique combination of genetic instructions. Whether the zygote is XX or XY, and so female or male, is decided at this moment by the sperm.

The zygote begins dividing by ordinary cell division within about a day, becoming two cells, then four, then eight, while the cilia and muscles of the oviduct move it towards the uterus. By about day four after fertilisation it is a solid ball of cells, and by about day five, when it reaches the uterus, it has become a hollow ball of about a hundred cells.

Implantation follows, about six to ten days after fertilisation, roughly a week. The hollow ball attaches to the thick, progesterone-maintained lining of the uterus and sinks into it, and its outer cells begin to form the structures that will become the placenta. From implantation onward the woman is pregnant, and the developing individual is called an embryo.

Twins can arise in two ways. Identical twins come from one zygote whose cells separate in the first days into two embryos, so they have the same genetic instructions and are always the same sex. Non-identical twins come from two eggs released in the same cycle and fertilised by two different sperm, so they are no more alike than other siblings and may be a boy and a girl.

For marking, the sequence: ovulation, fertilisation in the oviduct, zygote with 46 chromosomes, cell divisions to a ball of cells while moving down the oviduct, arrival in the uterus, implantation in the endometrium after about a week.

## Subtopic: Pregnancy and the Placenta

Pregnancy, or gestation, lasts about 40 weeks counted from the first day of the last period, which is about 38 weeks from fertilisation, and it is divided into three trimesters of about three months each. For the first eight weeks the developing baby is an embryo; in this time all the main organs form, and the heart begins beating at about four weeks after fertilisation. From about eight weeks, when it is recognisably human and about the length of a thumb joint, it is called a foetus, and the rest of pregnancy is mainly growth and maturing of the organs.

The foetus floats in amniotic fluid inside a membrane bag, the amnion. The fluid cushions it against bumps, keeps its temperature steady and allows it to move and exercise its muscles.

The placenta is a disc-shaped organ attached to the wall of the uterus, formed partly from the embryo's tissues and partly from the mother's, and by full term it is about 20 centimetres across. It is connected to the foetus by the umbilical cord, which contains two arteries and one vein. The placenta has many finger-like projections, called villi, filled with the foetus's capillaries and bathed in the mother's blood, giving a large surface area for exchange.

The essential fact is that the mother's blood and the foetus's blood do not mix. They are separated by a thin barrier, the walls of the villi and their capillaries, and substances cross by diffusion. From the mother to the foetus pass oxygen, glucose, amino acids, fats, water, mineral salts, vitamins and some antibodies that protect the baby for its first months. From the foetus to the mother pass carbon dioxide and urea, which the mother's lungs and kidneys then remove. The umbilical vein carries oxygenated, nutrient-rich blood from the placenta to the foetus; the umbilical arteries carry deoxygenated blood with wastes back to the placenta. The separation matters because the two may have different blood groups and different blood pressures, and because it limits what can cross. The placenta also makes progesterone to maintain the pregnancy.

So the placenta does for the foetus the jobs of the lungs, the gut and the kidneys until birth: gas exchange, nutrition and excretion, all by diffusion across a thin, folded surface with a rich blood supply.

## Subtopic: Birth, Harmful Substances and the Error Museum

Birth, or labour, normally begins at about 40 weeks. A hormone from the pituitary gland triggers strong, rhythmic contractions of the thick muscle of the uterus. In the first stage the contractions gradually open the cervix until it is about 10 centimetres wide, and the amnion usually bursts, which is called the waters breaking. In the second stage the contractions, helped by the mother pushing, move the baby, usually head first, through the cervix and the vagina. The umbilical cord is then clamped and cut; the stump falls off to leave the navel. In the third stage further contractions expel the placenta and membranes as the afterbirth. If a natural birth is unsafe, the baby is delivered by a Caesarean section, an operation through the abdomen. After birth, breastfeeding provides ideal nutrition, and the first milk, colostrum, is rich in antibodies.

The placenta is not a perfect barrier. Small molecules dissolved in the mother's blood cross it, including several harmful substances. Alcohol crosses freely and can cause foetal alcohol spectrum disorder, with a small head, distinctive facial features, learning difficulties and behaviour problems that last for life; South Africa, especially parts of the Western Cape, has some of the highest recorded rates in the world, and the only safe amount of alcohol in pregnancy is none. Nicotine and carbon monoxide from smoking narrow blood vessels and reduce the oxygen carried, leading to babies with low birth weight. Drugs such as tik and many medicines cross too, so a pregnant woman takes medicine only on medical advice. Some viruses cross, including rubella, which can cause deafness and heart defects, and HIV, which can pass from mother to child during pregnancy, birth or breastfeeding; antiretroviral treatment for the mother has cut that transmission in South Africa to well under five in a hundred babies. Early antenatal care at a clinic, in the first three months, and taking folic acid, which helps prevent defects of the spinal cord, give the baby the best start.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: a period is the release of the egg; menstruation is the shedding of the uterine lining, and ovulation happens about two weeks later. Two: ovulation always happens on day 14; it varies, and happens about 14 days before the next period. Three: the mother's blood flows into the baby; the two bloods never mix, and substances diffuse across the placenta. Four: the placenta blocks everything harmful; alcohol, nicotine, drugs and some viruses cross it. Five: the foetus breathes air in the uterus; it receives oxygen from the mother's blood through the placenta and umbilical cord, and first breathes at birth.

Layout for a cycle question: phase, days, hormone, what happens to the lining. Layout for a placenta question: structure, then what crosses in each direction, then that the bloods do not mix. Layout for a sequence question: ovulation, fertilisation, cell division, implantation, embryo, foetus, birth. The stages are then described exactly as the memorandum expects.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same stages again, through a monthly room booking, a meeting and a move-in, and a lifeline with a filter that leaks — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Monthly Room Booking

Think of the uterus as a hotel room that gets prepared every month for a guest who may or may not arrive. The guest is a fertilised egg.

Days 1 to 5: the old decorations come down. Last month's thick lining, the carpet, curtains and all, was never used, so it is stripped out and leaves the body through the vagina. That is the period: blood and bits of lining, about two or three tablespoons over the whole period, even if it looks like more.

Days 6 to 13: redecorating. In one ovary an egg is getting ready inside its little bubble, and that bubble sends out oestrogen, the decorator hormone, which lays a fresh, thick, blood-rich carpet in the room.

Around day 14: the egg is released from the ovary, ovulation, and sets off down the oviduct. It only lasts about a day, but sperm already waiting can live for up to five, so the few days around this point are when a pregnancy can start.

Days 15 to 28: holding the booking. The empty bubble in the ovary becomes a little yellow body that sends out progesterone, the hotel manager hormone, which says: keep the room ready, keep the carpet thick. If no guest arrives, the yellow body shrivels, progesterone drops, the manager goes home, nobody holds the room, the carpet comes down, and it is day 1 again.

If a guest does arrive and settles in, it sends out its own message telling the yellow body to keep making progesterone, so the room stays booked and there is no period. That message is exactly what a pregnancy test picks up.

One handy rule: cycles are not always 28 days, and the release of the egg happens about 14 days before the next period, not 14 days after the last.

## Subtopic: The Meeting and the Move-In

The meeting happens in the oviduct, the tube near the ovary, within about a day of the egg being released. Millions of sperm set off; only a few hundred make it to the egg. Together they chip away at its coat with their chemical caps, and then one gets in, and the coat instantly slams shut on all the others. The sperm's 23 chromosomes join the egg's 23, and there it is: one cell with 46, the zygote, the first cell of a brand-new person, already boy or girl depending on whether that sperm carried an X or a Y.

Then the journey and the move-in. The zygote does not grow bigger straight away; it splits, into two cells, then four, then eight, like a team that keeps doubling, while the waving hairs of the oviduct carry it slowly along. By about day five it reaches the uterus as a tiny hollow ball of about a hundred cells. Then, at about one week, it moves in: it sticks to the thick carpet of the uterus lining and burrows into it. That is implantation, and from that moment the woman is pregnant.

Twins are worth a quick word. If that first ball of cells splits into two and both halves keep growing, you get identical twins: same recipe, same sex, often hard to tell apart. If two eggs are released in the same month and each meets a different sperm, you get non-identical twins, as different as any brother and sister, and they can be a boy and a girl.

So: egg released, meeting in the oviduct, zygote with 46, doubling on the way down, move-in after about a week. Ovulation, fertilisation, cell division, implantation.

## Subtopic: The Lifeline and the Filter That Leaks

Now the guest has moved in and has about nine months to grow. For the first eight weeks it is called an embryo, and in that short time it builds every main organ; its heart starts beating at only about four weeks. After that it is called a foetus and mostly just grows. It floats in a bag of water, the amniotic fluid, which is like a waterbed and a shock absorber: it protects it from bumps, keeps it warm and gives it room to kick.

Its lifeline is the umbilical cord, plugged into the placenta, a big flat disc on the wall of the uterus, about the size of a dinner plate by the end. The placenta is the baby's lungs, kitchen and toilet all in one. And here is the clever part: the mother's blood and the baby's blood never mix. They run past each other on either side of a very thin wall in the placenta, and things just diffuse across. Into the baby: oxygen, food like glucose and amino acids, water, vitamins and minerals, and some antibodies. Out of the baby: carbon dioxide and urea, which the mother's lungs and kidneys get rid of. The baby does not breathe air until it is born.

But the wall is a filter that leaks. Small harmful things cross too. Alcohol goes straight through and can damage the baby's brain for life, foetal alcohol syndrome; the only safe amount in pregnancy is none. Smoking sends nicotine and carbon monoxide through, so less oxygen arrives and the baby is born too small. Drugs like tik cross. Some viruses cross, including HIV, which is why a pregnant mother with HIV takes treatment, which protects the baby in most cases.

At about 40 weeks, birth. The muscle of the uterus squeezes in waves, the cervix opens to about ten centimetres, the waters break, and the baby is pushed out head first through the vagina. The cord is cut, leaving your belly button, and then the placenta comes out, the afterbirth.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Cycle: shed days 1 to 5, rebuild with oestrogen, egg out around day 14, hold the lining with progesterone, shed again if no guest. Fertilisation in the oviduct, 23 plus 23, then doubling and implantation after about a week. Embryo for eight weeks, then foetus, in amniotic fluid. Placenta and cord: food and oxygen in, carbon dioxide and urea out, bloods never mix, but alcohol, smoke, drugs and some viruses cross. Birth at about 40 weeks.
