### subtopic_1 — What Puberty Is and What Starts It
**Assistant:** Why does puberty start earlier in some children than in others?

**Tutor:** The timing is set mainly by genes and partly by the body's condition, so it differs from person to person within a wide normal range. The genetic part is strong: children tend to start puberty at roughly the age their parents did, so a girl whose mother had her first period at 11 is more likely to start early herself. The body's condition matters because the brain does not switch on reproduction until the body seems ready to support it, and one of the signals it reads is the amount of stored energy, mainly fat. Children who are well nourished, or who carry more body fat, tend to start earlier, while children who are underweight, seriously ill for a long time, or training extremely hard in sports such as gymnastics or long-distance running often start later. This is also why the average age of the first period has fallen over the past century in many countries, including South Africa, as nutrition and health have improved. Stress and some medical conditions can shift the timing too. What matters for you is that the normal range is wide: in girls, first signs anywhere from about 8 to 13, and in boys from about 9 to 14, and someone at either end is perfectly healthy. The order of the changes is much more predictable than their timing; breasts usually come before periods, and in boys the testes grow before the voice deepens. A clinic check is advised only when there are no signs at all by about 13 in a girl or 14 in a boy, or when signs appear before about 8 in a girl or 9 in a boy.

### subtopic_2 — Hormones: Testosterone, Oestrogen and Progesterone
**Assistant:** If hormones go to the whole body in the blood, why do they only affect certain organs?

**Tutor:** Because only certain cells carry the matching receptor, a protein that fits that particular hormone the way a lock fits a key. Testosterone, oestrogen and progesterone do circulate through the entire body, reaching every organ the blood reaches, from the toes to the brain. But a cell responds only if it has receptors for that hormone, either on its membrane or inside it. The cells of the hair follicles on a young man's face, the cells of the larynx, and the muscle cells of his arms and shoulders have many testosterone receptors, so they respond to the rising level at puberty by growing hair, enlarging the voice box and building muscle. The cells of the breast tissue and the lining of the uterus in a girl are rich in oestrogen receptors, so they grow and develop when oestrogen rises. Cells that lack the receptor, such as most of the cells in the liver for these particular effects, are bathed in the same hormone and simply ignore it. The organs that respond are called target organs, and the receptor idea explains several things you may have noticed. It is why a single hormone can have different effects in different organs: the receptors trigger different responses in different kinds of cells. It explains why scalp hair and facial hair behave differently under the same testosterone. And it is how many medicines work: a contraceptive pill, for example, contains hormones that fit the same receptors as the body's own and so change what the ovaries and uterus do. Grade 12 Life Sciences develops the endocrine system with this idea at its centre.

### subtopic_3 — Secondary Sexual Characteristics in Boys and Girls
**Assistant:** Is it normal for periods to be irregular when they first start?

**Tutor:** Yes, very normal, and it usually settles within about one to two years after the first period. A regular menstrual cycle depends on a precise conversation between the brain, the pituitary gland and the ovaries, with hormone levels rising and falling in a set pattern each month. When puberty begins that conversation is still being established, so in the first cycles the ovaries may not release an egg every month, and when no egg is released the hormone pattern changes and the timing of the next period becomes unpredictable. As a result, a girl in her first year may have cycles as short as three weeks or as long as two months or more, periods that last a few days one time and a week another, and lighter or heavier flow from month to month. This is why tracking periods on a calendar or a phone is useful: after several months a pattern begins to show, and the girl can be prepared. Some situations are worth a clinic visit even in the early years: bleeding so heavy that a pad has to be changed every hour or two, a period lasting more than about seven days, pain severe enough to keep her out of school regularly, or no period for three months once cycles have begun, because pregnancy and some treatable conditions need to be ruled out. A very low body weight, intense sports training or stress can also make periods irregular or stop them. One important caution follows from irregular cycles: because ovulation timing is unpredictable, counting days is not a reliable way to avoid pregnancy, a point the contraception lesson returns to.

### subtopic_4 — Health, Hygiene and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Do boys also go through emotional changes at puberty, or is that only girls?

**Tutor:** Both sexes go through emotional changes, and the causes are the same in both: rising sex hormones and a brain that is itself being rebuilt during the teenage years. Testosterone, oestrogen and progesterone act on the brain as well as on the body, and their levels rise and fluctuate during puberty, which can make moods stronger and quicker to change. At the same time the brain is reorganising. The regions that generate emotions and respond to rewards and risks mature earlier, while the front part of the brain, which plans ahead, weighs consequences and controls impulses, keeps developing into the early or mid-twenties. That mismatch helps explain why teenagers of both sexes feel things intensely, care a great deal about what friends think, and are sometimes drawn to risk. Boys may show the changes differently from girls, partly because of hormones and partly because of what society expects of them; a boy may become quieter, more irritable or more competitive rather than openly upset, and boys are often less likely to talk about what they feel, which makes it harder to notice when one is struggling. Common experiences for both include sudden mood swings, a stronger wish for privacy and independence, new romantic or sexual feelings, worries about appearance, and conflict with parents over rules. These are normal parts of development. What is not something to simply wait out is a low mood that lasts weeks, losing interest in everything, or thoughts of self-harm; then talking to a trusted adult, a teacher, a clinic nurse or a helpline is the healthy step, for boys exactly as much as for girls.
