# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the methods of contraception by the step of reproduction each one blocks, compares their reliability and their protection against infection, and then describes HIV and AIDS and the other common sexually transmitted diseases, with their transmission, prevention and treatment. The anchor numbers are 85 pregnancies in a year among 100 sexually active couples using no method, about 13 with condoms as people typically use them and fewer than 1 with an implant or an intrauterine device, and roughly one South African in eight living with HIV. The governing principle is that every method works by interrupting a known step, and that only abstinence and condoms block both pregnancy and the passing of infections.

## Subtopic: Contraception: How the Methods Work

Contraception is the deliberate prevention of pregnancy. Each method interrupts one of the steps of reproduction: ovulation, the meeting of sperm and egg, or implantation. Grouping the methods by the step they block is the clearest way to learn them.

Abstinence means not having sexual intercourse at all. It is the only method that is completely effective against both pregnancy and sexually transmitted infections, and it is a valid choice at any age.

Barrier methods stop sperm from reaching the egg. The male condom is a thin latex sheath rolled onto the erect penis before any contact, which collects the semen. The female condom is a soft pouch placed inside the vagina. Condoms are the only contraceptives that also reduce the passing of HIV and other sexually transmitted infections, because they stop the exchange of body fluids. They are available free at clinics, and each condom is used once only, with care to check the expiry date and avoid tearing.

Hormonal methods contain synthetic versions of oestrogen and progesterone, or progesterone alone. They mainly prevent ovulation, and they also thicken the mucus of the cervix so that sperm cannot pass. They include the contraceptive pill, taken every day; the injection, given at a clinic every eight or twelve weeks depending on the type; and the implant, a small flexible rod placed under the skin of the upper arm that works for about three years.

The intrauterine device, or copper loop, is a small device placed in the uterus by a nurse or doctor. Copper is toxic to sperm and the device makes the uterus unsuitable for implantation; it lasts five to ten years. Emergency contraception, the morning-after pill, delays ovulation and should be taken as soon as possible after unprotected intercourse, within three days and preferably within one, from a clinic or pharmacy; it is a back-up, not a regular method.

Permanent methods are operations for adults who want no more children: a vasectomy cuts and seals the sperm ducts so that semen contains no sperm, and tubal ligation cuts or blocks the oviducts so that egg and sperm cannot meet.

Two practices are unreliable. Withdrawal before ejaculation fails often, because fluid released before ejaculation can contain sperm. Calendar counting fails often, because ovulation cannot be predicted reliably, especially in young people with irregular cycles.

## Subtopic: Choosing a Method: Reliability and Protection

Reliability is measured by how many pregnancies occur among 100 couples using a method for one year, the way people actually use it, including mistakes. With no method about 85 of the 100 would become pregnant. With withdrawal about 20 do; with calendar counting a similar number or more; with male condoms about 13; with the daily pill about 7, mostly because pills are forgotten; with the injection about 4, mostly because a date is missed; and with the implant or the copper loop fewer than 1, because once in place they cannot be forgotten. The pattern is that the methods that depend least on remembering are the most reliable.

Protection against infection is a separate question, and here the ranking reverses. The pill, the injection, the implant and the loop give no protection at all against HIV or other sexually transmitted infections. Only abstinence and condoms do. Health workers therefore recommend dual protection for anyone who is sexually active: a condom every time to prevent infection, together with a reliable hormonal method or loop to prevent pregnancy.

A good choice also considers side effects, which differ from person to person and can include irregular bleeding, weight change or headaches with hormonal methods; a clinic nurse can help a person change to a method that suits them better. It considers access: in South Africa, condoms, the pill, the injection and the implant are free at public clinics. And it considers the law and personal rights. From the age of 12 a young person may obtain contraceptives and advice at a clinic confidentially, without a parent's consent, and the clinic must keep that confidential. The legal age of consent to sexual activity is 16. Every person has the right to say no to any sexual activity at any time, and a decision about sex is one each person makes freely, without pressure or force.

The choices are therefore weighed on four criteria: reliability, protection against infection, side effects and convenience. No single method wins on all four, which is why dual protection exists.

## Subtopic: HIV and AIDS

HIV, the human immunodeficiency virus, is a virus that infects and destroys a type of white blood cell, the helper cells, also called CD4 cells, which coordinate the immune system's defence against germs. After infection the virus multiplies inside these cells and kills them. For years the person may feel well, but the number of helper cells steadily falls, and without treatment, after about ten years on average, the immune system becomes so weak that infections a healthy person would fight off, such as tuberculosis, pneumonia, severe thrush and certain cancers, take hold. These are called opportunistic infections. This late stage is AIDS, the acquired immune deficiency syndrome. HIV is the virus; AIDS is the condition it causes when it is not treated.

HIV is passed on only through certain body fluids: blood, semen, vaginal fluids and breast milk. It is transmitted by unprotected vaginal or anal intercourse with an infected person, by sharing needles, syringes or razor blades contaminated with infected blood, and from an infected mother to her baby during pregnancy, birth or breastfeeding. It is not passed on by hugging, kissing on the cheek, shaking hands, sharing cups, plates or toilets, through sweat, tears or saliva, or by mosquito bites. A person living with HIV can study, work, play sport and share a home with others without risk to them.

A person cannot tell from someone's appearance whether they have HIV; the only way to know is a test. Testing is free and confidential at clinics, using a finger-prick sample with a result in about 15 minutes, and self-test kits are available. A test soon after exposure may not yet detect the infection, a gap called the window period, so a negative test after a recent risk is repeated later.

There is no cure for HIV, but antiretroviral treatment, ART, is a combination of medicines taken every day for life that stops the virus multiplying. A person on ART can keep a healthy immune system and live a normal lifespan. When treatment keeps the virus so low that tests cannot detect it, the person does not pass HIV on through sex. South Africa has the largest HIV epidemic in the world, about eight million people, and the largest treatment programme.

Prevention combines several layers: abstinence; being faithful to one partner who has tested negative; using condoms every time; testing regularly and knowing your partner's status; voluntary medical male circumcision, which reduces a man's risk of infection during vaginal sex by about 60 percent; PrEP, a daily pill that prevents infection in HIV-negative people at high risk; PEP, a 28-day course of medicine started within 72 hours after a possible exposure, such as rape; and treatment for pregnant mothers, which protects their babies.

## Subtopic: Other Sexually Transmitted Diseases and the Error Museum

Sexually transmitted diseases, also called sexually transmitted infections, are caused by bacteria, viruses or parasites passed on through sexual contact. The bacterial ones can be cured with the correct antibiotics; the viral ones cannot be cured but can be controlled.

Syphilis is caused by a bacterium. It begins with a painless sore at the site of infection, followed weeks later by a rash, and if untreated it can, after years, damage the heart, brain and nerves; it also crosses the placenta and can kill or harm a baby, which is why pregnant women are tested at their first clinic visit. It is cured with penicillin injections. Gonorrhoea, also bacterial, causes a burning feeling when urinating and a discharge from the penis or vagina, though many women have no symptoms; it is treated with antibiotics, though some strains have become resistant. Chlamydia, also bacterial, often causes no symptoms at all, yet in women it can spread to the oviducts and scar them, causing infertility and ectopic pregnancy; it is cured with antibiotics.

Genital herpes is caused by a virus and produces painful blisters that heal and return from time to time; medicine shortens the outbreaks but the virus stays in the body for life. Genital warts are caused by the human papillomavirus, HPV, and certain types of HPV cause cervical cancer, which is why the HPV vaccine is given to Grade 5 girls. Trichomoniasis is caused by a tiny parasite and is cured with medicine.

Several points are common to all of them. Many cause no symptoms, so people spread them unknowingly. Sores and inflammation from any of them make HIV infection more likely. Signs that should send anyone to a clinic include an unusual discharge, burning when urinating, sores, blisters or warts on or near the genitals, or pain in the lower abdomen. Clinics treat these infections free and confidentially, and the partner must be treated too, or the infection is passed straight back.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: the pill protects against HIV; it prevents pregnancy only, and only condoms and abstinence protect against infection. Two: HIV and AIDS are the same thing; HIV is the virus, AIDS is the late stage of untreated infection. Three: HIV is spread by sharing cups or by mosquitoes; it is spread only by blood, semen, vaginal fluids and breast milk. Four: a girl cannot fall pregnant the first time she has sex; she can, on any occasion. Five: no symptoms means no infection; many infections have no symptoms, and only a test tells.

## Subtopic: Blocking the Meeting

A pregnancy needs a chain of events: an egg released, sperm meeting the egg, and the ball of cells moving into the uterus. Every way of preventing pregnancy simply cuts one link of that chain.

Cut the link at the start: stop the egg from being released. That is what hormone methods do, the pill every day, the injection every two or three months, and the implant, a little rod under the skin of the arm that lasts about three years. They also make the mucus at the cervix thick, like a gate that sperm cannot get through.

Cut the link in the middle: stop the sperm reaching the egg. That is the condom, a thin rubber cover that catches the sperm, and the female condom inside the vagina. The condom has a superpower the other methods do not: it also blocks the germs that are passed on during sex, including HIV. One condom, one time, from start to finish.

Cut the link at the end: stop the move-in. The copper loop sits inside the uterus, harms sperm and makes the room unwelcoming; it can stay for years.

And the morning-after pill is the emergency exit: after a condom breaks or unprotected sex, taken as soon as possible, within three days, from a clinic or pharmacy. It is for emergencies, not a regular plan.

Then the honest scoreboard, out of 100 couples for a year. Nothing at all: about 85 pregnancies. Pulling out early: about 20, because sperm leak out before. Condoms the way people really use them: about 13. The pill, forgotten now and then: about 7. The injection: about 4. Implant or loop: less than 1, because you cannot forget them. And not having sex at all: none, and no infections either.

So the best plan for anyone who is having sex is two methods at once: a condom every time against infection, plus a reliable method against pregnancy. That is dual protection. And at any clinic, from the age of 12, you can ask about all of this in confidence.

## Subtopic: The Virus That Attacks the Defenders

Your body has an army of white blood cells that fights germs, and the army has generals, special white blood cells called helper cells, that tell the soldiers what to attack. HIV is a virus that attacks exactly those generals. It gets inside them, uses them to make copies of itself, and kills them. Slowly, over years, the generals disappear, the army stops being organised, and germs that a healthy body would beat easily, like TB or pneumonia, start winning. That late stage is AIDS. So HIV is the virus; AIDS is what happens when the virus is left alone for years.

How does it get from one person to another? Only through four fluids: blood, semen, the fluids of the vagina, and breast milk. So, through sex without a condom; through sharing needles or razor blades with someone's blood on them; and from a mother to her baby. That is it. Not by hugging, not by holding hands, not by sharing a cup, a spoon, a toilet or a bath, not through spit, sweat or tears, and not from mosquitoes. A classmate living with HIV is no danger to you at all in everyday life.

You cannot see HIV on someone. They can look perfectly healthy for years. The only way to know is a test: a finger prick, free at any clinic, result in about 15 minutes.

And the good news is big. There is no cure, but there is treatment, ART, pills taken every day, that stop the virus copying itself. The generals come back, and the person can live a long, normal life. When the treatment pushes the virus so low the tests cannot find it, the person does not pass HIV on through sex. South Africa has about eight million people living with HIV, about one in eight of us, and runs the biggest treatment programme in the world.

## Subtopic: Know, Protect, Test, Treat

Four words cover everything in this lesson: know, protect, test, treat.

Know. Know how infections pass: through sex and blood. Know that many infections give no signs at all, so feeling fine proves nothing. Know that a girl can fall pregnant the very first time. Know that the pill and the injection stop pregnancy but do nothing against germs. Know your rights: you can say no at any moment, the age of consent is 16, and a clinic must keep your visit private.

Protect. Not having sex is the safest choice there is. For anyone who does, a condom every time, plus a reliable method against pregnancy. For men, medical circumcision cuts the risk of catching HIV during sex by more than half. For people at high risk, a daily pill called PrEP prevents HIV. And after an emergency, like rape or a condom breaking, a course of medicine called PEP, started within three days, can stop HIV taking hold.

Test. If there are signs, like a strange discharge, burning when you pee, sores, blisters or warts down there, or pain low in the belly, go to the clinic. Even without signs, testing is how people find out early. HIV testing is free, quick and private.

Treat. The infections caused by bacteria, syphilis, gonorrhoea and chlamydia, are cured with the right antibiotics, and both partners must be treated or the germ just bounces back. The ones caused by viruses, HIV and herpes, cannot be cured, but medicine controls them. HPV, the virus behind genital warts and cervical cancer, is mostly prevented by the vaccine given to Grade 5 girls. And any sore down there makes HIV easier to catch, so treating one infection helps prevent another.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Each contraceptive cuts one link: hormones stop the egg, condoms block the sperm, the loop stops the move-in. Most reliable are the ones you cannot forget. Only abstinence and condoms also stop infections, hence dual protection. HIV attacks the helper white blood cells; untreated, it leads to AIDS; it spreads only through blood, semen, vaginal fluids and breast milk; ART controls it for life. Bacterial STDs are cured; viral ones are controlled. Know, protect, test, treat. Grade 10 to 12 Life Sciences returns to immunity and disease.
