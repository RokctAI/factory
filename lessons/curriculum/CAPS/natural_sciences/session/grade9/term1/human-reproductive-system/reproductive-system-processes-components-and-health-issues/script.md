# Part 1 — Expert

This session gives the overview of the human reproductive system: why humans reproduce sexually, what gametes are, the components of the male and female systems, the sequence of processes from gamete production to birth, and the main health issues of the system. The anchor numbers are 23 chromosomes in each gamete, 46 in every other body cell, one egg released about every 28 days in a woman of reproductive age, and many millions of sperm made every day by a man. The governing principle is that each parent contributes half of the genetic instructions through a gamete, and the whole system exists to make those gametes, bring them together and protect the new individual while it develops.

## Subtopic: Sexual Reproduction and Gametes

Reproduction is the production of new individuals of the same species, and it is one of the seven life processes. Without it a species dies out with its last members. There are two broad kinds. In asexual reproduction a single parent produces offspring that are genetically identical to it, as when a bacterium splits in two or a potato tuber sprouts a new plant. In sexual reproduction two parents each contribute a special cell, and the offspring is a new combination of both, like neither parent exactly. Humans reproduce only sexually.

The special cells are called gametes, or sex cells. The male gamete is the sperm cell and the female gamete is the egg cell, also called the ovum, plural ova. Gametes differ from every other cell in the body in one essential way: they carry half the usual number of chromosomes. A normal human body cell, a skin cell or a muscle cell, has 46 chromosomes in its nucleus, arranged as 23 pairs, one of each pair having come from the mother and one from the father. A gamete has 23 chromosomes, one from each pair. When a sperm fuses with an egg, the two nuclei combine and the new cell, the zygote, has 23 plus 23, which is 46 chromosomes again. If gametes carried the full 46, the number would double every generation, which is impossible.

The organs that make gametes are called gonads. In males the gonads are the two testes, which make sperm; in females they are the two ovaries, which contain and release eggs. The gonads also make the sex hormones: testosterone in the testes, and oestrogen and progesterone in the ovaries. Those hormones drive puberty and control the menstrual cycle, which the next lessons cover.

Sexual reproduction has a cost: it needs two individuals, special organs and a lot of energy. Its great advantage is variation. Because each gamete carries a different selection of chromosomes, brothers and sisters from the same parents are all different, except identical twins, who come from a single zygote that split in two. Variation means that in a changing environment, or when a new disease arrives, some individuals are more likely to survive. Grade 12 Life Sciences explains how the gametes get their half set, a cell division called meiosis.

The questions for this section are with you now: sexual against asexual reproduction, what a gamete is, and the chromosome numbers 23 and 46.

## Subtopic: Components of the Male and Female Systems

The male reproductive system has three groups of parts. The two testes, held outside the body in a skin bag called the scrotum, make sperm and testosterone; they hang outside because sperm develop best a few degrees below body temperature. A coiled tube on each testis, the epididymis, stores the sperm while they mature. Two sperm ducts carry the sperm from the testes towards the urethra. Along the way, glands add fluids: the seminal vesicles and the prostate gland produce a fluid that provides sugar for energy and a medium for the sperm to swim in, and the mixture of sperm and fluid is called semen. Finally the urethra, a tube running through the penis, carries semen out of the body; in males the urethra also carries urine, though never at the same time. The penis becomes erect to place semen in the vagina during sexual intercourse.

The female reproductive system is mostly inside the body, in the lower abdomen. The two ovaries, each about the size of an almond, contain thousands of immature eggs from before birth and release one mature egg roughly every 28 days after puberty. Next to each ovary is an oviduct, also called a Fallopian tube, which catches the released egg and carries it towards the uterus; fertilisation normally happens in the oviduct. The uterus, or womb, is a hollow, pear-shaped organ with a thick muscular wall and a soft, blood-rich inner lining, and it is where a baby develops. The narrow neck of the uterus is the cervix, a ring of muscle that keeps the uterus closed during pregnancy and widens during birth. The cervix opens into the vagina, a muscular tube leading to the outside, which receives the penis during intercourse and forms the birth canal. The external parts are together called the vulva.

The summary for marking is a function next to each name. Testis: makes sperm and testosterone. Scrotum: holds the testes at a lower temperature. Sperm duct: carries sperm. Prostate and seminal vesicles: add fluid to make semen. Urethra and penis: carry semen out and deliver it. Ovary: produces eggs and the hormones oestrogen and progesterone. Oviduct: carries the egg and is the site of fertilisation. Uterus: where the baby develops. Cervix: the neck of the uterus. Vagina: receives sperm and is the birth canal.

The questions for this section are ready: which organ makes sperm, where fertilisation happens, and the function of the uterus.

## Subtopic: The Processes: From Gametes to a Baby

The reproductive system carries out a chain of processes, and each happens in a particular place. The chain has six links.

First, gamete production. From puberty onward the testes make sperm continuously, many millions every day, for the rest of a man's life. The ovaries work differently: a girl is born with all the immature eggs she will ever have, about one to two million, of which a few hundred thousand remain by puberty, and from puberty to menopause, at about 50, the ovaries release usually one mature egg in each menstrual cycle of about 28 days. Only about 400 to 500 eggs are ever released in a lifetime.

Second, the transfer of sperm. During sexual intercourse semen is released into the vagina. One release of a few millilitres carries tens to hundreds of millions of sperm, which swim through the cervix and the uterus into the oviducts. Most never get that far.

Third, fertilisation. If an egg is present in an oviduct, one sperm can penetrate it, and the nucleus of the sperm fuses with the nucleus of the egg. The fertilised egg is the zygote, the first cell of a new individual, with 46 chromosomes. Once one sperm has entered, the egg's membrane changes so that no other sperm can.

Fourth, implantation. The zygote divides repeatedly as it travels down the oviduct, becoming a ball of cells, and after about a week it sinks into the thick lining of the uterus. From that moment the woman is pregnant.

Fifth, pregnancy, or gestation. Over about 38 weeks from fertilisation the ball of cells develops into an embryo and then a foetus, fed and supplied with oxygen through the placenta, an organ that grows from both the embryo's tissues and the uterine lining.

Sixth, birth. Strong contractions of the uterus muscle open the cervix and push the baby out through the vagina, followed by the placenta, the afterbirth.

Running alongside the chain is the menstrual cycle. Each month the lining of the uterus thickens in preparation for a possible pregnancy. If no fertilised egg implants, the lining breaks down and leaves the body as the menstrual flow, the period, over about three to seven days, and the cycle starts again. The details are the subject of the stages-of-reproduction lesson.

## Subtopic: Health Issues of the Reproductive System and the Error Museum

Sexually transmitted infections are the most common health issue of the system. They are passed on by unprotected sexual contact and include HIV, syphilis, gonorrhoea, chlamydia, genital herpes and the human papillomavirus, HPV. Many cause no symptoms at first, which is why they spread, and untreated bacterial infections can scar and block the oviducts or the sperm ducts. The lesson on contraception and HIV treats prevention in full; for now note that infections caused by bacteria can be cured with antibiotics, while viral infections such as HIV and herpes cannot be cured, only controlled.

Infertility is the inability to conceive after a year of regular unprotected intercourse, and it affects roughly one couple in six worldwide. It is not a woman's problem: the cause lies with the male partner about as often as with the female. Causes include oviducts blocked by scarring from untreated infections, failure to ovulate because of hormone imbalances, a low sperm count or poorly swimming sperm, and lifestyle factors that harm both sexes: smoking, heavy drinking, drug use and being severely overweight. Fertility in women also declines steadily after the mid-thirties.

Cancers of the reproductive organs are common and are often curable when found early. Cervical cancer is caused by long-term infection with certain types of HPV and is one of the most common cancers among South African women; the HPV vaccine, offered free to Grade 5 girls in public schools, prevents most cases, and a Pap smear at a clinic detects early changes in the cervix before they become cancer. Breast cancer is the most common cancer in South African women, and monthly self-examination and screening find lumps early. Prostate cancer is the most common cancer in South African men, mostly after the age of 50. Testicular cancer is rare but is the most common cancer in young men aged about 15 to 35, and a monthly self-examination for lumps is the recommended habit.

Teenage pregnancy is a further health and social issue. A young body has a higher risk of complications in pregnancy and birth, and many teenage mothers leave school. Menstrual health matters too: severe period pain or very irregular cycles are reasons to visit a clinic, and access to sanitary products keeps girls in school, which is why government programmes supply free pads in many schools.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: sperm are made in the penis; they are made in the testes, and the penis only delivers them. Two: fertilisation happens in the uterus; it happens in the oviduct, and the uterus is where the embryo implants and grows. Three: gametes have 46 chromosomes like other cells; they have 23, so that fertilisation restores 46. Four: a woman releases many eggs every month; usually one egg is released per cycle. Five: infertility is always the woman's problem; the cause lies with the man about as often as with the woman.

Layout for a health question: cause, effect on the system, prevention or treatment. The reproductive system is then described exactly as the memorandum expects.

## Subtopic: Half a Recipe From Each Parent

Every cell in your body carries a recipe book for building you, and the recipes are written on 46 chromosomes, which come in 23 pairs. Think of a recipe book with 23 chapters, and two versions of every chapter, one version from your mother and one from your father.

Now, how do you make a new person who is a mix of two parents? You cannot just add two whole recipe books together, or the baby would have 92 chromosomes, the grandchild 184, and so on. So the body makes special cells that carry only one version of each chapter: 23 chromosomes, half a recipe book. These half-cells are the gametes, the sex cells. The father's gamete is the sperm. The mother's gamete is the egg, or ovum. When a sperm and an egg join, 23 plus 23 makes 46, a complete recipe book again, and that first complete cell is the zygote, the very first cell of a brand-new person.

Because each sperm and each egg picks its chapters in a different mix, every child is a new combination. That is why you and your brother or sister look related but not the same, unless you are identical twins, who came from one zygote that split in two. This mixing is the big advantage of having two parents: a family, and a whole population, ends up full of differences, and differences help some individuals survive when things change, like a new disease.

Some living things do not bother with two parents. A bacterium simply splits into two identical copies, and a potato grows new plants from its own tuber. That is asexual reproduction, one parent, identical copies. People always need two parents and two gametes. That is sexual reproduction.

Keep the half recipe in mind and try the questions for this part: 23 or 46, which cell is the gamete, and why we need halves.

## Subtopic: Two Factories and a Nursery

Picture the reproductive system as two factories and a nursery. The factories make gametes. The nursery is where a baby grows.

The male factory is the pair of testes. They hang outside the body in a pouch of skin, the scrotum, because they are like a fridge section: sperm are made best a little cooler than the rest of the body. The testes make millions of sperm every day from puberty onwards, plus the hormone testosterone. The sperm are stored in a coiled tube on the back of each testis, then travel along the sperm ducts, like delivery pipes. On the way, the prostate gland and the seminal vesicles add a sugary fluid, fuel and swimming water for the sperm, and the mix is called semen. The last pipe is the urethra, through the penis, which carries semen out. The same pipe carries urine, never at the same time.

The female factory is the pair of ovaries, inside the lower belly, each about the size of an almond. They are not really a factory so much as a warehouse: a baby girl is born with all her eggs already stored there, and from puberty the ovaries send out usually one egg about every 28 days, plus the hormones oestrogen and progesterone. Next to each ovary is an oviduct, a narrow tube with fingers at its end that catch the egg and carry it along. If a sperm meets the egg, it is here, in the oviduct, not in the womb.

The nursery is the uterus, the womb. It is a hollow, pear-shaped bag of strong muscle with a soft, blood-rich lining, a little bigger than a fist. A fertilised egg settles into the lining and grows there for about nine months. The neck of the nursery is the cervix, a tight ring that keeps the door shut during pregnancy and opens wide at birth. Below it the vagina leads to the outside: it receives the sperm, and it is the birth canal.

Every month the nursery gets ready for a baby by thickening its lining. If no baby arrives, the lining is not needed, so it breaks down and leaves through the vagina as the period, and then the nursery gets ready all over again.

Hold the factories and the nursery in mind and try this part's questions: who makes the sperm, where the egg is fertilised, and what the nursery is.

## Subtopic: Looking After the System

Like every system, the reproductive system can get sick, and most of its problems can be prevented or caught early.

Infections passed on by sex are the biggest group. Some are caused by bacteria, like syphilis, gonorrhoea and chlamydia, and a clinic can cure them with antibiotics. Others are viruses, like HIV and herpes, and those cannot be cured, only kept under control with medicine. The sneaky part is that many of these infections cause no signs at first, so people pass them on without knowing. Left alone, a bacterial infection can scar the delicate tubes, the oviducts or the sperm ducts, and blocked tubes mean the egg and sperm cannot meet.

That leads to infertility: a couple trying for a year without a pregnancy. It is common, about one couple in every six, and it is not just a woman's issue: the problem is in the man just about as often. Old infections, hormone problems, a low number of sperm, smoking, heavy drinking and drugs all play a part.

Then cancers. One special virus, HPV, can cause cancer of the cervix many years later. There is a vaccine for it, given free to Grade 5 girls at school, and a quick test at the clinic called a Pap smear finds changes long before they become cancer. Breast cancer is the most common cancer in South African women and prostate cancer the most common in South African men. Young men should check their testes for lumps once a month, because testicular cancer, though rare, mostly hits men between about 15 and 35, and is very curable when found early. Girls and women should know what their breasts normally feel like for the same reason.

And taking care of your body during your periods matters too: strong pain or very irregular periods are worth a clinic visit, and free pads in many schools mean no one should miss class because of a period.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Gametes carry 23 chromosomes, half a recipe; together they make a zygote with 46. Testes make sperm and testosterone; semen gets its fluid from the prostate and seminal vesicles. Ovaries release usually one egg about every 28 days and make oestrogen and progesterone. Fertilisation happens in the oviduct, the baby grows in the uterus, the cervix is the door, the vagina is the birth canal. Infections, infertility and cancers can mostly be prevented or caught early. In Grade 10 and 12 Life Sciences you will meet meiosis and human reproduction in full.
