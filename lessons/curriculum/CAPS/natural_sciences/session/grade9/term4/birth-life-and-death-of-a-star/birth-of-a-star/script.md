# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains how stars are born. A star is a huge, hot ball of gas, mainly hydrogen and helium, that gives off its own light and heat because of nuclear fusion in its core. The anchor facts are that stars form in nebulae, vast clouds of gas and dust in space; that gravity pulls denser parts of a nebula together in a process called gravitational collapse; that as the gas collapses it heats up and forms a protostar; that when the temperature in the core reaches about 10 million °C, nuclear fusion begins, in which hydrogen nuclei join to form helium and release enormous amounts of energy; and that a star is born when the outward push of this energy balances the inward pull of gravity. Our Sun formed in this way about 4,6 billion years ago.

## Subtopic: Stars and Nebulae

A star is a huge ball of very hot gas that produces its own light and heat. The Sun is our nearest star. Planets and moons, by contrast, do not make their own light; we see them because they reflect sunlight.

The Sun is about 150 million kilometres away, so its light takes about 8 minutes to reach us. The next nearest stars, in the Alpha Centauri system, are about 4,2 to 4,4 light-years away. A light-year is the distance light travels in one year, about 9,5 million million kilometres, so these stars are about 40 million million kilometres away. From South Africa, the bright pair Alpha and Beta Centauri, called the Pointers, can be seen pointing towards the Southern Cross.

Because light takes time to travel, looking at the stars means looking into the past. We see the Sun as it was about 8 minutes ago, Alpha Centauri as it was over four years ago, and the Orion Nebula as it was about 1 300 years ago. The stars look like tiny points only because they are so far away; many are larger and brighter than the Sun.

Stars are made mainly of hydrogen, the simplest element, with about a quarter of their mass as helium and small amounts of heavier elements such as oxygen, carbon and iron.

Space between the stars is not completely empty. It contains extremely thin gas and tiny grains of dust. In some places, this material is gathered into enormous clouds called nebulae, from the Latin word for mist or cloud. One such cloud is a nebula. Nebulae can be many light-years across. They are made mostly of hydrogen gas, with some helium and dust made of carbon, silicon and other elements.

Some nebulae glow, because hot young stars inside them make the gas give off light, often in shades of red and pink from hydrogen. Others are dark, because their dust blocks the light of stars behind them. The Coalsack, a dark nebula next to the Southern Cross, looks like a black hole in the bright band of the Milky Way. The cold, dense, dark clouds are where new stars form, so they are called stellar nurseries.

## Subtopic: Gravitational Collapse

Every particle of matter attracts every other particle by gravity. In a thin nebula, the pull between widely spread particles is weak, and the cloud can float for millions of years. But a nebula is not perfectly even. Some regions are slightly denser than others. If a dense region has enough mass, its gravity begins to pull its gas and dust inwards. This is called gravitational collapse.

Collapse can be triggered by a disturbance, such as the shock wave from a nearby exploding star, called a supernova, the strong light and winds of hot young stars, or a collision between clouds. The disturbance squeezes part of the cloud enough that gravity takes over.

As the cloud collapses, it breaks up into smaller clumps, so a single nebula usually gives birth to a cluster of hundreds or thousands of stars. The Jewel Box, a cluster of young stars next to the Southern Cross, was born together from one cloud.

Each clump keeps shrinking. As the particles fall inwards, they speed up and collide more often, so their energy of movement turns into heat. The centre of the clump becomes denser and hotter. The clump also starts to spin, and spins faster as it shrinks, in the same way that a figure skater spins faster when she pulls in her arms. The spinning flattens the outer material into a disc around the centre.

The hot, dense ball forming at the centre is called a protostar. It glows because it is hot, but it is not yet a true star, because no nuclear fusion is happening in its core. It is usually hidden inside its dusty cocoon, so astronomers study protostars using infrared light, which passes through dust. A protostar like the young Sun takes millions of years, and tens of millions to fully settle, to become a star.

Young protostars are restless. They often shoot out narrow jets of gas from their north and south poles at hundreds of kilometres per second, and strong winds blow away the remaining gas of their cocoons. Once the surrounding cloud has been cleared, the new star becomes visible in ordinary light.

The disc of gas and dust around the protostar is where planets form. Dust grains stick together into pebbles, then rocks, then planets. The Earth and the other planets formed from the disc around the young Sun.

The Sun formed in exactly this way, about 4,6 billion years ago. Scientists know its age partly from meteorites, rocks that fell to Earth from space. The oldest grains in some meteorites formed in the disc around the young Sun, and radioactive dating shows that they are about 4,57 billion years old. The Sun, the Earth and the other planets are all about this age.

## Subtopic: Nuclear Fusion Switches On

As the protostar continues to contract, its core becomes hotter and denser. When the temperature in the core reaches about 10 million °C, something new happens. The hydrogen nuclei are moving so fast that, when they collide, they overcome the repulsion between their positive charges and join together. This joining of light nuclei to form a heavier nucleus is called nuclear fusion.

In stars like the Sun, four hydrogen nuclei are joined, in several steps, to form one helium nucleus. The helium nucleus has slightly less mass than the four hydrogen nuclei that formed it: about 0,7 per cent of the mass is converted into energy. That sounds tiny, but the conversion of a small mass releases an enormous amount of energy. Every second, the Sun fuses about 600 million tonnes of hydrogen, and about 0,7 per cent of that, roughly 4 million tonnes, is turned into energy.

Once fusion is under way, the core of a star like the Sun settles at about 15 million °C. The core is also extremely dense, much denser than lead, because of the weight of the layers above pressing down on it. These conditions exist only in the cores of stars, which is why fusion cannot happen in ordinary matter on Earth.

Nuclear fusion is different from burning. Burning is a chemical reaction, in which atoms rearrange and join with oxygen, but the nuclei are unchanged. In fusion, the nuclei themselves join and new elements form. Fusion releases millions of times more energy than burning the same mass of fuel.

The energy from fusion heats the core and creates an outward pressure from the hot gas and radiation. This pressure pushes against the inward pull of gravity. When the two balance, the star stops shrinking and settles down to shine steadily. At this moment a star is born. The energy slowly works its way out from the core to the surface, where it escapes as light and heat.

The energy made in the core does not reach the surface quickly. The core and the layers around it are so dense that light is absorbed and given off again countless times, bouncing in random directions. It takes tens of thousands of years, possibly more than a hundred thousand years, for energy made in the Sun's core to reach its surface, but then only about 8 minutes for the light to cross space to the Earth.

If a collapsing clump has too little mass, less than about 8 per cent of the Sun's mass, its core never gets hot enough for hydrogen fusion. It becomes a brown dwarf, a failed star that glows dimly and slowly cools.

## Subtopic: Star Nurseries and the Error Museum

Several star nurseries can be seen from South Africa. The Orion Nebula is visible to the naked eye as a fuzzy patch in Orion's sword, which hangs from the three stars of Orion's belt that are often called the Three Kings or Drie Konings. It is about 1 300 light-years away. The Carina Nebula, much further away, glows near the Southern Cross. The Tarantula Nebula lies in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a small galaxy visible from the Southern Hemisphere as a faint smudge near the south celestial pole.

South Africa is one of the best places in the world to study the sky, because the Karoo is dry, high and far from city lights. The Southern African Large Telescope, SALT, near Sutherland, has a main mirror about 11 metres across, made of 91 hexagonal segments, and is the largest single optical telescope in the Southern Hemisphere. The MeerKAT radio telescope, near Carnarvon, uses 64 dishes to detect radio waves from cold gas in nebulae and distant galaxies, and is part of the international Square Kilometre Array project.

To see a star nursery yourself, choose a moonless night away from city lights between about December and March, when Orion is high in the evening sky. From South Africa, Orion appears upside down compared with pictures drawn in the Northern Hemisphere, so the sword hangs above the belt, and the Orion Nebula is the fuzzy middle star of the sword. Binoculars show it as a soft glowing cloud.

A worked example: how far does light travel in 8 minutes? Light travels about 300 000 kilometres per second, and 8 minutes is 8 × 60 = 480 seconds, so it travels about 300 000 × 480 = 144 000 000 kilometres, close to the 150 million kilometres to the Sun.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: stars burn like a fire; they shine because of nuclear fusion, not chemical burning. Two: a protostar is already a true star; it is not a star until hydrogen fusion begins in its core. Three: stars form from nothing in empty space; they form from gas and dust in nebulae. Four: gravity pushes the cloud apart; gravity pulls the cloud together, and fusion pressure pushes outwards. Five: the Sun is a planet that gives light; the Sun is a star, and planets only reflect light.

## Subtopic: Clouds in Space

A star is a giant ball of very hot gas that makes its own light. The Sun is a star. Planets only reflect light.

Stars are born inside huge clouds of gas and dust called nebulae. One cloud is a nebula. They are made mostly of hydrogen gas.

Some nebulae glow pink and red. Others are dark and block the stars behind them, like the Coalsack next to the Southern Cross.

The cold, dark, thick parts of these clouds are where stars are born. We call them star nurseries.

These clouds are unbelievably big. Light, the fastest thing there is, can take years to cross one.

Our own Sun was born in a cloud like this about 4,6 billion years ago, probably together with many sister stars that have since drifted apart.

## Subtopic: Gravity Squeezes

Gravity pulls all matter together. In a nebula, a thicker patch of gas pulls more and more gas towards it.

Sometimes a nearby exploding star gives the cloud a push to get started.

As the gas falls inwards, it gets squashed and heats up, like the air in a bicycle pump getting warm.

The ball also starts spinning, faster and faster, like a skater pulling in her arms. Leftover gas and dust spread out into a flat disc, where planets can later form.

The hot ball in the middle is a protostar: a baby star that is not shining by itself yet.

## Subtopic: Switching On

The protostar keeps getting squeezed. Its centre gets hotter and hotter.

At about 10 million °C, hydrogen bits crash together so hard that they join to make helium. This is nuclear fusion. It is not burning. It releases huge amounts of energy.

That energy pushes outwards, and gravity pulls inwards. When the push and the pull balance, the star stops shrinking. A star is born!

If the ball is too small, it never gets hot enough, and it becomes a failed star, a brown dwarf.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Stars form in nebulae, clouds of mainly hydrogen gas and dust. Gravity pulls dense regions together; the gas heats up and forms a protostar. At about 10 million °C in the core, hydrogen fuses into helium, releasing energy. When outward pressure balances gravity, a stable star is born.
