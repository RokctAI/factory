### subtopic_1 — Running Out of Fuel
**Assistant:** If the Sun becomes a red giant, could people move to the outer planets or their moons to survive?

**Tutor:** In theory, the habitable zone, the region where a planet could have liquid water on its surface, will move outwards as the Sun brightens and swells. During the red giant stage, the zone may move out to the region of Jupiter and Saturn. Some of their large icy moons, such as Jupiter's moon Europa or Saturn's moon Titan, might then warm up enough for their ice to melt, at least for a while. However, this would be a very difficult and uncertain refuge. The red giant stage lasts only up to about a billion years, which is short compared with the billions of years that life on Earth took to develop, and the Sun's brightness will change a great deal during that time. When the Sun finally sheds its outer layers and becomes a white dwarf, it will give off only a tiny fraction of its present energy, and the whole solar system will freeze. It is also worth remembering how far away this is. The Sun will not become a red giant for about 5 billion years, and the Earth will already be too hot for complex life in roughly a billion years. Humans have existed for only about 300 000 years, so this future is unimaginably distant.

### subtopic_2 — The Sun's Future as a Red Giant
**Assistant:** Why does fusion stop at iron in massive stars?

**Tutor:** Because fusing elements lighter than iron releases energy, but fusing iron uses up energy instead. The nuclei of atoms are held together by a very strong force acting between protons and neutrons. When small nuclei such as hydrogen and helium join, the new nucleus is more tightly bound, and the difference in energy is released, which powers the star. This continues as the star builds heavier elements: carbon, oxygen, neon, magnesium and silicon. But each step releases less energy than the one before, and the fusion becomes faster: in a massive star, hydrogen fusion lasts millions of years, but silicon fusion lasts only about a day. Iron nuclei are the most tightly bound of all. Joining iron nuclei to make heavier elements would require energy rather than releasing it, so an iron core cannot produce the pressure needed to hold up the star. As iron builds up in the core, it eventually becomes too massive to support itself. It collapses in less than a second, from the size of the Earth to a ball about 20 kilometres across, and the rebound and the flood of particles from the collapse blast the outer layers apart in a supernova. Elements heavier than iron are made during such explosions and in neutron star collisions.

### subtopic_3 — Planetary Nebulae and White Dwarfs
**Assistant:** How can a white dwarf be as small as the Earth yet have half the mass of the Sun?

**Tutor:** Because its matter is squeezed far more tightly than any matter on Earth. Ordinary atoms are mostly empty space: a tiny nucleus surrounded by a large region in which electrons move. In a white dwarf, the enormous gravity of the star's leftover mass crushes the atoms so that the electrons are no longer arranged around individual nuclei. Instead, the nuclei and electrons are packed into a dense sea, and the empty space inside atoms is largely removed. What stops the white dwarf from collapsing further is a rule of physics that says no two electrons can be in exactly the same state, so electrons strongly resist being squeezed any closer together. This resistance is called electron degeneracy pressure. It is so strong that it holds up the white dwarf without any fusion or heat. The result is an object with roughly half the Sun's mass in a volume similar to the Earth's, about a million times denser than water. There is a limit, however. Indian-American astronomer Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar calculated that a white dwarf cannot be more than about 1,4 times the mass of the Sun. Above this, even the electrons cannot hold it up, and it collapses further.

### subtopic_4 — Explosive Endings, Star Stuff and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do astronomers know what will happen to the Sun if no one has watched a star die from start to finish?

**Tutor:** Because the sky contains millions of stars at every stage of life, like a photograph of a crowd that includes babies, adults and elderly people. Watching one person for a few minutes would not show you how people age, but by studying many people of different ages you could work out the whole life story. Astronomers do the same with stars. They observe nebulae with protostars, main sequence stars of every mass, red giants, planetary nebulae and white dwarfs. Star clusters are especially useful, because all their stars formed at the same time from the same cloud, so differences between them are caused mainly by differences in mass. In an older cluster, the more massive stars have already left the main sequence and become red giants or white dwarfs, while the smaller ones are still on the main sequence. Astronomers combine these observations with physics, using computer models that calculate how a star's core changes as it fuses hydrogen, helium and heavier elements. The models successfully predict the numbers of stars seen at each stage in real clusters. Stars similar to the Sun that are older than it, such as some red giants, show what lies ahead for our Sun.
