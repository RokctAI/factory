### subtopic_1 — Stars and Nebulae
**Assistant:** If space is so cold, why are some nebulae glowing so brightly?

**Tutor:** Because hot young stars inside or near them light them up, in the same way that a street light makes fog glow. Most of the gas in a nebula is cold, but when massive, very hot stars form inside it, they give off huge amounts of ultraviolet radiation. This radiation has enough energy to knock electrons off the hydrogen atoms in the surrounding gas. When the electrons are recaptured by the hydrogen nuclei, they release energy as light of particular colours. The strongest is a deep red-pink colour, which is why so many photographs of nebulae look pink or red. Oxygen gives off greenish light in a similar way. Nebulae that glow like this are called emission nebulae; the Orion, Carina and Tarantula Nebulae are examples. Other nebulae, called reflection nebulae, contain dust that scatters starlight, and they usually look blue, for the same reason the daytime sky is blue. Dark nebulae, such as the Coalsack, contain so much dust that they block the light of stars behind them. Many famous colour pictures are made by combining long exposures taken through different filters, so the colours are stronger than what the eye would see through a telescope.

### subtopic_2 — Gravitational Collapse
**Assistant:** Why does a collapsing cloud start to spin faster as it gets smaller?

**Tutor:** Because of a rule of physics called conservation of angular momentum. Any cloud in space has at least a tiny amount of rotation, because the gas within it is moving in slightly different directions. Angular momentum depends on how much mass is spinning, how fast it spins, and how far the mass is from the centre. If nothing outside twists the cloud, its total angular momentum stays the same. So when the mass moves closer to the centre, it must spin faster to make up for it. You can feel this on a swivel chair. Sit with your arms stretched out holding something heavy, push off to spin slowly, then pull your arms in, and you speed up. A figure skater uses the same trick to go from a slow turn to a fast spin. A collapsing cloud shrinks from about a light-year across to the size of a star, so even a very slow rotation becomes very fast. The spinning prevents the material around the equator from falling straight in, so it flattens into a disc. This is why the planets of our solar system all orbit in nearly the same flat plane and in the same direction.

### subtopic_3 — Nuclear Fusion Switches On
**Assistant:** If fusion releases so much energy, why don't we use it in power stations on Earth?

**Tutor:** Scientists have been trying for decades, because fusion would be a wonderful energy source. Its fuel, forms of hydrogen, can be obtained from seawater, it releases no greenhouse gases, and it produces much less long-lasting radioactive waste than the fission reactors at Koeberg. The problem is that it is extremely hard to achieve. The Sun manages fusion because its enormous gravity squeezes the core to a very high density, so nuclei collide very often even at about 15 million °C. On Earth we cannot create such pressure, so fusion reactors must heat their fuel to over 100 million °C, hotter than the core of the Sun, to make nuclei collide hard enough. No material can touch gas that hot, so it is held in place by powerful magnetic fields in doughnut-shaped machines called tokamaks, or squeezed by giant laser pulses. In 2022, a laser facility in the United States released more energy from fusion than the lasers delivered to the fuel for the first time, which was a major milestone. However, a practical power station that produces more electricity than it uses, continuously and cheaply, is still probably decades away. A large international experiment called ITER is being built in France to test this.

### subtopic_4 — Star Nurseries and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do astronomers see stars being born if they are hidden inside dusty clouds?

**Tutor:** They use kinds of light that our eyes cannot see, which can pass through dust. Visible light is blocked by the tiny dust grains in a nebula, much as fog blocks a car's headlights. But infrared radiation has longer wavelengths, and it passes through dust much more easily. Protostars are also warm rather than very hot, so they give off most of their energy as infrared radiation anyway. Infrared telescopes therefore reveal protostars glowing inside their cocoons. Because the Earth's atmosphere absorbs much infrared radiation, the best infrared telescopes are in space; the James Webb Space Telescope, launched in 2021, has taken detailed pictures of newborn stars and planet-forming discs in nebulae such as Carina. Radio telescopes detect radio waves given off by cold gas molecules in the densest parts of the clouds, where collapse is just beginning, and show how the gas is moving. South Africa's MeerKAT radio telescope in the Northern Cape studies cold hydrogen gas in our galaxy and others. By combining observations in visible, infrared and radio light, astronomers can see clouds at every stage, from cold clumps to protostars to young stars, and piece together the life story of a star.
