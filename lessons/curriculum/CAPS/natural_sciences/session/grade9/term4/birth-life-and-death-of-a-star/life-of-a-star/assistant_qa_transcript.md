### subtopic_1 — A Long, Steady Life
**Assistant:** If the Sun's surface is 5 500 °C, why is its outer atmosphere, the corona, over a million degrees?

**Tutor:** This is one of the big puzzles of solar science, called the coronal heating problem, and scientists are still working on the full answer. The corona is the thin, pearly white outer atmosphere of the Sun, normally hidden by the Sun's glare but visible during a total solar eclipse. Measurements show that it is at one to two million °C, hundreds of times hotter than the visible surface below it, even though it is further from the core. Heat should not flow from a cooler place to a hotter one, so something else must be carrying energy into the corona. The answer seems to lie in the Sun's magnetic field. The churning, bubbling gas at the surface twists and tangles magnetic field lines. Scientists think the energy is carried upwards in two main ways: by waves travelling along the magnetic field lines, rather like waves on a shaken rope, and by countless small explosions, called nanoflares, that happen when tangled field lines snap and reconnect. Spacecraft such as NASA's Parker Solar Probe, which has flown closer to the Sun than any other spacecraft, and the European Solar Orbiter, are studying the corona up close to test these ideas.

### subtopic_2 — Colour and Temperature
**Assistant:** How do astronomers measure the temperature of a star that is trillions of kilometres away?

**Tutor:** They analyse the star's light, using two main methods. The first is colour. Every hot object gives off light over a range of colours, and the colour at which it gives off the most light depends on its temperature: the hotter it is, the shorter the wavelength, moving from red towards blue. By measuring how bright a star is through several different coloured filters, astronomers can find where the peak lies and calculate the temperature. The second method uses the star's spectrum. When starlight is passed through a prism or a device called a spectrograph, it spreads into a rainbow crossed by thin dark lines. Each line is caused by a particular element in the star's outer layers absorbing light of a particular colour. Which lines appear, and how strong they are, depends very sensitively on the temperature. For example, helium lines are strong only in very hot blue stars, while lines from molecules appear only in cool red stars, because molecules break apart at high temperatures. Spectra also reveal what a star is made of and how fast it is moving towards or away from us. SALT, near Sutherland, is mainly used to collect spectra of faint stars and galaxies.

### subtopic_3 — Mass, Lifetime and Age
**Assistant:** If massive stars live only a few million years, why can we still see so many of them?

**Tutor:** Because new massive stars are still being born, and because they are so bright that we can see them from very far away. Massive stars are rare. For every star much more massive than the Sun, there are hundreds of smaller stars. However, a massive blue star can give off tens of thousands of times more light than the Sun, so it can be seen across thousands of light-years, while a dim red dwarf is invisible to the naked eye even when it is very close. When we look at the night sky, we therefore see a sample that is heavily biased towards powerful stars, many of them far away. In fact, most of the stars visible to the naked eye are more luminous than the Sun, while most of the stars in our neighbourhood are faint red dwarfs we cannot see without a telescope. Massive stars appear mainly in regions of the galaxy where stars are still forming, such as the spiral arms of the Milky Way and nebulae like Orion and Carina. A few million years is short compared with the Sun's life, but long compared with human history, so these stars appear unchanging to us.

### subtopic_4 — The Sun as a Medium-Sized Yellow Star and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Will the Sun keep getting brighter, and what will that mean for life on Earth?

**Tutor:** Yes. As the Sun turns hydrogen into helium, its core becomes slightly denser and hotter, and the Sun slowly brightens, by roughly 10 per cent every billion years. When it formed, it gave off about 30 per cent less light than it does now. This is far too slow to affect us on human timescales, and it is not the cause of today's global warming, which is happening over decades and is caused by greenhouse gases. Over very long times, however, it matters a great deal. Scientists estimate that in roughly one billion years, the Sun will be bright enough to heat the Earth so much that the oceans will start to evaporate rapidly. Water vapour is a greenhouse gas, so this would cause runaway warming, and the water would eventually be broken apart by sunlight high in the atmosphere and lost to space. Complex life on Earth would probably end long before the Sun leaves the main sequence. This is a very distant future, about 200 000 times longer than the whole of written human history. Some scientists have even suggested that future civilisations might find ways to move to cooler places in the solar system, such as the moons of Jupiter or Saturn.
