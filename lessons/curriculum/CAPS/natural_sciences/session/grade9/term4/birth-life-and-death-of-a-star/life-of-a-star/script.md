# Part 1 — Expert

This session describes the life of a star after its birth. The anchor facts are that a star spends most of its life, about 90 per cent of it, steadily fusing hydrogen into helium in its core, in a stage called the main sequence; that during this time the inward pull of gravity is balanced by the outward pressure of the hot gas and radiation; that the colour of a star shows its surface temperature, from red, the coolest at about 3 000 °C, through orange, yellow and white, to blue, the hottest at over 20 000 °C; that the more mass a star has, the hotter, brighter and bluer it is and the faster it uses its fuel, so massive stars live only a few million years while small red stars can live for many billions of years; and that the Sun is a medium-sized yellow star, with a surface temperature of about 5 500 °C, an age of about 4,6 billion years and a total main sequence lifetime of about 10 billion years.

## Subtopic: A Long, Steady Life

Once nuclear fusion has switched on in its core, a star settles down into the longest and most stable stage of its life. In its core, hydrogen nuclei are fused into helium, releasing energy. Astronomers call stars in this stage main sequence stars, because when they plot stars on a graph of brightness against temperature, these stars fall along a band called the main sequence. About 90 per cent of all the stars we see, including the Sun, are in this stage.

During the main sequence, a star is in balance. Gravity pulls all its gas inwards, trying to make it shrink. The energy released by fusion heats the gas, and the hot gas and radiation push outwards. These two effects balance, so the star stays the same size.

This balance corrects itself. If the core became slightly hotter, fusion would speed up, the extra pressure would make the star expand slightly, the core would cool a little and fusion would slow down again. If the core cooled, gravity would squeeze it, heating it up again. A star is therefore like a thermostat that keeps itself steady for billions of years.

During this long stage, the star slowly changes. As hydrogen in its core is turned into helium, the core becomes denser and slightly hotter, and the star becomes gradually brighter. When the Sun was born, it gave off about 30 per cent less light than it does today.

The energy produced in the core travels outwards, first as radiation slowly passing from particle to particle, and then, in the outer layers of stars like the Sun, by convection, in which hot gas rises, cools at the surface and sinks again, like boiling porridge. The surface of the Sun is covered with bubbling cells of rising gas, called granules, each typically about 1 000 kilometres across.

## Subtopic: Colour and Temperature

Stars come in different colours: red, orange, yellow, white and blue. The colour depends on the temperature of the star's surface.

You can see the same thing on Earth. When a blacksmith heats a piece of iron, it first glows dull red, then orange, then yellow, and finally white-hot as it gets hotter. An electric stove plate glows red. All hot objects give off light, and the hotter they are, the more their light shifts from red towards blue.

Red stars are the coolest, with surface temperatures of about 3 000 °C. Orange stars are about 4 000 °C, and yellow stars, like the Sun, about 5 500 °C. White stars are about 10 000 °C. Blue stars are the hottest, from about 20 000 °C to over 30 000 °C. This may seem the wrong way round, because we usually use red for hot and blue for cold on taps, but for stars and hot objects, blue means hotter.

You can find examples in the South African sky. Antares in Scorpius and Betelgeuse in Orion are red, with surfaces of only about 3 000 to 3 500 °C. Alpha Centauri A, one of the Pointers, is yellow, like the Sun. Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky, is white, at nearly 10 000 °C. Rigel in Orion is blue-white, and Acrux, the brightest star of the Southern Cross, is a hot blue-white star at over 20 000 °C.

Astronomers sort stars into classes by temperature and colour, labelled O, B, A, F, G, K and M, from the hottest blue O stars to the coolest red M stars. The Sun is a G star.

The brightness of a star as seen from Earth depends on two things: how much light it actually gives off and how far away it is. Sirius looks so bright mainly because it is close, about 8,6 light-years away, while Rigel looks almost as bright although it is hundreds of light-years away, because it truly gives off over a hundred thousand times more light than the Sun.

When astronomers plot many stars on a graph, with surface temperature along the bottom and true brightness up the side, most stars fall on a diagonal band running from hot, bright, blue stars at the top left to cool, dim, red stars at the bottom right. This band is the main sequence. A few stars lie above it, cool but very bright, and these are giant stars nearing the end of their lives; a few lie below it, hot but dim, and these are the small remains of dead stars. You will meet both kinds in the next lesson. The graph is called the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram, after the two astronomers who first drew it, more than a hundred years ago.

## Subtopic: Mass, Lifetime and Age

The most important property of a star is its mass, because mass decides its temperature, colour, brightness and lifetime.

A star with more mass has stronger gravity, which squeezes its core harder. Its core is therefore hotter and denser, so fusion happens much faster. More massive stars are hotter, bluer and brighter. A star with 10 times the mass of the Sun gives off thousands of times more light.

Because they use their fuel so quickly, massive stars live short lives. Although a massive star has more hydrogen, it burns through it so fast that it lasts only a few million to tens of millions of years. A star like the Sun lasts about 10 billion years. Small, cool red stars, called red dwarfs, use their fuel so slowly that they can shine for hundreds of billions of years or longer, far longer than the universe has existed, which is about 13,8 billion years. Red dwarfs are the most common stars, but they are so dim that not one is visible to the naked eye. The nearest star to the Sun, Proxima Centauri, is a red dwarf.

It is a bit like comparing a powerful sports car with a small, economical car. The sports car has a bigger tank, but it guzzles fuel so fast that it runs out first.

This means that the colour of a star also gives a clue to its age. A hot blue star must be young, because it cannot live long. In very old groups of stars, called globular clusters, the blue stars have long since died, and only yellow, orange and red stars remain. Young clusters, such as the Jewel Box near the Southern Cross, still contain brilliant blue stars.

Compare three main sequence stars. A red dwarf with a tenth of the Sun's mass has a surface of about 2 500 to 3 000 °C, gives off far less than one per cent of the Sun's light, and may shine for over a trillion years. The Sun, with one solar mass, is about 5 500 °C and lasts about 10 billion years. A blue star with 20 times the Sun's mass is over 30 000 °C, gives off tens of thousands of times more light than the Sun, and lasts less than 10 million years.

Most stars have masses between about a tenth of the Sun's mass and about 100 times the Sun's mass. Below that, objects cannot fuse hydrogen; above that, stars would blow themselves apart.

## Subtopic: The Sun as a Medium-Sized Yellow Star and the Error Museum

The Sun is an ordinary main sequence star. It is often called a medium-sized yellow star, or a yellow dwarf. It is much bigger than the many red dwarfs, but much smaller than the giant blue and red stars. Its diameter is about 1,4 million kilometres, about 109 times the diameter of the Earth, so about 1,3 million Earths could fit inside it. Its mass is about 333 000 times the Earth's mass, which is more than 99,8 per cent of the mass of the whole solar system.

The Sun's surface temperature is about 5 500 °C, giving it a yellowish-white colour. Seen from space, the Sun is actually white; it looks yellow from the Earth's surface because the atmosphere scatters some of the blue light, which is also why the sky is blue. Its core is about 15 million °C.

The Sun formed about 4,6 billion years ago and will spend about 10 billion years on the main sequence. A simple subtraction gives its remaining time: 10 − 4,6 = 5,4 billion years. The Sun is therefore roughly halfway through its life, a middle-aged star.

The Sun's surface shows dark patches called sunspots, cooler areas where strong magnetic fields block the flow of heat. They are about 3 500 to 4 500 °C, cooler than the surrounding surface, so they look dark. The number of sunspots rises and falls in a cycle of about 11 years. Never look directly at the Sun, even during an eclipse, without proper protection, because its light can permanently damage your eyes.

Although it is an ordinary star, the Sun is the source of almost all the energy on Earth. Its light drives photosynthesis, the water cycle and the winds, and the energy in coal, oil and gas is ancient sunlight stored by plants long ago. South Africa receives more sunshine than most countries, which is why solar panels and solar water heaters work so well here.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: red stars are the hottest; red stars are the coolest and blue stars the hottest. Two: big stars live the longest because they have more fuel; massive stars use their fuel so fast that they live the shortest lives. Three: the Sun is a very large star; it is a medium-sized star, and many stars are far larger. Four: the brightest-looking star is always the most powerful; apparent brightness also depends on distance. Five: the Sun is near the end of its life; it is about halfway through its 10-billion-year main sequence.

## Subtopic: The Long, Steady Shine

After it is born, a star spends most of its life, about nine tenths, quietly turning hydrogen into helium in its centre.

Gravity pulls the star inwards. The energy from fusion pushes outwards. They balance, like a tug of war that nobody wins, so the star stays the same size for billions of years.

If the star gets too hot inside, it puffs out a bit and cools down. If it cools, gravity squeezes it and it heats up again. It keeps itself steady, like a thermostat.

The energy slowly travels from the centre to the surface, and the surface bubbles like boiling porridge.

## Subtopic: The Colours of Stars

Stars have colours, and the colour tells us how hot the surface is.

Think of a piece of iron heated in a fire: first red, then orange, then yellow, then white-hot.

Red stars: coolest, about 3 000 °C.
Yellow stars, like the Sun: about 5 500 °C.
White stars: about 10 000 °C.
Blue stars: hottest, over 20 000 °C.

Yes, blue is hotter than red for stars!

Big, heavy stars are hot, blue and bright, but they use up their fuel very fast and die young, after just a few million years. Small red stars are cool and dim, but they last for hundreds of billions of years.

## Subtopic: Our Sun

Our Sun is a medium-sized yellow star.

It is about 109 times wider than the Earth, and its surface is about 5 500 °C. Its centre is about 15 million °C.

It is about 4,6 billion years old and will shine steadily for about 10 billion years in total. So it is about halfway through its life. It still has around 5 billion years to go.

Never look straight at the Sun. It can damage your eyes.
