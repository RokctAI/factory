# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains how stars die. The anchor facts are that when a star has used up the hydrogen in its core, fusion there stops, the core shrinks and heats, and the outer layers swell enormously and cool, turning the star into a red giant; that in a red giant the core becomes hot enough, about 100 million °C, to fuse helium into carbon and oxygen; that a star like the Sun eventually pushes its outer layers gently out into space, forming a glowing shell of gas called a planetary nebula; that the exposed core, no longer producing energy by fusion, becomes a white dwarf, a very hot, very dense object about the size of the Earth that slowly cools over billions of years; and that stars more than about 8 times the mass of the Sun die more violently, as supernova explosions, leaving neutron stars or black holes. The Sun will become a red giant in about 5 billion years.

## Subtopic: Running Out of Fuel

For most of its life, a star fuses hydrogen into helium in its core, with gravity balanced by the outward pressure of the hot gas. But the supply of hydrogen in the core is limited. Over billions of years for a star like the Sun, the core gradually fills up with helium, which cannot fuse at the core's temperature of about 15 million °C. Eventually, the hydrogen in the core runs out.

When fusion in the core stops, the outward pressure there drops, and gravity takes over. The helium core contracts and becomes hotter and denser. The heat from the shrinking core makes the hydrogen in a thin shell around the core start fusing, even faster than before.

The extra energy pushes the outer layers of the star outwards. The star swells to many times its original size. As the outer layers spread out over a much larger area, they cool, so the surface changes colour from yellow to orange-red. The star becomes a red giant: red because its surface is cool, about 3 000 to 4 000 °C, and giant because it is enormous. It is also much brighter than before, because its huge surface gives off far more light in total.

It may seem strange that a cooler star can be brighter. The reason is size. Each square metre of a red giant's surface gives off less light than a square metre of the Sun's surface, but a red giant has an enormous surface, thousands or even tens of thousands of times larger, so its total light output is much greater. A red giant can give off hundreds or thousands of times more light than the Sun does today.

When the core temperature reaches about 100 million °C, the helium nuclei begin to fuse, forming carbon, and then some of the carbon fuses with helium to form oxygen. The star gains a new, short-lived source of energy.

Red giants can be seen in the South African sky. Aldebaran, the orange eye of Taurus the bull, near Orion, and Gacrux, the reddish star at the top of the Southern Cross, are red giants. Much bigger still are red supergiants such as Betelgeuse and Antares, which are massive stars approaching their end. If Antares were put in the place of the Sun, its surface would reach beyond the orbit of Mars.

## Subtopic: The Sun's Future as a Red Giant

The Sun is about 4,6 billion years old and is expected to leave the main sequence in about 5 billion years. It will then slowly expand into a red giant. Its diameter may grow to roughly 200 times its present size. A quick calculation shows what this means: the Sun's diameter is about 1,4 million kilometres, so 1,4 × 200 = 280 million kilometres across, giving a radius of about 140 million kilometres, close to the Earth's distance of 150 million kilometres from the Sun.

Mercury, about 58 million kilometres from the Sun, and Venus, about 108 million kilometres away, will be swallowed. Whether the Earth is swallowed too is uncertain. As the Sun swells, it will lose a lot of mass in a strong wind from its surface, so its gravity will weaken and the planets' orbits will move outwards. Even if the Earth survives, it will be scorched: its oceans will have boiled away long before, and its surface will be melted rock.

The red giant stage lasts much less time than the main sequence: for the Sun, roughly a billion years in total, including a period of helium fusion in its core.

During this time, the outer layers of the red giant become loosely held, because they are so far from the core, and the star pulsates, swelling and shrinking. Each pulse pushes gas outwards, and strong winds carry the outer layers away into space. Dust grains of carbon and silicon compounds form in the cooling gas.

Other stars show us this future. Astronomers have found red giants with planets, and white dwarfs surrounded by the debris of rocky bodies that were torn apart, like the remains of a solar system.

It is worth putting this future in perspective. Five billion years is more than the whole age of the Earth so far. Long before the Sun becomes a red giant, its slow brightening on the main sequence will have made the Earth too hot for liquid oceans, probably in about one billion years. None of these changes has anything to do with the climate change happening today, which is caused by greenhouse gases over decades.

## Subtopic: Planetary Nebulae and White Dwarfs

Eventually, a star like the Sun pushes off all its outer layers. The gas spreads out into space as an expanding shell, at speeds of tens of kilometres per second. The hot, exposed core in the middle gives off intense ultraviolet radiation, which makes the gas shell glow in beautiful colours. This glowing shell is called a planetary nebula.

The name is misleading. Planetary nebulae have nothing to do with planets. In the 1700s, astronomers using small telescopes thought these round, fuzzy objects looked like the discs of planets, and the name stuck. A planetary nebula glows for only about 10 000 to 20 000 years before its gas spreads out and fades, which is a very short time for a star. Examples visible in the southern sky include the Southern Ring Nebula in the constellation Vela, and the Helix Nebula, one of the closest at about 650 light-years.

The core left behind is called a white dwarf. It is made mainly of carbon and oxygen, the products of helium fusion. No fusion happens in a white dwarf, so it no longer produces new energy. It shines only because it is still extremely hot, at first over 100 000 °C, and it is white or blue-white because it is so hot. White dwarfs are very small, about the size of the Earth, yet contain about half the mass of the Sun. They are incredibly dense: a teaspoon of white dwarf material would have a mass of several tonnes.

A white dwarf does not collapse further because its tightly packed electrons resist being squeezed together. Over billions of years, it slowly cools and dims, becoming a cool, dark object called a black dwarf. The universe is not yet old enough for any white dwarf to have cooled completely.

The nearest white dwarf to the Sun is Sirius B, a small companion of the bright star Sirius.

A white dwarf in a close pair of stars can have an unusual fate. If it pulls gas from a companion star, the gas builds up on its surface and can explode in a flash called a nova, or, if the white dwarf grows too massive, the whole white dwarf can be destroyed in a different kind of supernova. Astronomers use these very bright, very similar explosions to measure distances to faraway galaxies.

## Subtopic: Explosive Endings, Star Stuff and the Error Museum

Stars with more than about 8 times the mass of the Sun have a much more dramatic end. They swell into red supergiants, and their cores are so hot that they fuse elements beyond carbon and oxygen: neon, magnesium, silicon and finally iron. Fusing iron does not release energy, so when the core is iron, fusion stops. In less than a second, the core collapses under gravity, and the outer layers rebound in a gigantic explosion called a supernova. For a few weeks, a single supernova can outshine a whole galaxy of billions of stars.

What remains of the core becomes either a neutron star, an object with more mass than the Sun squashed into a ball only about 20 kilometres across, or, for the most massive stars, a black hole, whose gravity is so strong that not even light can escape. In 1987, a supernova was seen in the Large Magellanic Cloud, visible to the naked eye from the Southern Hemisphere, including from South Africa.

Betelgeuse, the red supergiant in Orion, is expected to explode as a supernova some time in the next 100 000 years or so. When it does, it will shine brightly enough to be seen in daylight for weeks. It is about 550 to 700 light-years away, far enough that it poses no danger to the Earth.

Dying stars return their material to space. Red giant winds, planetary nebulae and supernovae carry carbon, oxygen, nitrogen, silicon, iron and many other elements into the clouds between the stars, and supernovae and collisions of neutron stars make elements heavier than iron, such as gold. These enriched clouds form new stars and planets. The Sun and the Earth formed from such recycled material. The carbon in your cells, the oxygen you breathe, the calcium in your bones and the iron in your blood were all made inside stars that died before the Sun was born. In a real sense, we are made of star stuff.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: the Sun will explode as a supernova; it does not have enough mass, so it will become a red giant, form a planetary nebula and leave a white dwarf. Two: a planetary nebula is made of planets; it is a glowing shell of gas pushed off by a dying star. Three: red giants are red because they are hot; they are red because their surface is cool. Four: a white dwarf is a small, young star; it is the dead core of an old star, with no fusion. Five: the Sun will die soon; it has about 5 billion years left on the main sequence.

## Subtopic: Swelling Up

Stars do not last forever. One day, the hydrogen fuel in a star's centre runs out.

Without fusion in the middle, gravity squeezes the centre, and it gets hotter. Hydrogen around the centre starts fusing faster.

The extra energy makes the outside of the star puff up, like a balloon blown up huge. The outside spreads out and cools, so it turns red.

The star is now a red giant: red because it has cooled on the outside, giant because it is enormous.

The centre then gets so hot, about 100 million °C, that helium fuses into carbon and oxygen.

## Subtopic: The Last Breath

Our Sun will become a red giant in about 5 billion years. It may grow about 200 times wider, swallowing Mercury and Venus. The Earth will be scorched, even if it is not swallowed.

Then the red giant gently blows its outer layers into space, like a last breath. The gas forms a glowing shell called a planetary nebula. It has nothing to do with planets. Old telescopes just made it look like a planet.

The tiny centre left behind is a white dwarf: about the size of the Earth, very hot at first, and so heavy that a teaspoon of it would weigh as much as a few cars. It has no fusion, so it slowly cools and fades over billions of years.

Keep the last breath in mind and try this part's questions: planetary nebulae and white dwarfs.

## Subtopic: Made of Star Stuff

Very big stars, more than about 8 times the Sun's mass, die in a gigantic explosion called a supernova. They leave behind a tiny neutron star or a black hole.

When stars die, they throw the elements they made back into space: carbon, oxygen, iron and more. New stars and planets form from this material.

The carbon in your body and the iron in your blood were made inside stars long ago. You are made of star stuff!

Pocket summary of the lesson. When a star runs out of hydrogen in its core, it swells into a red giant. A star like the Sun then pushes off its outer layers as a planetary nebula, and its core becomes a white dwarf that slowly cools. Much more massive stars explode as supernovae, leaving neutron stars or black holes. Dying stars recycle elements into new stars, planets and living things. The Sun will become a red giant in about 5 billion years. This completes the life story of a star: born in a nebula, living on the main sequence, and dying as a red giant, planetary nebula and white dwarf.
