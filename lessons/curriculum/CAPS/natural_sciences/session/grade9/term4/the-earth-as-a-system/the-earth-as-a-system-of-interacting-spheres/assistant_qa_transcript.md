### subtopic_1 — Systems and Spheres
**Assistant:** Where exactly does the atmosphere end and space begin?

**Tutor:** There is no sharp edge, because the atmosphere gets thinner and thinner with height until it fades gradually into space. Gravity holds the air close to the Earth, so most of the air is packed into the lowest layers: about half of all the air is below about 5,5 kilometres, roughly the height of Mount Kilimanjaro, and about 99 per cent is below about 30 kilometres. Above that, the air becomes so thin that it would be almost impossible to tell apart from empty space without sensitive instruments. Because there is no clear boundary, scientists and space agencies have agreed on a convenient line. The most widely used is the Kármán line at 100 kilometres above sea level, where the air is so thin that an aircraft would have to fly as fast as an orbiting spacecraft to get enough lift from its wings. Some agencies use 80 kilometres instead. Even far above 100 kilometres, a few gas particles remain, which is why satellites in low orbit slowly lose speed and eventually fall unless they are boosted. You will study the layers of the atmosphere, and why the air gets thinner with height, later this term.

### subtopic_2 — Interactions Between the Spheres
**Assistant:** Are the spheres always in balance, or can the system change?

**Tutor:** The Earth system is always changing, but it often stays close to a balance for long periods because of feedbacks that tend to undo changes. For example, if the oceans warm slightly, more water evaporates, more clouds may form, and some clouds reflect sunlight back to space, which can cool the surface again. Over very long times, though, the Earth system has changed enormously. There have been ice ages when glaciers covered large parts of the continents, and warm periods with no polar ice at all. Long ago, before plants evolved, there was almost no oxygen in the atmosphere; it was the biosphere, through photosynthesis over hundreds of millions of years, that filled the air with oxygen. Continents have drifted across the globe, and South Africa was once joined to South America, Antarctica, India and Australia in a supercontinent called Gondwana. What worries scientists today is the speed of current change. By burning fossil fuels and clearing forests, people are changing the composition of the atmosphere much faster than natural processes usually do, and the other spheres are responding, with warming oceans, melting ice and shifting rainfall.

### subtopic_3 — Cycles that Link the Spheres
**Assistant:** Why is most of the Earth's fresh water not available for us to use?

**Tutor:** Because most fresh water is locked away as ice or lies deep underground, leaving only a tiny fraction in rivers, lakes and the soil. Of all the water on Earth, about 97 per cent is salt water in the oceans, which cannot be drunk or used for most farming without removing the salt, a process called desalination that uses a lot of energy. Of the remaining fresh water, roughly two thirds is frozen in the ice sheets of Antarctica and Greenland and in glaciers. Most of the rest is groundwater, some of it very deep or in rocks that release it only slowly. Less than one per cent of all the water on Earth is easily reachable fresh water in rivers, lakes, dams and shallow underground stores. South Africa is a water-scarce country, with an average rainfall of roughly 450 to 500 millimetres a year, well below the world average, and much of the rain evaporates before it reaches rivers. That is why we build dams, transfer water between catchments such as the Lesotho Highlands Water Project, protect wetlands and catchment areas, and need to use water carefully. Water that we pollute also becomes unusable, which makes protecting rivers and groundwater just as important as saving water.

### subtopic_4 — Human Impacts and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Can the actions of one person really affect the whole Earth system?

**Tutor:** On its own, one person's action is tiny, but the Earth system responds to the total of everyone's actions, and there are now more than eight billion people. Each kilowatt-hour of electricity from a coal power station releases roughly a kilogram of carbon dioxide into the atmosphere. One household using a few hundred kilowatt-hours a month adds a few hundred kilograms a month, and millions of households together add millions of tonnes. The same is true of litter: one plastic bottle dropped in a street may seem harmless, but rivers carry vast numbers of such bottles into the oceans. Because the effects add up, so do the solutions. When many people save electricity, use water carefully, reduce waste and recycle, the combined effect is large. Individual choices also influence others: families copy good habits, businesses respond to what customers want to buy, and communities can organise clean-ups, plant indigenous trees or protect a local wetland. Learners can also become informed citizens who understand the science and can speak up when decisions about mining, water or energy are made. Understanding the Earth as a system is the first step to looking after it.
