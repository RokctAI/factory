# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the Earth as a complex system of four interacting spheres: the lithosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere and biosphere. It describes what each sphere consists of, explains how matter and energy move between the spheres, uses the water cycle and carbon cycle as examples of interactions, and shows how human activities affect the system. The anchor facts are that a system is a set of parts that interact and affect one another; that the lithosphere is the rigid, rocky outer layer of the Earth, made of the crust and the uppermost part of the mantle; that the hydrosphere is all the water on Earth, about 97 per cent of which is salt water in the oceans; that the atmosphere is the layer of gases around the Earth, mostly nitrogen and oxygen; that the biosphere is all living organisms and the places where they live; that the Sun is the main source of energy driving the system; and that a change in one sphere causes changes in the others.

## Subtopic: Systems and Spheres

A system is a group of parts that work together and affect one another. You have already met systems in this subject: the human body is made of the digestive, circulatory and other systems, and the national electricity grid is a system of power stations, lines and users. The Earth itself can also be studied as a system. Thinking of the Earth this way helps scientists understand how a change in one place, such as a volcanic eruption or the clearing of a forest, can have effects far away.

Scientists divide the Earth system into four spheres.

The lithosphere is the solid, rocky outer layer of the Earth. It includes the crust, the rocks and soil we live on, and the uppermost, rigid part of the mantle beneath it. It is on average about 100 kilometres thick, but thinner under the oceans and thicker under the continents. The word comes from the Greek lithos, meaning stone.

The hydrosphere is all the water on, under and above the Earth's surface: oceans, rivers, lakes, dams, groundwater, ice and water vapour in the air. Water covers about 71 per cent of the Earth's surface, and about 97 per cent of all the water is salt water in the oceans. Less than 3 per cent is fresh water, and most of that is frozen in ice sheets and glaciers or lies underground.

The atmosphere is the layer of gases surrounding the Earth, held in place by gravity. Dry air is about 78 per cent nitrogen and 21 per cent oxygen, with small amounts of argon, carbon dioxide and other gases. The atmosphere protects life from harmful radiation, keeps the planet warm, and carries water around the world as vapour and clouds.

The biosphere is all living organisms on Earth, including plants, animals, fungi and micro-organisms, together with the parts of the other spheres where they live. Life is found from deep in the oceans, where bacteria live around hot springs on the sea floor, to high in the air and even in rocks deep underground, but most life exists in a thin zone near the surface.

Some scientists add a fifth part, the cryosphere, for all the frozen water on Earth: ice sheets, glaciers, sea ice and snow. In this course the frozen water is counted as part of the hydrosphere.

It helps to picture the sizes involved. The Earth's radius is about 6 371 kilometres, yet the lithosphere is only about 100 kilometres thick, the deepest ocean is about 11 kilometres deep, and most of the air lies within about 30 kilometres of the surface. If the Earth were the size of an apple, the oceans, the atmosphere and almost all of life would fit within the thickness of its skin. All four spheres are crowded together in this thin outer zone, which is one reason they interact so strongly.

## Subtopic: Interactions Between the Spheres

The spheres are not separate. They overlap and constantly exchange matter and energy. An interaction is any process in which one sphere affects another.

Here are some examples.

Lithosphere and atmosphere: a volcano releases gases such as carbon dioxide, water vapour and sulfur dioxide, and ash, into the atmosphere. Wind, which is moving air, erodes rocks and carries dust and sand, building dunes such as those in the Kalahari and Namib.

Lithosphere and hydrosphere: rain and rivers weather and erode rocks, carrying sediment to the sea, where it settles and may form new rock. Groundwater dissolves minerals from rocks, forming caves such as the Sterkfontein and Cango Caves in limestone.

Hydrosphere and atmosphere: water evaporates from oceans and dams into the air, and returns as rain, hail or snow. Oceans absorb heat and carbon dioxide from the air and influence weather. The warm Agulhas Current off the east coast and the cold Benguela Current off the west coast help explain why the east coast is humid and wet and the west coast is dry.

Biosphere and atmosphere: plants take in carbon dioxide and release oxygen during photosynthesis; animals and plants take in oxygen and release carbon dioxide during respiration.

Biosphere and lithosphere: plant roots break up rocks and help form soil; dead organisms decompose and add nutrients to the soil; coral reefs build limestone; and fossil fuels such as coal formed from the remains of ancient plants.

Biosphere and hydrosphere: organisms live in water, take it in and release it. Plants draw water from the soil and release it as water vapour from their leaves in transpiration.

Often, a single event involves all four spheres. A thunderstorm, for example, forms when the Sun heats the ground, warm moist air rises, water vapour condenses into clouds, rain falls, soil is washed away, and plants and animals receive water.

## Subtopic: Cycles that Link the Spheres

Some interactions form cycles, in which matter moves from one sphere to another and eventually returns. Matter is not created or destroyed, but it changes place and form.

The water cycle links all four spheres. Energy from the Sun evaporates water from the oceans, rivers and dams, and plants release water vapour by transpiration. The water vapour rises, cools and condenses into tiny droplets that form clouds. The droplets join and fall as precipitation: rain, hail or snow. On land, water runs off into rivers, soaks into the soil and rocks as groundwater, or is taken up by living things, and eventually returns to the sea or the air.

The carbon cycle also links all four spheres. Carbon dioxide in the atmosphere is taken in by plants during photosynthesis and built into sugars. Animals eat plants and use the carbon in their bodies. Respiration by plants, animals and decomposers returns carbon dioxide to the air. Some carbon is stored for very long times: in the shells of sea animals that become limestone, in the oceans, and in coal, oil and natural gas formed from organisms buried millions of years ago. Volcanoes return some carbon from deep in the Earth. When people burn fossil fuels, carbon that was locked away in the lithosphere for millions of years is released into the atmosphere quickly.

Energy also flows through the Earth system. The Sun is the main source of energy. Sunlight warms the land and oceans, drives the water cycle and the winds, and is captured by plants in photosynthesis. Some energy also comes from inside the Earth, as heat that drives volcanoes and the slow movement of the plates of the lithosphere. Unlike matter, energy is not recycled: most of it is eventually radiated back into space as heat.

Rocks also take part in a very slow cycle, the rock cycle, in which rocks are weathered, eroded, buried, heated and squeezed, and sometimes melted and brought back to the surface. You will study it in the next lessons. It reminds us that even the solid lithosphere is constantly, if slowly, changing and exchanging matter with the other spheres.

## Subtopic: Human Impacts and the Error Museum

People are part of the biosphere, but our activities now affect all the other spheres. Mining and building change the lithosphere and produce large amounts of waste rock. Farming can cause soil erosion, which is a serious problem in parts of the Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal. Factories, vehicles and coal-fired power stations release carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide and other pollutants into the atmosphere, changing its composition and contributing to acid rain and global warming. Dams, irrigation and pollution change rivers and wetlands, and plastic waste reaches the oceans. Clearing forests and grasslands for farms and cities destroys habitats and reduces biodiversity.

Because the spheres are linked, one human action can cause a chain of effects. Clearing vegetation from a slope exposes the soil, so rain erodes it more quickly; the soil washes into rivers, making the water muddy and filling dams with silt; less vegetation means less carbon dioxide taken from the air and less habitat for animals.

Not all of the effects are local. Gases released in South Africa mix through the whole atmosphere within months, and plastic dropped in a Durban stormwater drain can travel across the Indian Ocean. In the same way, changes far away affect us: warming of the global oceans changes rainfall patterns over southern Africa.

Understanding the Earth as a system helps us to manage it better: for example by protecting wetlands, which store water, filter pollution and provide habitats, or by planting vegetation to hold soil in place.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: the lithosphere is only the soil; it is the rigid outer layer of rock, including the crust and upper mantle. Two: most of the Earth's water is fresh water; about 97 per cent is salt water in the oceans. Three: the atmosphere is mostly oxygen; it is mostly nitrogen, about 78 per cent. Four: the spheres work separately; they constantly interact and exchange matter and energy. Five: the biosphere is only animals; it includes all living things, including plants and micro-organisms, and the places they live.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through four spheres on one planet, spheres working together, and people as part of the system: plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Four Spheres, One Planet

Think of the Earth as having four big parts, called spheres.

Rock: the lithosphere. The hard outer shell of the Earth, made of rocks, soil and the top of the mantle underneath.

Water: the hydrosphere. Oceans, rivers, dams, ice, groundwater and even the water vapour in the air. Most of it, about 97 per cent, is salty sea water.

Air: the atmosphere. The blanket of gas around us, mostly nitrogen with about a fifth oxygen.

Life: the biosphere. Every living thing, from the tiniest bacteria to baobab trees and elephants, plus the places they live.

An easy way to remember them: litho means stone, hydro means water, atmo means vapour or air, and bio means life.

And together these four parts make a system: parts that work together and affect one another.

## Subtopic: Spheres Working Together

The spheres are always swapping things with each other.

Water evaporates from the sea into the air, forms clouds and falls as rain on the land. That is hydrosphere, atmosphere and lithosphere working together.

Plants take carbon dioxide from the air and give out oxygen. That is biosphere and atmosphere.

Rain washes soil down rivers to the sea. That is hydrosphere and lithosphere.

Roots crack rocks, and dead leaves rot into the soil. That is biosphere and lithosphere.

A volcano throws ash and gas into the air. That is lithosphere and atmosphere.

Water and carbon go round and round in cycles. They are never used up; they just move from one sphere to another.

And the Sun powers it all, heating the land and sea, making winds blow and helping plants grow.

Here is one drop of water's journey. It leaves the Indian Ocean as vapour, rides a cloud over KwaZulu-Natal, falls as rain on a hillside, is sucked up by a tree's roots, escapes from a leaf into the air, and falls again as rain into the Tugela River, which carries it back to the sea.

## Subtopic: People Are Part of the System

We are part of the biosphere, and what we do changes the other spheres.

Mining digs into the lithosphere. Burning coal puts carbon dioxide and other gases into the atmosphere. Pollution and dams change the hydrosphere. Clearing land takes away homes from plants and animals.

Because everything is linked, one change sets off others. Cut down the plants on a hill, and rain washes the soil away into the river. The river turns brown, the dam fills with mud, and animals lose their homes.

And it works the other way too. Changes in the other spheres affect people: droughts, floods and storms all come from the system we live in.

But we can also help: by planting trees and grass, protecting wetlands, and using less energy.

Water and carbon cycle between them, driven mainly by energy from the Sun. People are part of the biosphere and affect all the spheres.
