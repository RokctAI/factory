Climbers on Mount Everest carry bottles of oxygen, yet the air at the top of the mountain contains exactly the same mix of gases as the air on the beach in Durban: about 78 per cent nitrogen and 21 per cent oxygen. So why do climbers struggle to breathe up there? Because the air is much thinner. At the summit, each breath holds only about a third as much air as a breath at sea level. Even in Johannesburg, about 1 750 metres above sea level, the air is thinner than at the coast, which is why athletes from the Highveld have an advantage when they race at sea level. Today we find out what air is made of, why each gas matters, and how the density and pressure of the air decrease as you go higher. We also learn why the atmosphere has no sharp edge but slowly fades away into space.
