# Part 1 — Expert

This session describes the structure of the atmosphere. Scientists divide the atmosphere into four layers according to how the temperature changes with height: the troposphere, the stratosphere, the mesosphere and the thermosphere. The anchor facts are that in the troposphere, from the ground to about 12 kilometres, temperature decreases with height by about 6,5 °C per kilometre and almost all weather happens; that in the stratosphere, from about 12 to 50 kilometres, temperature increases with height because the ozone layer absorbs ultraviolet radiation from the Sun; that in the mesosphere, from about 50 to 85 kilometres, temperature decreases again to about −90 °C, the coldest part of the atmosphere, and meteors burn up; and that in the thermosphere, above about 85 kilometres, the very thin gas absorbs high-energy radiation from the Sun and its temperature rises to over 1 000 °C. The boundaries between the layers are the tropopause, the stratopause and the mesopause.

## Subtopic: The Troposphere

Scientists could have divided the atmosphere in many ways. They chose temperature, because the way the temperature changes with height reveals what is heating the air. A graph of temperature against altitude zig-zags: down, up, down, up. Each straight-ish section is one layer, and the turning points, where the temperature stops falling and starts rising or the other way round, are the boundaries. Each boundary is named after the layer below it, with the ending -pause, meaning a stop.

The troposphere is the lowest layer, the one we live in. Its name comes from a Greek word meaning turning or mixing. It reaches from the ground to about 12 kilometres on average: about 8 kilometres over the poles and 16 to 18 kilometres over the equator, where the warm air rises higher.

The troposphere is heated mainly from below. Sunlight passes through the air and warms the ground and the oceans, which then warm the air touching them. So the air is warmest at the bottom, and its temperature decreases with height by about 6,5 °C for every kilometre. If it is 15 °C at sea level, it will be about −50 to −60 °C at the top of the troposphere. This is why mountain tops such as those of the Drakensberg are colder than the valleys below, and often covered with snow in winter.

Because warm air near the ground rises and cool air sinks, the troposphere is constantly mixed by moving air. It contains about three quarters of the mass of the atmosphere and almost all of its water vapour. Clouds, rain, hail, snow, wind, thunderstorms and cyclones all form here. Almost all weather happens in the troposphere.

The rate of cooling with height explains a lot about South African weather. On the same winter morning, it may be 15 °C in Durban at sea level while Johannesburg, about 1 750 metres higher, wakes to frost. Air that is pushed up over a mountain range, such as the Drakensberg or the mountains of the Western Cape, cools as it rises. Cool air cannot hold as much water vapour, so the vapour condenses into clouds and falls as rain on the side facing the wind. This is why the eastern slopes of the escarpment are green and wet, while the land behind the mountains, in their rain shadow, is drier.

The boundary at the top of the troposphere is the tropopause. Here the temperature stops falling. The tropopause acts like a lid: rising storm clouds spread out sideways when they reach it, giving thunderclouds their flat, anvil-shaped tops.

## Subtopic: The Stratosphere and the Ozone Layer

Above the tropopause is the stratosphere, from about 12 kilometres to about 50 kilometres. Its name means layered, because the air here is calm and stable and does not mix up and down the way air in the troposphere does.

In the stratosphere, the temperature increases with height, from about −55 °C at the bottom to about 0 °C at the top, at the stratopause. The air is warmed from above, not from below. The cause is the ozone layer.

Ozone, O₃, is a molecule made of three oxygen atoms. It is found throughout the stratosphere, but is most concentrated at about 15 to 35 kilometres. Ozone absorbs most of the ultraviolet, or UV, radiation coming from the Sun. When it absorbs this energy, the ozone and the surrounding air warm up. Because more UV is absorbed near the top of the stratosphere, where the sunlight first arrives, the air there is warmer.

The ozone layer is vital for life. UV radiation damages living cells and their DNA. It causes sunburn, skin cancer and eye damage called cataracts in people, and harms plants and the tiny plankton at the base of ocean food chains. Without the ozone layer, life on land would hardly be possible.

In the 1980s, scientists discovered that the ozone layer over Antarctica was becoming much thinner every spring, forming what is called the ozone hole. The cause was human-made chemicals called CFCs, chlorofluorocarbons, which were used in fridges, air conditioners and aerosol spray cans. In 1987, countries signed the Montreal Protocol, an agreement to stop making these chemicals. South Africa is one of the countries that signed it. It has been very successful: the ozone layer is slowly recovering and is expected to return to its 1980 levels over Antarctica around 2066.

The amount of UV that reaches the ground depends on the thickness of the ozone layer, the height of the Sun in the sky, cloud cover and altitude. Weather forecasters report it as the UV index. South Africa, with its sunny climate and high plateau, often has a very high or extreme UV index in summer, which is why sunscreen, hats and shade are important, and why South Africa has one of the highest rates of skin cancer in the world.

Because the stratosphere is calm and above most clouds, long-distance passenger jets often cruise in its lowest part. Weather balloons rise into it before they burst.

## Subtopic: The Mesosphere and Thermosphere

Above the stratopause is the mesosphere, meaning middle sphere, from about 50 to about 85 kilometres. There is very little ozone here to absorb sunlight, so the temperature decreases with height again. At the mesopause, at the top of the mesosphere, it falls to about −90 °C. This is the coldest place in the whole atmosphere.

The air in the mesosphere is very thin, but there is still enough of it to slow down rocks from space. Every day, millions of tiny meteoroids, most of them no bigger than grains of sand, enter the atmosphere at tens of kilometres per second. Friction and compression of the air in front of them heat them until they glow and burn up, mostly in the mesosphere. We see these streaks of light as meteors, or shooting stars. The mesosphere protects us from most of these space rocks.

The mesosphere is too high for aeroplanes and weather balloons and too low for satellites, so it is the least explored layer. Scientists study it with rockets that fly up and fall back.

Above the mesopause is the thermosphere, meaning heat sphere, which reaches from about 85 kilometres up to about 500 to 1 000 kilometres, depending on the activity of the Sun. The gas here is extremely thin. Its few particles absorb the most energetic X-rays and ultraviolet rays from the Sun, and they become very hot: the temperature rises to over 1 000 °C, and up to about 2 000 °C when the Sun is very active.

Strangely, the thermosphere would not feel hot. Temperature measures how fast the particles move, but the particles are so far apart that very few would hit your skin, so very little heat would be transferred. A thermometer there would actually lose heat and feel cold.

Part of the thermosphere, together with the upper mesosphere, is called the ionosphere, because the Sun's radiation knocks electrons off atoms there, forming charged particles called ions. The ionosphere reflects some radio waves back to the ground, which allows radio signals to travel around the curve of the Earth. Solar storms can disturb it and interfere with radio communication and GPS signals.

The International Space Station orbits in the thermosphere, at about 400 kilometres. The beautiful auroras, the southern lights, also form here when charged particles from the Sun collide with gas particles and make them glow. Above the thermosphere, the atmosphere fades into the exosphere and then into space.

## Subtopic: The Temperature Profile and the Error Museum

The four layers can be summarised as a pattern: down, up, down, up. Troposphere: temperature decreases, heated from the ground below. Stratosphere: temperature increases, heated by ozone absorbing UV. Mesosphere: temperature decreases, little to absorb sunlight. Thermosphere: temperature increases, thin gas absorbs X-rays and UV.

A worked example uses the troposphere's rate of cooling. If the temperature at sea level in Durban is 25 °C, what is the temperature 3 kilometres above the city? The temperature falls about 6,5 °C for each kilometre, so in 3 kilometres it falls about 3 × 6,5 = 19,5 °C, giving 25 − 19,5 = 5,5 °C. This is why pilots and mountaineers dress warmly even in summer.

When you draw the temperature profile, put temperature on the horizontal axis and altitude on the vertical axis, so that height on the page matches height in the sky. Mark the three boundaries with dashed horizontal lines at about 12, 50 and 85 kilometres, and label each layer between them. The line slopes to the left, towards colder temperatures, in the troposphere and mesosphere, and to the right, towards warmer temperatures, in the stratosphere and thermosphere.

Remember that air pressure and density do not zig-zag. They decrease steadily with height through all four layers. Only the temperature changes direction.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: the higher you go, the colder it always gets; temperature falls in the troposphere and mesosphere but rises in the stratosphere and thermosphere. Two: the ozone layer is in the troposphere; it is in the stratosphere, mostly between about 15 and 35 kilometres. Three: the air gets warmer near the Sun because you are closer to the Sun; the distance is tiny compared with the 150 million kilometres to the Sun, and the warming is caused by gases absorbing radiation. Four: weather happens in every layer; almost all weather happens in the troposphere. Five: the thermosphere would burn you because it is over 1 000 °C; its gas is so thin that it would transfer very little heat.

Layout for an atmosphere layer answer: name the layer, give its approximate height range, state whether temperature increases or decreases with height, explain the cause, and give one feature or event that happens there.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through weather world, the sunscreen layer, and shooting stars and space stations: plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Weather World

The atmosphere has four layers. We sort them by how the temperature changes as you go up.

The bottom layer is the troposphere, from the ground up to about 12 kilometres. We live here.

The Sun warms the ground, and the ground warms the air, like a stove warming a pot from the bottom. So the higher you go in this layer, the colder it gets: about 6,5 °C colder for every kilometre. That is why mountain tops have snow.

Warm air rises and cool air sinks, so the air is always moving and mixing. Nearly all the clouds, rain, wind and storms are here. That is why we call it weather world.

At the top is the tropopause, a lid where the temperature stops dropping. Big storm clouds flatten out against it.

## Subtopic: The Sunscreen Layer

Next is the stratosphere, from about 12 to 50 kilometres.

Here something strange happens: it gets warmer as you go up.

The reason is ozone. Ozone soaks up harmful ultraviolet light from the Sun, like sunscreen for the whole planet. When it soaks up that energy, the air warms.

Without the ozone layer, we would get bad sunburn, more skin cancer and eye damage.

Chemicals called CFCs, once used in fridges and spray cans, made a hole in the ozone layer over Antarctica. Countries agreed to ban them in 1987, and the ozone layer is slowly healing.

The air here is calm, so long-distance jets often fly in its lowest part.

Keep the sunscreen layer in mind and try this part's questions: ozone and the second layer.

## Subtopic: Shooting Stars and Space Stations

Third is the mesosphere, from about 50 to 85 kilometres. It gets colder again, down to about −90 °C, the coldest place in the atmosphere.

Space rocks smaller than sand grains hit the air here so fast that they burn up. We see them as shooting stars.

Last is the thermosphere, above about 85 kilometres. The few gas particles here soak up powerful rays from the Sun and get very hot, over 1 000 °C. But there are so few particles that it would not feel hot.

The space station flies here, about 400 kilometres up. Southern lights glow here too.

Easy way to remember the pattern: down, up, down, up.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The four layers, from the bottom: troposphere, where temperature falls and weather happens; stratosphere, where temperature rises because ozone absorbs UV; mesosphere, where temperature falls to about −90 °C and meteors burn up; and thermosphere, where very thin gas heats to over 1 000 °C. Pressure and density decrease steadily all the way up. In the next lesson you will see how gases in the lowest layer trap heat in the greenhouse effect.
