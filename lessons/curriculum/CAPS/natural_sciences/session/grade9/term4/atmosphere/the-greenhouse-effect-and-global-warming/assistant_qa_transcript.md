### subtopic_1 — How the Greenhouse Effect Works
**Assistant:** Why do greenhouse gases absorb infrared radiation but nitrogen and oxygen do not?

**Tutor:** It comes down to the shape of the molecules and how they can vibrate. Infrared radiation is absorbed when it makes the atoms in a molecule vibrate, stretching and bending the bonds between them, but only if the vibration changes how the electric charge is spread across the molecule. Nitrogen, N₂, and oxygen, O₂, each consist of two identical atoms. When they vibrate, the two atoms simply move towards and away from each other, and the charge stays evenly balanced, so they cannot absorb infrared radiation. Molecules with three or more atoms, or with different kinds of atoms, can vibrate in ways that make one end slightly more positive or negative than the other. Carbon dioxide, a straight molecule of one carbon atom between two oxygen atoms, can bend and stretch unevenly. Water, methane and nitrous oxide behave similarly. These vibrations match the wavelengths of the infrared radiation given off by the warm Earth, so the molecules absorb it, vibrate more strongly, bump into neighbouring molecules and warm the air, and then emit infrared radiation in all directions. This is why gases making up less than one per cent of the air have such a large effect on temperature.

### subtopic_2 — The Greenhouse Gases
**Assistant:** If water vapour is the biggest greenhouse gas, why do scientists focus on carbon dioxide?

**Tutor:** Because carbon dioxide controls the temperature, while water vapour mainly follows it. The amount of water vapour in the air is set by temperature. If extra water vapour is added, for example by irrigating fields or by a power station's cooling towers, it rains out within about ten days, and the air returns to its usual humidity. People cannot directly change the global amount of water vapour for long. Carbon dioxide is different. It does not condense or rain out, and once added, a large part of it remains in the atmosphere for centuries, so it keeps building up as long as we keep releasing it. When carbon dioxide warms the air a little, the warmer air can hold more water vapour, about 7 per cent more for every 1 °C, and that extra water vapour traps even more heat. So carbon dioxide acts like the control knob, and water vapour amplifies its effect, roughly doubling the warming. Scientists have shown this using computer models and records of past climates, which show temperature and carbon dioxide rising and falling together. Reducing carbon dioxide emissions is therefore the main way to limit warming.

### subtopic_3 — Global Warming and Climate Change
**Assistant:** How can scientists be sure the warming is caused by people and not by the Sun?

**Tutor:** Several separate lines of evidence point to human-made greenhouse gases. First, the Sun's energy output has been measured accurately by satellites since the late 1970s, and it has not increased; it has even decreased slightly, while the Earth has warmed fastest. Second, the pattern of warming matches greenhouse gases, not the Sun. If the Sun were brighter, the whole atmosphere would warm, including the stratosphere. Instead, the troposphere is warming while the stratosphere is cooling, which is exactly what is expected when greenhouse gases trap heat lower down. Nights are also warming faster than days, again fitting a blanket rather than a stronger heater. Third, the extra carbon dioxide carries a chemical fingerprint. Carbon from ancient plants in fossil fuels contains a different mix of carbon atoms, called isotopes, from the carbon in volcanic gas or the oceans, and the changing mix in the air shows that the extra carbon comes from burning fossil fuels. Finally, scientists understand the physics of how carbon dioxide absorbs infrared radiation, and satellites measure less infrared escaping to space at exactly the wavelengths that carbon dioxide absorbs. Natural factors alone cannot explain the warming.

### subtopic_4 — Reducing Emissions and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why does South Africa still rely so much on coal, and can it really switch to renewables?

**Tutor:** South Africa has very large coal deposits, mostly in Mpumalanga and Limpopo, that are cheap to mine, so in the twentieth century the country built large coal power stations near the coalfields, and an economy, including mining, steel and smelters, that uses a lot of electricity. Coal still generates most of the country's electricity, and many jobs and towns depend on it. However, South Africa also has some of the best solar resources in the world, especially in the Northern Cape, and strong winds along the coasts and in the Karoo. Since 2011, the government's renewable energy programme has added many solar and wind farms, and after years of load shedding, households and businesses have installed large amounts of rooftop solar. The cost of solar and wind electricity has fallen sharply and is now among the cheapest sources of new power. The challenge is that the Sun does not always shine and the wind does not always blow, so the grid also needs batteries, pumped storage schemes, other backup and stronger transmission lines. The plan is a gradual change, called a just energy transition, with new jobs and training for coal workers and communities so that people are not left behind.
