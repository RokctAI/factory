# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains the greenhouse effect, the greenhouse gases and global warming. The anchor facts are that sunlight passes through the atmosphere and warms the Earth's surface, which then gives off infrared radiation; that greenhouse gases, mainly water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide, absorb some of this infrared radiation and send part of it back towards the surface, keeping the Earth about 33 °C warmer than it would otherwise be, at an average of about 15 °C instead of about −18 °C; that human activities, especially burning coal, oil and natural gas, clearing forests and farming, have increased the amount of greenhouse gases, raising carbon dioxide from about 280 to about 420 parts per million; that this enhanced greenhouse effect is causing global warming, so the Earth's average temperature has risen by about 1,2 °C since the late 1800s; and that global warming is changing climates, with serious effects for South Africa, which can be reduced by cutting emissions.

## Subtopic: How the Greenhouse Effect Works

The Sun is very hot, so it gives off mainly visible light, with some ultraviolet and infrared radiation. When this sunlight reaches the Earth, about 30 per cent is reflected straight back into space by clouds, ice, snow and bright surfaces. The rest is absorbed, mostly by the ground and the oceans, which warm up.

Every warm object gives off energy as infrared radiation, which we cannot see but can feel as heat, like the warmth you feel from a stove plate before it glows. The Earth's surface is much cooler than the Sun, so it gives off almost all its energy as infrared radiation, sending it up towards space.

The gases nitrogen and oxygen, which make up most of the air, let both sunlight and infrared radiation pass through. But some gases, present in small amounts, let sunlight through while absorbing infrared radiation. These are called greenhouse gases. When they absorb the infrared radiation, they warm up and give off infrared radiation in all directions, some out to space and some back down to the surface. This extra energy coming back down keeps the surface and the lower atmosphere warmer. This process is called the greenhouse effect.

The name comes from greenhouses, the glass buildings in which farmers and gardeners grow plants. Sunlight passes through the glass and warms the inside, and the warm air is trapped. A real greenhouse works mainly by stopping warm air from escaping, so the comparison is not exact, but the name has stuck. A better comparison is a blanket: it does not make heat, but it slows down the loss of heat from your body.

Without the natural greenhouse effect, the Earth's average surface temperature would be about −18 °C, and most of the water would be frozen. With it, the average is about 15 °C. The difference, 15 − (−18) = 33 °C, is what makes the Earth habitable. The greenhouse effect is natural and essential.

Our neighbouring planets show how powerful the effect can be. Venus has a thick atmosphere that is about 96 per cent carbon dioxide, and its surface is about 465 °C, hotter than Mercury even though Mercury is closer to the Sun. Mars has a very thin atmosphere with little greenhouse warming, and its average temperature is about −60 °C. The Earth, with a modest amount of greenhouse gas, sits in between, at a temperature where liquid water and life can exist.

## Subtopic: The Greenhouse Gases

The main greenhouse gases are water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide and ozone, together with some human-made gases such as CFCs.

Water vapour, H₂O, is the most abundant greenhouse gas and causes the largest part of the natural greenhouse effect. However, its amount is controlled by temperature: warm air holds more water vapour, and extra water vapour falls out as rain within days. So water vapour responds to warming rather than starting it. When other gases warm the planet, more water evaporates, which then adds more warming. This is called a feedback.

Carbon dioxide, CO₂, is the most important greenhouse gas added by people. It is released when fossil fuels, coal, oil and natural gas, and wood are burned, and when forests are cleared. It stays in the atmosphere for hundreds of years, so it builds up. Its natural sources include respiration, decay and volcanoes, and it is removed by photosynthesis and by dissolving in the oceans.

Methane, CH₄, is released by wetlands, by cattle, sheep and goats as they digest grass, by rice paddies, by rotting rubbish in landfills, and by leaks from coal mines and gas wells. Molecule for molecule, it traps much more heat than carbon dioxide, about 80 times more over 20 years, but it breaks down in about a decade.

Nitrous oxide, N₂O, comes mainly from nitrogen fertilisers on farms, and from burning fuels.

CFCs, the chemicals that damaged the ozone layer, are also very powerful greenhouse gases. Banning them under the Montreal Protocol has also helped to limit warming.

The natural amounts of greenhouse gases are kept in balance by the carbon cycle, which you met earlier in this term. Plants and the oceans take carbon dioxide out of the air, and respiration, decay and volcanoes put it back. For thousands of years before industry, these flows were roughly equal, so the concentration stayed close to 280 parts per million. Burning fossil fuels adds carbon faster than plants and the oceans can remove it: they absorb roughly half of what we release each year, and the rest builds up in the air. The carbon dioxide that dissolves in the oceans makes seawater slightly more acidic, which harms shellfish and corals.

## Subtopic: Global Warming and Climate Change

Since the Industrial Revolution, which began in the late 1700s, people have burned enormous amounts of coal, oil and natural gas. These fuels formed from plants and organisms that lived millions of years ago, so burning them releases carbon that had been locked away underground for millions of years. Together with clearing forests and farming, this has raised the concentration of carbon dioxide in the air from about 280 parts per million to about 420 parts per million, an increase of 50 per cent, and the amount of methane has more than doubled.

More greenhouse gas means that more infrared radiation is absorbed and returned to the surface. This strengthening of the natural effect is called the enhanced greenhouse effect. The result is global warming: a rise in the average temperature of the Earth's surface and lower atmosphere. The world is now about 1,2 °C warmer than it was in the late 1800s, and each of the last several decades has been warmer than the one before.

Global warming is one part of climate change: long-term changes in temperature, rainfall, winds and extreme weather. Weather is the condition of the atmosphere on one day; climate is the average pattern over 30 years or more. A single cold winter does not disprove global warming, just as one hot day does not prove it.

The effects are already being felt. Glaciers and ice sheets are melting, and the sea is expanding as it warms, so sea level is rising and threatening coastal cities such as Durban and Cape Town. Heat waves are more frequent and intense. In South Africa, the interior is warming faster than the global average. Droughts are expected to become more frequent and severe in the west and south, as in the Cape Town drought of 2015 to 2018, when the city came close to Day Zero, the day the taps would be turned off. At the same time, more intense storms and floods are expected, such as the devastating KwaZulu-Natal floods of April 2022. Crops such as maize may suffer, coral reefs bleach in warmer seas, and species in the fynbos and Succulent Karoo may lose the areas where they can survive.

Some effects of warming cause even more warming. White ice and snow reflect most sunlight, but when they melt they expose darker land or sea, which absorbs more sunlight and warms further. Thawing frozen soil in the Arctic, called permafrost, releases stored carbon dioxide and methane. These feedbacks are one reason why scientists urge that warming be limited as soon as possible.

## Subtopic: Reducing Emissions and the Error Museum

South Africa produces most of its electricity in coal-fired power stations, so it emits more carbon dioxide per person than most countries in Africa, and is one of the world's larger emitters overall. Reducing emissions is called mitigation. Ways to do this include generating electricity from renewable sources such as solar and wind power, which South Africa has been building in large numbers in the Northern Cape and Eastern Cape; using energy efficiently, for example with LED lights and insulated ceilings; solar water heaters; public transport, walking and cycling; reducing waste and methane from landfills; and protecting and planting forests and grasslands, which absorb carbon dioxide.

Because some warming is already unavoidable, people must also adapt, for example by saving water, growing drought-resistant crops, and building away from flood zones. In the Paris Agreement of 2015, almost every country, including South Africa, agreed to try to keep warming well below 2 °C, and preferably to 1,5 °C.

A worked example: a household replaces a 2 000-watt electric geyser element, used for 3 hours a day, with a solar water heater. It saves about 2 kW × 3 h = 6 kWh per day. In South Africa, generating 1 kWh from coal releases roughly 1 kilogram of carbon dioxide, so the household avoids about 6 kilograms a day, or about 6 × 365 = 2 190 kilograms, more than 2 tonnes, in a year.

Everyone has a carbon footprint, the total amount of greenhouse gas released by the things they do and use. Because South African electricity comes mostly from coal, simple habits such as switching off lights and appliances at the wall, boiling only as much water as you need, and drying clothes in the sun instead of a tumble dryer reduce both the electricity bill and the carbon footprint.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: the greenhouse effect is bad and caused by people; the natural greenhouse effect keeps the Earth about 33 °C warmer and is essential for life; it is the enhanced effect that causes global warming. Two: global warming is caused by the hole in the ozone layer; they are different problems: the ozone hole lets in more UV, while global warming is caused by extra greenhouse gases trapping infrared radiation. Three: greenhouse gases trap sunlight; they let sunlight through and absorb infrared radiation given off by the Earth. Four: a cold winter proves global warming is not happening; climate is the long-term average, not one season. Five: carbon dioxide is a pollutant with no natural role; it is essential for photosynthesis, and the problem is the extra amount.

## Subtopic: The Planet's Blanket

Sunlight shines through the air and warms the ground and the sea.

The warm ground then gives off heat as invisible infrared rays, heading back up to space.

Some gases in the air, called greenhouse gases, catch some of that heat and send part of it back down. It is like a blanket: it does not make heat, but it stops heat escaping so quickly.

This is the greenhouse effect. It is natural and good. Without it, the Earth would be about −18 °C, frozen. With it, the Earth is about 15 °C on average.

A closed car in the sun is a small example: light gets in easily, but the heat cannot get out as easily.

## Subtopic: The Heat-Trapping Gases

Water vapour: the biggest natural blanket gas.

Carbon dioxide: from burning coal, oil, gas and wood, and cutting down forests. It stays in the air for hundreds of years.

Methane: from cows and sheep burping, rubbish dumps, wetlands and coal mines. It traps a lot of heat.

Nitrous oxide: from fertilisers.

Nitrogen and oxygen, the main gases in air, do not trap heat.

People have been adding these gases faster and faster. Carbon dioxide has gone up by half since the 1800s.

## Subtopic: A Warmer World

More blanket gases means a thicker blanket, so the Earth warms. This is global warming. The Earth is about 1,2 °C warmer than in the 1800s.

That might sound small, but it is changing the climate. Ice is melting, and the sea is rising. Heat waves, droughts and floods are getting worse. Cape Town nearly ran out of water. Floods hit KwaZulu-Natal in 2022.

We can help by using solar and wind power, saving electricity, using solar water heaters, walking or taking public transport, wasting less and planting trees.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Sunlight warms the surface; the surface gives off infrared radiation; greenhouse gases absorb and return part of it, keeping the Earth about 33 °C warmer. People have added extra carbon dioxide and methane, mainly by burning fossil fuels, causing the enhanced greenhouse effect and global warming of about 1,2 °C, with serious effects for South Africa. Cutting emissions and adapting can reduce the harm. In the next lesson you will leave the Earth behind and find out how stars, like the Sun, are born, live and die.
