# Part 1 — Expert

This session introduces the atmosphere, the layer of gases that surrounds the Earth and is held in place by gravity. It describes the composition of dry air, explains the role of each of the main gases, and shows how the density and pressure of the air decrease with altitude. The anchor facts are that dry air is about 78 per cent nitrogen, 21 per cent oxygen and just under 1 per cent argon, with about 0,04 per cent carbon dioxide and traces of other gases; that air also contains a variable amount of water vapour, plus dust and other particles; that gravity pulls the air towards the Earth, so most of the air is packed close to the surface; that air pressure and density decrease as altitude increases, roughly halving for every 5,5 kilometres of height; and that about three quarters of the mass of the atmosphere lies in its lowest layer, below about 12 kilometres.

## Subtopic: The Gases in Air

The atmosphere is one of the four spheres of the Earth system. It is a mixture of gases, not a compound: the gases are not chemically joined together, and each keeps its own properties. Because air is a mixture, its gases can be separated, for example by cooling air until it becomes liquid and then letting the different gases boil off at their different boiling points. This is how pure oxygen for hospitals and pure nitrogen for industry are produced.

Scientists usually describe dry air, because the amount of water vapour changes from place to place and from day to day. By volume, dry air contains about 78 per cent nitrogen, 21 per cent oxygen, 0,9 per cent argon and about 0,04 per cent carbon dioxide. The remaining tiny fraction is made up of other gases such as neon, helium, methane, krypton, hydrogen and ozone.

It helps to imagine 100 balloons full of dry air, with the gases sorted out. About 78 balloons would hold nitrogen, 21 would hold oxygen, and just under 1 would hold argon. All the carbon dioxide would fit into less than one twentieth of one balloon.

Water vapour is the invisible gas form of water. Over a hot, humid area such as the KwaZulu-Natal coast in summer, it can make up about 3 to 4 per cent of the air, but over a dry desert such as the Kalahari, or in very cold air, it can be close to zero.

Air also carries tiny solid and liquid particles called aerosols: dust, smoke, pollen, sea salt, ash from volcanoes and droplets of pollution. Although they are small, these particles matter, because water vapour condenses onto them to form cloud droplets.

The atmosphere has not always had this composition. The early Earth's atmosphere, more than 3 billion years ago, contained almost no oxygen. It was made mainly of gases released by volcanoes, such as carbon dioxide, water vapour and nitrogen. Oxygen built up slowly only after tiny photosynthesising organisms, called cyanobacteria, appeared in the oceans and released oxygen as a waste product. Evidence of this change is preserved in South African rocks: banded iron formations in the Northern Cape, such as those mined at Sishen, formed when oxygen in the sea reacted with dissolved iron and settled as layers of iron oxide.

## Subtopic: Why Each Gas Matters

Each gas in the atmosphere plays a part in keeping the Earth habitable.

Nitrogen, N₂, is the most abundant gas but is very unreactive, so it dilutes the oxygen and makes fires burn less fiercely than they would in pure oxygen. Plants need nitrogen to make proteins, but they cannot use nitrogen gas directly. Bacteria in the soil and in the root nodules of legumes, such as beans, peas and rooibos, convert nitrogen gas into compounds that plants can absorb. Lightning also turns a small amount of nitrogen into nitrogen compounds that rain carries into the soil.

Oxygen, O₂, is needed by almost all living organisms for cellular respiration, which releases energy from food in the mitochondria. It is also needed for burning, which is a reaction with oxygen, as you learnt when you studied the reactions of metals and non-metals with oxygen. Almost all the oxygen in the air was produced by photosynthesis over billions of years, by plants, algae and microscopic plankton in the oceans.

Carbon dioxide, CO₂, makes up only about 0,04 per cent of the air, but it is essential. Plants use it in photosynthesis to make food, so all food chains depend on it. It is also a greenhouse gas that traps heat and keeps the Earth warm. Its concentration has risen from about 280 parts per million before the Industrial Revolution to about 420 parts per million today, mainly because of burning coal, oil and gas. That is an increase of about 50 per cent.

Argon is an unreactive noble gas, used to fill light bulbs and in welding to keep oxygen away from hot metal.

Methane, CH₄, is present in only tiny amounts, less than 2 parts per million, but it is a powerful greenhouse gas. It is released by wetlands, by cattle and sheep as they digest grass, by rubbish dumps and by coal mines.

Water vapour forms clouds and rain, carries heat around the planet and is the most abundant greenhouse gas.

Ozone, O₃, is a form of oxygen with three atoms in each molecule. It is found mainly high up, in the stratosphere, where it absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation from the Sun.

## Subtopic: Density and Pressure with Altitude

Air has mass. A cubic metre of air at sea level has a mass of about 1,2 kilograms, and the whole atmosphere has a mass of about 5 million billion tonnes. Gravity pulls this air towards the Earth, so the air presses down on everything beneath it. This push is called air pressure, or atmospheric pressure.

At sea level, the air pressure is about 101 kilopascals. This is the weight of the whole column of air above each square metre, reaching all the way up to space. We do not feel crushed, because the air inside our bodies pushes outwards with the same pressure.

Air is a gas, so it can be compressed. The air near the bottom of the atmosphere is squashed by the weight of all the air above it, so its particles are packed closely together. Higher up, there is less air above pressing down, so the particles spread further apart. This means that as altitude increases, both the air pressure and the density of the air decrease.

The decrease is not steady. Pressure and density roughly halve for every 5,5 kilometres you climb. In Johannesburg, at about 1 750 metres, the pressure is about 82 kilopascals, or roughly four fifths of the pressure at sea level. At 5,5 kilometres, it is about half. At the top of Mount Everest, 8 849 metres high, it is only about a third. Passenger jets cruise at about 11 kilometres, where the pressure is less than a quarter of that at sea level, so their cabins must be pressurised.

Because most of the air is squashed close to the ground, about half the mass of the atmosphere lies below 5,5 kilometres, about three quarters below 12 kilometres, and about 99 per cent below about 30 kilometres. There is no sharp top to the atmosphere. It becomes thinner and thinner and slowly fades into space. Scientists often use the Kármán line, 100 kilometres above sea level, as a convenient boundary where space begins.

Temperature also affects the density of air. When air is heated, its particles move faster and spread further apart, so warm air is less dense than cool air and rises. This is why hot-air balloons float, and why warm air rises from the ground on a sunny day to form the towering thunderclouds of a Highveld summer afternoon.

Air pressure can be measured with a barometer. A simple experiment shows that air presses on things: if you fill a glass with water, cover it with a piece of card and turn it upside down, the air pressure pushing up on the card holds the water in.

## Subtopic: Living with Thin Air and the Error Museum

Thin air affects living things. At high altitude, each breath contains fewer oxygen molecules, even though oxygen is still 21 per cent of the air. People who travel quickly to high altitude may suffer altitude sickness, with headaches, dizziness, tiredness and shortness of breath. Over a few weeks, the body adapts by making more red blood cells to carry oxygen. This is why some athletes train at high altitude, and why runners who train in places such as Dullstroom or Johannesburg often perform well at the coast.

Thin air also affects cooking. Water boils when its vapour pressure equals the air pressure. Because air pressure is lower in Johannesburg than in Cape Town, water boils at about 94 °C there instead of 100 °C, so food takes a little longer to cook. On Mount Everest, water boils at about 70 °C.

Sealed packets and bottles show the change in pressure. A sealed chip packet taken from the coast up to the Highveld, or up a mountain pass, puffs up, because the air inside is at the higher pressure of the coast while the air outside pushes less.

Your ears may pop when you drive up a steep pass or take off in an aeroplane. This happens because the air pressure outside drops, and air trapped behind your eardrum pushes outwards until air escapes through the tubes that connect your ears to your throat.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: air is mostly oxygen; it is mostly nitrogen, about 78 per cent, with only about 21 per cent oxygen. Two: there is less oxygen in the air on a mountain because the percentage of oxygen is lower; the percentage is the same, but the air is less dense, so each breath holds fewer molecules. Three: air has no mass or weight; a cubic metre of air at sea level has a mass of about 1,2 kilograms, and the weight of the air causes air pressure. Four: the atmosphere ends at a sharp edge; it fades gradually into space. Five: air is a compound; it is a mixture of gases that can be separated.

## Subtopic: What Is in the Air

Air is a mixture of gases. The gases are just mixed, not joined together.

Imagine 100 balloons of dry air. About 78 are nitrogen. About 21 are oxygen. Nearly 1 is argon. The carbon dioxide would fill less than one twentieth of one balloon.

Air also has water vapour, which is invisible water gas. Humid air at the coast has a lot. Dry desert air has almost none.

Air also carries dust, smoke, salt and pollen. Water vapour sticks to these tiny bits to make clouds.

Because air is a mixture, we can separate it. Factories cool air until it turns liquid, then collect the different gases one by one. That is where hospital oxygen comes from.

## Subtopic: Every Gas Has a Job

Nitrogen: the biggest part of the air. It makes fires burn more gently. Bacteria in the soil turn it into food for plants.

Oxygen: we breathe it to release energy from food. Fires need it to burn. Plants and tiny ocean plankton made almost all of it.

Carbon dioxide: a tiny amount, but plants need it to make food. It also traps heat and keeps the Earth warm. We have added a lot of it by burning coal, oil and gas.

Argon: a lazy gas that does not react. It is used in light bulbs and welding.

Water vapour: makes clouds and rain.

Ozone: high up in the sky, it blocks harmful ultraviolet light from the Sun, like sunscreen for the whole planet.

## Subtopic: Higher Means Thinner

Air has weight. Gravity pulls it down, so the air presses on everything. That is air pressure.

The air at the bottom is squashed by all the air on top, like the bottom person in a pile of rugby players. Higher up, there is less air on top, so the air spreads out and becomes thin.

Every 5,5 kilometres up, the air is about half as thick. In Johannesburg it is a bit thinner than at the coast. At the top of Mount Everest, it is only about a third.

Oxygen is still 21 per cent up there, but each breath has fewer molecules, so climbers get out of breath.

Water boils at a lower temperature high up: about 94 °C in Johannesburg. Chip packets puff up, and your ears pop.

Each gas has a role. Gravity holds the air down, so air pressure and density are greatest at the surface and decrease with altitude, roughly halving every 5,5 kilometres. The atmosphere has no sharp edge. In the next lesson you will see how the atmosphere is divided into four layers by the way temperature changes with height.
