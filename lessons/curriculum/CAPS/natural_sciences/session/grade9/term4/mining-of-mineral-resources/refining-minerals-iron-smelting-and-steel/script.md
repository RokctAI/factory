# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains how minerals are refined after mining, using physical and chemical processes; describes the iron smelting practised in southern Africa for well over a thousand years; and explains how iron is extracted from its ore in a modern blast furnace and turned into steel. The anchor facts are that refining separates a pure substance from the other materials in an ore, by physical processes such as crushing, magnetic separation, flotation and dissolving, or by chemical processes such as smelting and electrolysis; that iron ore contains iron oxides, such as haematite, Fe2O3, so iron must be extracted chemically by removing the oxygen; that early southern African smiths smelted iron in clay furnaces using charcoal and bellows; that in a blast furnace, carbon monoxide made from coke removes oxygen from iron oxide: Fe2O3 + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO2; that limestone removes impurities as slag; that blast furnace iron contains about 4 per cent carbon and is brittle; and that steel is made by blowing oxygen through molten iron to reduce the carbon content, and is often recycled from scrap in electric arc furnaces.

## Subtopic: Physical and Chemical Refining

After ore has been mined, crushed and concentrated, the valuable substance must still be separated from what remains. This is called refining. Refining can use physical processes, chemical processes, or both.

Physical processes separate substances without changing them into new substances. They use differences in physical properties such as size, density, magnetism, solubility and melting point. Examples are crushing and sieving to sort pieces by size; panning or shaking tables, in which water washes away lighter grains and leaves dense minerals like gold behind; magnetic separation, which pulls magnetic minerals such as magnetite away from non-magnetic ones; and froth flotation, in which air bubbles carry certain mineral grains to the top of a tank as a froth. Salt is refined physically by dissolving it in water, filtering out the sand and evaporating the water.

Chemical processes produce new substances. They are needed when the valuable metal is combined with other elements in a compound. Most metals in the crust are found as oxides, sulfides or carbonates, not as pure metals. To obtain the metal, a chemical reaction must remove the other elements.

Smelting is heating an ore, usually with carbon, to a high temperature so that the metal is released and melted. Carbon takes the oxygen away from the metal oxide; this is called reduction. Iron, copper, tin and lead are extracted by smelting.

Electrolysis uses electricity to split compounds. Aluminium is extracted by passing a large electric current through molten aluminium oxide. South Africa's large aluminium smelters near Richards Bay use huge amounts of electricity for this reason. Copper is also purified by electrolysis.

Gold is refined using both. Crushed gold ore is mixed with a dilute cyanide solution, which dissolves the gold; the gold is then recovered from the solution, melted into bars and sent to the Rand Refinery in Germiston, where it is refined to 99,99 per cent pure gold.

Whether a metal needs smelting or electrolysis depends on its reactivity. You met the reactivity series in Term 2: metals high in the series, such as sodium, magnesium and aluminium, hold on to oxygen very strongly, and carbon cannot remove it, so electrolysis is needed. Metals in the middle, such as zinc, iron, tin and lead, can be extracted by heating their oxides with carbon. The least reactive metals, such as gold and platinum, are often found uncombined, as the native metal, and only need to be separated physically or dissolved and recovered.

## Subtopic: Ancient Iron Smelting in South Africa

Iron-working farming communities spread into southern Africa about 1 700 to 1 800 years ago, bringing knowledge of how to smelt iron. Archaeologists have found remains of their furnaces, slag heaps and tools at many sites, including Melville Koppies in Johannesburg, Phalaborwa in Limpopo and Mapungubwe, which was also famous for its gold.

The smelting process went like this. First, iron ore was collected, often haematite or magnetite from surface outcrops, and broken into small pieces. Charcoal was made by slowly burning wood with little air. The furnace was a chimney-like structure of clay, about a metre or more high, sometimes built into a termite mound or a pit.

Layers of charcoal and ore were loaded into the furnace and the charcoal was lit. Air was pumped in through clay pipes called tuyères, using bellows made of goatskin or other animal skins, worked by hand for many hours. The extra air made the charcoal burn very hot, roughly 1 200 °C. As the charcoal burned in a limited supply of air, it produced carbon monoxide, which took oxygen away from the iron oxide, leaving iron.

The furnace was not hot enough to melt iron completely, which needs about 1 540 °C. Instead, a spongy lump of iron mixed with slag, called a bloom, formed at the bottom. The impurities melted and ran out as slag. After the furnace was broken open, smiths reheated the bloom and hammered it repeatedly on a stone anvil to squeeze out the slag and shape the iron into hoes, axes, spearheads, knives and ornaments.

Iron smelting was a highly skilled craft, often surrounded by ritual and knowledge passed down within families. Iron hoes made farming much more productive and were valuable trade items. The same basic chemistry, carbon removing oxygen from iron ore, is used in modern steelworks, only on an enormously larger scale.

Copper was also smelted in southern Africa, especially at Phalaborwa and in the Musina area of Limpopo, where copper ore was smelted and drawn into wire for bangles and ornaments. Gold from Mapungubwe was beaten into thin sheets to cover carved wooden objects, including the famous golden rhinoceros, made about 800 years ago.

## Subtopic: The Blast Furnace and Steel

A modern blast furnace is a tall steel tower, lined with heat-resistant bricks, that can be about 30 metres high and run day and night for years. Three raw materials are loaded in at the top: iron ore, mainly haematite, Fe2O3, from mines such as Sishen; coke, which is almost pure carbon made by heating coal without air; and limestone, calcium carbonate, CaCO3. Very hot air is blasted in near the bottom, which gives the furnace its name.

The reactions happen in stages. Coke burns in the hot air, giving carbon dioxide and a great deal of heat: C + O2 → CO2. The carbon dioxide reacts with more hot coke to form carbon monoxide: CO2 + C → 2CO. Carbon monoxide then reduces the iron oxide, removing its oxygen to leave iron: Fe2O3 + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO2.

At the bottom of the furnace, the temperature is about 1 500 °C or more, so the iron melts and collects as liquid at the base.

Limestone removes impurities. The heat decomposes it: CaCO3 → CaO + CO2. The calcium oxide reacts with sand, silicon dioxide, in the ore to form calcium silicate, called slag: CaO + SiO2 → CaSiO3. The molten slag is less dense than the iron, so it floats on top and is tapped off separately. Slag is used in cement and road building.

The iron tapped from the blast furnace is called pig iron. It contains about 4 per cent carbon and other impurities, which make it hard but brittle; it cracks if hit hard. Cast iron, used for pots and engine blocks, is similar.

To make steel, the molten pig iron is poured into a basic oxygen furnace, and pure oxygen is blown through it. The oxygen burns off most of the carbon as carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide and removes other impurities. Most steel contains less than about 1 per cent carbon, which makes it strong and tough, so it can bend without breaking. Other metals can be added to make alloy steels: chromium and nickel, both mined in South Africa, make stainless steel that does not rust.

Steel is also made by melting scrap steel in an electric arc furnace, which recycles old cars, appliances and building steel. Major South African steelworks include those at Vanderbijlpark and Newcastle.

Steel is one of the most important materials in the modern world. It is used in the reinforcing bars inside concrete buildings and bridges, in railway lines, cars, ships, pipes, mining equipment, cans, cutlery and the pylons of the national grid. Its strength, its relatively low cost and the fact that it can be recycled again and again make it hard to replace.

## Subtopic: Comparing Methods and the Error Museum

The ancient furnace and the modern blast furnace use the same chemistry but differ greatly.

Fuel: the ancient furnace used charcoal from wood; the blast furnace uses coke from coal.
Air: bellows worked by hand compared with powerful hot-air blowers.
Temperature: about 1 200 °C, too low to melt iron, compared with about 1 500 °C, which melts it.
Product: a solid spongy bloom hammered into shape, compared with liquid iron poured off continuously.
Scale: a few kilograms per smelt compared with thousands of tonnes per day.
Impurities: slag ran out of the bloom; in the blast furnace limestone forms slag.

Iron and steel production has environmental costs. Coke making and smelting release carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas, as well as dust and other pollutants. Making steel from recycled scrap uses much less energy and produces much less carbon dioxide than making it from iron ore.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: iron ore is pure iron; it contains iron oxides, so the oxygen must be removed chemically. Two: limestone provides the iron; it removes impurities as slag. Three: pig iron is the strongest form of iron; its high carbon content makes it brittle. Four: steel has more carbon than pig iron; steel has much less carbon, because oxygen burns most of it off. Five: iron working only arrived in South Africa with European settlers; African communities smelted iron here for more than a thousand years before that.

## Subtopic: Cleaning Up the Ore

Ore from a mine still has lots of unwanted stuff in it. Refining means getting the useful part out pure.

Some ways are physical: nothing new is made. You can crush and sieve, use a magnet to pull out magnetic bits, wash away light grains to leave heavy ones, or dissolve salt and evaporate the water.

Other ways are chemical: new substances are made. Most metals are locked up with oxygen or sulfur. To get the metal out, you need a chemical reaction.

Smelting means heating the ore with carbon. The carbon grabs the oxygen, and the metal is left behind.

Electrolysis means using electricity to split the compound. Aluminium is made this way, which uses enormous amounts of electricity.

Here is a quick test: if you could get the substance back by undoing what you did, like evaporating water to get salt back, it is physical. If a brand-new substance appears, it is chemical.

## Subtopic: The Clay Furnace

Long before modern factories, iron was made right here in southern Africa, for well over a thousand years.

Smiths dug up iron ore and made charcoal from wood. They built a clay furnace, like a chimney.

They filled it with layers of charcoal and ore and lit it. Then came the hard work: pumping goatskin bellows for hours to blow in air and make the fire roaring hot.

The burning charcoal stole the oxygen from the iron ore, leaving iron behind.

It wasn't hot enough to melt the iron, so a spongy lump called a bloom formed. The smith reheated it and hammered it again and again to squeeze out the rubbish and shape it into hoes, axes and spears.

Remains of these furnaces have been found at places like Melville Koppies in Johannesburg and Phalaborwa.

## Subtopic: From Iron to Steel

Today, iron is made in giant blast furnaces as tall as a ten-storey building.

In go three things: iron ore, coke made from coal, and limestone. Hot air is blasted in at the bottom.

The coke burns and makes carbon monoxide, which pulls the oxygen off the iron oxide. Liquid iron runs to the bottom.

The limestone grabs the sand and other rubbish and turns it into slag, which floats on top of the iron.

But this iron has too much carbon, about 4 per cent, so it is brittle and snaps. Blow pure oxygen through it to burn off most of the carbon, and you get steel: strong and tough.

Add chromium and nickel and you get stainless steel. And old steel can be melted down and recycled, which saves a lot of energy.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Refining uses physical processes, such as magnets, flotation and dissolving, and chemical processes, such as smelting and electrolysis. African smiths smelted iron in clay furnaces with charcoal and bellows for over a thousand years. In a blast furnace, carbon monoxide reduces iron oxide: Fe2O3 + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO2, and limestone removes impurities as slag. Blowing oxygen through iron lowers its carbon to make steel.
