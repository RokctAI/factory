### subtopic_1 — Physical and Chemical Refining
**Assistant:** Why is so much electricity needed to make aluminium compared with iron?

**Tutor:** Because aluminium holds on to oxygen much more strongly than iron does, so carbon cannot remove it, and electricity must do the job instead. Aluminium is a very reactive metal, high in the reactivity series, so aluminium oxide is an extremely stable compound. Carbon can take oxygen from iron oxide at blast furnace temperatures, but it cannot take oxygen from aluminium oxide at any practical temperature. Instead, the aluminium oxide is dissolved in a molten mineral called cryolite at about 950 °C, and a huge electric current is passed through it. The current splits the compound, and liquid aluminium collects at the negative electrode at the bottom of the cell. Each tonne of aluminium needs roughly 13 000 to 15 000 kilowatt-hours of electricity, about as much as a typical South African household uses in two to four years. That is why aluminium smelters are built where large, reliable supplies of electricity are available, and why the smelters at Richards Bay have special electricity supply agreements. It is also why recycling aluminium cans is so worthwhile: melting down used aluminium needs only about 5 per cent of the energy needed to make new aluminium from its ore.

### subtopic_2 — Ancient Iron Smelting in South Africa
**Assistant:** How do archaeologists know that iron was smelted in South Africa so long ago?

**Tutor:** They use several kinds of evidence found at ancient sites. The most direct is the waste left behind by smelting. Slag, the glassy, bubbly residue from the furnace, does not rot or rust away, so heaps of slag show where smelting took place. Archaeologists also find pieces of broken clay furnace walls, often reddened and glazed on the inside by the heat, and fragments of the clay tuyères through which air was blown, sometimes with their ends melted. Iron tools, ore, charcoal and the remains of smithing hearths add to the picture. To find the age, they use radiocarbon dating on charcoal from the furnaces and from the settlements. Living things take in carbon, including a small amount of radioactive carbon-14, which decays at a known rate after death, so measuring how much remains in charcoal tells how long ago the wood was burned. Pottery styles, which changed over time, help to link sites together. Oral histories, recorded from communities in which iron working continued into the twentieth century, describe the methods, the songs and the rituals of smelting, and some smiths have even rebuilt furnaces and demonstrated the process, which helps archaeologists understand what they find.

### subtopic_3 — The Blast Furnace and Steel
**Assistant:** Why does adding a small amount of carbon make iron so much stronger?

**Tutor:** Because the carbon atoms change the way the iron atoms can move past each other. Pure iron is made of layers of iron atoms arranged in a regular pattern. When a force is applied, these layers can slide over one another fairly easily, which is why pure iron is quite soft and bends easily. When a small amount of carbon is added, the much smaller carbon atoms fit into the spaces between the iron atoms. They distort the pattern and act like grit, making it harder for the layers to slide. This makes the metal stronger and harder while it can still bend a little before breaking, which is what we want in steel for buildings, bridges, cars and tools. But there is a balance. With too much carbon, about 4 per cent as in pig iron or cast iron, the carbon forms hard, brittle crystals and flakes within the iron, and these act like built-in cracks, so the metal shatters if it is hit or bent. Steelmakers control the carbon content very carefully, from about 0,1 per cent for soft, easily shaped steel used in car bodies and wire, up to about 1 per cent for hard steel used in cutting tools, and they adjust the properties further by heating and cooling the steel in particular ways.

### subtopic_4 — Comparing Methods and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Does steelmaking harm the environment, and can it be made cleaner?

**Tutor:** Yes, traditional steelmaking has a large environmental impact, but there are ways to reduce it. Making each tonne of steel from iron ore in a blast furnace releases roughly two tonnes of carbon dioxide, mainly from the coke used to remove the oxygen and from burning coal for heat, and steelmaking is one of the largest industrial sources of greenhouse gases in the world. The process also produces dust, sulfur dioxide and other air pollutants, and needs large amounts of water and energy. Mining the iron ore and coal disturbs land too. Several solutions are being used or developed. Recycling scrap steel in electric arc furnaces uses much less energy and releases far less carbon dioxide, especially if the electricity comes from clean sources such as solar or wind power. Steel can be recycled again and again without losing its quality. Some new plants use natural gas, or, in future, hydrogen made with renewable electricity, to remove oxygen from iron ore instead of carbon; when hydrogen is used, the main product is water instead of carbon dioxide. South Africa, with its rich iron ore and strong sunshine and wind, is exploring this kind of green steel. Capturing waste gases, cleaning them with filters and reusing heat also help.
