# Part 1 — Expert

This session describes the importance of mining in South Africa and the environmental and social impacts of mining. It outlines the main minerals mined and where they are found; explains the impacts of mining on the land, including habitat loss, waste dumps and land subsidence; on water, especially acid mine drainage; on the air, through dust and pollution; and on people's health and communities; and describes ways of reducing these impacts, such as environmental impact assessments, rehabilitation and water treatment. The anchor facts are that South Africa is one of the world's leading producers of platinum group metals, chromium, manganese, gold, coal and diamonds; that mining contributes jobs, export income and raw materials but disturbs large areas of land; that acid mine drainage forms when water and oxygen react with sulfide minerals such as pyrite exposed by mining, producing sulfuric acid that dissolves toxic metals; that dust from mining and mine dumps harms health, and that breathing in silica dust can cause the lung disease silicosis; and that South African law requires mines to assess environmental impacts and to rehabilitate the land.

## Subtopic: Mining in South Africa

Mining has shaped South Africa for more than 150 years, and long before that, African communities mined iron, copper and gold on a small scale. Diamonds were found near Hopetown in 1867, and in 1871 diggers rushed to the area that became Kimberley. In 1886 gold was discovered on the Witwatersrand, and within a few years Johannesburg had grown into a busy city. Money from mining built railways, harbours, factories and cities, and attracted workers from all over southern Africa.

Today South Africa mines a wide range of minerals.

Platinum group metals and chromium: the Bushveld Complex in Limpopo, North West and Mpumalanga, with most of the world's known reserves. Platinum is used in catalytic converters that clean car exhaust gases, in jewellery and in fuel cells.

Gold: the Witwatersrand Basin in Gauteng, the Free State and North West. South Africa was the world's largest gold producer for most of the twentieth century, though production has fallen as the remaining ore lies deeper and is more costly to mine.

Coal: mainly Mpumalanga and Limpopo. Coal is burned to generate most of the country's electricity, is used to make fuels and chemicals, and is exported through Richards Bay.

Iron ore and manganese: the Northern Cape, around Sishen, Kathu and Hotazel. South Africa has a large share of the world's manganese reserves; manganese is essential in steelmaking.

Diamonds: Kimberley, Cullinan near Pretoria, Venetia in Limpopo, and the gravels of the west coast and the Orange River.

Other minerals include copper, titanium from the coastal sands of KwaZulu-Natal, phosphate at Phalaborwa, and building materials such as limestone, sand and granite.

Mining brings benefits. It employs hundreds of thousands of people directly and supports many more in industries that supply mines or process their products. Minerals are a large share of South Africa's exports, bringing foreign income, and the taxes and royalties mines pay help fund government services. The minerals themselves are raw materials for steel, electricity, fertilisers, cars and electronics.

## Subtopic: Impacts on Land and Water

Mining changes the land dramatically. Surface mining removes the vegetation, soil and rock from large areas, destroying the habitats of plants and animals. Open pits can be kilometres wide and hundreds of metres deep. Waste rock and tailings are piled into dumps that can cover many square kilometres; the flat-topped dumps around Johannesburg are an example. Without plant cover, dumps erode easily, and their sand and dust blow and wash into the surroundings.

Underground mining can make the land surface sink, which is called subsidence. Old coal mines in Mpumalanga, where pillars of coal have collapsed or underground coal seams have caught fire, have caused cracks and holes at the surface. On the West Rand, pumping water out of mines in dolomite rock has contributed to sinkholes, sudden collapses of the ground that can swallow buildings and roads.

Mining affects water in several ways. Mines use large amounts of water for processing ore and controlling dust, in a country that is already short of water. Mines must pump out groundwater that seeps into them, lowering the water table and drying up springs and boreholes nearby.

The most serious water problem is acid mine drainage. Many ores and coal seams contain sulfide minerals, especially pyrite, iron sulfide, often called fool's gold. When mining exposes pyrite to air and water, it reacts to form sulfuric acid and dissolved iron. The acidic water dissolves heavy metals from the rocks, such as iron, manganese, aluminium and sometimes uranium and arsenic, which are toxic to living things. The water often turns orange-red from iron compounds.

When old mines are abandoned and the pumps are switched off, groundwater slowly fills the tunnels, reacts with the exposed rock and eventually overflows, or decants, at the surface. Acid mine drainage began decanting from old gold mines on the West Rand in 2002, polluting streams that flow towards the Cradle of Humankind and the Crocodile River. In Mpumalanga, acid water from coal mines has polluted rivers that feed the Olifants River, which flows through the Kruger National Park.

In simple words, the reaction can be summarised as: pyrite + oxygen + water → iron sulfate + sulfuric acid. Bacteria that live in the acidic water speed up the reaction greatly, so once it starts, it can continue on its own for a very long time.

Acid mine drainage kills fish and other aquatic life, makes water unsafe to drink or use for irrigation, and corrodes pipes and concrete. It can continue for decades or even centuries after mining has stopped.

## Subtopic: Impacts on Air, People and Communities

Mining affects the air. Blasting, crushing, trucks on dirt roads and wind blowing over dry dumps all produce dust. Dust from gold mine dumps can contain fine silica and traces of heavy metals and uranium, and blows into nearby homes and schools, especially in the windy months of late winter and spring. Burning coal in power stations and industries releases sulfur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, fine particles and carbon dioxide. Parts of the Mpumalanga Highveld, where many coal mines and coal-fired power stations are concentrated, have some of the worst air quality in the country, and the government has declared the area a priority area for air-quality management.

Mining affects people's health and safety. Underground mining is dangerous because of rockfalls, rockbursts, flooding, fires, explosions of methane gas in coal mines, and accidents with machinery. Safety laws and training have greatly reduced the number of deaths over the decades, but accidents still happen every year. Breathing in fine silica dust from crushing and drilling quartz-rich rock damages the lungs and causes silicosis, an incurable disease that scars the lungs and makes people much more vulnerable to tuberculosis. Coal dust can cause a similar disease. Noise from machinery damages hearing. In 2018, gold mining companies agreed to a large settlement to compensate former miners who developed silicosis or tuberculosis.

Mining also affects communities. For generations, the mining industry relied on migrant workers who lived in single-sex hostels far from their families, which caused great hardship and broke up family life. Informal settlements often grow near mines, sometimes without proper water, sanitation and electricity. When a mine closes, towns that depended on it can lose most of their jobs. Abandoned mines attract illegal mining, which is extremely dangerous and is often linked to crime. Communities living on land with mineral deposits may be moved for new mines, and disputes over land, jobs and benefits are common.

Mining's impacts are linked across all four spheres of the Earth system. Removing vegetation affects the biosphere; dumps and pits change the lithosphere; acid drainage pollutes the hydrosphere; and dust and gases change the atmosphere. A single coal mine supplying a power station can affect all four at once, and the effects can reach people living far downstream or downwind.

## Subtopic: Reducing the Impacts and the Error Museum

Many of the impacts of mining can be reduced, and South African law requires mining companies to manage them. Before a new mine may operate, the company must carry out an environmental impact assessment, which studies the likely effects on land, water, air, wildlife and people, and must consult the affected communities. It must have an environmental management plan and set aside money to pay for rehabilitation. Laws such as the Mineral and Petroleum Resources Development Act and the National Environmental Management Act set out these duties.

Methods of reducing impacts include the following. Rehabilitation: storing topsoil before mining and replacing it afterwards, reshaping dumps and planting grasses and indigenous plants. Dust control: spraying water on roads and dumps and covering dumps with vegetation. Water management: separating clean and dirty water, recycling water within the mine, and treating acid mine drainage, for example by neutralising it with lime, which you met in the acids and bases lessons, so that metals settle out. Some treated mine water is now supplied as drinking water, such as at the eMalahleni water reclamation plant in Mpumalanga. Health and safety: ventilation, dust suppression, protective equipment and medical checks. Social plans: training workers, building houses and investing in local development so towns can survive after the mine closes.

Everyone can help by reducing the demand for newly mined materials, especially by recycling metals, saving electricity, which reduces the coal burned, and buying products that last.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: mining only affects the land where the mine is; dust, polluted water and air pollution travel far beyond the mine. Two: acid mine drainage stops when mining stops; it can continue for decades after the mine closes. Three: mine dumps are harmless sand; they can contain fine silica, heavy metals and traces of uranium. Four: mining brings only benefits or only harm; it brings both, and they must be weighed and managed. Five: once a mine closes, the company has no responsibility; the law requires rehabilitation and closure planning.

## Subtopic: Riches from the Ground

South Africa is one of the richest countries in the world when it comes to minerals.

Gold from the Witwatersrand built Johannesburg. Diamonds started the city of Kimberley.

The Bushveld has most of the world's platinum, used to clean car exhaust. Mpumalanga has the coal that makes most of our electricity. The Northern Cape has iron ore and manganese for making steel.

Mining gives jobs to hundreds of thousands of people, earns money from other countries and pays taxes that help run the country.

And the minerals become things we use every day: steel for buildings, coal for electricity, phosphate for fertiliser, and metals in phones and cars.

But mining has a cost too.

Some of these minerals are needed for clean energy too: platinum for fuel cells, and manganese and other metals for batteries.

## Subtopic: Scars on the Land

Mining leaves its mark.

On the land: plants and animals lose their homes, giant holes and dumps appear, and old mines can make the ground sink or crack.

In the water: when rocks with a mineral called pyrite meet air and water, they make acid. The acid water dissolves poisonous metals and turns orange. When old mines flood, this acid water can spill out into rivers, killing fish and making water unsafe. It can carry on for many years after a mine closes.

In the air: dust blows from dumps and roads, and coal power stations release polluting gases. Parts of Mpumalanga have some of the dirtiest air in the country.

In people's lungs: breathing in rock dust can cause silicosis, a disease that scars the lungs for life.

And in communities: families split by migrant work, and towns left without jobs when mines close.

Remember: the damage doesn't always stay at the mine. Acid water flows down rivers, and dust and smoke blow with the wind.

## Subtopic: Mining More Responsibly

The good news is that much of the damage can be reduced.

Before a mine opens, the company must study its effects on the environment and the people, and talk to the community.

During mining, it must control dust, look after water and keep workers safe.

After mining, it must fix the land: put back the soil, reshape the dumps and plant them.

Acid water can be treated with lime to neutralise it. In eMalahleni, mine water is even cleaned and turned into drinking water.

Mine workers are protected better than before too: ventilation, water sprays to keep dust down, masks and regular health checks.

And you can help: recycle metal cans and old electronics, and save electricity so less coal is burned.

Pocket summary of the lesson. South Africa mines platinum, gold, coal, iron ore, manganese, diamonds and more, bringing jobs, income and raw materials. Mining harms the land, water, air and people's health: habitat loss, dumps, subsidence, acid mine drainage, dust, air pollution and silicosis. Laws require impact assessments and rehabilitation, and good practice can reduce the harm. This completes the topic on mining; next you will study the atmosphere.
