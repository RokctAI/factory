### subtopic_1 — Igneous Rocks
**Assistant:** If granite forms deep underground, how does it end up at the surface like Paarl Rock?

**Tutor:** Through uplift and erosion over many millions of years. The granite of Paarl Rock and the Cape Peninsula formed from magma that pushed up into older rocks, the Malmesbury shales, and cooled slowly several kilometres below the surface, around 540 million years ago. At that time the granite was buried beneath a thick cover of other rocks. Over the following hundreds of millions of years, movements of the lithosphere slowly pushed the land upwards, while weathering and erosion steadily wore away the rocks on top. Rain, rivers, wind and temperature changes stripped off the overlying layers, grain by grain, until the granite itself was exposed. Once at the surface, the pressure on the granite was released, and it often cracks into curved sheets parallel to the surface, which peel off like the layers of an onion. This process, called exfoliation, is one reason granite hills such as Paarl Rock have smooth, rounded dome shapes. The granite is now itself being weathered, and the sand and clay it produces will be carried away to form new sedimentary rocks, which shows that the rock cycle is still going on.

### subtopic_2 — Sedimentary Rocks
**Assistant:** How do scientists know the age of rocks like the Table Mountain sandstone?

**Tutor:** They use two main approaches: the order of rock layers and the steady decay of radioactive elements. The first, called relative dating, uses the principle that in undisturbed sedimentary rocks, younger layers lie on top of older ones. Fossils also help, because particular organisms lived only during certain periods, so layers containing the same fossils in different places are of similar age. The second approach, called radiometric dating, gives an age in years. Some minerals contain tiny amounts of radioactive elements, such as uranium, which slowly change into other elements at a steady, known rate. By measuring how much of the original element remains and how much of the product has formed, scientists can calculate how long ago the mineral crystallised. This works best for igneous rocks, whose crystals formed at a known moment as they cooled. For sedimentary rocks such as sandstone, geologists date igneous layers, such as volcanic ash, above and below them, or date the grains of resistant minerals like zircon within the sandstone, which tells them the oldest possible age. Combining these methods gives the age of the Table Mountain Group sandstones as roughly 400 to 500 million years.

### subtopic_3 — Metamorphic Rocks
**Assistant:** Why are fossils found in sedimentary rocks but almost never in igneous rocks?

**Tutor:** Because of how the rocks form. Fossils are preserved remains or traces of living things, and preserving them requires the organism to be buried gently and quickly, before it rots or is eaten, and then protected for a long time. Sedimentary rocks form in exactly these conditions. Mud, sand or lime settling at the bottom of a lake, river or sea can quickly cover a dead animal or plant. As more layers pile up, the soft parts usually decay, but hard parts such as bones, teeth and shells remain. Over time, minerals in water seeping through the sediment can replace the original material, turning it to stone while keeping its shape. Footprints, burrows and leaf impressions can also be preserved in mud that hardens. Igneous rocks, on the other hand, form from molten rock at temperatures of roughly 700 to 1 200 °C, which would burn up any living thing, so fossils are almost never found in them. Metamorphic rocks are changed by heat and pressure, which usually distorts or destroys any fossils the original sedimentary rock contained. That is why palaeontologists search the sedimentary rocks of places like the Karoo, the Cradle of Humankind and the West Coast Fossil Park.

### subtopic_4 — The Rock Cycle and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Is the rock cycle still happening today, or did it stop long ago?

**Tutor:** It is happening right now, everywhere, though most of it is too slow for us to notice in one lifetime. Some parts happen fast enough to see. After heavy rain, rivers turn brown with mud eroded from the land, and that sediment is carried to the sea and deposited at river mouths, as at the Tugela and Orange River mouths. Waves grind rocks into sand along the coast. Sand dunes move across the Namib and Kalahari. In volcanic regions such as Iceland, Hawaii and the East African Rift, lava erupts and cools into new igneous rock, sometimes making new land within days. Deep inside the Earth, where plates collide, such as under the Himalayas and the Andes, rocks are being buried, heated, squeezed and melted at this moment, forming metamorphic rock and magma we cannot see. Mountain ranges are still rising by millimetres each year while erosion wears them down. People also speed up parts of the cycle: mining moves huge amounts of rock, and poor farming and the clearing of vegetation increase soil erosion many times over. So the rock cycle has never stopped, and it will continue as long as the Sun shines on the Earth and the Earth's interior stays hot.
