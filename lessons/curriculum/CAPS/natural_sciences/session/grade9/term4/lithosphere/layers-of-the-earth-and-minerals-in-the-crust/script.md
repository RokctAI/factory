# Part 1 — Expert

This session describes the internal structure of the Earth, crust, mantle, outer core and inner core; explains what the lithosphere is and the three parts that make it up, continental crust, oceanic crust and the rigid uppermost mantle; and introduces minerals as the naturally occurring substances that make up the rocks of the crust, including important minerals mined in South Africa. The anchor facts are that the Earth's radius is about 6 371 kilometres; that the crust is the thin outer layer, about 5 to 10 kilometres thick under the oceans and on average about 35 kilometres thick under the continents; that the mantle extends to a depth of about 2 900 kilometres and is solid rock that can flow very slowly; that the outer core is liquid iron and nickel and the inner core is solid iron and nickel; that temperature and pressure increase with depth; that the lithosphere is the crust together with the rigid uppermost mantle; that a mineral is a naturally occurring, solid, inorganic substance with a definite chemical composition and crystal structure; and that the most common elements in the crust are oxygen and silicon.

## Subtopic: Layers of the Earth

The Earth is made of layers, like an onion or a boiled egg. From the outside inwards, they are the crust, the mantle, the outer core and the inner core.

The crust is the thin, solid, rocky outer layer on which we live. Oceanic crust, beneath the oceans, is about 5 to 10 kilometres thick and is made mainly of dark, dense rocks such as basalt. Continental crust, which forms the continents, is thicker, about 35 kilometres on average and up to about 70 kilometres under high mountain ranges, and is made of lighter rocks such as granite.

The mantle lies beneath the crust and extends to a depth of about 2 900 kilometres. It makes up about 84 per cent of the Earth's volume. The mantle is made of hot rock rich in iron and magnesium. Although it is solid, the rock is so hot that, over millions of years, it can flow very slowly, like extremely thick toffee. Slow currents in the mantle help move the plates of the lithosphere.

The outer core, from about 2 900 to about 5 100 kilometres deep, is made of liquid iron and nickel. The movement of this liquid metal produces the Earth's magnetic field, which you studied in the forces lessons this year.

The inner core, from about 5 100 kilometres to the centre at about 6 371 kilometres, is a solid ball of iron and nickel. Its temperature is estimated at about 5 000 to 6 000 °C, similar to the surface of the Sun, but it remains solid because the pressure is so enormous.

As you go deeper into the Earth, both the temperature and the pressure increase. In deep South African gold mines, the rock at the bottom can be about 55 to 60 °C, and the mines must be cooled with chilled air and ice so that people can work there.

How do scientists know about the layers when no one has drilled even through the crust? The deepest hole ever drilled reached only about 12 kilometres. The answer comes mainly from earthquake waves. When an earthquake happens, waves travel through the Earth and are recorded by instruments called seismometers all around the world. The waves change speed and direction as they pass through different materials, and one type of wave cannot travel through liquids at all. By studying where and when the waves arrive, scientists worked out that the outer core must be liquid and found the boundaries between the layers.

## Subtopic: The Lithosphere and Its Parts

The word lithosphere describes the outer layer of the Earth that behaves as a rigid, brittle shell. It is not the same as the crust. The lithosphere is made of three parts.

Continental crust: the thick, lighter crust of the continents, made mainly of granite-like rocks rich in silicon and aluminium. Some continental crust is extremely old. Rocks in the Barberton Makhonjwa Mountains in Mpumalanga are about 3,5 billion years old, among the oldest well-preserved rocks on Earth.

Oceanic crust: the thinner, denser crust beneath the oceans, made mainly of basalt, rich in iron and magnesium. Oceanic crust is young compared to continental crust, mostly less than 200 million years old, because it is constantly made at mid-ocean ridges and destroyed where it sinks back into the mantle.

The rigid uppermost mantle: the top part of the mantle just below the crust, which is cool enough to be rigid and is firmly attached to the crust above it.

Together these make up a layer about 100 kilometres thick on average, thinner under the oceans and thicker under old continents. Below the lithosphere is a weaker, hotter layer of the mantle, the asthenosphere, where the rock can flow slowly.

The lithosphere is not one unbroken shell. It is cracked into about a dozen large pieces, and many smaller ones, called tectonic plates, which move very slowly across the asthenosphere at a few centimetres a year, about as fast as your fingernails grow. Most earthquakes and volcanoes happen near the edges of these plates. South Africa lies near the middle of the African Plate, far from plate edges, which is one reason we have few strong natural earthquakes and no active volcanoes. Most earthquakes felt in South Africa are small and are caused by mining, though occasional natural earthquakes do occur.

The idea that the continents move was first suggested over a century ago, partly because the coastlines of Africa and South America fit together like puzzle pieces, and because the same fossils, such as the reptile Mesosaurus and the plant Glossopteris, are found on continents now separated by oceans. Glossopteris fossils are common in the coal-bearing rocks of the Karoo, and identical fossils are found in India, Antarctica and Australia, which were once joined to Africa in the supercontinent Gondwana.

## Subtopic: Minerals in the Crust

The rocks of the crust are made of minerals. A mineral is a naturally occurring, solid, inorganic substance with a definite chemical composition and an orderly arrangement of particles, which often shows as crystals. A rock is a mixture of one or more minerals. For example, granite is a rock made mainly of the minerals quartz, feldspar and mica, and you can often see the separate crystals.

Only a few elements make up most of the crust. By mass, the crust is about 46 per cent oxygen, 28 per cent silicon, 8 per cent aluminium and 5 per cent iron, with smaller amounts of calcium, sodium, potassium and magnesium. These eight elements make up about 98 per cent of the crust. It may seem surprising that oxygen, which we think of as a gas, is the most common element in the crust, but it is combined with silicon and metals in minerals. The most common minerals are silicates, compounds of silicon and oxygen with metals; feldspar and quartz are the most common of all.

Minerals can be identified by their properties: colour, lustre or shine, hardness, the way they break, and the colour of their powder, called the streak. Hardness is measured on the Mohs scale from 1 to 10. Talc, used in baby powder, has a hardness of 1; quartz has a hardness of 7; diamond, the hardest natural mineral, has a hardness of 10.

Some minerals are valuable because they contain useful elements or have useful properties. A rock containing enough of a valuable mineral to be mined at a profit is called an ore. South Africa is extraordinarily rich in minerals. The Witwatersrand Basin has produced a large share of all the gold ever mined. The Bushveld Complex holds most of the world's known reserves of platinum group metals and very large deposits of chromium and vanadium. The Kalahari manganese field in the Northern Cape holds a large share of the world's manganese. Diamonds are mined at Kimberley and other places, iron ore at Sishen, and coal in Mpumalanga and Limpopo.

Many everyday objects depend on minerals from the crust: the copper in electrical wires, the iron in steel, the silicon in computer chips, the gypsum in plaster and the clay in bricks and cups.

## Subtopic: Comparing the Layers and the Error Museum

A table helps to compare the layers.

Crust: solid rock; about 5 to 70 kilometres thick; granite on continents and basalt under the oceans.
Mantle: solid rock that flows very slowly; to about 2 900 kilometres deep; rich in iron and magnesium.
Outer core: liquid; about 2 900 to 5 100 kilometres deep; iron and nickel; produces the magnetic field.
Inner core: solid; about 5 100 to 6 371 kilometres deep; iron and nickel; extremely hot and under enormous pressure.

The layers are arranged by density: the densest materials, iron and nickel, sank to the centre when the young Earth was very hot and partly molten, and the lightest rocks rose to form the crust.

A useful scale model: if the Earth were shrunk to the size of a soccer ball with a radius of about 11 centimetres, the continental crust would be only about 0,6 millimetres thick, thinner than a fingernail, while the core would be a ball with a radius of about 6 centimetres, more than half the radius of the whole ball.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: the lithosphere is the same as the crust; it is the crust together with the rigid uppermost mantle. Two: the mantle is liquid lava; it is solid rock that flows extremely slowly. Three: the inner core is liquid because it is the hottest part; it is solid because of the enormous pressure. Four: oceanic crust is thicker than continental crust; it is thinner, about 5 to 10 kilometres, but denser. Five: rocks and minerals are the same thing; a rock is a mixture of minerals.

Layout for a layers answer: name the layer, give its depth range or thickness, state whether it is solid or liquid and what it is made of, and give one special feature.

## Subtopic: A Journey to the Centre

Imagine a lift that could take you to the centre of the Earth, about 6 371 kilometres down.

First you pass through the crust, the thin rocky skin we live on. Under the sea it is only 5 to 10 kilometres thick. Under the land it is thicker, about 35 kilometres on average.

Next comes the mantle, almost 3 000 kilometres of hot rock. It is solid, but so hot that it creeps very slowly, like very thick toffee.

Then the outer core: liquid iron and nickel. As it swirls, it makes the Earth's magnetic field, the one your compass needle follows.

Finally the inner core: a ball of solid iron and nickel, as hot as the surface of the Sun. It stays solid because it is squeezed so hard.

The deeper you go, the hotter it gets and the harder everything is squeezed.

Nobody has made this journey. The deepest hole ever drilled is only about 12 kilometres. We know about the layers from earthquake waves that travel through the Earth.

## Subtopic: The Cracked Shell

The lithosphere is the Earth's hard outer shell. It is made of three parts: continental crust under the land, oceanic crust under the sea, and the stiff top part of the mantle stuck underneath.

Think of a cracked eggshell. The lithosphere is broken into giant pieces called tectonic plates.

The plates slide very slowly over a softer, hotter layer of the mantle, about as fast as your fingernails grow.

Where plates meet, there are earthquakes and volcanoes. South Africa sits in the middle of the African Plate, which is why we have no active volcanoes and few big natural earthquakes.

Some of the oldest rocks on Earth, about 3,5 billion years old, are found near Barberton in Mpumalanga.

Long ago, Africa, South America, India, Antarctica and Australia were joined together in one giant continent called Gondwana. The same fossil plants are found on all of them, which is part of the evidence.

## Subtopic: Treasure in the Rocks

Rocks are made of minerals. A mineral is a natural solid with a fixed chemical makeup and a neat, regular pattern of particles, often forming crystals.

Granite, for example, is a rock made of the minerals quartz, feldspar and mica.

The two most common elements in the crust are oxygen and silicon. Together they make quartz and many other minerals.

Some minerals are treasure. South Africa has gold from the Witwatersrand, platinum and chrome from the Bushveld, diamonds from Kimberley, iron ore from Sishen, manganese from the Kalahari and coal from Mpumalanga.

A rock rich enough in a useful mineral to be worth mining is called an ore.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The Earth has four layers: crust, mantle, liquid outer core and solid inner core. Temperature and pressure increase with depth. The lithosphere is the continental crust, the oceanic crust and the rigid upper mantle, broken into tectonic plates. Rocks are made of minerals, and oxygen and silicon are the most common elements in the crust. South Africa is very rich in minerals.
