### subtopic_1 — Layers of the Earth
**Assistant:** If the mantle is solid, where does the lava from volcanoes come from?

**Tutor:** Lava comes from magma, which forms in pockets where the rock of the mantle or crust partly melts, not from a liquid layer. The mantle is mostly solid because, although it is very hot, the high pressure deep underground keeps the rock from melting. In a few places, conditions change enough for some of the rock to melt. At mid-ocean ridges and hot spots such as Hawaii, hot mantle rock rises slowly towards the surface; as it rises, the pressure on it falls, and some of the rock melts, rather like a bottle of fizzy drink releasing gas when the cap is loosened. Where one plate sinks beneath another, water carried down with the sinking plate is released into the hot mantle above it, and the water lowers the melting temperature of the rock, so it partly melts. The magma that forms is less dense than the surrounding solid rock, so it rises, collects in magma chambers within the crust, and may erupt through volcanoes as lava. Only a small fraction of the rock melts, typically a few per cent, which is why seismic waves still show the mantle as solid. South Africa has no active volcanoes today, but ancient volcanic rocks, such as the basalts of the Drakensberg, show that huge eruptions happened here about 180 million years ago.

### subtopic_2 — The Lithosphere and Its Parts
**Assistant:** How can a soft mantle move giant tectonic plates?

**Tutor:** The mantle is solid, but it behaves over very long times like an extremely thick fluid, and it carries enormous amounts of heat from the Earth's interior towards the surface. Heat from the core and from radioactive elements in the rocks warms the deep mantle. Warmer rock is slightly less dense, so it rises very slowly, cools near the top, becomes denser and sinks again. These slow convection currents move at only a few centimetres a year, but they act over millions of years and over enormous areas. Scientists now think that the main forces moving plates come from the plates themselves. Where old oceanic lithosphere has become cold and dense, it sinks back into the mantle at deep ocean trenches, pulling the rest of the plate behind it, a force called slab pull. At mid-ocean ridges, new lithosphere is raised high on hot rising mantle and tends to slide away from the ridge, which is called ridge push. The mantle convection and the plate motions are two parts of the same system of heat escaping from the Earth. Over about 200 million years these movements broke up the supercontinent Gondwana and opened the Atlantic and Indian Oceans around southern Africa.

### subtopic_3 — Minerals in the Crust
**Assistant:** Why is South Africa so rich in minerals compared to many other countries?

**Tutor:** Because South Africa's crust is extremely old and has had a long, unusual geological history that concentrated valuable minerals in a few special places. Parts of the Kaapvaal Craton, the ancient core of continental crust beneath much of north-eastern South Africa, formed more than 3 billion years ago and have stayed stable ever since, so deposits that formed long ago were not destroyed. About 2,9 to 2,7 billion years ago, rivers carried gold and other heavy minerals into a huge basin, where they settled in layers of gravel that later became the gold-bearing rocks of the Witwatersrand. About 2 billion years ago, an enormous volume of magma rose into the crust and cooled slowly to form the Bushveld Complex. As it cooled, crystals of different minerals settled out in layers, concentrating platinum group metals and chromite in thin, rich bands that run for many kilometres. Diamonds formed deep in the mantle and were carried rapidly to the surface in volcanic pipes called kimberlites, named after Kimberley. Ancient seas deposited iron and manganese in the Northern Cape, and swamps over 250 million years ago became the Karoo coalfields. Each of these events was rare, and having so many in one country is what makes South Africa special.

### subtopic_4 — Comparing the Layers and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How do geologists tell minerals apart if many look the same?

**Tutor:** They use a set of simple tests on several properties together, because colour alone is often misleading. Many minerals come in different colours because of tiny amounts of impurities, while some different minerals have the same colour. Pyrite, for example, is a shiny gold-coloured mineral that has fooled so many people that it is called fool's gold. One test is the streak: rubbing the mineral on a piece of unglazed white tile leaves a powder whose colour is more reliable than the colour of the lump. Gold leaves a golden-yellow streak, but pyrite leaves a greenish-black one. Another is hardness, tested by trying to scratch the mineral with objects of known hardness, such as a fingernail, about 2,5 on the Mohs scale, a copper coin, about 3, or a steel knife, about 5,5. Quartz, at 7, scratches glass; calcite, at 3, does not. Geologists also look at lustre, whether metallic, glassy or dull, at the way the mineral breaks into flat faces or uneven surfaces, at its density by feeling how heavy it is for its size, and sometimes at whether it is magnetic or fizzes in dilute acid. In laboratories they use microscopes and X-rays to identify the exact crystal structure.
