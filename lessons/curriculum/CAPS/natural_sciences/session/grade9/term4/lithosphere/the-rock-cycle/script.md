# Part 1 — Expert

This session describes the three main types of rock, igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic, explains how each type forms, and shows how they are linked in the rock cycle, in which rocks are formed, broken down and re-formed over very long periods of time. The anchor facts are that igneous rocks form when molten rock, called magma underground and lava at the surface, cools and solidifies; that slow cooling underground gives large crystals, as in granite, and fast cooling at the surface gives small crystals, as in basalt; that sedimentary rocks form when sediments are deposited in layers, compacted and cemented together, as in sandstone, shale and limestone, and that they may contain fossils; that metamorphic rocks form when existing rocks are changed by heat and pressure without melting, as when shale becomes slate, limestone becomes marble and sandstone becomes quartzite; and that the rock cycle is driven by the Sun's energy, through weathering and erosion, and by the Earth's internal heat, through melting, metamorphism and uplift.

## Subtopic: Igneous Rocks

The word igneous comes from the Latin ignis, meaning fire. Igneous rocks form when molten rock cools and becomes solid. Molten rock below the surface is called magma; when it reaches the surface, through a volcano or a crack, it is called lava.

How fast the molten rock cools controls the size of the crystals. As magma cools, mineral crystals start to grow, and the longer they have, the bigger they grow.

Intrusive igneous rocks form when magma cools slowly deep underground, insulated by the rocks around it. Cooling can take thousands to millions of years, so the crystals grow large enough to see with the naked eye. Granite is the best-known example: it is light-coloured, with visible crystals of glassy quartz, pink or white feldspar and black mica. Paarl Rock, near Paarl in the Western Cape, is a huge dome of granite, and granite also forms the base of the mountains around Cape Town. The Bushveld Complex, north of Pretoria, is one of the largest bodies of intrusive igneous rock in the world.

Extrusive igneous rocks form when lava cools quickly at the surface, in days or weeks. The crystals have little time to grow, so they are very small or the rock is glassy. Basalt is the most common example: a dark, fine-grained rock. The high cliffs of the Drakensberg are made of layer upon layer of basalt from enormous lava flows that poured out about 180 million years ago, when Gondwana began to break apart. If lava cools almost instantly, for example when it flows into water, it can form a natural glass called obsidian. Pumice is a light igneous rock full of gas bubbles, so light that it can float on water.

Igneous rocks are usually hard and made of interlocking crystals. They do not contain fossils, because any living thing would be destroyed by the heat of the molten rock.

You can model the effect of cooling rate in a simple experiment. Melt some sugar or wax, pour half onto a cold metal tray and let the other half cool slowly in a warm, insulated container. The quickly cooled sample has tiny crystals or none, and the slowly cooled one has larger crystals, just like basalt and granite.

Igneous rocks are very useful. Granite is cut and polished for kitchen counters, gravestones and buildings, and crushed basalt and dolerite are used as stone for roads and railway beds. Dolerite, a dark rock that formed when magma squeezed into cracks between the Karoo's sedimentary layers, caps many of the flat-topped hills of the Karoo and protects them from erosion.

## Subtopic: Sedimentary Rocks

Sedimentary rocks form from sediments: pieces of other rocks, the remains of living things, or minerals that settle out of water. They form in four main stages.

Weathering: rocks at the surface are broken down into smaller pieces by the action of water, ice, wind, temperature changes, plants and chemical reactions. For example, water seeping into cracks freezes and expands, splitting the rock, and roots growing in cracks widen them.

Erosion and transport: the broken pieces are carried away by rivers, wind, glaciers and waves. As they are carried, they are rubbed against each other and become smaller and more rounded.

Deposition: when the water or wind slows down, it drops the sediment. Large, heavy pebbles settle first; fine sand and mud are carried further and settle in quiet water, such as lakes and the deep sea. Sediment builds up in layers called strata, with younger layers on top of older ones.

Compaction and cementation: as more layers pile up, the weight squeezes the lower layers, pressing the grains together and squeezing out water. Minerals dissolved in the water, such as silica, calcite or iron oxide, crystallise between the grains and cement them together into solid rock.

Common sedimentary rocks include sandstone, made of cemented sand grains; shale and mudstone, made of fine clay and mud; conglomerate, made of rounded pebbles cemented together; and limestone, made mainly of calcium carbonate, often from the shells and skeletons of sea creatures. Coal is a sedimentary rock formed from layers of plant material buried in ancient swamps.

Sedimentary rocks usually show layers, and they are the only rocks that commonly contain fossils, the preserved remains or traces of ancient organisms. The rocks of the Karoo Basin are famous for fossils of mammal-like reptiles that lived over 250 million years ago, and the Table Mountain sandstone was laid down over 400 million years ago.

Sedimentary rocks are economically important too. Coal from the Karoo sedimentary layers in Mpumalanga provides most of South Africa's electricity. Limestone is used to make cement and to neutralise acidic soils, and sandstone is a popular building stone, used in many old buildings in the Free State and Eastern Cape.

## Subtopic: Metamorphic Rocks

The word metamorphic means changed in form. Metamorphic rocks form when an existing rock, igneous, sedimentary or even metamorphic, is changed by great heat, great pressure or both, without melting. The minerals recrystallise and rearrange themselves, and new minerals may grow, so the rock becomes a new type of rock, usually harder and denser than before.

This can happen in two main ways. When tectonic plates collide and mountains are pushed up, rocks deep in the crust are buried, squeezed and heated over large regions. And where hot magma pushes into cooler rocks, the rocks right next to it are baked by the heat.

Here are some common changes.

Shale or mudstone becomes slate, a hard rock that splits into thin flat sheets, used for roof tiles and in the past for school writing boards. With more heat and pressure, slate can become schist, which sparkles with mica, and then gneiss.

Limestone becomes marble. The calcite crystals grow larger and interlock, and the rock can be polished. Marble is used in buildings and sculptures.

Sandstone becomes quartzite, an extremely hard rock in which the sand grains are fused together.

Granite can become gneiss, a rock with light and dark bands formed as the minerals line up under pressure.

Many metamorphic rocks show bands or layers called foliation, formed when pressure lines up flat minerals such as mica. Fossils in the original rock are usually distorted or destroyed.

South Africa has many metamorphic rocks. The Malmesbury shales near Cape Town were changed by heat where granite magma pushed into them, and some of the oldest rocks around Barberton are metamorphic.

## Subtopic: The Rock Cycle and the Error Museum

The rock cycle shows how the three types of rock are connected and how any rock can change into any other type over time. There is no single starting point or fixed order; rocks can take many different paths.

Follow one path. Granite cools slowly from magma deep in the crust. Uplift and erosion of the rocks above slowly expose it at the surface. Weathering breaks it into sand and clay, which rivers carry to the sea and deposit in layers. Compaction and cementation turn the sand into sandstone. Plate movements bury the sandstone deep in the crust, where heat and pressure change it into quartzite. If it is pushed even deeper, it may melt to form magma, which cools to form igneous rock again, and the cycle continues.

But there are shortcuts. An igneous rock can be changed directly into a metamorphic rock by heat and pressure. A metamorphic rock can be uplifted and weathered to form sediment. A sedimentary rock can be weathered and eroded again to form new sediment.

Two energy sources drive the rock cycle. Energy from the Sun drives the water cycle and the winds, which cause weathering, erosion and transport at the surface. The Earth's internal heat drives melting, metamorphism, volcanoes and the movement of plates that buries rocks and lifts them up again. Gravity pulls sediment downhill and compacts it.

The rock cycle is extremely slow. Most changes take thousands to millions of years, which is why we see only a snapshot of it in our lifetimes.

A rock cycle diagram is usually drawn with the three rock types and magma at the corners, joined by arrows labelled with processes: melting, cooling and crystallisation, weathering and erosion, deposition, compaction and cementation, and heat and pressure. When you draw one, label every arrow with its process.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: metamorphic rocks form by melting; they form by heat and pressure without melting, and melting forms magma. Two: granite has small crystals because it cooled quickly; it has large crystals because it cooled slowly underground. Three: igneous rocks often contain fossils; fossils are found mainly in sedimentary rocks. Four: the rock cycle always goes igneous, then sedimentary, then metamorphic; rocks can follow many paths. Five: rocks never change; all rocks are slowly changing over very long times.

Layout for a rock cycle answer: name the starting rock, name each process, name the rock or material it produces, and state the energy source driving that step.

## Subtopic: Fire, Layers and Squeezing

There are three families of rocks. Each one is made in a different way.

Fire rocks: igneous rocks. They form when hot melted rock cools and hardens. Underground, melted rock is called magma; above ground it is lava.

Cool it slowly underground, and the crystals grow big, like in granite. Cool it fast on the surface, and the crystals stay tiny, like in dark basalt. Think of making ice: freeze water slowly and you can get big clear crystals; freeze it fast and they stay small.

Layer rocks: sedimentary rocks. Bits of rock, sand, mud and shells settle in layers, get pressed down by the layers above and glued together by minerals. Sandstone, shale and limestone are layer rocks. They are the ones that hold fossils.

Squeezed rocks: metamorphic rocks. Any rock, deep underground, gets heated and squeezed so much that it changes into a new rock, without melting. Shale becomes slate. Limestone becomes marble. Sandstone becomes quartzite.

Here is a picture to hold on to: fire rocks are baked, layer rocks are stacked, and squeezed rocks are pressed.

## Subtopic: Round and Round

Rocks go round and round in the rock cycle, very, very slowly.

Melted rock cools to make igneous rock. Wind, water and ice break it into bits. Rivers carry the bits away and drop them in layers. The layers are pressed and glued into sedimentary rock. Deep underground, heat and pressure squeeze it into metamorphic rock. If it gets hot enough to melt, it becomes magma again, and the cycle starts over.

There are shortcuts too. Any rock can be broken down into sediment. Any rock can be squeezed into a metamorphic rock. Any rock can melt.

What powers it all? The Sun at the top: it drives wind and rain, which break down rocks. The Earth's heat at the bottom: it melts and squeezes rocks and moves plates.

One trip round the cycle can take millions of years.

## Subtopic: Reading the Rocks Around Us

South Africa is a great place to read rocks.

The cliffs of the Drakensberg are basalt: layers of lava that flowed about 180 million years ago.

Paarl Rock is a huge dome of granite that cooled slowly underground and was later uncovered.

Table Mountain's top is sandstone, laid down as sand more than 400 million years ago.

The Karoo's flat layers of sedimentary rock hold fossils of animals that lived more than 250 million years ago.

And the marble in some buildings was once limestone made from ancient shells.

Next time you see a rock, ask three questions. Does it have crystals? Then it may be igneous. Does it have layers or fossils? Then it may be sedimentary. Is it banded, shiny or split into sheets? Then it may be metamorphic.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Igneous rocks form when magma or lava cools; slow cooling gives large crystals and fast cooling small ones. Sedimentary rocks form when sediments are deposited in layers, compacted and cemented, and may contain fossils. Metamorphic rocks form when rocks are changed by heat and pressure without melting. The rock cycle links them all, driven by the Sun and the Earth's internal heat. In the next topic you will see how people mine useful minerals from these rocks.
