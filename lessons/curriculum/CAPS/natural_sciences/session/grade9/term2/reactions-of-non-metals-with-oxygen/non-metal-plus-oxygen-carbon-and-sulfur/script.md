# Part 1 — Expert

This session sets out the general reaction of a non-metal with oxygen, applies it to carbon and sulfur with word equations and balanced chemical equations, describes the observations and tests for the products, and shows that non-metal oxides form acidic solutions. The anchor examples are carbon burning to carbon dioxide, C + O2 → CO2, in which 12 grams of carbon and 32 grams of oxygen form 44 grams of carbon dioxide, incomplete burning to carbon monoxide, 2C + O2 → 2CO, and sulfur burning to sulfur dioxide, S + O2 → SO2. The governing principle is that a non-metal combines with oxygen to form a non-metal oxide, usually a gas made of molecules, and that these oxides react with water to form acids, in contrast with metal oxides, which are bases.

## Subtopic: The General Reaction: Non-Metal + Oxygen → Non-Metal Oxide

Non-metals react with oxygen following one general word equation: non-metal + oxygen → non-metal oxide. As with metals, two reactants form one product, so it is a synthesis reaction, and the product is named after the non-metal with the ending oxide, using prefixes where needed: carbon forms carbon dioxide or carbon monoxide, sulfur forms sulfur dioxide, and hydrogen forms hydrogen oxide, better known as water.

The products differ from metal oxides in three important ways. First, metal oxides are solids made of ions in a lattice, while most non-metal oxides are made of small molecules and are gases at room temperature: carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, sulfur dioxide and the nitrogen oxides. Water is a liquid. Second, because they are gases, they escape into the air, so a burning non-metal appears to lose mass, although the atoms are conserved. Third, non-metal oxides that dissolve in water form acidic solutions, while metal oxides form basic ones.

The reactions are carried out in the laboratory by heating a small amount of the non-metal on a deflagrating spoon, a long-handled metal spoon, until it starts to burn in air, and then lowering it into a gas jar of oxygen, where it burns far more brightly because oxygen is about five times more concentrated than in air. After burning, a little water and a few drops of universal indicator are shaken in the gas jar to test the product.

Other examples follow the same pattern. Hydrogen burns with a squeaky pop: 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O. Phosphorus burns with a bright white flame and dense white smoke. Nitrogen does not react with oxygen under ordinary conditions, which is fortunate since air is mostly nitrogen and oxygen, but in the extreme heat of a car engine or a lightning bolt it forms nitrogen oxides.

A comparison with the previous topic makes the pattern clear. Magnesium on a scale gains mass when it burns because its solid oxide stays behind; charcoal on a scale loses mass because its gaseous oxide escapes. If the charcoal were burnt in a sealed flask filled with oxygen and weighed before and after, the mass would not change at all, which shows that conservation of mass holds for non-metals exactly as it does for metals.

The questions for this section are with you now: the general word equation, how non-metal oxides differ from metal oxides, and the deflagrating spoon method.

## Subtopic: Carbon Reacting with Oxygen

Carbon is found as charcoal, as graphite in pencils, as diamond, and as the main element in coal. When a piece of charcoal is heated until it glows and then lowered into a gas jar of oxygen, it glows brightly, bright orange to yellow-white, often with sparks, and gradually disappears. No solid product is left, apart from a little ash from impurities. A colourless, odourless gas forms.

Word equation: carbon + oxygen → carbon dioxide.

Balanced chemical equation: C + O2 → CO2. Check: carbon 1 and 1; oxygen 2 and 2. It is balanced as written.

Test for carbon dioxide: bubble the gas through clear limewater, a solution of calcium hydroxide. The limewater turns milky, because insoluble white calcium carbonate forms: Ca(OH)2 + CO2 → CaCO3 + H2O.

Mass: 12 grams of carbon combine with 32 grams of oxygen to form 12 + 32 = 44 grams of carbon dioxide. Charcoal on a scale appears to lose mass because the carbon dioxide escapes as a gas.

When the supply of oxygen is limited, carbon burns incompletely, forming carbon monoxide: 2C + O2 → 2CO. Check: carbon 2 and 2; oxygen 2 and 2. Carbon monoxide is colourless, odourless and very poisonous, because it binds to haemoglobin in red blood cells far more strongly than oxygen does and stops the blood carrying oxygen. Every winter in South Africa people die after bringing a burning brazier, called an imbawula, or a charcoal fire indoors to keep warm in a closed room. Fires and paraffin heaters must only be used with good ventilation.

Burning coal, wood, petrol and natural gas all release carbon dioxide, the main greenhouse gas linked to global warming, which you will study in Term 4.

The carbon cycle depends on this reaction and its reverse. Animals and plants release carbon dioxide in cellular respiration, which, like burning, combines carbon compounds with oxygen, though slowly and with the energy released in small, controlled steps inside the mitochondria. Plants take carbon dioxide back out of the air in photosynthesis. Burning fossil fuels adds carbon that was locked underground for millions of years, which is why the amount of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere has risen from about 280 parts per million before industry to well over 400 parts per million today.

The questions for this section are ready: observations, the balanced equation, the limewater test and carbon monoxide safety.

## Subtopic: Sulfur Reacting with Oxygen

Sulfur is a yellow, brittle non-metal found near volcanoes and recovered from crude oil and coal. When powdered sulfur is heated on a deflagrating spoon, it first melts into a red-brown liquid and then catches fire with a small, pale blue flame. Lowered into a gas jar of oxygen, it burns with a brighter, bright blue flame. A colourless gas with a sharp, choking smell forms.

Word equation: sulfur + oxygen → sulfur dioxide.

Balanced chemical equation: S + O2 → SO2. Check: sulfur 1 and 1; oxygen 2 and 2. It is balanced as written.

Mass: 32 grams of sulfur combine with 32 grams of oxygen to form 32 + 32 = 64 grams of sulfur dioxide.

Safety: sulfur dioxide irritates the eyes, nose, throat and lungs and can trigger asthma attacks, so the experiment must be done in a fume cupboard or a very well-ventilated room, using only a small amount of sulfur, and the gas jar must be covered as soon as the burning stops.

Sulfur dioxide has some uses. It is added in small, controlled amounts as a preservative to dried fruit, such as dried apricots, and to wine, and it bleaches some materials. It is also the first step in making sulfuric acid, the most widely used industrial chemical, at plants such as those at Secunda and in the Richards Bay area: sulfur dioxide is converted to sulfur trioxide, which is then turned into sulfuric acid.

As a pollutant, sulfur dioxide is released whenever coal containing sulfur is burnt. South African coal contains about one percent sulfur, so the coal-fired power stations of the Mpumalanga Highveld release large amounts of sulfur dioxide.

Notice that sulfur and carbon both give a single oxide when they burn in air or oxygen, written with simple one-to-one coefficients, so neither equation needs balancing: C + O2 → CO2 and S + O2 → SO2 already have the same atoms on both sides. This is a common examination trap, because learners sometimes add coefficients that are not needed. Always do the stocktake before changing anything.

## Subtopic: Non-Metal Oxides Are Acidic and the Error Museum

When the gas jars are tested with water and universal indicator, the results differ from the metal oxide jars. Carbon dioxide dissolves to form a weak acid, carbonic acid: CO2 + H2O → H2CO3. Universal indicator turns yellow to orange, showing a pH of about 5 to 6. Sulfur dioxide dissolves to form sulfurous acid: SO2 + H2O → H2SO3. The indicator turns orange to red, showing a stronger acid with a pH of about 2 to 3.

So the pattern is: metal oxides form bases; non-metal oxides form acids. Magnesium oxide turns indicator blue; carbon dioxide and sulfur dioxide turn it yellow, orange or red.

This explains acid rain. Rain dissolves carbon dioxide naturally, so even clean rain has a pH of about 5,6. Where sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from power stations, factories and vehicles are present, rain becomes far more acidic, damaging buildings, crops, forests, lakes and the health of people who breathe the air.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: carbon burning forms carbon and oxygen; it forms one product, carbon dioxide. Two: burning charcoal loses mass because atoms are destroyed; the product is a gas that escapes. Three: non-metal oxides are bases like metal oxides; they are acidic. Four: carbon monoxide is safe because it has no smell; that is precisely why it is dangerous. Five: sulfur dioxide is written SO; the formula is SO2.

Layout for a non-metal and oxygen answer: general word equation; specific word equation; balanced chemical equation; observations, such as the colour of the flame and the smell; the test for the product, such as limewater or indicator colour; and the safety precaution.

A useful summary table compares the two families. Metal oxides: usually solids, ionic, stay behind when the metal burns, form bases, turn indicator blue or purple, examples magnesium oxide and iron oxide. Non-metal oxides: usually gases, molecular, escape into the air, form acids, turn indicator yellow, orange or red, examples carbon dioxide and sulfur dioxide.

## Subtopic: The Vanishing Charcoal

Non-metals also grab oxygen, just like metals. The pattern is the same: non-metal plus oxygen gives non-metal oxide. Two things in, one thing out, named after the non-metal.

But there is a big difference in what comes out. A metal oxide is a solid powder that stays behind. A non-metal oxide is usually a gas that floats away. Watch a lump of charcoal in a braai: it glows, gets smaller and smaller, and finally almost nothing is left but a little ash. The carbon has not been destroyed. It has joined with oxygen to make carbon dioxide gas, which drifts off into the air.

In words: carbon plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide. In symbols: C + O2 → CO2. One carbon and two oxygens on each side.

How do you know the invisible gas is carbon dioxide? Bubble it through limewater. Clear limewater turns milky white.

There is a dangerous twist. If the fire does not get enough air, carbon makes carbon monoxide instead: 2C + O2 → 2CO. Carbon monoxide has no colour and no smell, and it stops your blood carrying oxygen. That is why you must never bring a burning imbawula or a charcoal fire into a closed room to keep warm. Every winter people die this way, often in their sleep.

If you trapped all that gas, nothing would be missing. Burn the charcoal inside a sealed glass jar full of oxygen, weigh the jar before and after, and the scale does not move. The carbon is still there, just hiding inside carbon dioxide molecules. The same carbon dioxide is what you breathe out, and what plants take in to make their food.

Remember the vanishing charcoal and try the questions for this part: the pattern, the limewater test and carbon monoxide.

## Subtopic: The Blue Flame

Sulfur is a yellow powder. Heat it on a long metal spoon and it melts into a dark red-brown liquid, then catches fire with a small blue flame. Lower it into a jar of pure oxygen and the blue flame becomes much brighter. What forms is an invisible gas with a sharp, choking smell, like a struck match.

In words: sulfur plus oxygen gives sulfur dioxide. In symbols: S + O2 → SO2. One sulfur and two oxygens on each side.

Because the gas hurts your eyes, nose and lungs, this is done in a fume cupboard, with only a tiny bit of sulfur.

Sulfur dioxide is not all bad. A little of it keeps dried apricots orange and stops them going mouldy. Factories use it to make sulfuric acid. But when coal is burnt at power stations, the small amount of sulfur in the coal turns into tonnes of sulfur dioxide, and that causes trouble in the air.

Notice something about both equations. C + O2 → CO2 and S + O2 → SO2 are already balanced. One atom of the non-metal, two oxygens each side. Do the count before you add any big numbers, so that you do not change something that was already right.

Keep the blue flame in mind and try this part's questions: what you see and smell, the equation, and the safety rule.

## Subtopic: The Acid Pattern

Shake a little water and a few drops of universal indicator into each jar after the burning.

Charcoal jar: the indicator goes yellow to orange. Carbon dioxide has made a weak acid.

Sulfur jar: the indicator goes orange to red. Sulfur dioxide has made a stronger acid.

Now remember the magnesium jar from the last topic: blue. Metal oxide, base.

So here is the pattern to learn. Metal oxides make bases. Non-metal oxides make acids.

That is exactly why rain near coal power stations in Mpumalanga can turn into acid rain. Sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides in the smoke dissolve in the raindrops and make them acidic. Acid rain eats away stone buildings and statues, damages plants and harms fish in lakes.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Non-metal plus oxygen gives a non-metal oxide, usually a gas. Carbon burns to carbon dioxide, C + O2 → CO2, which turns limewater milky; with too little air it makes poisonous carbon monoxide. Sulfur burns with a blue flame to choking sulfur dioxide, S + O2 → SO2, in a fume cupboard. Non-metal oxides make acids, metal oxides make bases, and that is the root of acid rain. Grade 10 to 12 chemistry builds on these reactions.

A quick memory trick: metals are on the left of the Periodic Table and make bases; non-metals are on the right and make acids. Left, base. Right, acid.

Hold the acid pattern in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: indicator colours, metal against non-metal oxides, and acid rain.
