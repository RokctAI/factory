### subtopic_1 — The General Reaction: Non-Metal + Oxygen → Non-Metal Oxide
**Assistant:** Why are most non-metal oxides gases, but metal oxides are solids?

**Tutor:** It comes down to the kind of particles and the forces between them. In a metal oxide, such as magnesium oxide, the metal atoms have given electrons to the oxygen atoms, forming positive and negative ions. These ions attract each other strongly in every direction and pack into a giant three-dimensional lattice, so a huge amount of energy is needed to separate them, and the substance is a solid with a very high melting point. Magnesium oxide melts at about 2850 degrees Celsius and is used to line furnaces. In a non-metal oxide, such as carbon dioxide or sulfur dioxide, the atoms share electrons in covalent bonds to form small, separate molecules. The bonds inside each molecule are strong, but the forces between neighbouring molecules are weak, so very little energy is needed to pull the molecules apart, and the substance is a gas at room temperature. Carbon dioxide only becomes a solid, dry ice, at minus 78 degrees Celsius. There are exceptions on the non-metal side. Water, hydrogen oxide, is a liquid because of unusually strong attractions between its molecules. Silicon dioxide, which is sand and quartz, is a solid because its atoms form a giant covalent network rather than small molecules. You will study these bonding types properly in Grades 10 and 11. For now, link small molecules with gases and giant lattices with solids.

### subtopic_2 — Carbon Reacting with Oxygen
**Assistant:** How exactly does carbon monoxide poison people?

**Tutor:** It takes the place of oxygen in your blood. Red blood cells carry oxygen around the body using a protein called haemoglobin, which picks up oxygen in the lungs and releases it in the tissues. Carbon monoxide molecules can attach to the same place on haemoglobin, and they attach roughly 200 times more strongly than oxygen does, so they are not easily released. As more haemoglobin becomes blocked by carbon monoxide, less oxygen reaches the brain, heart and muscles. The early symptoms are a headache, dizziness, tiredness and nausea, which people often mistake for flu or simply feeling sleepy, so they lie down, and in a closed room they may never wake up. Because the gas is colourless, odourless and tasteless, the senses give no warning. This is why carbon monoxide poisoning from braziers, charcoal fires, gas heaters, generators and car exhausts in closed garages is a serious problem in South Africa every winter, especially in informal settlements where people need warmth. The protections are simple: never burn anything indoors without good ventilation, never run a generator or car engine in a closed space, and if possible install a carbon monoxide alarm. Anyone with symptoms should be moved into fresh air immediately and taken for medical help, where they may be given pure oxygen to drive the carbon monoxide off the haemoglobin.

### subtopic_3 — Sulfur Reacting with Oxygen
**Assistant:** If sulfur dioxide is poisonous, how can it be safe in dried fruit?

**Tutor:** Because the dose makes the poison, and the amount in food is small and strictly controlled. Sulfur dioxide and related compounds called sulfites have been used for centuries to preserve food and wine. They kill or slow the growth of moulds, yeasts and bacteria, and they stop the browning reactions that would turn dried apricots and peaches dark brown, which is why sulfured dried apricots stay bright orange. The amount allowed in food is set by food safety regulations and is measured in milligrams per kilogram, far below the concentration that irritates the lungs when breathed in as a gas. Most people eat these amounts without any problem. Some people, particularly some with asthma, are sensitive to sulfites and can have breathing difficulties, which is why food labels must state when sulfites or sulfur dioxide have been added, often with the preservative number 220. The situation is different in the laboratory and around industry, where pure sulfur dioxide gas is breathed in directly and at much higher concentrations, so the fume cupboard is essential. The same substance can be useful and safe in one setting and harmful in another, and good science asks how much, for how long, and by what route a person is exposed.

### subtopic_7 — The Acid Pattern
**Assistant:** What is South Africa doing about acid rain from coal power stations?

**Tutor:** Several things, though progress has been slow and the problem is not yet solved. Most of South Africa's electricity comes from coal-fired power stations, many of them on the Mpumalanga Highveld, which is one of the areas with the highest sulfur dioxide pollution in the world, and the region has been declared a priority area for air quality. The main technical solution is flue gas desulfurisation, in which the waste gases from the boilers are sprayed with a slurry of crushed limestone or lime before they reach the chimney. The sulfur dioxide reacts with the base to form calcium sulfite and then calcium sulfate, which is gypsum, removing most of the sulfur dioxide. This is an application of the idea that non-metal oxides are acids and react with bases. The newest power station, Kusile, was built with this technology, while many older stations do not yet have it and have applied for extra time to meet emission limits. Other approaches include washing coal to remove some of the sulfur before it is burnt, using cleaner fuels, and building more renewable energy from wind and solar, which produces no sulfur dioxide at all. You will study electricity generation and alternative energy in Term 3, and neutralisation, including adding lime to soils and lakes affected by acid rain, later this term.
