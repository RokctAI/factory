### subtopic_1 — What Neutralisation Is
**Assistant:** If the salt is sodium chloride, is the neutral solution safe to drink?

**Tutor:** In principle the products are only salt and water, but you should never drink anything made in a laboratory, and there are good reasons for that rule. First, you can never be sure the neutralisation was exact. Near the end point a single drop changes the pH sharply, so the solution might still contain some hydrochloric acid or some sodium hydroxide, either of which would harm your mouth and throat. Second, laboratory glassware and chemicals are not kept food-clean; the beaker may have traces of other substances from earlier experiments. Third, the universal indicator itself is not meant to be swallowed. Fourth, the solution might be much more concentrated in salt than seawater, depending on the strengths of the acid and base used. That said, the chemistry is real: if pure hydrochloric acid and pure sodium hydroxide are reacted in exactly the right amounts, the product is genuinely a solution of sodium chloride. Industries do use neutralisation to make salts deliberately. Fertiliser factories make ammonium nitrate by neutralising nitric acid with ammonia, and potassium nitrate and ammonium sulfate are made in similar ways. In those factories, the amounts and purity are carefully controlled and tested. In the school laboratory, the safe thing to do with the neutral solution is to evaporate it to see the crystals, or to pour it down the sink with plenty of water.

### subtopic_2 — Following the pH During Neutralisation
**Assistant:** Why does the colour change so suddenly near pH 7?

**Tutor:** Because of the tenfold steps on the pH scale. Imagine you start with acid at pH 1, and you add base steadily. To raise the pH from 1 to 2, you must use up nine tenths of all the hydrogen ions present, which takes a lot of base. To go from 2 to 3, you must use up nine tenths of what is left, which is only a tenth as much, so it takes only a tenth as much base. From 3 to 4 takes a hundredth as much as the first step, and so on. As you approach pH 7 there are hardly any hydrogen ions left, so a tiny amount of base, sometimes a single drop, is enough to push the pH from about 4 all the way past 7 to about 10. On a graph of pH against the volume of base added, this shows as a nearly flat line at first, then a sudden near-vertical rise around the neutral point, then a flattening again at high pH. That steep section is what makes neutralisation so useful for measurement: because one drop gives a sharp colour change, a chemist can tell the exact volume of base needed very precisely, which is the basis of the titrations you will do in Grade 12. It also means you must add base very slowly as the colour reaches yellow, swirling after each drop.

### subtopic_3 — Naming Salts and Everyday Neutralisation
**Assistant:** Is baking soda in baking also a neutralisation?

**Tutor:** Yes, it is a closely related reaction, and it is the reason cakes and scones rise. Baking soda is sodium hydrogencarbonate, a mild base. When it meets an acid, it neutralises the acid and, because it is a hydrogencarbonate, it also releases carbon dioxide gas: acid plus hydrogencarbonate gives salt plus water plus carbon dioxide. In baking, the acid comes from ingredients such as buttermilk, amasi, yoghurt, lemon juice or honey, or from a powdered acid included in baking powder. Baking powder is simply baking soda already mixed with a dry acid, so that it reacts as soon as it is moistened and heated. The carbon dioxide forms bubbles in the batter, which expand in the heat of the oven and set as the cake cooks, giving a light, airy texture. If too much baking soda is used without enough acid, the extra base gives a bitter, soapy taste and can turn the crumb yellowish, which is a direct example of the properties of bases. The same fizzing reaction is used when bicarbonate of soda is sprinkled on an acid spill in the laboratory, and in some antacid powders that fizz in water. You will study reactions of acids with carbonates more fully in the next lessons, where the general equation is acid plus metal carbonate gives salt plus water plus carbon dioxide.

### subtopic_4 — Neutralisation Problems and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why do we need 2NaOH for sulfuric acid but only 1NaOH for hydrochloric acid?

**Tutor:** Because sulfuric acid has two hydrogen atoms that it can release as hydrogen ions, while hydrochloric acid has only one. Each hydroxide ion from sodium hydroxide neutralises one hydrogen ion to make one water molecule. One molecule of hydrochloric acid, HCl, releases one hydrogen ion, so one unit of NaOH is enough: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H2O. One molecule of sulfuric acid, H2SO4, can release two hydrogen ions, so it needs two units of NaOH, and two water molecules form: H2SO4 + 2NaOH → Na2SO4 + 2H2O. The salt formula also reflects this. The sulfate ion has a charge of 2−, so it needs two sodium ions, each 1+, to balance it, giving Na2SO4. You can check the balancing in the usual way: sodium 2 and 2; sulfur 1 and 1; hydrogen 2 + 2 = 4 on the left and 2 × 2 = 4 on the right; oxygen 4 + 2 = 6 on the left and 4 + 2 = 6 on the right. Acids are sometimes described by how many hydrogen ions each molecule can give: hydrochloric and nitric acids give one, sulfuric acid gives two, and phosphoric acid can give three. The same idea works for bases. Calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)2, has two hydroxide units, so one unit of it neutralises two of hydrochloric acid: Ca(OH)2 + 2HCl → CaCl2 + 2H2O.
