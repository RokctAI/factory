# Part 1 — Expert

This session describes the two common laboratory acids, hydrochloric acid and sulfuric acid, their formulae, properties, hazards and uses, and then sets out the classification of oxides and related compounds: non-metal oxides tend to be acidic, while metal oxides, metal hydroxides and metal carbonates are bases. The anchor facts are hydrochloric acid, HCl, found in the stomach at about pH 1 to 2 and sold as pool acid; sulfuric acid, H2SO4, used in car batteries and in fertiliser production; the acidic oxides CO2, SO2 and NO2; and the bases MgO, CaO, NaOH, Ca(OH)2 and CaCO3. The governing principle is that whether a compound is acidic or basic can be predicted from its composition, with metals giving bases and non-metal oxides giving acids.

## Subtopic: Hydrochloric Acid and Sulfuric Acid

Hydrochloric acid, HCl, is a solution of hydrogen chloride gas in water. Pure hydrogen chloride is a colourless gas with a sharp smell. Concentrated hydrochloric acid, about 32 to 37 percent hydrogen chloride, gives off choking fumes; the dilute acid used in school, often labelled 1 mol per litre or weaker, is a colourless liquid with a pH of about 0 to 1, which on the Grade 9 scale is treated as pH 1.

Sulfuric acid, H2SO4, is a dense, oily, colourless liquid when concentrated, about 98 percent. It has no smell. It is a powerful dehydrating agent, meaning it pulls water out of other substances: poured onto sugar, it removes the hydrogen and oxygen as water and leaves a column of black carbon, and it does the same to skin, paper and fabric, causing severe burns. Diluting concentrated sulfuric acid releases a great deal of heat, which is why acid is always added slowly to water, never water to acid.

Both are strong acids. When dissolved in water, practically all of their molecules release hydrogen ions, so even fairly dilute solutions have a low pH. Hydrochloric acid gives one hydrogen ion per molecule; sulfuric acid can give two.

The general properties of acids, which you have met, apply to both. They turn blue litmus red and universal indicator red. They react with bases to form a salt and water: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H2O and H2SO4 + 2NaOH → Na2SO4 + 2H2O. They react with reactive metals such as magnesium and zinc to release hydrogen gas: Mg + 2HCl → MgCl2 + H2. They react with carbonates to release carbon dioxide.

A third laboratory acid, nitric acid, HNO3, is also strong and is used to make fertilisers and explosives, but it is less common in Grade 9 practical work.

Laboratory bottles of both acids carry hazard symbols: the corrosive symbol, showing a liquid eating into a hand and a surface, and often the irritant or health hazard symbol. The label states the concentration, which tells you how dangerous the contents are. A bottle of dilute acid in a school is usually prepared by the teacher from a concentrated stock that is kept locked away. Learners should check the label before using any bottle, carry acid bottles with both hands, keep the stopper on when not pouring, and never return unused acid to the stock bottle, since it may have been contaminated.

The two acids also behave slightly differently in some reactions. Calcium carbonate reacts briskly with hydrochloric acid, but with sulfuric acid the reaction soon slows and stops, because the calcium sulfate formed is only slightly soluble and coats the surface of the solid. This is why hydrochloric acid, not sulfuric acid, is used to make carbon dioxide from marble chips in the laboratory.

## Subtopic: Uses of Hydrochloric and Sulfuric Acid

Hydrochloric acid in the body: the cells lining the stomach secrete hydrochloric acid, giving a pH of about 1 to 2. It helps the enzyme pepsin digest proteins and kills many bacteria swallowed with food. The stomach protects itself with a layer of mucus; when that protection fails, an ulcer can form.

Hydrochloric acid in industry and the home: it is sold at hardware and pool shops as pool acid, also called muriatic acid or spirits of salts, to lower the pH of swimming pools and to clean cement and mortar off new brickwork. Steel works use it to remove rust and scale from steel before it is galvanised or coated, a process called pickling. It is also used in food processing and to make many other chemicals.

Sulfuric acid is the most widely produced industrial chemical in the world, and the amount a country produces has long been used as a rough indicator of its industrial activity. Its main use is making fertilisers: phosphate rock, such as that mined at Phalaborwa in Limpopo, is treated with sulfuric acid to make soluble phosphate fertilisers, and ammonia is neutralised with sulfuric acid to make ammonium sulfate. Car batteries, the lead-acid batteries in most vehicles, contain sulfuric acid at about 30 to 40 percent strength as the electrolyte. In mining, sulfuric acid is used to dissolve uranium and copper from their ores. It is also used in oil refining, in making detergents, paints and paper, and in treating water.

South Africa produces millions of tonnes of sulfuric acid each year, partly from sulfur recovered at the Sasol plant in Secunda and partly from the sulfur dioxide released when metal ores are smelted, which turns a pollutant into a product.

Transporting these acids is regulated. Road tankers carrying sulfuric or hydrochloric acid display hazard diamonds and numbers so that emergency crews know immediately what they are dealing with if there is an accident, and spills are contained and neutralised with lime.

## Subtopic: Acidic and Basic Compounds

The reactions of this term can now be gathered into a single classification.

Non-metal oxides tend to be acidic. When they dissolve in water they form acids: CO2 + H2O → H2CO3, carbonic acid; SO2 + H2O → H2SO3, sulfurous acid; SO3 + H2O → H2SO4, sulfuric acid. Nitrogen dioxide, NO2, forms nitric acid. These reactions are the source of acid rain and of the fizz in cooldrinks. The word tend matters: some non-metal oxides, such as carbon monoxide and water itself, are neutral.

Metal oxides are bases. Some react with water to form hydroxides: CaO + H2O → Ca(OH)2 and MgO + H2O → Mg(OH)2. Others, such as copper oxide and iron oxide, do not dissolve in water but still react with acids to form a salt and water, which is what makes them bases.

Metal hydroxides are bases. Sodium hydroxide, potassium hydroxide and calcium hydroxide dissolve in water to give alkaline solutions; magnesium hydroxide dissolves only slightly but neutralises acids, which is why it is used in antacids.

Metal carbonates and hydrogencarbonates are bases. Calcium carbonate, which is limestone, marble and chalk, sodium carbonate, washing soda, and sodium hydrogencarbonate, bicarbonate of soda, all neutralise acids, and in doing so they release carbon dioxide: CaCO3 + 2HCl → CaCl2 + H2O + CO2.

A quick way to predict: look at the formula. If it starts with H and is followed by a non-metal or non-metal group, such as HCl, H2SO4 or HNO3, it is an acid. If it is the oxide of a non-metal, such as CO2 or SO2, it tends to be acidic. If it contains a metal combined with O, OH or CO3, such as MgO, NaOH or CaCO3, it is a base.

Notice how the position of an element in the Periodic Table predicts the behaviour of its oxide. Elements on the left, the metals of Groups 1 and 2 such as sodium, magnesium and calcium, form strongly basic oxides. Elements on the right, the non-metals such as carbon, nitrogen, sulfur and phosphorus, form acidic oxides. Moving from left to right across a period, the oxides change from basic to acidic, which is one of the patterns that makes the Periodic Table so useful.

## Subtopic: Classifying in Practice and the Error Museum

A classification question might give a list: SO2, MgO, KOH, H2SO4, CaCO3, CO2, Na2O, HCl. Sorted: acids and acidic oxides are SO2, H2SO4, CO2 and HCl; bases are MgO, KOH, CaCO3 and Na2O. A practical check is to shake each with water and universal indicator, if soluble: the acidic ones give red, orange or yellow; the soluble bases give blue or purple.

Predicting reactions follows directly. An acidic oxide reacts with a base: carbon dioxide reacts with limewater, Ca(OH)2 + CO2 → CaCO3 + H2O, which is exactly the limewater test. A basic oxide reacts with an acid: magnesium oxide dissolves in hydrochloric acid to form magnesium chloride and water. Two acids, or two bases, do not neutralise each other.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: sulfuric acid is safe because it has no smell; concentrated sulfuric acid is one of the most dangerous laboratory chemicals. Two: carbonates are acids because their names contain carbonic; carbonates are bases. Three: copper oxide is not a base because it does not dissolve in water; it is a base because it neutralises acids. Four: all oxides are acidic; metal oxides are basic. Five: hydrochloric acid in the stomach should not be there; it is needed for digestion and to kill bacteria.

Layout for a classification answer: list each substance under the heading acidic or basic; give a reason for each, such as non-metal oxide or metal hydroxide; and where asked, write the equation of its reaction with water or with an acid or base.

A second worked example asks for predictions. Will sulfur dioxide react with sodium hydroxide solution? Yes: an acidic oxide and a base, forming a salt, sodium sulfite, and water. Will carbon dioxide react with hydrochloric acid? No: both are acidic. Will calcium oxide react with sulfuric acid? Yes: a basic oxide and an acid, forming calcium sulfate and water. Will sodium hydroxide react with calcium carbonate? No: both are bases. The rule is simple: an acid or acidic oxide reacts with a base, and like does not neutralise like.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through two famous acids, acid makers and base makers, and a cheat sheet for formulae — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Two Famous Acids

In the school lab you will mostly meet two acids.

Hydrochloric acid, HCl. You already have some: your stomach makes it to digest food and kill germs. In shops it is sold as pool acid, used to adjust swimming pools and to clean cement off new bricks. Concentrated, it gives off stinging fumes. Dilute, it is the acid in most school experiments.

Sulfuric acid, H2SO4. It is the acid inside a car battery. Concentrated, it looks like thick oil and has no smell, which makes it sneaky. It grabs water out of anything: pour it on sugar and the sugar turns into a tower of black carbon. It does the same to skin. Factories use enormous amounts of it, mainly to make fertilisers so that farms can grow more food.

Both are strong acids. Both turn blue litmus red, fizz with magnesium to give hydrogen, fizz with limestone to give carbon dioxide, and cancel bases to give salt and water.

The golden rules: goggles on, use dilute acid, and when diluting, acid goes into water, never water into acid.

Every bottle has a label with a corrosive symbol. Read it before you pour, carry the bottle with two hands, and never pour leftover acid back into the bottle.

## Subtopic: Acid Makers and Base Makers

Here is the big pattern of the whole term, in one line: non-metals make acids, metals make bases.

Acid makers. Burn a non-metal and you get its oxide, carbon dioxide or sulfur dioxide. Dissolve that oxide in water and you get an acid. That is why fizzy cooldrinks are acidic and why smoke from coal power stations causes acid rain.

Base makers. Burn a metal and you get its oxide, like magnesium oxide. Put that oxide in water and you get a hydroxide, a base. Even metal oxides that do not dissolve, like the black copper oxide, still cancel acids, so they are bases too.

The base family has three members: metal oxides, metal hydroxides and metal carbonates. Limestone, chalk, washing soda and bicarbonate of soda are all carbonates, and they all cancel acids. When they do, they fizz, because they give off carbon dioxide.

Look at the Periodic Table and the pattern jumps out. Metals on the left: sodium, magnesium, calcium. Their oxides are bases. Non-metals on the right: carbon, nitrogen, sulfur. Their oxides are acids. Left makes bases, right makes acids.

## Subtopic: The Formula Cheat Sheet

You can often tell acid or base just from the formula.

Starts with H, then a non-metal: acid. HCl, H2SO4, HNO3.

A non-metal stuck to oxygen: tends to be acidic. CO2, SO2.

A metal stuck to O, OH or CO3: base. MgO, NaOH, Ca(OH)2, CaCO3.

Test yourself. SO2: non-metal oxide, acidic. KOH: metal hydroxide, base. CaCO3: metal carbonate, base. HCl: acid. Na2O: metal oxide, base.

And opposites react. Breathe out carbon dioxide, an acidic oxide, through limewater, a base, and it turns milky. Drop magnesium oxide, a base, into hydrochloric acid and it dissolves to make a salt and water.

Pocket summary of the lesson. The two common lab acids are hydrochloric acid, HCl, in your stomach and in pool acid, and sulfuric acid, H2SO4, in car batteries and fertiliser factories. Both are strong and must be handled with care. Non-metal oxides tend to be acidic. Metal oxides, hydroxides and carbonates are bases. Opposites neutralise each other. Grade 10 to 12 chemistry builds on this classification.

Quick predictions. Sulfur dioxide and sodium hydroxide: acid maker plus base, so they react. Carbon dioxide and hydrochloric acid: both acidic, so nothing. Calcium oxide and sulfuric acid: base plus acid, so they react. Two acids never cancel, and two bases never cancel.
