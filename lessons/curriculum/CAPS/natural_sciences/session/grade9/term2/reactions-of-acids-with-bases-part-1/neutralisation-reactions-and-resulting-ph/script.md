# Part 1 — Expert

This session defines neutralisation, gives its general word equation and balanced chemical examples, shows how the pH changes as a base is added to an acid, explains how to name the salt formed, and describes everyday applications. The anchor examples are HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H2O, H2SO4 + 2NaOH → Na2SO4 + 2H2O, and the colour sequence of universal indicator from red through orange and yellow to green at pH 7 as sodium hydroxide is added to hydrochloric acid. The governing principle is that an acid and a base react to form a salt and water, and that the resulting pH depends on whether the acid or the base is in excess, being exactly 7 only when they are in the correct proportions.

## Subtopic: What Neutralisation Is

A neutralisation reaction is a reaction between an acid and a base in which the acidic and basic properties cancel each other out. The general word equation is: acid + base → salt + water.

The particle explanation is short. An acid in water releases hydrogen ions, H+. An alkali in water releases hydroxide ions, OH−. When they meet, the two ions combine to form water molecules: H+ + OH− → H2O. Since the ions responsible for acidity and basicity have been used up, the solution is no longer acidic or basic. The other ions, a metal ion from the base and a non-metal or polyatomic ion from the acid, remain in solution and together make up the salt.

In chemistry the word salt means any ionic compound formed when the hydrogen of an acid is replaced by a metal. Table salt, sodium chloride, is just one example. Others include potassium nitrate, used in fertilisers, calcium sulfate, which is gypsum and plaster of Paris, and magnesium sulfate, Epsom salts.

Examples with balanced chemical equations: hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide, HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H2O. Nitric acid and potassium hydroxide, HNO3 + KOH → KNO3 + H2O. Sulfuric acid and sodium hydroxide, H2SO4 + 2NaOH → Na2SO4 + 2H2O, where two hydroxide units are needed because sulfuric acid has two hydrogen atoms to give up. Hydrochloric acid and magnesium hydroxide, 2HCl + Mg(OH)2 → MgCl2 + 2H2O.

Neutralisation releases energy as heat, so it is exothermic: the mixture warms up. With dilute solutions the rise is a few degrees; with concentrated solutions it can be dangerous, which is one reason concentrated acids and bases are never mixed in school.

Neutralisation can be recognised in a practical in several ways: an indicator changes colour towards neutral, the temperature of the mixture rises, the pH measured with a meter moves towards 7, and if the solution is evaporated a solid salt is left. With an insoluble base such as copper oxide or magnesium hydroxide powder, a further sign is that the solid disappears as it reacts with the acid, until no more will dissolve once the acid is used up.

It is useful to classify the bases you will meet. Metal hydroxides, such as sodium hydroxide, potassium hydroxide and calcium hydroxide, react with acids to give a salt and water. Metal oxides, such as magnesium oxide and copper oxide, also give a salt and water. Metal carbonates and hydrogencarbonates, such as calcium carbonate and sodium hydrogencarbonate, give a salt, water and carbon dioxide gas. All three types are covered in the next lessons.

## Subtopic: Following the pH During Neutralisation

The progress of a neutralisation can be followed with universal indicator or a pH meter.

The standard demonstration: pour about 10 millilitres of dilute hydrochloric acid into a beaker or conical flask, add a few drops of universal indicator, and note the red colour, pH about 1. Then add dilute sodium hydroxide solution from a dropper or burette, a few drops at a time, swirling after each addition.

The colour moves in stages: red, then orange as the pH rises to about 4 or 5, then yellow at about 6, then green at 7. Green means the acid has been exactly neutralised: the number of hydroxide ions added equals the number of hydrogen ions present at the start, and the solution contains only salt and water. If more sodium hydroxide is added after the green point, the colour turns blue and then purple, because there are now spare hydroxide ions and the solution is basic.

So the resulting pH depends on the proportions: acid in excess, pH below 7; exact amounts, pH 7; base in excess, pH above 7. A graph of pH against volume of base added starts low, rises slowly at first, jumps steeply near the neutral point, and then levels off at a high pH. The steep jump is why a single drop can change the colour from yellow-green to blue near the end.

If the neutral solution is then gently evaporated, crystals of the salt remain. Evaporating the green sodium chloride solution in an evaporating basin leaves white sodium chloride crystals, coloured slightly by indicator unless the experiment is repeated without it.

Precise neutralisations, measuring exact volumes with a burette, are called titrations, and they are a central skill in Grade 12 Physical Sciences.

A temperature probe tells the same story from a different angle. If the temperature of the mixture is recorded after each addition of base, it rises steadily while acid and base are reacting and reaches its highest value at the neutral point. After that, adding more base only adds cooler liquid, so the temperature begins to fall. The peak of a temperature graph and the green colour of the indicator therefore occur at the same volume, which is a neat check that the two methods agree.

Accuracy improves with a few simple habits: rinse the dropper or burette with the base before filling it, swirl after every addition, place the flask on a white tile to see the colour clearly, and repeat the experiment to check that the volume needed is the same each time.

## Subtopic: Naming Salts and Everyday Neutralisation

The name of a salt has two parts. The first part comes from the metal in the base; the second part comes from the acid. Hydrochloric acid gives chlorides. Sulfuric acid gives sulfates. Nitric acid gives nitrates. Carbonic acid gives carbonates.

So: sodium hydroxide with hydrochloric acid gives sodium chloride; potassium hydroxide with nitric acid gives potassium nitrate; magnesium hydroxide with sulfuric acid gives magnesium sulfate; calcium hydroxide with hydrochloric acid gives calcium chloride.

Neutralisation in everyday life:

Indigestion. The stomach contains hydrochloric acid at about pH 1 to 2. Too much acid causes heartburn and indigestion. Antacid tablets and medicines contain mild bases, such as magnesium hydroxide, aluminium hydroxide, calcium carbonate or sodium hydrogencarbonate, which neutralise the excess acid.

Teeth. Bacteria in plaque produce acid that dissolves enamel. Toothpaste is slightly basic, about pH 9, and helps neutralise the acid.

Soil. Most crops grow best at pH 6 to 7. Farmers spread agricultural lime, calcium carbonate, or slaked lime, calcium hydroxide, on acidic soil to raise the pH. This is covered in more detail in Part II of acids and bases.

Water and industry. Acidic waste water from mines and factories is neutralised with lime before release. Swimming pools are kept close to neutral by adding small amounts of acid or base.

Spills. A small acid spill in a laboratory is neutralised by sprinkling it with sodium hydrogencarbonate, which fizzes until the acid is used up.

Insect stings are often quoted as examples, but the chemistry of venom is complicated and most of the pain comes from proteins, not acidity, so rinsing and cooling are the main first aid. The clearest examples to use in answers are antacids, toothpaste, liming soil, treating acidic waste water and neutralising spills.

## Subtopic: Neutralisation Problems and the Error Museum

Examination questions ask you to predict products, write equations, interpret colour changes and explain applications. A worked example: a learner adds sodium hydroxide solution to 20 millilitres of hydrochloric acid containing universal indicator. After 15 millilitres the indicator is yellow; after 20 millilitres it is green; after 25 millilitres it is blue. Interpretation: at 15 millilitres the acid is still in excess and the pH is about 6; at 20 millilitres the acid has been exactly neutralised and the pH is 7; at 25 millilitres the base is in excess and the pH is above 7. The salt formed is sodium chloride, and the equation is HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H2O.

Not every neutralisation gives exactly pH 7 at the point where the amounts match. When a strong acid reacts with a weak base, or a weak acid with a strong base, the salt solution can be slightly acidic or slightly basic. At Grade 9 you may assume that matching amounts of a strong acid and strong base give pH 7.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: neutralisation always produces table salt; it produces a salt, which depends on the acid and base. Two: acid + base → salt + hydrogen; the second product is water. Three: adding more base always makes the solution more neutral; past the neutral point it becomes basic. Four: the product of a neutralisation is always pH 7; only when the amounts are exactly right. Five: H2SO4 + NaOH → Na2SO4 + H2O is balanced; it needs 2NaOH and 2H2O.

Layout for a neutralisation answer: general word equation; specific word equation with the salt named; balanced chemical equation; the indicator colour change and the resulting pH; and an explanation in terms of excess acid or base where relevant.

A second worked example uses a solid base. Magnesium hydroxide powder is added a spatula at a time to dilute sulfuric acid with universal indicator. At first the powder disappears and the colour moves from red towards green. Once the acid is used up, extra powder no longer disappears but collects at the bottom of the beaker, which shows that there is no acid left for it to react with, and the indicator moves past green towards blue. The salt is magnesium sulfate, and the equation is H2SO4 + Mg(OH)2 → MgSO4 + 2H2O.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through a cancelling act, a colour walk and salts in your daily life — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Cancelling Act

Acids and bases are opposites. Put them together and they cancel each other out. That cancelling is called neutralisation.

What comes out? Always two things: a salt and water. Acid plus base gives salt plus water.

The famous example: hydrochloric acid plus sodium hydroxide gives sodium chloride plus water. Sodium chloride is the salt on your chips. So a dangerous acid and a dangerous base can, in exactly the right amounts, make salty water. In symbols: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H2O.

What is really happening? The acid carries little hydrogen particles, H+. The base carries hydroxide particles, OH−. When they meet, they snap together into water. Once they have paired off, there is nothing acidic or basic left.

In chemistry, salt does not only mean table salt. Any compound made this way is a salt. Epsom salts, plaster of Paris and some fertilisers are all salts.

You can feel neutralisation too: the beaker gets warm, because the reaction gives off heat.

Bases come in three families that all cancel acids. Hydroxides, like sodium hydroxide. Oxides, like magnesium oxide. Carbonates, like limestone and bicarbonate of soda, which fizz as they work because they give off carbon dioxide as well.

## Subtopic: The Colour Walk

Put some hydrochloric acid in a beaker and add a few drops of universal indicator. It goes red, because the pH is about 1.

Now add sodium hydroxide, a few drops at a time, swirling as you go. Watch the colour walk: red, then orange, then yellow, then green. Green means pH 7. The acid has been exactly cancelled. Stop there and you have salty water.

Keep adding sodium hydroxide and the colour walks on: blue, then purple. Now there is too much base, and the solution is basic.

So the answer to what pH do you get depends on how much of each you add. Not enough base: still acidic, below 7. Just right: neutral, 7. Too much base: basic, above 7.

Near the end, one drop can change yellow-green to blue, so go slowly.

If you gently boil away the water from the green solution, white salt crystals appear in the dish.

A thermometer in the beaker tells the same story. It warms up while acid and base are cancelling, and is warmest at the green point. After that, extra base just cools it slightly.

## Subtopic: Salts and Neutralisation in Your Day

Naming a salt is easy. First name: the metal from the base. Surname: from the acid. Hydrochloric acid gives chloride. Sulfuric acid gives sulfate. Nitric acid gives nitrate. So magnesium hydroxide and sulfuric acid make magnesium sulfate.

Neutralisation is everywhere. Heartburn? Your stomach has too much acid, and an antacid tablet, a mild base, cancels it. Toothpaste is slightly basic and helps fight the acid that bacteria make on your teeth. Farmers spread lime on sour soil so that crops grow better. Mines neutralise acidic water with lime before it reaches rivers. And if acid is spilt in the lab, a sprinkle of bicarbonate of soda fizzes and cancels it.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Acid plus base gives salt plus water. HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H2O. The H+ from the acid and the OH− from the base make water. With universal indicator the colour walks from red to green at pH 7, and on to blue and purple if there is too much base. Salt names come from the metal and the acid: chloride, sulfate, nitrate. Antacids, toothpaste and lime all neutralise. Grade 10 to 12 chemistry turns this into precise titrations.

Try a few names yourself. Potassium hydroxide and nitric acid: potassium nitrate, a fertiliser. Calcium hydroxide and hydrochloric acid: calcium chloride. Sodium hydroxide and sulfuric acid: sodium sulfate. Just remember that sulfuric acid needs two sodium hydroxides, because it has two hydrogens to cancel.
