### subtopic_1 — Hydrochloric Acid and Sulfuric Acid
**Assistant:** Why does the stomach not digest itself with all that hydrochloric acid?

**Tutor:** Because it has several layers of protection that work together. The most important is a thick layer of mucus, a slippery gel made by cells in the stomach lining, which coats the inner surface. The cells under the mucus also release hydrogencarbonate ions, a mild base, into the mucus, so the acid is neutralised before it reaches the stomach wall, and the pH right next to the cells stays close to neutral even while the pH in the middle of the stomach is about 1 to 2. Second, the cells of the stomach lining are replaced very rapidly, every few days, so any damaged cells are shed and renewed. Third, the protein-digesting enzyme pepsin is released in an inactive form and only switched on by the acid inside the stomach space, so it does not attack the cells that make it. When this protection breaks down, the acid and enzymes can eat into the lining and cause a stomach ulcer, a painful sore. Most ulcers are caused by a bacterium, Helicobacter pylori, which weakens the mucus layer, or by frequent use of some painkillers. The discovery of the bacterium earned two Australian scientists the Nobel Prize in 2005. Acid that escapes upwards into the oesophagus, which has no such protection, causes the burning feeling of heartburn, and that is what antacids neutralise.

### subtopic_2 — Uses of Hydrochloric and Sulfuric Acid
**Assistant:** What makes concentrated sulfuric acid so much more dangerous than the dilute acid?

**Tutor:** Concentrated sulfuric acid causes harm in two ways at once, and dilution removes most of both. First, it is a strong acid, so it attacks tissue like any strong acid. Second, and more important, the concentrated acid is about 98 percent sulfuric acid with almost no water, and it attracts water so strongly that it pulls water out of whatever it touches. Sugar, paper, cotton and skin are all made of compounds containing hydrogen and oxygen; concentrated sulfuric acid removes these as water and leaves black carbon behind. That is the dramatic sugar experiment, done only by teachers in a fume cupboard, which produces a hot, steaming column of black carbon. On skin, the same dehydration happens together with intense heat, because sulfuric acid releases a great deal of heat when it mixes with water. This heat is also why adding water to concentrated acid is so dangerous: the first drops of water boil instantly and spray acid out of the container. Adding acid slowly to a large amount of water spreads the heat through the water safely. Dilute sulfuric acid of the strength used in school still needs goggles and care but does not dehydrate in this way. If concentrated acid touches the skin, the treatment is to rinse with large amounts of running water for a long time, because the water carries away both the acid and the heat.

### subtopic_3 — Acidic and Basic Compounds
**Assistant:** If carbon monoxide is a non-metal oxide, why is it not acidic?

**Tutor:** Because the rule is that non-metal oxides tend to be acidic, not that all of them are. Whether an oxide forms an acid depends on whether it reacts with water and bases in the right way, and a few non-metal oxides do not. Carbon monoxide, CO, barely dissolves in water and does not react with it to form an acid, nor does it neutralise ordinary bases, so it is classed as a neutral oxide. Dinitrogen monoxide, N2O, the laughing gas used in dentistry, and nitrogen monoxide, NO, are also neutral oxides. Water itself, which is hydrogen oxide, is neutral. In contrast, carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide, sulfur trioxide, nitrogen dioxide and phosphorus oxides all react with water to form acids, which is why they are described as acidic oxides. There is a pattern to these exceptions: the neutral oxides tend to be those in which the non-metal has combined with relatively little oxygen. You will also meet a few metal oxides, such as aluminium oxide and zinc oxide, that can act as both acids and bases, called amphoteric oxides, in later grades. So when you classify substances, use the general rule, but remember that chemistry is full of interesting exceptions, and an examination will normally choose clear examples such as CO2, SO2, MgO and CaO.

### subtopic_4 — Classifying in Practice and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why is limestone a base when it does not contain hydroxide?

**Tutor:** Because a base is defined by what it does, not by whether it contains hydroxide. A base is a substance that reacts with an acid and neutralises it, forming a salt and water. Limestone, which is calcium carbonate, CaCO3, does exactly that: when it meets hydrochloric acid, the carbonate ions take the hydrogen ions from the acid, forming carbonic acid, which immediately breaks down into water and carbon dioxide gas. The overall equation is CaCO3 + 2HCl → CaCl2 + H2O + CO2. The acid has been used up, the pH rises, and a salt, calcium chloride, is left in solution, which are all the signs of neutralisation. The extra product, carbon dioxide, causes the fizzing that distinguishes carbonates from oxides and hydroxides. This is why limestone is so useful. Farmers grind it into agricultural lime and spread it on acidic soils. Power stations with scrubbers use limestone slurry to remove sulfur dioxide from their waste gases. Limestone also explains some damage. Acid rain dissolves limestone and marble buildings and statues. Caves in limestone and dolomite areas, such as the Sterkfontein caves in the Cradle of Humankind and the Cango Caves near Oudtshoorn, were carved over very long periods by rainwater containing dissolved carbon dioxide, a weak acid, slowly dissolving the rock.
