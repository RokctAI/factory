### subtopic_1 — Acids, Bases and Neutral Substances
**Assistant:** If acids and bases both burn skin, how is a base different from an acid?

**Tutor:** They burn in different ways, and they are chemical opposites. An acid releases hydrogen ions in water, and those ions attack many substances: they react with metals to release hydrogen gas, dissolve carbonates such as limestone and marble with fizzing, and break down proteins in skin. A base, or an alkali in solution, releases hydroxide ions, which react with fats and oils and break them into soap-like substances, and also break down proteins. That is why a dilute alkali feels slippery: it is turning a tiny amount of the oil on your fingers into soap. It is also why strong bases are especially dangerous to the eyes, because they keep penetrating into the tissue rather than forming a barrier, so an alkali splash in the eye is a medical emergency needing long rinsing. The crucial difference for chemistry is what happens when an acid and a base meet. The hydrogen ions from the acid and the hydroxide ions from the base combine to form water, and the two cancel each other's properties, producing a salt and water. That reaction, neutralisation, is the subject of the next topic and has many uses, from indigestion tablets to treating acidic soil. So acids and bases are both reactive and can both be corrosive, but they react in opposite ways and can be used to neutralise each other.

### subtopic_2 — The pH Scale from 1 to 14
**Assistant:** Why does the scale go from 1 to 14 and not 1 to 100?

**Tutor:** Because pH is not a direct measure of the amount of acid. It is a compressed way of writing a quantity that varies over an enormous range. The concentration of hydrogen ions in a strong acid can be more than ten trillion times greater than in a strong base. Writing those concentrations directly would need numbers with many zeros, such as 0,1 or 0,0000001 or 0,00000000000001. The pH scale replaces each of these with a simple number by counting the powers of ten: 0,1 becomes pH 1, 0,0000001 becomes pH 7, and so on. That is why each step is a factor of ten, and why the scale is short. The neutral value is 7 because, in pure water at 25 degrees Celsius, the concentration of hydrogen ions is exactly one ten-millionth of the standard unit, which is ten to the power of minus seven. The value 14 comes from the same property of water, which links the hydrogen ion and hydroxide ion concentrations so that their pH-style values always add up to 14. The scale can extend slightly beyond 1 and 14 for very concentrated acids and bases, but the range from 1 to 14 covers almost every solution you will meet. You will use the mathematics behind it, called logarithms, in Grade 11 mathematics and Grade 12 chemistry.

### subtopic_3 — pH in Everyday Life and Safe Handling
**Assistant:** Does drinking a fizzy cooldrink really change the pH in my mouth?

**Tutor:** Yes, for a while, and doing it often is one of the main causes of tooth damage in teenagers. Most fizzy cooldrinks have a pH of about 2,5 to 3, because they contain carbonic acid from the dissolved carbon dioxide, and many also contain phosphoric acid or citric acid for flavour. Tooth enamel is made mostly of a calcium phosphate mineral that starts to dissolve when the pH at the tooth surface drops below about 5,5. Every sip lowers the pH in your mouth, and saliva then takes around twenty to thirty minutes to bring it back up, because saliva contains natural bases that neutralise acid and minerals that help repair enamel. If you sip a cooldrink slowly over an hour or two, your teeth sit in acid the whole time and never get a chance to recover. The sugar adds a second attack, because bacteria in plaque feed on it and make lactic acid. Practical advice that follows from the chemistry: drink cooldrinks with a meal rather than sipping all day, rinse your mouth with water afterwards, use a straw, wait a while before brushing, because acid-softened enamel can be brushed away, and use fluoride toothpaste, which has a pH of about 9 and makes enamel more resistant to acid.

### subtopic_4 — Interpreting pH Data and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Can I tell if a liquid is an acid or a base just by looking or smelling?

**Tutor:** No, and trying to do so can be dangerous. Most acids and bases in solution are clear and colourless, exactly like water. Hydrochloric acid, sulfuric acid, sodium hydroxide solution and pure water all look identical in a test tube. A few have recognisable smells, such as the sharp smell of vinegar or the strong smell of ammonia, but sniffing directly from a container can damage the lining of your nose and lungs, so in the laboratory you never smell a substance directly. If you are told to note a smell, you waft a little of the vapour towards your nose with your hand from a distance. Taste is never used, because even a small amount of a strong acid or base can cause serious burns. This is exactly why scientists use indicators and pH meters. An indicator changes colour depending on the pH, so it makes an invisible property visible, and a pH meter gives a numerical reading. It is also why every container in a laboratory and every cleaning product at home must be clearly labelled, and why chemicals should never be stored in old cooldrink bottles, which has caused children to drink drain cleaner or paraffin by mistake. In the next lesson you will learn to use universal indicator and even make your own indicator from red cabbage.
