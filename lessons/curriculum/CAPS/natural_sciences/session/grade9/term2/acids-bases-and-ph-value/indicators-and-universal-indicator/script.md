# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains what an acid-base indicator is, describes the colours of the common laboratory indicators, sets out the colour range of universal indicator and how to use it to estimate pH, and shows how natural indicators can be extracted from plants such as red cabbage. The anchor facts are litmus, red in acid and blue in base; phenolphthalein, colourless in acid and neutral solutions and pink in base; universal indicator, red at pH 1 to 3 through orange, yellow, green at pH 7, blue and purple at pH 11 to 14; and red cabbage extract, red or pink in acid, purple when neutral and green to yellow in base. The governing principle is that an indicator is a substance whose colour depends on the pH of the solution it is in, so it makes the invisible property of acidity visible.

## Subtopic: What an Indicator Is: Litmus and Others

An indicator is a substance that has one colour in an acidic solution and a different colour in a basic solution. Most indicators are weak acids or weak bases themselves, made of large molecules whose structure, and therefore colour, changes slightly when they gain or lose a hydrogen ion. Because that change depends on how many hydrogen ions are present, the colour depends on the pH.

Litmus is the oldest indicator, extracted from lichens. It is supplied as paper strips or as a solution. Blue litmus turns red in an acid. Red litmus turns blue in a base. In a neutral solution, neither changes colour. A useful memory aid: acids turn blue litmus red, bases turn red litmus blue, so a test needs both colours to tell all three groups apart. If blue litmus stays blue and red litmus stays red, the solution is neutral.

Phenolphthalein is colourless in acidic and neutral solutions and turns pink in basic solutions, from about pH 8 or above. It cannot tell an acid from a neutral solution, but it is excellent for showing the exact moment a base is neutralised, because the pink suddenly disappears.

Methyl orange is red in acidic solutions with a pH below about 3 and yellow above about 4,5, with orange in between.

Each of these indicators changes colour over a narrow pH range, so it can show only which side of that range a solution is on. Litmus tells you acid, neutral or base; it cannot tell you whether an acid has pH 1 or pH 5.

When testing, dip a clean glass rod into the solution and touch it onto the indicator paper, rather than dipping the paper directly into the bottle, so that the solution is not contaminated with paper fibres and indicator.

A worked example of reasoning with litmus. Solution X turns blue litmus red and leaves red litmus red: X is acidic. Solution Y leaves blue litmus blue and turns red litmus blue: Y is basic. Solution Z leaves both papers unchanged: Z is neutral. Now suppose only blue litmus is available, and solution W leaves it blue. W could be neutral or basic; a second test, with red litmus or phenolphthalein, is needed to decide. Good answers state which paper changed, to what colour, and what that shows.

## Subtopic: Universal Indicator and Its Colours

Universal indicator is a mixture of several indicators, each changing colour at a different pH. Together they give a smooth series of colours across the whole pH scale, so universal indicator shows not just whether a solution is acidic or basic but approximately how strongly.

The colours, from acidic to basic, follow roughly the order of the rainbow: red at pH 1 to 3, a strong acid; orange at pH 4 to 5; yellow at pH 6, a weak acid; green at pH 7, neutral; blue at pH 8 to 10, a weak base; and purple or violet at pH 11 to 14, a strong base. Different manufacturers' mixtures differ slightly in exact shades, so every bottle or packet of paper comes with a colour chart.

To use it: add two or three drops of universal indicator solution to a few millilitres of the test solution in a test tube, or touch the solution onto a strip of universal indicator paper, then compare the colour immediately with the chart and read off the pH. The result is an estimate, usually to the nearest whole number. For a precise reading to one or two decimal places, a pH meter is used.

Typical results: lemon juice, red to orange; vinegar, orange; tap water, green or yellow-green; bicarbonate of soda solution, blue-green to blue; soap solution, blue; household ammonia, purple; drain cleaner, dark purple.

Universal indicator is also used to follow a neutralisation, as you will see in the next topic: adding a base drop by drop to an acid containing universal indicator, the colour moves from red through orange and yellow to green, and past green to blue if too much base is added.

Reading the chart well takes care. Look at the colour against a white background, in good light, as soon as the indicator has been added, because some colours drift as the solution stands or as carbon dioxide from the air dissolves in it. Use the same number of drops each time, since too much indicator darkens every colour and too little makes the colours pale. When a colour falls between two chart colours, record the pH as a range, such as 5 to 6, rather than guessing a single number.

## Subtopic: Natural Indicators

Many plants contain coloured substances that act as indicators. The best known are the anthocyanins, the purple, red and blue pigments in red cabbage, beetroot, red onion skins, black grapes, blueberries, red rose petals and hibiscus flowers.

Making red cabbage indicator: chop a few leaves of red cabbage finely, cover them with hot water, stir and leave to soak for about ten to fifteen minutes until the water is deep purple, then strain and keep the liquid. Hot water from a kettle is enough; a blender also works. The indicator should be used within a day or two, or kept in a fridge, as it slowly spoils.

Red cabbage colours: red to pink in strong acids such as lemon juice; pink to violet in weak acids such as vinegar; purple in neutral solutions such as water; blue to blue-green in weak bases such as bicarbonate of soda solution; green in stronger bases such as washing soda; and yellow in very strong bases such as drain cleaner. Because it changes through so many colours, red cabbage acts as a home-made universal indicator.

Other natural indicators. Turmeric, the yellow spice in curry powder, stays yellow in acid and neutral solutions and turns red-brown in bases; a curry stain on a white shirt turns red when it is washed with soap. Beetroot juice is red in acid and turns yellowish or purple in base. Tea becomes lighter when lemon juice is added, because its pigments are indicators too. In nature, hydrangea flowers are blue in acidic soil and pink in alkaline soil.

Natural indicators have the advantage of being cheap, safe and readily available, which makes them ideal for home experiments and schools without laboratory supplies. Their disadvantages are that their colours are less sharp, vary from one batch to the next, and fade over time.

Indicators were discovered long before anyone understood pH. In the 1600s the English scientist Robert Boyle described how the juices of violets and other plants turned red in acids and green in alkalis, and he used them to classify substances. Litmus has been used since the Middle Ages, when it was extracted from lichens and also used as a dye. Today, laboratory indicators are mostly made synthetically so that their colours are reliable from one bottle to the next.

## Subtopic: Investigating with Indicators and the Error Museum

A typical investigation tests household substances. Aim: to classify household substances as acidic, neutral or basic and estimate their pH. Method: place equal small volumes of each substance in labelled test tubes; add the same number of drops of indicator to each; record the colour; use the chart to estimate pH. Variables: the substance is the independent variable, the colour and pH the dependent variable, and the volume of substance, number of drops of indicator and temperature are controlled. A test tube of distilled water with indicator serves as a control for comparing colours.

Results are recorded in a table with columns for substance, colour with indicator, estimated pH and classification. For example: lemon juice, red, about 2, acidic; distilled water, green, 7, neutral; soap solution, blue, about 9 to 10, basic.

Coloured substances such as cooldrinks, tea or juice can mask the indicator colour; for these, indicator paper or a pH meter gives a more reliable result.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: acids turn red litmus blue; acids turn blue litmus red. Two: phenolphthalein turns pink in acid; it turns pink only in bases. Three: green universal indicator means a strong base; green means neutral, pH 7. Four: one strip of blue litmus can classify every solution; blue litmus that stays blue could be neutral or basic, so red litmus is also needed. Five: natural indicators do not work properly; they work, just with less precise colours.

Layout for an indicator answer: name the indicator, state the colour observed, state what the colour shows about the pH, and classify the substance. For universal indicator, include the estimated pH from the chart.

A safety note for indicator investigations: household substances such as bleach, drain cleaner and oven cleaner are strong bases and must only be tested by the teacher or with gloves and goggles, in very small amounts. Phenolphthalein solution is usually made up in alcohol, which is flammable, so it must be kept away from flames.

## Subtopic: Colour Spies

Most acids and bases look exactly like water. You cannot see the difference, and you must never taste or sniff to find out. So chemists use spies: indicators. An indicator is a substance that changes colour depending on whether it is in an acid or a base.

The most famous spy is litmus. It comes on little strips of paper, red ones and blue ones. Here is how it works. Acid turns blue litmus red. Base turns red litmus blue. If neither strip changes, the liquid is neutral. That is why you need both colours.

Another spy is phenolphthalein, a long name for a simple trick. It stays see-through in acids and in neutral water, and turns bright pink in a base.

The problem with these spies is that they only answer one question: acid or base? They cannot tell you how strong.

Remember to use a clean glass rod to put a drop on the paper, so you do not spoil the whole bottle.

Here is how to think it through. A liquid turns blue litmus red: acid. Another turns red litmus blue: base. A third leaves both alone: neutral. But if you only have blue litmus and it stays blue, you cannot be sure. It might be neutral, or it might be a base. You need the red strip too.

## Subtopic: The Rainbow Test

Universal indicator is a team of spies mixed together. Each one changes colour at a different pH, so together they make a rainbow.

Red: strong acid, pH 1 to 3. Orange: pH 4 to 5. Yellow: weak acid, about pH 6. Green: neutral, pH 7. Blue: weak base, pH 8 to 10. Purple: strong base, pH 11 to 14.

So now you get more than acid or base: you get a rough number. Add a few drops to your liquid, or touch it to a strip of universal indicator paper, then hold it next to the colour chart and read off the pH.

Try it in your head. Lemon juice: red. Vinegar: orange. Tap water: green. Bicarbonate of soda: blue. Ammonia cleaner: purple.

If you need an exact number, use a pH meter. The rainbow gives you a good estimate.

And here is a neat trick for later. Put universal indicator in an acid and add a base drop by drop. Watch the colour walk from red through orange and yellow to green. Green means you have cancelled the acid exactly.

Keep the rainbow in mind and try this part's questions: which colour, which pH.

## Subtopic: Cabbage in the Kitchen

You can make your own rainbow spy at home. Chop some red cabbage, pour hot water over it, wait about fifteen minutes until the water is deep purple, then pour off the liquid. That purple juice is an indicator.

Add a little to lemon juice: pink to red. To vinegar: pink. To water: stays purple. To bicarbonate of soda in water: blue-green. To washing soda: green. To drain cleaner, only with an adult and gloves: yellow.

Other kitchen spies: turmeric, the yellow spice in curry, turns red-brown in soapy water, which is why a curry stain on a white school shirt goes red in the wash. Beetroot juice changes colour too. Add lemon to tea and watch it go lighter.

Home-made indicators are cheap and safe, but their colours are less sharp and fade after a day or two.

Pocket summary of the lesson. An indicator changes colour with pH. Acids turn blue litmus red; bases turn red litmus blue. Phenolphthalein is pink in bases. Universal indicator goes red, orange, yellow, green at 7, blue and purple, and gives an approximate pH. Red cabbage is a natural indicator: pink in acid, purple neutral, green to yellow in base. Grade 10 to 12 chemistry uses indicators in titrations.

A fun fair test: make juice from red cabbage, red onion skins and hibiscus flowers in exactly the same way, then test each in lemon juice, water and bicarbonate of soda. Which plant gives the clearest colours? That is a real investigation you can do at home.
