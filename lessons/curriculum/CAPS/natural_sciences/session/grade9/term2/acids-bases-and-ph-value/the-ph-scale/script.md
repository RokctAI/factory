# Part 1 — Expert

This session defines acids, bases and neutral substances, introduces the pH scale from 1 to 14 as the measure of how acidic or basic a solution is, places common household and laboratory substances on that scale, and sets out the properties and safe handling of acids and bases. The anchor values are pure water at pH 7, lemon juice at about pH 2, vinegar at about pH 3, bicarbonate of soda solution at about pH 8 to 9, household ammonia at about pH 11 and drain cleaner at about pH 13 to 14. The governing principle is that pH below 7 means acidic, pH 7 means neutral and pH above 7 means basic, with each whole step on the scale representing a tenfold change in acidity.

## Subtopic: Acids, Bases and Neutral Substances

Substances that dissolve in water fall into three groups according to how they behave.

Acids are substances that release hydrogen ions, H+, when dissolved in water. Every acid formula you meet at this level starts with H: hydrochloric acid, HCl; sulfuric acid, H2SO4; nitric acid, HNO3; carbonic acid, H2CO3. Many foods contain weak acids: citric acid in citrus fruit, acetic acid, also called ethanoic acid, in vinegar, lactic acid in sour milk and amasi, and malic acid in apples. Acids taste sour, although nothing in a laboratory should ever be tasted. Strong acids are corrosive: they attack metals, skin, fabric and stone.

Bases are substances that react with acids and cancel out their acidity. Metal oxides, metal hydroxides and metal carbonates are bases. A base that dissolves in water is called an alkali, and alkalis release hydroxide ions, OH−, in water. Examples are sodium hydroxide, NaOH, also called caustic soda, in drain and oven cleaners; calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)2, slaked lime, used by builders and farmers; ammonia solution in window cleaners; and sodium hydrogencarbonate, bicarbonate of soda, used in baking. Bases taste bitter and feel soapy or slippery, because a strong base reacts with the oils in skin and begins to turn them into soap. Strong bases are as corrosive as strong acids, and more dangerous to the eyes.

Neutral substances are neither acidic nor basic. Pure water, a solution of table salt in pure water, and a solution of sugar in pure water are neutral.

Concentration and strength are different ideas. A concentrated solution has a lot of acid or base dissolved in a small volume of water; a dilute solution has a little. A strong acid such as hydrochloric acid releases all of its hydrogen ions in water; a weak acid such as vinegar releases only a small fraction of them. Grade 11 develops this distinction.

Acids share a set of chemical properties that can be used to recognise them. They turn blue litmus paper red. They react with reactive metals such as magnesium and zinc to release hydrogen gas, which burns with a squeaky pop. They react with carbonates, such as limestone, marble chips or bicarbonate of soda, fizzing as carbon dioxide is released. And they react with bases to form a salt and water. Bases have the matching set: they turn red litmus paper blue, feel slippery, and react with acids to form a salt and water.

The questions for this section are with you now: identifying acids and bases, their properties, and the meaning of alkali.

## Subtopic: The pH Scale from 1 to 14

The pH scale is a number scale that shows how acidic or basic a solution is. The scale used in Grade 9 runs from 1 to 14. It can in fact extend a little below 1 and above 14 for very concentrated solutions, but those values are not needed here.

The three regions of the scale: a pH of exactly 7 is neutral; a pH less than 7 is acidic; a pH greater than 7 is basic, or alkaline.

The further a pH is from 7, the stronger the acid or base. A solution of pH 1 is very strongly acidic; pH 6 is only slightly acidic. A solution of pH 14 is very strongly basic; pH 8 is only slightly basic.

The scale is not an ordinary ruler. Each whole step represents a factor of 10 in the concentration of hydrogen ions. A solution of pH 3 is 10 times more acidic than one of pH 4, and 10 × 10 = 100 times more acidic than one of pH 5. A drop of two pH units means one hundred times more acidic, and a drop of three pH units means 10 × 10 × 10 = 1000 times more acidic. This is why a small change in pH, such as in a swimming pool, a fish tank or human blood, can matter a great deal.

Approximate pH values of common substances, from acidic to basic: battery acid about 1; stomach acid about 1 to 2; lemon juice about 2; fizzy cooldrinks about 2,5 to 3; vinegar about 3; tomatoes about 4; black coffee about 5; clean rain about 5,6; milk about 6,5; pure water 7; human blood 7,4; sea water about 8; bicarbonate of soda solution about 8 to 9; toothpaste about 9; milk of magnesia about 10; household ammonia about 11; household bleach about 12 to 13; and drain or oven cleaner about 13 to 14.

The pH of a solution can be measured with an indicator, which changes colour, or with a pH meter, an electronic probe that gives a numerical reading, as used in water treatment works, swimming pools and laboratories. Indicators are the subject of the next lesson.

A worked comparison shows how to use the tenfold rule. Lemon juice at pH 2 compared with black coffee at pH 5: the difference is three steps, so lemon juice is 10 × 10 × 10 = 1000 times more acidic. Drain cleaner at pH 13 compared with sea water at pH 8: the difference is five steps, so the drain cleaner is 100 000 times more basic. These huge differences explain why a few drops of a strong acid in a glass of water still give an acidic solution, and why dilution alone is not a safe way to deal with a spill of concentrated acid.

## Subtopic: pH in Everyday Life and Safe Handling

The pH of a substance matters in the body, in the home and in the environment.

In the body: the stomach makes hydrochloric acid with a pH of about 1 to 2, which helps digest proteins and kills many microorganisms in food. Blood must stay between about 7,35 and 7,45; a change outside this narrow range can be life-threatening. Tooth decay happens when bacteria in plaque feed on sugar and produce acid, lowering the pH at the tooth surface below about 5,5, which dissolves enamel. Fizzy cooldrinks, with a pH around 2,5 to 3, attack enamel directly.

In the home: cleaning products include strong bases such as bleach and drain cleaner, and acids such as toilet bowl cleaners and descalers for kettles. Never mix cleaning products: bleach mixed with an acidic cleaner releases poisonous chlorine gas.

In farming and gardening: most crops grow best in soil with a pH between about 6 and 7. Many soils in the high-rainfall areas of KwaZulu-Natal and Mpumalanga are too acidic, and farmers add agricultural lime to raise the pH, as you will see in the next topic. Hydrangeas are a natural pH display: they flower blue in acidic soil and pink in alkaline soil.

In the environment: rain with a pH below about 5,6 is called acid rain. Water from old mines on the Witwatersrand, called acid mine drainage, can have a pH as low as 2 to 3 and poisons rivers.

Safety rules for acids and bases in the laboratory: wear safety goggles; never taste or smell substances directly; use the most dilute solution that will do the job; when diluting a concentrated acid, always add the acid slowly to water, never water to the acid, because the heat released can make the mixture boil and spit; wipe up spills immediately; and if acid or base gets on your skin or in your eyes, rinse with plenty of running water for at least ten minutes and tell the teacher.

In industry and services: water treatment works keep municipal drinking water at a pH between about 6,5 and 8,5 so that it does not corrode pipes or taste unpleasant. Swimming pools are kept at about 7,2 to 7,6, because chlorine disinfects best in that range and the water does not irritate the eyes. Fish farms, breweries, bakeries and hospitals all monitor pH routinely.

## Subtopic: Interpreting pH Data and the Error Museum

Examination questions often give a table of pH values and ask you to classify, compare or order. Take this data: substance A, pH 2; substance B, pH 7; substance C, pH 9; substance D, pH 13. A is a strong acid; B is neutral; C is a weak base; D is a strong base. A is 10 × 10 = 100 times more acidic than a solution of pH 4. If a little of D is added to water, its pH falls towards 7 as it is diluted, but it never goes below 7 by dilution alone.

Remember that dilution moves pH towards 7 but cannot cross it: diluting an acid makes it less acidic, but it remains an acid however much water is added.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: a pH of 1 is a weak acid because the number is small; a low pH is a strong acid. Two: pH 10 is twice as basic as pH 5; pH 5 is acidic and pH 10 is basic, and each step is a factor of ten, not an addition. Three: bases are safe because they are not acids; strong bases are as corrosive as strong acids. Four: add water to concentrated acid to dilute it; always add acid to water. Five: all clear, colourless liquids are neutral; you cannot tell pH by looking.

Layout for a pH question: state the pH value, classify it as acidic, neutral or basic, say whether it is strong or weak by its distance from 7, and when comparing two acids, count the steps and multiply by ten for each.

The questions for this section are with you now: classifying from data, tenfold comparisons and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through sour and soapy, a number line and safety in the kitchen — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Sour and Soapy

Everything that dissolves in water lands in one of three groups.

Acids. Think lemon juice, vinegar and sour milk. Acids taste sour, though you must never taste anything in a laboratory. Strong ones can burn skin, eat through metal and wear away stone. The acid in your stomach is strong enough to dissolve a piece of meat.

Bases. Think bicarbonate of soda, soap, and drain cleaner. Bases taste bitter and feel soapy or slippery on your fingers. That slippery feeling is the base starting to turn the oils in your skin into soap, so strong bases can burn just as badly as strong acids. A base that dissolves in water is called an alkali.

Neutral. Pure water, sugar water and salt water. Not acid, not base.

Here is the key idea: acids and bases are opposites. Mix them and they cancel each other out. That is coming up soon.

Acids also have tell-tale tricks. Drop a piece of magnesium ribbon into an acid and it fizzes with hydrogen gas. Drop a piece of limestone or a pinch of bicarbonate of soda into an acid and it fizzes with carbon dioxide. Bases do not do either of those things.

Keep sour and soapy in mind and try the questions for this part: which group, and how to tell them apart.

## Subtopic: The Number Line

The pH scale is a number line from 1 to 14 that tells you how acidic or basic something is.

Seven is the middle: neutral, like pure water.

Below 7: acids. The lower you go, the stronger. Lemon juice is about 2, vinegar about 3, coffee about 5, milk just under 7.

Above 7: bases. The higher you go, the stronger. Sea water is about 8, bicarbonate of soda about 8 or 9, ammonia cleaner about 11, drain cleaner about 13 or 14.

Here is the surprise. Each step is ten times bigger than the last. Vinegar at 3 is ten times more acidic than tomatoes at 4, and a hundred times more acidic than coffee at 5. So when someone says the pool's pH dropped by one, that is a big change.

You can find the pH with an indicator that changes colour, or with a pH meter, a probe with a number on a screen.

Try the trick yourself. Lemon juice at 2 and coffee at 5 are three steps apart. Ten times ten times ten is a thousand, so lemon juice is a thousand times more acidic than coffee. That is why lemon juice stings a cut and coffee does not.

Hold the number line in mind and try this part's questions: placing things on the scale and the ten-times rule.

## Subtopic: pH All Around You

Your stomach sits at about pH 1 or 2, which helps break down food and kills germs. Your blood stays at about 7,4, and your body works hard to keep it there. Your teeth start to dissolve when the pH on them drops below about 5,5, which happens when bacteria feed on sugar or when you sip fizzy cooldrink all day.

Farmers test their soil because most crops like a pH of about 6 to 7. If it is too acidic, they spread lime, which is a base. Some gardeners in Pietermaritzburg can read their soil from their hydrangeas: blue flowers mean acidic soil, pink mean alkaline.

Kitchen safety. Never mix cleaning products; bleach and an acid cleaner together give off a poisonous gas. In the lab: goggles on, never taste, and if you dilute an acid, pour the acid into the water, never water into the acid. If anything splashes on you, rinse with lots of running water.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Acids have pH below 7, bases above 7, and 7 is neutral. The further from 7, the stronger. Each step is ten times. Lemon about 2, vinegar about 3, water 7, bicarbonate of soda about 8 or 9, drain cleaner about 13 or 14. Strong acids and strong bases are both dangerous. Grade 10 to 12 chemistry builds on the pH scale.
