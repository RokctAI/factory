### subtopic_1 — What an Indicator Is: Litmus and Others
**Assistant:** Why does an indicator change colour at all?

**Tutor:** Because its molecules change shape slightly when they gain or lose a hydrogen ion, and that change alters which colours of light they absorb. Most indicators are weak acids. In an acidic solution, where hydrogen ions are plentiful, the indicator molecules hold on to their hydrogen ions and have one structure, which absorbs certain wavelengths of light and so shows one colour. In a basic solution, where hydroxide ions remove hydrogen ions, the molecules lose a hydrogen ion and change to a second structure, which absorbs different wavelengths and so shows a different colour. The colour you see is the light that is not absorbed. For phenolphthalein, the form in acid absorbs only ultraviolet light, which our eyes cannot see, so it looks colourless, while the form in base absorbs green light, so we see the remaining light as pink. Each indicator switches over a particular range of pH, depending on how tightly its molecules hold the hydrogen ion. That is why litmus switches around pH 7, phenolphthalein around pH 8 to 10, and methyl orange around pH 3 to 4,5. Universal indicator is cleverly designed by mixing several indicators with different switching ranges, so that at every pH some of them are part-way through their change, and the blend of their colours gives a different shade at each pH. The red cabbage pigments, anthocyanins, have several forms with different numbers of hydrogen ions, which is why one plant pigment can show so many colours.

### subtopic_2 — Universal Indicator and Its Colours
**Assistant:** How accurate is universal indicator compared with a pH meter?

**Tutor:** Universal indicator is good to about one pH unit, while a decent pH meter is accurate to about a tenth or even a hundredth of a unit. With indicator, you judge a colour by eye against a printed chart, so the reading depends on the lighting, on how well you see colour, and on the colour of the solution itself. Shades between two chart colours, such as a yellow-green, have to be estimated, so the honest result is something like about 6 or between 6 and 7. A pH meter uses a special glass electrode that produces a tiny voltage depending on the concentration of hydrogen ions, and an electronic circuit converts the voltage into a pH reading on a display. It must be calibrated regularly with buffer solutions of known pH, and its probe must be kept moist and cleaned carefully. For most school work, universal indicator is perfectly adequate, and it is cheaper, quicker and does not need a power supply. Where precision matters, a meter is used: in water treatment works checking drinking water, in hospital laboratories testing blood, in farming laboratories testing soil samples, in the food and wine industries, and in swimming pool maintenance, where small test kits using indicators are also popular. Choosing the right tool for the precision you need is an important skill in science.

### subtopic_3 — Natural Indicators
**Assistant:** Can I use any purple or red plant as an indicator?

**Tutor:** Many, but not all, and the colours vary from plant to plant. The plants that work best contain anthocyanins, the water-soluble pigments that make red cabbage, beetroot leaves, red onion skins, black grapes, blueberries, mulberries, red rose petals, purple sweet potato and hibiscus flowers red, purple or blue. All of these change colour with pH, though each gives a slightly different range of colours. Beetroot itself mainly contains a different pigment group, betalains, which also change colour but less dramatically. Plants whose colour comes from pigments that do not respond to pH, such as the orange carotenes in carrots or the green chlorophyll in leaves, are poor indicators. Turmeric works for a different reason: its yellow pigment, curcumin, changes to red-brown in basic conditions. A good school investigation is to compare several plant extracts, made in the same way with the same volume of hot water and the same mass of plant, and test each with the same set of household solutions, such as lemon juice, vinegar, water, bicarbonate of soda solution and washing soda solution. Record the colours in a table and decide which plant gives the clearest and widest range of colours. That is a fair test with the plant as the independent variable. You may find that red cabbage is hard to beat, which is why it is the classic choice.

### subtopic_4 — Investigating with Indicators and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** In the investigation, why do I need distilled water as a control?

**Tutor:** Because it gives you a reference colour for a neutral solution made under exactly the same conditions as your tests. Indicator colours can be affected by small things: the batch of indicator, how many drops you add, how much water dilutes the colour, and the lighting in the room. By setting up a test tube containing distilled water and the same number of drops of the same indicator, you can see what neutral looks like in your experiment, and compare every other tube against it. Distilled water is used rather than tap water because tap water contains dissolved substances that can make it slightly acidic or basic, typically somewhere between pH 6,5 and 8,5 in South African municipal supplies. If your tap water test turns slightly blue or slightly yellow compared with distilled water, that is a genuine result about your tap water, not an error. A control also lets you detect contamination. If the control tube is not the expected green with universal indicator, something is wrong, perhaps a dirty test tube, and you know to repeat the experiment. In any investigation, a control is a set-up that is identical to the tests except that it lacks the factor being tested, and it allows you to say with confidence that differences in the results are caused by the factor you changed.
