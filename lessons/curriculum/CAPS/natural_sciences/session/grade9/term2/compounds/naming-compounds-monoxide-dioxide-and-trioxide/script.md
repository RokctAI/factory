# Part 1 — Expert

This session sets out the rules for naming simple compounds: the -ide ending for compounds of two elements, the Greek prefixes mono, di, tri, tetra and penta for compounds of two non-metals, the -ate ending for compounds containing groups with oxygen, and the common names that chemists still use. The anchor examples are carbon monoxide, CO, and carbon dioxide, CO2, sulfur dioxide, SO2, and sulfur trioxide, SO3, and magnesium oxide, MgO. The governing principle is that a systematic name is a code for the formula: it states the elements and, where needed, the number of each kind of atom, so that one name can belong to only one compound.

## Subtopic: Naming Compounds of a Metal and a Non-metal

A compound made of exactly two elements is called a binary compound. When one element is a metal and the other a non-metal, the name follows two rules. First, the metal is named first and keeps its own name unchanged. Second, the non-metal is named second, and the end of its name is replaced by the suffix -ide.

The non-metal endings must be learnt: oxygen becomes oxide, chlorine becomes chloride, sulfur becomes sulfide, nitrogen becomes nitride, bromine becomes bromide, iodine becomes iodide, fluorine becomes fluoride, and hydrogen, when it is the second element, becomes hydride.

So NaCl, made of sodium and chlorine, is sodium chloride. MgO, magnesium and oxygen, is magnesium oxide. CaS, calcium and sulfur, is calcium sulfide. KBr, potassium and bromine, is potassium bromide. Al2O3, aluminium and oxygen, is aluminium oxide. FeS, iron and sulfur, is iron sulfide, the black solid formed when iron filings and sulfur powder are heated together, a reaction often demonstrated in Grade 8 and 9. CuO is copper oxide, a black powder, and ZnO is zinc oxide, the white powder in sunscreen and nappy cream.

In these metal and non-metal compounds the number of atoms is usually not shown in the name. Aluminium oxide is Al2O3, but it is not called dialuminium trioxide, because aluminium and oxygen combine in only one ratio, so the name is not ambiguous. The ratio comes from the combining power of each element, which you will study in Grade 10. Some metals, such as iron and copper, can combine in more than one ratio, and then a Roman numeral in brackets shows which: iron(II) oxide, FeO, and iron(III) oxide, Fe2O3. In Grade 9 you will mostly see these written simply as iron oxide, with the formula given.

The order matters. The metal always comes first in both the name and the formula. Chloride sodium or ClNa are both wrong.

These rules are agreed internationally by IUPAC, the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry, so that a chemist in Johannesburg and one in Seoul give the same compound the same name. Before such rules, many substances had several names, often based on where they were found or what they looked like, such as oil of vitriol for sulfuric acid or blue vitriol for copper sulfate, and confusion was common. A systematic name is longer than a nickname, but it contains information: from magnesium oxide alone you know the compound contains exactly two elements, that one is the metal magnesium, and that the other is oxygen. Reading names this way turns every label into a small piece of chemistry.

## Subtopic: Prefixes for Compounds of Two Non-metals

When both elements are non-metals, they can often combine in several different ratios, so the name must state the number of atoms of each. This is done with Greek prefixes. Mono means one, di means two, tri means three, tetra means four, penta means five and hexa means six.

The rules are these. The element further to the left in the Periodic Table is usually named first. The second element takes the -ide ending, with a prefix showing how many of its atoms are present. The first element takes a prefix only if there is more than one of its atoms; mono is never used for the first element. And when the prefix ends in a or o and the next word begins with o, the final vowel of the prefix is dropped to make the name easier to say, so mono-oxide becomes monoxide and penta-oxide becomes pentoxide.

Applying the rules to carbon and oxygen: CO has one carbon and one oxygen, so it is carbon monoxide. CO2 has one carbon and two oxygens: carbon dioxide. For sulfur and oxygen: SO2 is sulfur dioxide and SO3 is sulfur trioxide. For nitrogen and oxygen, which form several compounds: NO is nitrogen monoxide, NO2 is nitrogen dioxide, N2O is dinitrogen monoxide, also known as laughing gas, and N2O4 is dinitrogen tetroxide. Other examples: CCl4, carbon tetrachloride; P2O5, diphosphorus pentoxide; SF6, sulfur hexafluoride.

The prefixes matter because the compounds are genuinely different. Carbon monoxide is a deadly, odourless gas formed by burning fuel in too little air; carbon dioxide is the gas we exhale and plants absorb. Sulfur dioxide is a choking gas released by burning coal; sulfur trioxide forms from it in the air and reacts with water to make sulfuric acid, a cause of acid rain. Nitrogen dioxide is a brown, toxic gas in vehicle exhaust and smog. Each name points to exactly one of them.

Writing a formula from a prefix name is the reverse: each prefix becomes the subscript after its element. Sulfur trioxide: sulfur, no prefix so one; tri-oxide so three oxygens; SO3. Dinitrogen tetroxide: two nitrogens, four oxygens; N2O4. Carbon tetrachloride: one carbon, four chlorines; CCl4.

## Subtopic: Compounds With Oxygen Groups and Common Names

Some compounds contain more than two elements because they include a group of atoms that stays together, usually a non-metal joined to oxygen. These groups have their own names, most ending in -ate, which signals that oxygen is present. The carbonate group is CO3, the sulfate group is SO4, and the nitrate group is NO3. The hydroxide group, OH, ends in -ide even though it contains oxygen, and is the main exception to learn.

The naming rule is the same as before: the metal first, then the name of the group. CaCO3 is calcium carbonate, the main substance in limestone, marble, chalk and eggshells. Na2CO3 is sodium carbonate, washing soda. CuSO4 is copper sulfate, a blue crystal used to kill fungi in gardens. MgSO4 is magnesium sulfate, Epsom salts. KNO3 is potassium nitrate, used in fertilisers. NaOH is sodium hydroxide, caustic soda, a powerful base in drain cleaners. Ca(OH)2 is calcium hydroxide, slaked lime, used to treat acidic soil. Compare the endings carefully: copper sulfide, CuS, has only copper and sulfur; copper sulfate, CuSO4, also contains oxygen.

Some compounds are almost always called by their common or traditional names rather than their systematic ones, and these must simply be known. H2O is water, not dihydrogen monoxide. NH3 is ammonia. CH4 is methane. HCl dissolved in water is hydrochloric acid, H2SO4 is sulfuric acid, also spelt sulphuric acid, and HNO3 is nitric acid. NaCl is better known as table salt and NaHCO3 as bicarbonate of soda, used in baking.

For marking, look at the formula to decide which rule applies. Two elements, metal and non-metal: metal name plus -ide. Two non-metals: prefixes plus -ide. A metal with a group containing oxygen: metal name plus the group name, usually -ate. A familiar substance: its common name.

A useful way to remember the endings is by how much they tell you. The -ide ending on its own, as in sodium chloride or calcium sulfide, says the compound has only the two elements named. The -ate ending says a third element, oxygen, is present as well, bound into a group with the non-metal. So when a test shows calcium sulfide and calcium sulfate side by side, the first is CaS, two elements, and the second is CaSO4, three elements, and the names alone allow you to tell which one contains oxygen without seeing the formulas.

## Subtopic: From Name to Formula and the Error Museum

Moving confidently in both directions is the skill that later chemistry depends on. From formula to name, ask three questions in order: how many elements are there, is the first one a metal, and are there any groups? From name to formula, translate each part: element names become symbols, prefixes become subscripts, and group names become their formulas.

Worked examples from formula to name. SO2: two elements, both non-metals, one sulfur and two oxygens, so sulfur dioxide. CaO: two elements, calcium a metal, so calcium oxide. K2SO4: a metal and the sulfate group, so potassium sulfate. PCl3: two non-metals, one phosphorus and three chlorines, so phosphorus trichloride.

Worked examples from name to formula. Carbon monoxide: one carbon, mono so one oxygen, CO. Sulfur trioxide: one sulfur, three oxygens, SO3. Dinitrogen monoxide: two nitrogens, one oxygen, N2O. Magnesium carbonate: magnesium and the carbonate group, MgCO3.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: CO2 is called carbon oxide; a compound of two non-metals needs prefixes, so it is carbon dioxide. Two: NaCl is sodium chlorine; the second element takes -ide, so sodium chloride. Three: CO is monocarbon monoxide; mono is never used for the first element, so carbon monoxide. Four: carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide are the same gas in different amounts; they are different compounds with different properties. Five: sulfide and sulfate mean the same thing; -ate signals that oxygen is also present, so copper sulfate contains oxygen and copper sulfide does not.

Layout for a naming question: state which rule applies, then give the name with correct spelling. Layout for a formula question: each element with its number, then the formula with subscripts. Compounds are then named exactly as the memorandum expects.

The questions for this section are ready: translating names and formulas both ways, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same rules again, through a first name and a surname, counting in Greek, and reading the label on a bottle — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: First Name, Surname

Think of a compound's name like a person's name: a first name and a surname. When a metal and a non-metal join, the metal is the first name and it never changes. Sodium stays sodium, magnesium stays magnesium, iron stays iron. The non-metal is the surname, and it always gets a new ending: -ide.

So oxygen as a surname becomes oxide. Chlorine becomes chloride. Sulfur becomes sulfide. Bromine becomes bromide. Iodine becomes iodide. Nitrogen becomes nitride.

Now put them together. NaCl: first name sodium, surname chloride, so sodium chloride, table salt. MgO: magnesium oxide, the white powder left when you burn magnesium ribbon. CaS: calcium sulfide. KBr: potassium bromide. ZnO: zinc oxide, the white stuff in sunscreen. FeS: iron sulfide, the black lump you get when you heat iron filings with yellow sulfur powder.

The first name always comes first, in the name and in the formula. You would never say chloride sodium, and you never write ClNa.

You may notice that aluminium oxide is Al2O3, but nobody calls it dialuminium trioxide. That is because aluminium and oxygen only ever join in one way, so there is no confusion, and you do not need to count in the name. A few metals, like iron, can join oxygen in more than one way, and then chemists add a Roman numeral, like iron(III) oxide, but in Grade 9 you will usually just see iron oxide with the formula next to it.

Keep first name and surname in mind and try the questions for this part: the -ide endings, which comes first, and naming from a formula.

## Subtopic: Counting in Greek

When two non-metals join, they can often join in different ways, so the name has to count. It counts in Greek: mono is one, di is two, tri is three, tetra is four, penta is five.

Take carbon and oxygen. One carbon with one oxygen: carbon monoxide, CO. One carbon with two oxygens: carbon dioxide, CO2. Same two elements, but two completely different gases. Carbon monoxide comes from braziers and engines burning without enough air, and it kills quietly. Carbon dioxide is what you breathe out and what makes cold drinks fizz.

Take sulfur and oxygen. One sulfur, two oxygens: sulfur dioxide, SO2, the choking smell of burning coal and matches. One sulfur, three oxygens: sulfur trioxide, SO3, which turns into acid in rain.

Two small rules make the names sound right. First, never say mono at the very start; it is carbon monoxide, not monocarbon monoxide. If the first element has two or more atoms, then you do count it: N2O is dinitrogen monoxide, laughing gas. Second, when the Greek word ends in o or a and the next word starts with o, drop one letter: mono-oxide becomes monoxide, penta-oxide becomes pentoxide.

Going backwards is just as easy. Hear sulfur trioxide: sulfur, one; tri, three oxygens; write SO3. Hear dinitrogen tetroxide: di, two nitrogens; tetra, four oxygens; write N2O4.

Here is a quick practice run, the way you would do it in a test. Formula NO2: two non-metals, so count. One nitrogen, no prefix; two oxygens, di; so nitrogen dioxide, the brown gas in traffic fumes. Formula CCl4: one carbon, no prefix; four chlorines, tetra; carbon tetrachloride. Name phosphorus pentachloride: one phosphorus, five chlorines, PCl5. Name diphosphorus pentoxide: two phosphorus, five oxygens, P2O5. Each time it is the same three moves: check that both are non-metals, count each kind of atom, and turn the count into a prefix or the prefix into a count.

Hold the Greek counting in mind and try this part's questions: what each prefix means, monoxide against dioxide, and writing a formula from a name.

## Subtopic: Reading the Label on a Bottle

Walk through a kitchen, a garden shed and a pharmacy, and you will find chemical names everywhere. Some end in -ate, and that ending is a clue: there is oxygen hiding in a little group. Calcium carbonate, CaCO3, is chalk, limestone and eggshell. Copper sulfate, CuSO4, is the blue crystal gardeners use. Magnesium sulfate is Epsom salts. Potassium nitrate is in fertiliser. Watch the difference between sulfide and sulfate: copper sulfide is just copper and sulfur, copper sulfate has oxygen too.

One sneaky exception: hydroxide. It has oxygen, OH, but it ends in -ide. Sodium hydroxide, NaOH, is caustic soda in drain cleaner, which burns skin, so handle it with gloves. Calcium hydroxide is slaked lime, which farmers spread on sour soil.

And some chemicals keep their old everyday names. H2O is just water. NH3 is ammonia, the sharp smell in some cleaning liquids. CH4 is methane, the gas in biogas. HCl in water is hydrochloric acid, H2SO4 is sulfuric acid, and NaHCO3 is bicarbonate of soda, which makes scones rise.

So when you meet a formula, ask: two elements, a metal and a non-metal? First name plus -ide. Two non-metals? Count in Greek, plus -ide. A metal plus a group with oxygen? The group's name, usually -ate. A famous substance? Its everyday name.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Metal plus non-metal: metal name, then non-metal with -ide, like sodium chloride and magnesium oxide. Two non-metals: Greek prefixes, mono, di, tri, tetra, penta, never mono first, like carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide and sulfur trioxide. -ate means an oxygen group, like calcium carbonate and copper sulfate; hydroxide is the exception. Common names: water, ammonia, methane. Grade 10 Physical Sciences extends these rules.

Hold the bottle labels in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: -ate against -ide, the hydroxide exception, and the common names.
