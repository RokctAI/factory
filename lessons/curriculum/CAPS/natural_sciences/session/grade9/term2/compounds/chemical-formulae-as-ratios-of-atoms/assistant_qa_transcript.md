### subtopic_1 — Elements, Compounds and Molecules
**Assistant:** If oxygen gas is O2, why is it called an element and not a compound?

**Tutor:** Because the word compound means different elements joined together, and O2 contains only one kind of atom. An element is defined by the kind of atom it contains, not by how many atoms are bonded in each particle. In oxygen gas, the atoms happen to pair up: each oxygen atom is more stable when it shares electrons with another oxygen atom, so the gas consists of molecules of two bonded oxygen atoms. But both atoms have eight protons; both are oxygen. Nothing else is present, so oxygen gas is an element, and its molecules are molecules of an element. Several other elements behave the same way: hydrogen, nitrogen, fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine all exist as diatomic molecules, H2, N2, F2, Cl2, Br2 and I2, which is why their formulas carry a 2 when they appear in equations as elements. Oxygen can even form a three-atom molecule, ozone, O3, found in the ozone layer high in the atmosphere, which you will meet in Term 4. O2 and O3 are both forms of the element oxygen, with different properties: O2 is the gas we breathe, while O3 is a pale blue, sharp-smelling gas that absorbs harmful ultraviolet light. Many other elements, such as the metals and the noble gases, do not form small molecules at all: a lump of iron is a giant structure of iron atoms, and helium exists as single atoms. So the test for element against compound is always the same: count the kinds of atom, not the atoms. One kind means an element; two or more kinds, chemically combined, means a compound. CO, with two kinds of atom, is a compound even though it has only two atoms, while O2, with two atoms of one kind, is an element.

### subtopic_2 — Reading a Chemical Formula
**Assistant:** Why do some formulas use brackets when you could just write the atoms out?

**Tutor:** Because the brackets show a group of atoms that stays together as a unit in the compound and in many reactions, and writing them out would hide that structure. In calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)2, the oxygen and hydrogen are bonded together as a hydroxide group, OH, and there are two such groups for every calcium. Writing CaO2H2 would contain the right numbers of atoms but would no longer show that the oxygen and hydrogen are paired, and it could be confused with other arrangements. The groups that need brackets are those that carry a charge and behave as a single unit, such as hydroxide, OH; nitrate, NO3; sulfate, SO4; carbonate, CO3; and ammonium, NH4. They appear again and again in different compounds and pass unchanged through many reactions. For example, sulfate appears in sulfuric acid, H2SO4, copper sulfate, CuSO4, and aluminium sulfate, Al2(SO4)3; carbonate appears in calcium carbonate, CaCO3, and sodium carbonate, Na2CO3. Brackets are needed only when there is more than one of the group: CaCO3 needs no brackets because there is only one carbonate, but Al2(SO4)3 does, because there are three sulfates. Recognising these groups makes chemistry much easier. When you see sulfate in sulfuric acid reacting with copper oxide, you can predict that copper sulfate forms, because the sulfate group moves across as a whole. In Grade 10 you will learn the charges on these groups and use them to write formulas yourself. For now, the skill is to read the brackets correctly: everything inside is multiplied by the number outside.

### subtopic_3 — Formulae as Fixed Ratios
**Assistant:** If the formula of glucose shows a ratio of 1 to 2 to 1, why not write it as CH2O?

**Tutor:** Because for a substance made of separate molecules the formula records what is actually in one molecule, and one glucose molecule genuinely contains six carbon, twelve hydrogen and six oxygen atoms. CH2O does exist, but it is a completely different substance: methanal, also called formaldehyde, a pungent, toxic gas used to preserve specimens in laboratories. It has the same ratio of atoms as glucose but each of its molecules contains only four atoms, so its properties are entirely different. The same is true of ethanoic acid, the acid in vinegar, C2H4O2, which also has the ratio 1 to 2 to 1. So a ratio alone cannot always identify a compound made of molecules. Chemists distinguish two kinds of formula. The molecular formula, such as C6H12O6, gives the actual number of each kind of atom in one molecule. The simplest ratio, CH2O, is called the empirical formula and is useful in calculations but does not identify the substance. For compounds that are giant lattices rather than molecules, such as sodium chloride or magnesium oxide, there is no single molecule to count, so the formula is always the simplest ratio: NaCl, MgO, Al2O3. That is why the lesson describes formulas as ratios in general but warns that for molecules the subscripts are the real counts. In practice, you will be given the formula in Grade 9 and asked to read it, count its atoms or state its ratio. Grade 10 and 11 Physical Sciences will teach you how chemists work out empirical and molecular formulas from measured masses.

### subtopic_4 — Counting Atoms in Formulae and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why is carbon monoxide so dangerous when carbon dioxide is something we breathe out every day?

**Tutor:** Because of what each gas does inside the body, which follows from their different formulas and structures. Carbon dioxide, CO2, is a normal waste product of respiration; the blood carries it to the lungs and we breathe it out, and at the low concentrations normally found in air it does no harm, although very high concentrations in an enclosed space can suffocate a person. Carbon monoxide, CO, is a different molecule with different properties. It forms when carbon-containing fuel, such as coal, wood, paraffin or petrol, burns with too little oxygen, so that each carbon atom combines with only one oxygen atom instead of two. Inside the body it binds to haemoglobin in the red blood cells about 200 times more strongly than oxygen does, so it takes up the places where oxygen should be carried and is released only very slowly. The blood keeps circulating but delivers less and less oxygen to the brain and heart. The early symptoms, headache, dizziness, tiredness and nausea, are easily mistaken for flu, and because the gas has no colour, smell or taste, people often fall asleep and never wake. In South Africa, deaths occur every winter when families bring a coal brazier, a paraffin heater or a generator into a closed room for warmth. The protection is ventilation: never burn fuel indoors without a chimney or open windows, never run a generator or car engine in a closed garage, and use a carbon monoxide detector where possible. The difference of a single oxygen atom, one subscript, separates a harmless waste gas from a deadly poison, which is exactly why chemists treat the numbers in a formula with such care.
