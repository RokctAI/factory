Hey — chemical formulas can look like a jumble of letters and numbers, but there is one rule that unlocks almost all of them. Here is the calm version.

An element has one kind of atom, like Fe or O2. A compound has two or more elements joined in a fixed ratio, like H2O or NaCl, and it has new properties, unlike its elements.

The rule: the little number belongs to the symbol right before it. No number means one. H2O is two hydrogens and one oxygen. CO2 is one carbon and two oxygens.

Brackets are packets: the number after the bracket multiplies everything inside. Ca(OH)2 is one calcium, two oxygens, two hydrogens.

To count, make a little table: element, number, then add. H2SO4: hydrogen 2, sulfur 1, oxygen 4, total 7. Count elements separately from atoms.

A formula is a ratio, and changing a subscript changes the substance: H2O is water, H2O2 is hydrogen peroxide.

Read every formula out loud, left to right, element then number, and you will not miss anything.
