### subtopic_1 — Naming Compounds of a Metal and a Non-metal
**Assistant:** Why do some names have Roman numerals, like iron(III) oxide?

**Tutor:** Because some metals can combine with the same non-metal in more than one ratio, and the name must show which compound is meant. Most metals in Grade 9 questions, such as sodium, magnesium, calcium, aluminium and zinc, combine with oxygen in only one way, so magnesium oxide can only be MgO and no extra information is needed. Iron is different. It can form iron(II) oxide, FeO, a black powder, and iron(III) oxide, Fe2O3, the reddish-brown substance in rust and in the iron ore mined at Sishen in the Northern Cape. A third oxide, Fe3O4, contains both kinds and is the black, magnetic oxide formed when steel wool burns. Copper likewise forms copper(I) oxide, Cu2O, which is red, and copper(II) oxide, CuO, which is black. The Roman numeral shows the combining power, or charge, of the metal in that compound: in iron(III) oxide each iron has a combining power of three, and each oxygen two, so two irons balance three oxygens, giving Fe2O3. You do not need to work these out in Grade 9; the formula is given, and many Grade 9 textbooks write iron oxide without the numeral. In Grade 10 you will learn the combining powers, called valencies or charges, of the common metals and groups, and then you will be able to write formulas such as Al2O3 and Fe2O3 from the names alone. The Roman numeral is therefore not an extra decoration but part of the code: it is the piece of information that tells a chemist which of two quite different compounds is meant.

### subtopic_2 — Prefixes for Compounds of Two Non-metals
**Assistant:** Why do we use prefixes for carbon dioxide but not for sodium chloride?

**Tutor:** Because the two kinds of compound behave differently, and prefixes are needed only where there would otherwise be confusion. Sodium chloride is a compound of a metal and a non-metal. In such compounds the atoms combine in a ratio fixed by their charges, and sodium and chlorine can combine in only one ratio, one to one. So the name sodium chloride can only mean NaCl, and adding monosodium monochloride would tell us nothing new. The same is true for magnesium oxide, calcium chloride, CaCl2, and aluminium oxide, Al2O3: each pair has only one possible ratio, so the name without prefixes is complete. Compounds of two non-metals are formed in a different way, by sharing electrons, and many pairs of non-metals can share in several different ratios, producing several different compounds. Carbon and oxygen form both CO and CO2; sulfur and oxygen form SO2 and SO3; nitrogen and oxygen form at least six oxides, including NO, NO2, N2O and N2O4. If we called them all simply carbon oxide, sulfur oxide or nitrogen oxide, we could not tell which compound was meant, and the difference matters: carbon monoxide is deadly, carbon dioxide is not; nitrogen dioxide is a toxic brown gas, while dinitrogen monoxide is used as an anaesthetic. So the prefixes carry essential information. A useful check in a test is to decide first whether the compound contains a metal. If it does, no prefixes, except where a Roman numeral is needed for metals like iron. If both elements are non-metals, count the atoms of each and use prefixes, remembering that mono is never used for the first element.

### subtopic_3 — Compounds With Oxygen Groups and Common Names
**Assistant:** Is dihydrogen monoxide a real name for water?

**Tutor:** Yes, it is a correct systematic name for H2O, since water is a compound of two non-metals with two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom, but chemists never use it, and the name is mainly famous as a joke. Water, like ammonia, NH3, and methane, CH4, was known and named long before the systematic rules were invented, and these traditional names are so universal that the rules make an exception for them. The joke works by describing water in alarming but true language: dihydrogen monoxide is found in acid rain, causes severe burns in its gaseous form as steam, is fatal if inhaled in large quantities, and is found in every cancer tumour. Every one of those statements is true of water, and yet water is essential to life. People have run surveys in which many members of the public, hearing only the scary description and the unfamiliar chemical name, signed petitions to ban the substance. The lesson behind the joke is a serious one for science learners: a chemical name that sounds frightening tells you nothing about whether something is dangerous. Everything is made of chemicals, including water, salt, sugar and the oxygen you breathe, and the danger of a substance depends on what it is and how much of it there is, not on how its name sounds. Equally, a natural-sounding or familiar name does not make something safe: carbon monoxide has a short, simple name and is deadly. So the habit to build is to learn what a name actually means, by reading it with the rules, and then to look up what that substance does, rather than judging by the sound of the word.

### subtopic_4 — From Name to Formula and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** How can I tell quickly whether a compound needs prefixes or not?

**Tutor:** Look at the first element in the formula and ask whether it is a metal, using its position on the Periodic Table. Metals are on the left of the staircase line, so if the formula begins with a symbol such as Na, K, Mg, Ca, Al, Fe, Cu or Zn, the compound is a metal compound and no prefixes are used: name the metal, then the non-metal with -ide, or the group with its own name. MgO is magnesium oxide, Al2O3 aluminium oxide, CaCO3 calcium carbonate, even though Al2O3 contains two and three atoms. If instead the formula begins with a non-metal, such as C, N, P, S or Cl, and contains no metal at all, prefixes are used: count the atoms of each element and translate the numbers, so PCl5 is phosphorus pentachloride and N2O4 is dinitrogen tetroxide, never using mono for the first element. Then check two exceptions. If the compound has a well-known common name, such as water, ammonia, methane or hydrochloric acid, use that name. And if it contains hydrogen first, as in HCl or H2SO4, it is usually an acid and is named as one when dissolved in water. A helpful last step is to read your answer back as a formula: if the name you wrote would lead someone to write exactly the formula you were given, the name is right. With practice the decision takes a second, and it prevents the two commonest errors in tests: adding prefixes to metal compounds, such as writing magnesium monoxide, and leaving them off non-metal compounds, such as writing carbon oxide for CO2.
