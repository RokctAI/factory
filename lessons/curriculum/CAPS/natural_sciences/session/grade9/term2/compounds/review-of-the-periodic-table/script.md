# Part 1 — Expert

This session reviews the Periodic Table: the names and symbols of the elements, the arrangement into groups and periods, the division into metals, non-metals and semi-metals, and the use of atomic number and mass number to describe an atom. The anchor numbers are 118 known elements, 18 groups and 7 periods, and the two numbers carried by every element: the atomic number, which counts the protons, and the mass number, which counts the protons and neutrons together. The governing principle is that the table is arranged in order of increasing atomic number so that elements with similar properties fall into the same vertical column.

## Subtopic: Elements, Symbols and Names

An element is a pure substance made of only one kind of atom, which cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical means. There are 118 known elements, of which about 90 occur naturally on Earth; the rest have been made in laboratories. Everything around you, from the air to your own body to the Highveld rocks, is made of these elements, either on their own or combined in compounds.

Every element has a name and a chemical symbol of one or two letters. The first letter is always a capital and the second, if there is one, is always small. So C is carbon, Ca is calcium, Cl is chlorine and Co is cobalt. The rule matters: Co is the element cobalt, while CO, with two capitals, is carbon monoxide, a compound of carbon and oxygen. Many symbols come from the English name, such as H for hydrogen, O for oxygen, N for nitrogen, S for sulfur, Mg for magnesium and Al for aluminium. Several come from Latin names used by early chemists: Na for sodium, from natrium; K for potassium, from kalium; Fe for iron, from ferrum; Cu for copper, from cuprum; Ag for silver, from argentum; Au for gold, from aurum; Pb for lead, from plumbum; and Hg for mercury, from hydrargyrum, meaning liquid silver.

The modern table grew from the work of the Russian chemist Dmitri Mendeleev, who in 1869 arranged the known elements in order of the mass of their atoms and placed elements with similar properties in the same column. Where the pattern demanded it, he left gaps and predicted that undiscovered elements would fill them, and he even predicted their properties. When gallium and germanium were discovered years later with almost exactly the properties he had foretold, his table was accepted. Today the elements are arranged in order of atomic number rather than mass, which removes the few places where Mendeleev's order seemed wrong.

South Africa's economy rests on elements from this table: gold, platinum, chromium, manganese, iron, and carbon in coal. The country produces most of the world's platinum, which is used in the catalytic converters of cars and in jewellery.

For marking, learn the symbols of the first 20 elements and the common metals, write every symbol with a capital first letter and a small second letter, and know the Latin-based symbols.

The questions for this section are with you now: the capital-and-small-letter rule, the Latin symbols, and what Mendeleev predicted.

## Subtopic: Groups and Periods

The vertical columns of the Periodic Table are called groups, numbered 1 to 18 from left to right. The horizontal rows are called periods, numbered 1 to 7 from top to bottom. Every element therefore has an address: sodium is in Group 1, Period 3; chlorine is in Group 17, Period 3; calcium is in Group 2, Period 4.

Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties, because their atoms have the same number of electrons in the outermost shell, and it is these outer electrons that take part in reactions. Several groups have family names. Group 1, apart from hydrogen, is the alkali metals: lithium, sodium and potassium are soft metals that react vigorously with water, fizzing and producing hydrogen, more violently the further down the group you go. Group 2 is the alkaline earth metals, including magnesium and calcium. Group 17 is the halogens, fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine, reactive non-metals; chlorine is used to disinfect swimming pools and drinking water. Group 18 is the noble gases, helium, neon and argon, which are so unreactive that they almost never form compounds; helium fills balloons and neon glows in signs. The broad central block, Groups 3 to 12, holds the transition metals, including iron, copper, zinc, silver, gold and platinum.

Elements in the same period have the same number of electron shells, but their properties change steadily across the period, from reactive metals on the left, through less metallic elements, to non-metals and finally a noble gas on the right. Period 3, for example, runs from sodium and magnesium, which are metals, through aluminium, silicon, a semi-metal, and then phosphorus, sulfur and chlorine, which are non-metals, to argon, a noble gas.

So the two directions of the table tell different stories. Down a group, the properties are similar. Across a period, they change gradually from metal to non-metal.

## Subtopic: Metals, Non-metals and Semi-metals

About three quarters of the elements are metals. They occupy the left side and the centre of the table. A staircase line that runs diagonally down from boron to astatine separates them from the non-metals on the right. Hydrogen, though placed at the top of Group 1, is a non-metal.

Metals share a set of physical properties. They are shiny, or lustrous, when freshly cut or polished. They are good conductors of heat and electricity, which is why copper is used in electrical wires and aluminium in pots. They are malleable, meaning they can be hammered into sheets, and ductile, meaning they can be drawn into wires. Most have high melting points and are solid at room temperature; mercury, a liquid, is the exception. Many are sonorous, ringing when struck, which is why bells are made of metal.

Non-metals have the opposite properties. They are usually dull, poor conductors of heat and electricity, and, when solid, brittle, shattering rather than bending. Many are gases at room temperature: hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine, chlorine and the noble gases. Bromine is a liquid, and carbon, sulfur, phosphorus and iodine are solids. Carbon in the form of graphite is the one non-metal that conducts electricity well, which is why it is used in the electrodes of batteries.

The semi-metals, or metalloids, lie along the staircase line and have properties in between: boron, silicon, germanium, arsenic, antimony and tellurium. Silicon, for example, is shiny like a metal but brittle like a non-metal, and it conducts electricity only partly, which makes it a semiconductor. Semiconductors are the basis of computer chips, cellphones and the solar panels appearing on South African roofs.

For marking, classify by position first and then confirm with properties: left of the staircase, metal; right, non-metal; on the staircase, semi-metal; and remember hydrogen.

This section's questions are available now: the properties of metals and non-metals, where the semi-metals sit, and why silicon is used in solar panels.

## Subtopic: Atomic Number, Mass Number and the Error Museum

Every block of the Periodic Table carries the element's symbol, its name, its atomic number and a value for the mass of its atoms. The atomic number, symbol Z, is the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom of that element. It identifies the element: every atom with 6 protons is carbon, every atom with 26 protons is iron. In a neutral atom the number of electrons equals the number of protons, because the positive charges of the protons are balanced by the negative charges of the electrons.

The mass number, symbol A, is the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus, the particles that make up almost all of the atom's mass. The number of neutrons is therefore the mass number minus the atomic number. Atoms are written with the mass number at the top left of the symbol and the atomic number at the bottom left.

Worked examples, each in the same three steps. Sodium has atomic number 11 and mass number 23: protons 11, electrons 11, neutrons 23 − 11 = 12. Chlorine has atomic number 17 and mass number 35: protons 17, electrons 17, neutrons 35 − 17 = 18. Iron has atomic number 26 and mass number 56: protons 26, electrons 26, neutrons 56 − 26 = 30. Gold has atomic number 79 and mass number 197: protons 79, electrons 79, neutrons 197 − 79 = 118.

On many Periodic Tables the mass value shown is not a whole number, for example 35,5 for chlorine, because natural chlorine is a mixture of atoms with different numbers of neutrons, and the table shows the average. The mass number of a single atom is always a whole number, and in Grade 9 questions give it or ask you to round. Grade 10 explains these different atoms, called isotopes.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: Co and CO mean the same; Co is the element cobalt, CO is the compound carbon monoxide. Two: the atomic number is the number of protons plus neutrons; that is the mass number, while the atomic number counts protons only. Three: elements in the same period have similar properties; it is elements in the same group that are similar. Four: hydrogen is a metal because it is in Group 1; hydrogen is a non-metal gas. Five: all metals are solids and all non-metals are gases; mercury is a liquid metal, bromine a liquid non-metal, and carbon and sulfur solid non-metals.

Layout for a particle question: protons equal Z, electrons equal Z in a neutral atom, neutrons equal A minus Z, each line with its value. Layout for a classification question: position, then two properties. The table is then read exactly as the memorandum expects.

The questions for this section are ready: protons, electrons and neutrons from Z and A, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same table again, through a street map, one block read like a label, and counting what is inside an atom — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Street Map of the Elements

Think of the Periodic Table as a street map of a town where 118 families live, the elements. Every family has a short name painted on its gate, its symbol: one or two letters, the first always a capital, the second always small. H for hydrogen, O for oxygen, C for carbon, Ca for calcium.

Some families use old Latin names on their gates, which is why the letters do not match the English. Iron's gate says Fe, from ferrum. Sodium's says Na, from natrium. Potassium's says K. Copper's says Cu, silver's Ag, gold's Au, lead's Pb. Learn those like nicknames.

Be careful with capitals. Co, a capital and a small letter, is one family, cobalt. CO, two capitals, is two families living together, carbon and oxygen, which is a compound called carbon monoxide.

The streets running up and down are called groups, and there are 18 of them. Families on the same street behave alike, like relatives. Street 1 is the wild one: sodium and potassium react with water so fast they fizz and dart around. Street 17 is the halogens, sharp and reactive, like chlorine in a swimming pool. Street 18, at the far right, is the quiet street: the noble gases, helium, neon and argon, which hardly ever react with anyone.

The roads running across are called periods, and there are 7. As you walk along one road from left to right, the families slowly change, from shiny metals on the left to non-metals on the right, ending at a quiet noble gas.

A man called Mendeleev drew the first version of this map in 1869. He even left empty plots for families nobody had met yet, and described what they would be like. Years later they were found, just as he had said.

Keep the street map in mind and try the questions for this part: capital and small letters, the Latin nicknames, and streets against roads.

## Subtopic: Reading One Block

Now walk up to one plot on the map and read its sign. Every block shows a symbol, a name, a small whole number called the atomic number, and a bigger number for the mass.

First, which side of town is it on? There is a staircase line running diagonally down the right-hand part of the map. Everything to the left of the staircase is a metal, about three quarters of all the families. Metals are shiny, they carry heat and electricity well, you can hammer them flat and stretch them into wire, and most are solid. That is why pots are aluminium and wires are copper. The odd one out is mercury, a liquid.

Everything to the right of the staircase is a non-metal. They are dull, they do not carry electricity well, and the solid ones snap or crumble instead of bending. Lots of them are gases: the oxygen and nitrogen you breathe, chlorine, the noble gases. Sulfur and carbon are solids, bromine is a liquid. And hydrogen, even though it sits at the top left, is a non-metal gas.

The families right on the staircase are the in-betweens, the semi-metals. Silicon is the famous one: shiny like a metal but brittle like a non-metal, and it carries electricity only a bit. That in-between behaviour is exactly what you need for computer chips and solar panels.

So reading a block starts with position: left, metal; right, non-metal; on the staircase, semi-metal. Then you can check with properties: shiny, conducts, bends, metal; dull, does not conduct, snaps, non-metal.

Hold the staircase in mind and try this part's questions: which side is which, the properties of each, and what silicon is used for.

## Subtopic: Counting What Is Inside an Atom

Every atom has a centre, the nucleus, holding protons and neutrons, with electrons zooming around outside. The two numbers on the block let you count all three.

The atomic number is the number of protons. It is like an ID number: change it, and you have a different element. Every atom with 8 protons is oxygen. Every atom with 79 is gold. And because an atom has no overall charge, the electrons match the protons: 8 protons, 8 electrons.

The mass number counts the heavy things in the middle, protons and neutrons together. So to find the neutrons, take the mass number and subtract the protons.

Try sodium: atomic number 11, mass number 23. Protons 11. Electrons 11. Neutrons 23 − 11 = 12. Try aluminium: atomic number 13, mass number 27. Protons 13, electrons 13, neutrons 27 − 13 = 14. Try calcium: atomic number 20, mass number 40. Protons 20, electrons 20, neutrons 40 − 20 = 20. Three numbers, every time, in the same order.

If your table shows a mass with a comma, like 35,5 for chlorine, that is an average over slightly different chlorine atoms; a single chlorine atom has mass number 35 or 37. Grade 10 tells you why.

Pocket summary of the lesson. 118 elements, each with a symbol: capital first, small second, some from Latin. Groups are the 18 columns, similar behaviour; periods are the 7 rows, changing from metal to non-metal. Metals left of the staircase, non-metals right, semi-metals on it, hydrogen a non-metal. Protons equal the atomic number, electrons equal the protons, neutrons equal the mass number minus the atomic number. Grade 10 Physical Sciences opens with this table.

Hold the three counts in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: protons, electrons and neutrons from the two numbers.
