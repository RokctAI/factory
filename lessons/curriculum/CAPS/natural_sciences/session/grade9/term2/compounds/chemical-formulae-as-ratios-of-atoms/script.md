# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains what a chemical formula tells us: which elements a compound contains and in what fixed ratio their atoms are combined, how to read subscripts and brackets, and how to count the atoms in a formula. The anchor examples are water, H2O, with a ratio of two hydrogen atoms to one oxygen atom; carbon dioxide, CO2, one carbon to two oxygen; and glucose, C6H12O6, with 24 atoms in each molecule. The governing principle is the law of constant composition: a given compound always contains the same elements in the same fixed ratio, and the formula is the record of that ratio.

## Subtopic: Elements, Compounds and Molecules

A pure substance is either an element or a compound. An element contains only one kind of atom. A compound contains two or more different elements chemically bonded together in a fixed ratio. A mixture, by contrast, contains substances that are not chemically joined and can be present in any proportion, like sugar dissolved in water.

When elements combine to form a compound, the compound has new properties, often completely unlike those of its elements. Sodium is a soft, silvery metal that reacts violently with water, and chlorine is a poisonous yellow-green gas, yet together they form sodium chloride, table salt, which we sprinkle on our food. Hydrogen is a flammable gas and oxygen supports burning, yet together they form water, which puts out fires. And a compound can be separated into its elements only by a chemical reaction, never by physical methods such as filtering or evaporating.

A molecule is a group of two or more atoms chemically bonded together. Some molecules are made of atoms of the same element: oxygen gas consists of O2 molecules, two oxygen atoms bonded together, and hydrogen, nitrogen and chlorine gases likewise exist as H2, N2 and Cl2. These are elements, not compounds, because only one kind of atom is present. Molecules of compounds contain different atoms: a water molecule has two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom; a carbon dioxide molecule has one carbon atom and two oxygen atoms.

Not every compound is made of separate molecules. Sodium chloride is a giant structure, a lattice, in which enormous numbers of sodium and chlorine particles alternate in a regular three-dimensional pattern, as you can see in the cube shape of salt crystals. There is no single sodium chloride molecule. Its formula, NaCl, tells you the ratio in the lattice: one sodium for every one chlorine.

For marking: an element has one kind of atom; a compound has two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio; a molecule is two or more atoms bonded together, which may be atoms of one element or of several.

The questions for this section are with you now: element, compound or mixture, why salt behaves unlike sodium and chlorine, and which molecules are elements.

## Subtopic: Reading a Chemical Formula

A chemical formula uses the symbols of the elements to show which elements are present, and small numbers written low and to the right, called subscripts, to show how many atoms of each are present. The rule is that a subscript applies only to the symbol immediately before it. If there is no subscript, the number is one.

So in H2O, the 2 belongs to H, and O has no subscript: two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom, three atoms in all. In CO2, the C has no subscript and the 2 belongs to O: one carbon and two oxygen atoms. In NH3, ammonia: one nitrogen, three hydrogen. In CH4, methane, the main gas in biogas: one carbon, four hydrogen. In CaCO3, calcium carbonate, the substance of limestone and chalk: one calcium, one carbon and three oxygen atoms, which is 1 + 1 + 3 = 5 atoms.

Brackets gather a group of atoms that act together, and a subscript after the closing bracket multiplies everything inside. In Ca(OH)2, calcium hydroxide, the 2 after the bracket applies to both the O and the H: one calcium, two oxygen and two hydrogen atoms, 1 + 2 + 2 = 5 atoms. In Mg(NO3)2, magnesium nitrate: one magnesium, two nitrogen, and three times two, six, oxygen atoms. In Al2(SO4)3, aluminium sulfate: two aluminium, three sulfur and four times three, twelve, oxygen atoms, which is 2 + 3 + 12 = 17 atoms in one formula unit.

Read every formula aloud in the same way to avoid errors: symbol, then how many, moving left to right, and multiply anything inside a bracket by the number outside it. Sulfuric acid, H2SO4: hydrogen two, sulfur one, oxygen four, total 2 + 1 + 4 = 7 atoms. Glucose, C6H12O6: carbon six, hydrogen twelve, oxygen six, total 6 + 12 + 6 = 24 atoms.

A formula also tells you at a glance whether a substance is an element or a compound. Count the different symbols, ignoring the numbers. One symbol, such as Fe, O2, N2 or S, means one kind of atom and therefore an element, however many atoms are in each particle. Two or more different symbols, such as CO, NaCl or H2SO4, mean a compound. Capital letters are the quickest guide, because every symbol begins with exactly one capital: CO has two capitals, so two elements; Co has one, so one element, cobalt. Counting capitals in Al2(SO4)3 gives three, aluminium, sulfur and oxygen, so it is a compound of three elements.

Take this section's questions now: the rule for subscripts, the effect of brackets, and the number of atoms of each element in a formula.

## Subtopic: Formulae as Fixed Ratios

A formula is above all a ratio. H2O means hydrogen and oxygen atoms in the ratio 2 to 1. CO2 means carbon and oxygen atoms in the ratio 1 to 2. NaCl means sodium and chlorine in the ratio 1 to 1. Fe2O3, iron oxide, means iron to oxygen in the ratio 2 to 3. Al2O3, aluminium oxide, also 2 to 3. MgO, magnesium oxide, 1 to 1.

The ratio is fixed. Every sample of pure water ever tested, from rain, the sea, ice or a laboratory, contains hydrogen and oxygen atoms in exactly the ratio 2 to 1. Because the atoms have fixed masses, the ratio by mass is fixed too: an oxygen atom has 16 times the mass of a hydrogen atom, so in water the 2 hydrogen atoms have a combined mass of 2 units against 16 for the oxygen, and hydrogen always makes up about 11 percent of the mass of water. This is the law of constant composition, first stated by the French chemist Joseph Proust around 1800, and it is what distinguishes a compound from a mixture, which can be made in any proportions.

Change the ratio and you have a different compound with different properties. H2O is water, which you drink; H2O2 is hydrogen peroxide, a bleach and disinfectant that is harmful to swallow. CO, carbon monoxide, is a colourless, deadly gas produced by burning fuel with too little air; CO2, carbon dioxide, is the gas you breathe out. SO2, sulfur dioxide, and SO3, sulfur trioxide, are different compounds too. So the subscripts in a formula are never changed to suit a calculation: changing a subscript changes the substance. The next topic, balancing equations, depends on this rule.

The ratio in a formula is the simplest whole-number ratio for lattice compounds such as NaCl or MgO, but for molecules the formula gives the actual number of each atom in one molecule. Glucose is C6H12O6, not CH2O, because one glucose molecule really contains six carbon atoms. The ratio of carbon to hydrogen to oxygen in glucose is still 1 to 2 to 1, but the formula records the true molecule.

This section's questions are available now: the ratio in a given formula, why water is always 2 to 1, and what happens when a subscript changes.

## Subtopic: Counting Atoms in Formulae and the Error Museum

Counting atoms is the skill that every later chemistry calculation depends on, so it is worth setting out as a table. Write the formula, list each element, write the number of atoms of each, then add for the total.

Carbon dioxide, CO2: carbon 1, oxygen 2, total 3. Ammonia, NH3: nitrogen 1, hydrogen 3, total 4. Calcium carbonate, CaCO3: calcium 1, carbon 1, oxygen 3, total 5. Sulfuric acid, H2SO4: hydrogen 2, sulfur 1, oxygen 4, total 7. Calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)2: calcium 1, oxygen 2, hydrogen 2, total 5. Aluminium sulfate, Al2(SO4)3: aluminium 2, sulfur 3, oxygen 12, total 17. Glucose, C6H12O6: carbon 6, hydrogen 12, oxygen 6, total 24.

Count the different elements separately from the atoms. Glucose contains three elements but 24 atoms. Sulfuric acid contains three elements and seven atoms. A question asking how many elements are in a compound wants the number of different symbols.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: O2 is a compound because it has two atoms; it is an element, because both atoms are oxygen. Two: in Ca(OH)2 there is one hydrogen atom; the bracket subscript multiplies both the oxygen and the hydrogen, giving two of each. Three: in CO2 there are two carbon atoms; the 2 belongs only to the oxygen. Four: a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen gases is water; it is a mixture until the gases react, and then the product is a compound with new properties. Five: you can change H2O to H2O2 to balance an equation; changing a subscript makes a different substance.

Layout for a counting question: a table of element, number of atoms, then total. Layout for a ratio question: the elements in order and their ratio, such as carbon to oxygen 1 to 2. Formulas are then read exactly as the memorandum expects.

The questions for this section are ready: atom counts with and without brackets, elements against atoms, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through recipes written in letters and numbers, a little number that belongs to its neighbour, and the same letters making a different recipe — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Recipes in Letters and Numbers

Imagine making vetkoek. The recipe says how much flour, how much water, how much yeast. If you change the amounts too much, you get something else, maybe bread or a pancake. Chemical compounds work the same way, except that the recipe is fixed perfectly: the same elements, in exactly the same ratio, every single time.

An element is one kind of atom only, like pure oxygen or pure iron. A compound is two or more different elements joined together chemically. And here is the surprising part: the compound is nothing like its ingredients. Sodium is a metal that explodes in water. Chlorine is a poisonous green gas. Join them and you get table salt, which you put on your chips. Hydrogen burns and oxygen helps things burn, but join them and you get water, which puts fires out.

The formula is the recipe card, written in a short code. Symbols say which ingredients, and the small numbers say how many of each. H2O: two hydrogens, one oxygen. CO2: one carbon, two oxygens. NaCl: one sodium, one chlorine.

Some elements come in pairs. The oxygen in the air is O2, two oxygen atoms holding hands. That is still an element, because both atoms are oxygen. Water is a compound because it has two different kinds of atom.

And salt is a bit different again: it is not made of separate little molecules but of a huge crowd of sodium and chlorine particles stacked in a pattern, one for one, like the black and white squares of a chessboard in three dimensions. The formula NaCl just says: one to one.

Keep the recipe card in mind and try the questions for this part: element or compound, why salt is nothing like sodium, and what O2 is.

## Subtopic: The Little Number Belongs to the Letter Before It

There is one rule for reading formulas, and almost every mistake comes from forgetting it: the little number belongs to the letter right before it, and only to that letter. No little number means one.

Try H2O. The 2 sits after H, so two hydrogens. O has no number, so one oxygen. Three atoms. Try CO2. C has no number, one carbon. The 2 sits after O, two oxygens. Not two carbons. Try NH3: one nitrogen, three hydrogens. Try CH4, the gas in biogas: one carbon, four hydrogens.

Now brackets. Brackets are like a packet. Ca(OH)2 means one calcium and two packets of OH. Open the packets: two oxygens and two hydrogens. So one calcium, two oxygen, two hydrogen, five atoms. Without seeing the bracket you would say one hydrogen, and lose the mark.

A bigger one: Al2(SO4)3. Two aluminium. Then three packets of SO4. Each packet has one sulfur and four oxygens, so three packets give three sulfurs and twelve oxygens. Total: 2 + 3 + 12 = 17 atoms.

The best habit is to read every formula out loud, left to right, element then number. Sulfuric acid, H2SO4: hydrogen two, sulfur one, oxygen four, seven atoms altogether. Glucose, C6H12O6: carbon six, hydrogen twelve, oxygen six, that is 24 atoms, but only three different elements. Count elements and atoms separately; questions ask for both.

Hold the little-number rule and the packets in mind and try this part's questions: whose number is it, what brackets do, and elements against atoms.

## Subtopic: Same Letters, Different Recipe

Because the recipe is fixed, every glass of pure water in the world has exactly two hydrogens for every oxygen. Water from the Vaal, from the sea, from melting ice in a cooler box: always 2 to 1. And because the atoms always weigh the same, the hydrogen is always about 11 percent of the mass of water. That fixed recipe is what makes something a compound and not a mixture. A mixture, like sugar in tea, can be made any strength you like. A compound cannot.

Now the dangerous bit. Change one little number and you change the whole substance. H2O is water, safe to drink. H2O2, one extra oxygen, is hydrogen peroxide, which bleaches hair and kills germs, and you must never drink it. CO2 is the gas you breathe out. CO, one oxygen fewer, is carbon monoxide, a gas with no colour and no smell that kills people every winter when they use a brazier or a generator in a closed room.

That is why, in the next lessons, when you balance chemical equations, you will never be allowed to change the little numbers inside a formula. Change them and you are writing about a different substance. You can only put bigger numbers in front of whole formulas.

Pocket summary of the lesson. An element has one kind of atom; a compound has two or more elements joined in a fixed ratio, with new properties; a molecule is atoms bonded together. The little number belongs to the symbol before it; no number means one; a number after a bracket multiplies everything inside. A formula is a ratio: H2O is 2 to 1, CO2 is 1 to 2, Fe2O3 is 2 to 3. Change a subscript and you change the substance. Grade 10 Physical Sciences uses formulas in every calculation.

Hold the fixed recipe in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: ratios, the water and hydrogen peroxide difference, and why subscripts never change.
