### subtopic_1 — Elements, Symbols and Names
**Assistant:** Why do some elements have symbols that do not match their English names?

**Tutor:** Because the symbols were standardised in the early 1800s, when much of European science was still written in Latin, and for elements that had been known since ancient times the Latin names were the ones chemists shared across languages. The Swedish chemist Jöns Jacob Berzelius proposed the system of one- or two-letter symbols in 1813, taking them from the Latin name where one existed so that a Swedish, French, German or English chemist would all write the same symbol. Metals used for thousands of years, such as iron, copper, silver, gold, lead, tin and mercury, all had Latin names: ferrum, cuprum, argentum, aurum, plumbum, stannum and hydrargyrum. Sodium and potassium were isolated only in 1807, by Humphry Davy, but their compounds natron and kali had long been known, so the Latinised names natrium and kalium gave Na and K. You can still see the Latin roots in everyday words: a plumber was originally someone who worked with lead pipes, the Spanish word for silver gives Argentina its name, and ferrous metals are those containing iron. Tungsten's symbol W comes from its German name, wolfram. The great advantage of the system is that it is international: a chemist in Durban, Tokyo or Lima reads Fe and knows it means iron without translation, and the same symbol appears in every language's textbooks. For tests, the Latin-based symbols simply have to be memorised, and the common ones are Na, K, Fe, Cu, Ag, Au, Pb, Sn and Hg; all the other elements you meet in Grade 9 have symbols that follow their English names.

### subtopic_2 — Groups and Periods
**Assistant:** Why do elements in the same group behave in similar ways?

**Tutor:** Because their atoms have the same number of electrons in the outermost shell, and chemical reactions are carried out by those outer electrons. Electrons in an atom are arranged in shells around the nucleus, filling the inner shells first. When atoms react, they gain, lose or share electrons in the outer shell, the part of the atom that touches other atoms. So the number of outer electrons largely decides how an element reacts. Every element in Group 1, lithium, sodium, potassium and so on, has exactly one outer electron, which it loses easily, so they all react vigorously with water and oxygen and all form compounds with similar formulas, such as sodium chloride and potassium chloride. Every element in Group 17 has seven outer electrons and readily gains one more, so fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine all react strongly with metals to form salts. The noble gases in Group 18 have full outer shells, which are very stable, so they have almost no tendency to react. Going down a group, the atoms gain extra inner shells, so they become larger, and the outer electrons are further from the nucleus. That is why the properties within a group are similar but not identical: in Group 1, potassium loses its outer electron even more easily than sodium and so reacts more violently with water. Across a period, by contrast, each element has one more outer electron than the one before it, so the properties change step by step. Mendeleev noticed the patterns without knowing about electrons; they were explained only decades later, and Grade 10 Physical Sciences works out the electron arrangements in detail.

### subtopic_3 — Metals, Non-metals and Semi-metals
**Assistant:** If silicon only partly conducts electricity, why is it so useful?

**Tutor:** Because a material that conducts only partly can be controlled, and control is exactly what electronics needs. A metal such as copper always conducts and an insulator such as plastic never does, so neither can act as a switch by itself. In a semiconductor such as silicon, only a few electrons are free to move at room temperature, and the number can be changed greatly by adding tiny, carefully measured amounts of other elements, a process called doping, or by applying light, heat or a small voltage. Engineers build regions of differently doped silicon side by side to make transistors, tiny switches that turn a current on or off when a small signal is applied, with no moving parts. A modern computer chip, the size of a fingernail, contains billions of transistors switching billions of times each second, and every cellphone, laptop and bank card chip depends on them. Silicon is also what most solar panels are made of. When light falls on a solar cell, its energy frees electrons in the silicon, and the arrangement of doped layers pushes those electrons in one direction, producing an electric current from sunlight. Silicon has further advantages: it is the second most common element in the Earth's crust after oxygen, found in sand and quartz as silicon dioxide, so it is plentiful, although purifying it to the extremely high standard needed for chips is costly. Its in-between position on the staircase line of the Periodic Table, shiny like a metal but brittle like a non-metal, matches its in-between electrical behaviour, which is precisely why it became the basis of modern technology.

### subtopic_4 — Atomic Number, Mass Number and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** If all atoms of an element have the same protons, why is chlorine's mass shown as 35,5?

**Tutor:** Because atoms of the same element can have different numbers of neutrons, and the number on the table is an average over the mixture found in nature. Every chlorine atom has 17 protons; that is what makes it chlorine. But in a sample of natural chlorine, about three quarters of the atoms have 18 neutrons, giving them a mass number of 35, and about one quarter have 20 neutrons, giving a mass number of 37. These different forms of the same element, with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons, are called isotopes. They behave the same way chemically, because chemistry depends on electrons, and the number of electrons matches the protons. When the masses are averaged, weighting each by how common it is, the result is about 35,5. No single chlorine atom has a mass number of 35,5; mass numbers always count whole particles. Most elements are mixtures of isotopes, which is why the masses on a Periodic Table are usually not whole numbers, although for many elements one isotope is so dominant that the average is close to a whole number, such as 12,0 for carbon or 23,0 for sodium. Some isotopes are unstable and radioactive, and these have important uses: carbon-14 is used to date ancient bones and wood, and uranium-235 is the fuel in the reactors at Koeberg near Cape Town, which you will meet in Term 3. For Grade 9, you will be given the mass number of a specific atom, or asked to round the table's value, and Grade 10 Physical Sciences develops isotopes and the calculation of these averages.
