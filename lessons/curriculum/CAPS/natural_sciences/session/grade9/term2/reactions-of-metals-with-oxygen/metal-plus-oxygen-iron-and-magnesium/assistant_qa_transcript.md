### subtopic_1 — The General Reaction: Metal + Oxygen → Metal Oxide
**Assistant:** Why do some metals react with oxygen so much more readily than others?

**Tutor:** It depends on how easily the metal's atoms give away their outer electrons, which is what happens when a metal forms an oxide. In the reaction, each metal atom loses electrons and becomes a positive ion, and each oxygen atom gains two electrons and becomes an oxide ion with a charge of 2−. Metals on the far left of the Periodic Table, such as sodium and potassium in Group 1, have one outer electron that is held loosely, so they give it up very easily and react with the oxygen in air in seconds; freshly cut sodium is shiny for a moment and then goes dull grey. Magnesium in Group 2 has two outer electrons that are held more firmly, so it needs heating before it burns, but then it burns fiercely. Iron and copper, among the transition metals, hold their electrons more tightly still, and gold holds them so tightly that it does not react with oxygen at all. Chemists arrange metals in an order of how readily they react, called the reactivity series, and you will use it in Grade 10 and later. Reactivity also explains which metals were discovered first. Gold, silver and copper were used for thousands of years, because they are found as the metal or are easy to extract, while very reactive metals such as sodium and aluminium were only isolated about 200 years ago, using electricity, because they cling so firmly to the oxygen in their ores.

### subtopic_2 — Magnesium Reacting with Oxygen
**Assistant:** If magnesium oxide is heavier, why do I see white smoke leaving the crucible?

**Tutor:** Because some of the magnesium oxide escapes as a fine white smoke, and that is the main source of error in the experiment. When magnesium burns, it forms tiny particles of magnesium oxide, and the hot air rising from the crucible carries some of them away. If too much smoke escapes, the mass of product you weigh at the end is less than it should be, and you might even conclude that the mass has decreased. The standard method reduces this loss by heating the magnesium in a crucible with a lid, and lifting the lid briefly with tongs from time to time to let fresh air in, then replacing it quickly. The magnesium is often coiled loosely so that air can reach all of it. Heating continues until the magnesium no longer glows when the lid is lifted, which shows the reaction is complete. The crucible is then cooled and weighed with its lid. With care, the gain in mass comes close to the expected value. For 2,4 grams of magnesium the expected product is 4,0 grams, a gain of 1,6 grams, and a careful class experiment might give something like 3,8 or 3,9 grams. Another small error is that magnesium can also react with nitrogen in the air at high temperature, forming a little magnesium nitride. When writing up the practical, mention the escaping smoke as a source of error and the lid as the way to reduce it.

### subtopic_3 — Iron Reacting with Oxygen
**Assistant:** Is the black stuff on burnt steel wool the same as rust?

**Tutor:** They are both iron oxides, but they are not identical, and the difference is a good example of how textbook equations simplify real chemistry. When steel wool burns in a hot flame with plenty of oxygen, it forms a dark, blue-black brittle solid that is mostly the oxide with formula Fe3O4, a mixture of two kinds of iron ions; it is the same black, magnetic oxide found in the mineral magnetite. Rust, which forms slowly at room temperature when iron is exposed to oxygen and water together, is a reddish-brown, flaky substance that is mostly iron(III) oxide, Fe2O3, with water molecules held within it, so it is called hydrated iron(III) oxide. The Grade 9 curriculum writes both reactions with Fe2O3, as 4Fe + 3O2 → 2Fe2O3, which is acceptable for now because the key ideas are the same: iron combines with oxygen to form an iron oxide, the product is heavier than the iron, and the oxide is a base. In Grade 10 and later you will see that iron can form ions with a charge of 2+ or 3+, and that is why it has more than one oxide. South Africa has large deposits of iron ores, mostly hematite, which is Fe2O3, mined at Sishen in the Northern Cape and elsewhere, and smelting those ores in a blast furnace reverses the reaction, removing the oxygen to give back the iron.

### subtopic_4 — Comparing the Reactions and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why are metal oxides bases while non-metal oxides are acids?

**Tutor:** Because of what each kind of oxide does when it meets water or an acid. A metal oxide is made of metal ions and oxide ions. The oxide ion, O2−, readily takes hydrogen ions from water or from an acid, and taking hydrogen ions is exactly what a base does. When magnesium oxide or calcium oxide is shaken with water, some of it reacts to form a hydroxide, such as magnesium hydroxide or calcium hydroxide, and the solution has a pH above 7 and turns universal indicator blue or purple. Calcium oxide, called quicklime, reacts strongly with water and is used by farmers and builders, and magnesium hydroxide is the active ingredient in milk of magnesia, used to neutralise excess stomach acid. Non-metal oxides, such as carbon dioxide and sulfur dioxide, are made of molecules. When they dissolve in water they form acids, carbonic acid and sulfurous acid, which release hydrogen ions and lower the pH below 7. That is why rain that falls through air polluted with sulfur dioxide from coal-burning power stations in Mpumalanga becomes acid rain. You will study the non-metal oxides in the next topic and use both kinds of oxide in the reactions of acids and bases later this term, where metal oxide plus acid gives salt plus water.
