# Part 1 — Expert

This session sets out the general reaction of a metal with oxygen, applies it to magnesium and iron with word equations and balanced chemical equations, and explains the observations, the mass changes and the differences in reactivity between metals. The anchor examples are magnesium burning, 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO, in which 2,4 grams of magnesium form 4,0 grams of magnesium oxide, and iron burning as steel wool, written at Grade 9 level as 4Fe + 3O2 → 2Fe2O3. The governing principle is that a metal combines with oxygen to form a single product, a metal oxide, and that atoms are conserved, so the oxide weighs more than the metal by exactly the mass of oxygen that joined it.

## Subtopic: The General Reaction: Metal + Oxygen → Metal Oxide

When a metal reacts with oxygen, the atoms of the metal combine with atoms of oxygen to form a compound called a metal oxide. The general word equation is: metal + oxygen → metal oxide.

Three features of this reaction are worth stating precisely. First, there are two reactants and one product, so it is a synthesis or combination reaction: two substances join to make one. Second, the oxygen usually comes from the air, which is about 21 percent oxygen, so the reaction happens faster and more brightly in pure oxygen. Third, the product is named after the metal: the metal's name followed by oxide. Magnesium forms magnesium oxide, copper forms copper oxide, zinc forms zinc oxide, sodium forms sodium oxide, calcium forms calcium oxide and aluminium forms aluminium oxide.

Some balanced examples: 2Cu + O2 → 2CuO, with copper oxide a black solid; 2Zn + O2 → 2ZnO, a white solid that turns yellow when hot; 4Na + O2 → 2Na2O; and 4Al + 3O2 → 2Al2O3. In each case the oxygen molecule, O2, is diatomic, so the coefficients are needed to balance oxygen.

Metals differ greatly in how readily they react. Sodium and potassium react with oxygen in the air so quickly that they are stored under paraffin oil. Magnesium burns brightly once heated. Iron reacts slowly at room temperature, which is rusting, and burns only when finely divided, as in steel wool. Copper only forms a black coating when heated strongly. Gold does not react with oxygen at all, which is why gold from the Witwatersrand stays shiny for thousands of years.

Metal oxides have a chemical property that matters in the next topics: those that dissolve in water form basic solutions, and all of them react with acids. Magnesium oxide in water turns universal indicator blue.

Some metals protect themselves with their own oxide. Aluminium is a reactive metal, yet aluminium window frames, pots and cooldrink cans last for years. The reason is that aluminium reacts with oxygen at once to form a very thin, tough, transparent layer of aluminium oxide that sticks tightly to the surface and stops further oxygen reaching the metal underneath. Iron is the opposite: its oxide, rust, is loose and flaky, so it falls off and exposes fresh iron to attack. This contrast will matter in the next lesson.

The same general reaction also runs backwards in industry, which shows why it is important. Most metals are found in the ground not as the metal but as compounds, very often oxides. Iron ore mined at Sishen in the Northern Cape is mostly the iron oxide hematite. To obtain the metal, the oxygen must be removed again, which takes a great deal of energy, and that is what a blast furnace does. The more readily a metal combines with oxygen, the harder it is to separate the two afterwards.

The questions for this section are with you now: the general word equation, naming the product and reactivity.

## Subtopic: Magnesium Reacting with Oxygen

Magnesium is a light, silvery metal supplied in school laboratories as a thin ribbon. To burn it, a short piece is held with crucible tongs in the hottest part of a Bunsen burner flame. Once it ignites it burns with an intensely bright white light and leaves a white, powdery solid.

Word equation: magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide.

Balanced chemical equation: 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO. Check: magnesium 2 and 2; oxygen 2 and 2. The magnesium ion has a charge of 2+ and the oxide ion a charge of 2−, so the formula is MgO in a 1 to 1 ratio.

Safety: the light is bright enough to damage the eyes, so you must not stare directly at it; the teacher may ask you to look through blue glass or at the reflection. The ribbon must be held at arm's length with tongs over a heatproof mat. This same bright light is why magnesium is used in emergency flares and fireworks.

Mass: if the experiment is done in a crucible with a lid lifted slightly now and then, so that air can enter but smoke cannot easily escape, the product weighs more than the magnesium. With 2,4 grams of magnesium, 4,0 grams of magnesium oxide form. The increase, 4,0 − 2,4 = 1,6 grams, is the mass of oxygen that combined. Atoms are conserved: oxygen from the air was simply not weighed at the start.

A test: add a few drops of water to the white powder, then a drop of universal indicator. It turns blue or violet, showing that magnesium oxide forms a basic solution, magnesium hydroxide.

Notice how the observations are worded in a good answer. Before: a shiny, silvery-grey, flexible metal ribbon. During: a very bright white flame, with some white smoke. After: a white, crumbly powder. Each observation describes something you can see, not something you infer; saying that a new substance formed is a conclusion drawn from the observations, because the white powder has different properties from the shiny metal.

Magnesium is reactive enough that its surface slowly dulls even in air, forming a thin layer of oxide. That is why a ribbon is often rubbed with sandpaper before an experiment: the clean metal underneath lights more easily and reacts completely.

The questions for this section are ready: observations, the balanced equation and the mass change.

## Subtopic: Iron Reacting with Oxygen

An iron nail heated in a Bunsen flame barely changes, but steel wool, which is fine strands of mostly iron, burns readily. Because the strands are thin, a very large surface of iron is in contact with air, and the heat cannot escape quickly. Held in a flame or touched to the terminals of a battery, steel wool glows red and throws off bright orange sparks, leaving a brittle, dark blue-black solid.

Word equation: iron + oxygen → iron oxide.

At Grade 9 the product is written as iron(III) oxide, Fe2O3, giving the balanced equation 4Fe + 3O2 → 2Fe2O3. Check: iron 4 and 2 × 2 = 4; oxygen 3 × 2 = 6 and 2 × 3 = 6. The dark solid formed in a hot flame actually contains a mixture of iron oxides, mostly one with the formula Fe3O4, but the textbook and examination answer at this level is Fe2O3, which is also the main iron oxide in rust.

Mass: if a ball of steel wool is balanced on a scale and then burnt, the side holding the steel wool goes down. Iron combines with oxygen and the product is heavier. With 5,6 grams of iron fully converted to Fe2O3, 8,0 grams of iron oxide form, a gain of 8,0 − 5,6 = 2,4 grams of oxygen.

Iron also reacts with oxygen slowly at room temperature when water is present. That is rusting, the subject of the next lesson.

Surface area explains why the nail and the steel wool behave so differently. The more surface exposed, the more collisions between iron atoms and oxygen molecules each second, and the faster the reaction. Iron filings sprinkled into a flame sparkle for the same reason.

Why does the burnt steel wool become heavier, when a burnt log becomes lighter? The answer lies in the products. When wood burns, its carbon and hydrogen combine with oxygen to form carbon dioxide and water vapour, both gases, which escape. When iron burns, it combines with oxygen to form a solid oxide, which stays on the balance. In both cases oxygen is added and atoms are conserved, but only the iron keeps all of its product where it can be weighed.

Iron combining with oxygen also releases energy as heat. Hand warmers sold to hikers contain iron powder, salt, water and a little carbon in a sealed pouch; when the pouch is opened to the air, the iron reacts with oxygen quickly enough to warm the hands for hours. It is the same reaction as rusting, just speeded up.

## Subtopic: Comparing the Reactions and the Error Museum

Magnesium and iron both follow metal + oxygen → metal oxide, both form a solid oxide heavier than the metal, and both oxides are bases. They differ in vigour: magnesium burns brightly as a ribbon, while iron burns only as steel wool or filings. Magnesium oxide is white; iron oxide is dark or reddish brown.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: magnesium burns to form magnesium and oxygen; it forms one product, magnesium oxide. Two: the product weighs less because the metal burns away; it weighs more, because oxygen is added. Three: writing the equation as Mg + O → MgO; oxygen is O2, so the balanced equation is 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO. Four: rust and burning iron are unrelated; both are iron combining with oxygen, one fast and one slow. Five: metal oxides are acidic; metal oxides are basic.

Layout for a metal-and-oxygen answer: general word equation; specific word equation; balanced chemical equation with a stocktake; observations such as colour, light and the appearance of the product; and a mass statement if asked.

A useful comparison table has four rows. Reactants: magnesium and oxygen; iron and oxygen. Conditions: ribbon in a hot flame; steel wool in a flame or touched to a battery. Observations: bright white light, white powder; glowing, orange sparks, dark brittle solid. Equation: 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO; 4Fe + 3O2 → 2Fe2O3. In both, the mass increases and the product is basic.

The questions for this section are with you now: comparing the two metals and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same reactions again, through a metal meeting oxygen, a white flash and a sparkling sponge — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Metal Meets Oxygen

Most metals have a weak spot: oxygen. Give a metal the chance, with enough heat or enough time, and its atoms grab oxygen atoms from the air and join to make a new substance called an oxide.

The pattern is always the same: metal plus oxygen gives metal oxide. Two things go in, one thing comes out. And the name is easy: say the metal, then say oxide. Magnesium gives magnesium oxide. Copper gives copper oxide. Zinc gives zinc oxide.

Some metals are greedy for oxygen, others are lazy. Sodium is so greedy that it has to be kept under oil so the air cannot reach it. Magnesium burns like a sparkler once lit. Iron is slow; it takes years to rust. Gold is not interested at all, which is why an old gold ring still shines.

One more thing to remember: metal oxides are bases, the opposite of acids. That will matter when we get to acids next.

Aluminium is a clever one. It is quite greedy for oxygen, but the oxide it makes is a thin, tough skin that sticks tight and keeps the rest of the metal safe. That is why an aluminium pot or window frame lasts so long. Iron's oxide, rust, does the opposite: it flakes off and lets the air get at more iron.

Most metals in the ground are already joined to oxygen. Iron ore from the Northern Cape is iron oxide. Getting the iron back out means pulling the oxygen off again in a very hot furnace, which costs a lot of energy.

Keep the pattern in mind and try the questions for this part: the general equation and naming the oxide.

## Subtopic: The White Flash

Hold a strip of magnesium ribbon with tongs in a hot flame. For a second nothing happens, then it bursts into a dazzling white light, so bright you must not look straight at it. When it is over, a soft white powder is left. That powder is magnesium oxide.

In words: magnesium plus oxygen gives magnesium oxide. In symbols: 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO. Count it: two magnesiums on each side and two oxygens on each side.

Here is a surprise. Weigh the magnesium before and the powder after, and the powder is heavier. How can burning make something heavier? Because oxygen from the air has joined on. 2,4 grams of magnesium picks up 1,6 grams of oxygen and becomes 4,0 grams of magnesium oxide. Nothing was created; the oxygen was just not on the scale at the start.

That bright flash is the same one used in emergency flares and in fireworks.

When you write about it, keep what you saw separate from what you worked out. Saw: shiny ribbon, blinding white flame, white powder. Worked out: a new substance, magnesium oxide, formed. If the ribbon looks dull, rub it with sandpaper first, because a thin layer of oxide has already formed on it in the air.

Hold the white flash in mind and try this part's questions: what you see, the equation and the weight gain.

## Subtopic: The Sparkling Sponge

An iron nail in a flame just gets hot. But pull out a pad of steel wool, which is thin threads of iron, and touch it to a flame or to a battery, and it glows and sprays orange sparks. Thin threads mean lots of iron touching the air, so the reaction is much faster.

In words: iron plus oxygen gives iron oxide. In symbols, the Grade 9 version: 4Fe + 3O2 → 2Fe2O3. Count it: four irons each side, six oxygens each side.

And again it gets heavier. Hang a ball of steel wool on one side of a balance, burn it, and that side drops, because oxygen has joined the iron.

The same reaction happens slowly to a bicycle left out in the rain. That slow version is rust, and it is next.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A metal and oxygen make a metal oxide, one product named after the metal. Magnesium burns with a white flash to white magnesium oxide, 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO. Iron as steel wool sparkles to iron oxide, 4Fe + 3O2 → 2Fe2O3. The oxide weighs more because oxygen joined. Metal oxides are bases. Grade 10 Physical Sciences builds on these reactions.

Why does burnt wood get lighter but burnt steel wool get heavier? Wood turns into gases that float away. Iron turns into a solid oxide that stays put on the scale. Oxygen joins in both cases.
