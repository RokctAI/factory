# Part 1 — Expert

This session explains what rust is, the conditions needed for it to form, the experiment that proves those conditions, why rusting is so damaging, and the methods used to prevent it: painting and other barrier coatings, electroplating with chromium or zinc, and galvanising. The anchor facts are the word equation iron + oxygen + water → hydrated iron(III) oxide, the simplified equation 4Fe + 3O2 → 2Fe2O3 for the oxide part, the three-nail experiment, and the role of salt in speeding up rusting. The governing principle is that rusting needs both oxygen and water, so every method of prevention works by keeping one or both away from the iron, or by giving them a more reactive metal to attack instead.

## Subtopic: What Rust Is and the Conditions for Rusting

Rust is the reddish-brown, flaky solid that forms on iron and steel left outdoors. Chemically it is hydrated iron(III) oxide: iron(III) oxide, Fe2O3, with a variable number of water molecules held in its structure. Only iron and alloys containing iron, such as steel, rust. Other metals corrode, meaning they react with substances in their surroundings, but the word rust is reserved for iron.

The word equation is: iron + oxygen + water → hydrated iron(III) oxide. At Grade 9, the oxide part is written with the balanced equation 4Fe + 3O2 → 2Fe2O3, remembering that water is also involved. Rusting is the same kind of reaction as burning steel wool, metal plus oxygen giving metal oxide, but it happens slowly, over days and years, at ordinary temperatures, and it needs water.

Two conditions are essential: oxygen, usually from the air, and water, either liquid water or moisture in humid air. Without either one, iron does not rust. Several factors speed rusting up. Salt dissolved in the water makes rusting much faster, which is why cars and roofs in coastal towns such as Durban, Gqeberha and Cape Town, where sea spray carries salt inland, rust far sooner than those in dry inland areas such as the Karoo. Acids, including acid rain, speed it up. Higher temperatures speed it up. Scratches and dents in a protective coating expose the iron.

Rust is so destructive because of its physical properties. It is weak and crumbly, it takes up more space than the iron it replaces, and it does not stick firmly. It flakes off, exposing fresh iron beneath, so rusting continues inward until the metal is eaten through. Compare aluminium, whose thin oxide layer sticks tightly and protects the metal below.

The cost is enormous. Corrosion damages bridges, railway lines, ships, water pipes, cars, roofs and the steel reinforcing bars inside concrete buildings. When reinforcing steel rusts, the rust takes up more space than the steel, so it pushes the concrete apart and chunks break off, which can be seen on old buildings and bridges near the coast. Studies around the world estimate that corrosion costs a country a few percent of everything it produces each year, much of which could be saved with proper protection. Rusted water pipes also leak, wasting water in a country where water is scarce.

Rust can be recognised by its colour, reddish brown to orange, by its crumbly texture, and by the fact that it leaves a brown stain on fingers and on surfaces below a rusting object, such as the streaks running down a wall from a rusty gutter.

## Subtopic: Investigating the Conditions for Rusting

The conditions for rusting are proved by a controlled experiment with clean iron nails, usually rubbed with sandpaper to remove grease and any oxide.

Test tube A: a nail in tap water, open to the air. The nail has both water and oxygen.

Test tube B: a nail in water that has been boiled to drive out dissolved air, with a layer of oil poured on top to stop air dissolving back in, and sometimes a stopper. The nail has water but no oxygen.

Test tube C: a nail in dry air, with a drying agent such as anhydrous calcium chloride or silica gel in the bottom to absorb moisture, and a stopper to keep moist air out. The nail has oxygen but no water.

Often a fourth tube is added, D: a nail in salty water, open to the air.

After about a week, the results are clear. Nail A has rusted. Nail B has not rusted. Nail C has not rusted. Nail D has rusted the most.

The conclusions follow from comparing the tubes. A and B differ only in oxygen, so oxygen is needed. A and C differ only in water, so water is needed. A and D differ only in salt, so salt speeds rusting up. In this investigation, the independent variable is the condition the nail is exposed to, the dependent variable is the amount of rust formed, and the controlled variables include the type and size of nail, the cleaning, the temperature and the time.

A good investigation report also states a hypothesis before the experiment, for example: iron nails will rust only when both water and oxygen are present. The results are recorded in a table with a column for each tube and rows for what the tube contains, what it excludes, and the observation after one week, described in words such as no change, a little orange-brown rust, or heavy rust. The conclusion then accepts or rejects the hypothesis.

Possible sources of error are worth mentioning. If the stopper on tube C is loose, moist air can get in. If the oil layer on tube B is too thin, or the water was not boiled long enough, some oxygen may remain and a trace of rust may appear. Repeating each tube with two or three nails makes the results more reliable.

## Subtopic: Preventing Rusting

Since rusting needs oxygen and water, the main approach is a barrier that keeps them away from the iron. A second approach uses a more reactive metal that corrodes in place of the iron.

Barrier methods. Painting is used on bridges, cars, roofs and railings; it is cheap but works only while the paint is unbroken. Oiling or greasing is used on moving parts such as bicycle chains, engine parts and tools, where paint would rub off. Plastic coating is used on wire fencing, dish racks and garden furniture.

Electroplating with chromium. A thin, shiny layer of chromium is deposited on steel using electricity. The steel object is connected to the negative terminal of a power supply and dipped into a solution containing chromium ions, and the current makes a layer of chromium metal form on its surface. Chromium does not corrode in air and gives a bright, attractive finish, so it is used on taps, bicycle handlebars, car trim and tools. If the chromium is scratched, the steel beneath can rust.

Electroplating or galvanising with zinc. A layer of zinc is applied either by electroplating or by dipping the steel into molten zinc, which is called hot-dip galvanising. Galvanised steel is used for corrugated iron roofs, fence poles, buckets, water tanks and the steel pylons of the national grid. Zinc gives double protection. It is a barrier, and because zinc is more reactive than iron, it corrodes in preference to the iron even if the coating is scratched, so the exposed iron does not rust. This is called sacrificial protection.

The same principle protects large structures. Blocks of zinc or magnesium are bolted to the steel hulls of ships and to underground pipelines; they corrode slowly instead of the steel and are replaced every few years.

Alloying is another approach: stainless steel contains chromium and nickel mixed into the iron, so it forms a protective oxide skin like aluminium and does not rust, which is why it is used for sinks, cutlery and hospital equipment.

All protective methods need maintenance. Paint on a bridge or on the steel structure of a stadium is inspected and repainted every few years. Galvanised roofs are often painted as well, so that the zinc lasts longer. Oil on tools must be renewed after they are used in wet conditions.

## Subtopic: Choosing a Method and the Error Museum

Choosing a method is a matter of matching cost, appearance and the conditions of use. A bridge over a river is painted, because it is large and paint can be renewed. A bicycle chain is oiled, because it moves. A roof is galvanised, because it must last outdoors for decades. A tap is chromium-plated, because it must look shiny and resist water. A tin can for food is plated with tin, which is unreactive and non-toxic, though once it is dented and scratched the steel beneath rusts quickly.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: all metals rust; only iron and steel rust, while other metals corrode. Two: iron rusts in dry air; it needs water as well as oxygen. Three: boiled water in tube B is to kill germs; it removes dissolved oxygen. Four: paint protects even when scratched; a scratch lets water and oxygen in, while only zinc gives sacrificial protection. Five: rust protects iron like aluminium oxide protects aluminium; rust flakes off and exposes fresh iron.

Layout for a rusting investigation answer: aim; hypothesis; the set-up of each tube with what it contains and excludes; results in a table; a conclusion that names the variable proved by each comparison. Layout for a prevention answer: name the method, explain how it keeps oxygen or water away or how it acts sacrificially, and give an example of its use.

Cost matters too. Painting and oiling are the cheapest methods but need frequent renewal. Galvanising costs more at the start but can protect a fence pole for many decades. Stainless steel is the most expensive, so it is used where hygiene or appearance justifies the price.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through the slow fire, the nail detectives and the bodyguards for iron — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: The Slow Fire

Rust is iron burning, very, very slowly. When steel wool burns in a flame, iron grabs oxygen in a few seconds. When a gate is left in the rain, iron grabs oxygen over months and years. The product is the same family, iron oxide, but rust also has water locked inside it.

In words: iron plus oxygen plus water gives rust, hydrated iron oxide.

Two things must be there together: air and water. Take away either, and the iron stays shiny.

Some things make rust work faster. Salt is the big one. A car in Durban, where the sea breeze carries salt spray, rusts much faster than the same car in dry Kimberley. Acid rain speeds it up. Warmth speeds it up. Any scratch in the paint lets it start.

Why is rust such a problem? Because it is crumbly and puffy and does not stick. It flakes off and lets the air and water get to the fresh iron underneath. So it keeps eating inwards until there is a hole. Think of a rusty bakkie with holes in the bottom of its doors.

## Subtopic: The Nail Detectives

How do we know rust needs both air and water? We set up a fair test with shiny nails.

Tube one: nail in ordinary water, open to the air. It gets both.

Tube two: nail in water that was boiled to chase out the air, with a layer of oil on top so no air can get back in. It gets water but no air.

Tube three: nail in dry air, with a drying chemical at the bottom that soaks up every bit of moisture, and a stopper. It gets air but no water.

Tube four, for fun: nail in salty water, open to the air.

Wait a week. Tube one: rusty. Tube two: shiny. Tube three: shiny. Tube four: very rusty.

Now be a detective. Tubes one and two differ only in air, and only one rusted, so air is needed. Tubes one and three differ only in water, so water is needed. Tubes one and four differ only in salt, so salt makes it worse.

That is a fair test: change one thing at a time and keep everything else the same. Use the same kind of nail in every tube, clean them all the same way, and leave them all for the same time.

## Subtopic: Bodyguards for Iron

If rust needs air and water, protect the iron by keeping them out, or by giving the iron a bodyguard.

Keep them out. Paint a bridge or a car. Oil a bicycle chain, because paint would rub off. Coat a fence or a dish rack in plastic. These all work until they get scratched.

Shiny armour. Chromium is plated onto taps and handlebars using electricity: the object is dipped into a chromium solution and connected to the negative side of a power supply, and a thin shiny layer of chromium forms. It looks great and keeps water off.

The real bodyguard is zinc. Corrugated iron roofs, fence poles and the big pylons that carry electricity are coated with zinc, which is called galvanising. Zinc is more reactive than iron, so if the coating is scratched, the zinc gets attacked instead of the iron. It sacrifices itself. Ships use the same trick with blocks of zinc bolted to their hulls.

And stainless steel in your kitchen sink has chromium mixed right through it, so it never rusts.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Rust is hydrated iron oxide and needs oxygen and water together. Salt, acid and warmth speed it up. The nail experiment proves both are needed. Rust flakes and keeps spreading. Prevent it with paint, oil or plastic, by electroplating with chromium, or by galvanising with zinc, which protects even when scratched. Grade 10 to 12 chemistry explains the electricity behind it.

Which bodyguard for which job? Bridge: paint, because it is huge and can be repainted. Bicycle chain: oil, because it moves. Roof: zinc, because it must last for years in the rain. Tap: chromium, because it must shine. Food can: tin, because tin does not react with food, even though a dented can rusts fast.
