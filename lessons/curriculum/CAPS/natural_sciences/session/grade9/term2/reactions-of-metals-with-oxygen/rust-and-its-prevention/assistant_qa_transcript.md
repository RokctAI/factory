### subtopic_1 — What Rust Is and the Conditions for Rusting
**Assistant:** If rust and burning steel wool are the same reaction, why does rusting need water?

**Tutor:** They are both iron combining with oxygen, but they happen by different routes. Burning happens at a high temperature, where iron atoms have enough energy to react directly with oxygen molecules from the air, and the reaction runs fast enough to glow. At room temperature, iron and dry oxygen react so slowly that a thin, almost invisible oxide film forms and then the reaction practically stops. Water changes the route. A film of water on the iron acts as a liquid in which tiny electric currents can flow between one part of the metal surface and another. At one spot, iron atoms lose electrons and dissolve into the water as iron ions; the electrons travel through the metal to another spot, where they are taken up by oxygen dissolved in the water. The iron ions and the products from the oxygen then meet and form hydrated iron oxide, which is rust. Because the process involves ions moving through water, anything that helps ions move makes it faster, and that is why salt water is so much worse than pure water: dissolved salt makes the water a much better conductor. You will learn the full electrical explanation, called electrochemistry, in Grade 12. For Grade 9, the key statements are that rusting is a slow reaction of iron with oxygen, that water must be present, and that salt speeds it up.

### subtopic_2 — Investigating the Conditions for Rusting
**Assistant:** Why do we use iron nails and not steel wool in the rusting experiment?

**Tutor:** Both would rust, but nails are easier to compare fairly and give results that are easier to see. A nail has a regular shape, so you can use nails of the same size and type in every tube, which keeps the amount of iron and its surface area a controlled variable. Steel wool has an enormous and uneven surface area, so it rusts very quickly, and it is difficult to make sure that each tube receives exactly the same amount. Steel wool is useful for a different experiment, though. If you push a damp ball of steel wool into the bottom of a measuring cylinder and stand it upside down in a dish of water, the water level inside slowly rises over several days as the iron uses up the oxygen in the trapped air. The water rises by about one fifth of the volume of air, which shows both that rusting uses oxygen and that air is about 21 percent oxygen. Whichever iron sample you use, the nails or the wool should be cleaned with sandpaper first, so that grease or an existing oxide layer does not slow the reaction in one tube more than another. Galvanised nails must not be used, because the zinc coating would protect them, which would make it seem as if rust never forms, although it can make a good fifth tube to show the effect of galvanising.

### subtopic_3 — Preventing Rusting
**Assistant:** How does electroplating actually put chromium onto a tap?

**Tutor:** It uses an electric current to move metal ions out of a solution and turn them into a thin layer of metal on the object. The tap, made of steel or brass and cleaned very thoroughly, is connected to the negative terminal of a direct current power supply, so it becomes the negative electrode. It is hung in a tank containing a solution of a chromium compound, which contains positively charged chromium ions. A second electrode is connected to the positive terminal. When the current flows, the positive chromium ions are attracted to the negative tap, where each ion gains electrons and becomes a chromium atom attached to the surface. Over minutes to hours, the atoms build up into a smooth, even layer, typically only a fraction of a millimetre thick. Factories control the current, the temperature and the time to get the right thickness and shine. Zinc, nickel, copper, silver and gold can all be electroplated in the same way. Cheap jewellery is often plated with a thin layer of gold, and car parts are plated with nickel and then chromium. The chemicals used in chromium plating are toxic, so plating works must treat their waste water carefully. The principle of using electricity to drive a chemical change, called electrolysis, is studied in Grade 12 Physical Sciences.

### subtopic_4 — Choosing a Method and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** Why do food cans use tin if tin makes steel rust faster when scratched?

**Tutor:** Because for a food can, protecting the food matters more than protecting the steel after the can is damaged. A tin can is steel plated with a very thin layer of tin. Tin is far less reactive than iron and does not react with the acids in foods such as tomatoes and fruit, and it is not poisonous, so the food stays safe and does not taste of metal. Many cans also have a lacquer lining inside. Zinc would be a poor choice, because zinc is reactive, would dissolve into acidic food and could make it harmful. The disadvantage is exactly the one you describe. When a tin can is dented and the coating cracks, the iron is more reactive than the tin, so the iron corrodes even faster than it would on its own, and the tin offers no sacrificial protection. That is one reason shops are advised not to sell badly dented cans, because rust and a broken seal can let bacteria in. A can only needs to last until the food is eaten, so this is an acceptable trade-off. By contrast, a roof or a fence pole must last outdoors for decades and nobody eats from it, so zinc's sacrificial protection is the better choice. It is a good example of how engineers choose a material by thinking about how the object will be used.
