### subtopic_1 — What Happens in a Chemical Reaction
**Assistant:** Is cooking an egg a chemical change or a physical change?

**Tutor:** It is a chemical change, and it is a good example of how to decide. A raw egg white is a clear, runny liquid made mostly of water and dissolved proteins, long chain molecules folded into compact shapes. When you heat it, the heat breaks some of the weak bonds that hold each protein in its folded shape, and the chains unfold and then bond to each other in new ways, forming a tangled network that traps the water. The white turns opaque and firm. New bonds have formed, the substance now has different properties, and the change cannot be reversed: cooling a fried egg does not make it runny again. All the signs of a chemical change are there, even though no gas bubbles off and nothing burns. The same applies to many kinds of cooking: toasting bread browns it through reactions between sugars and proteins, and baking a cake involves the bicarbonate of soda or baking powder reacting to release carbon dioxide gas, which makes the cake rise. By contrast, some kitchen changes are physical. Melting butter is physical, because cooled butter becomes solid again and is still butter. Dissolving sugar or salt in water is physical, because evaporating the water gives back the sugar or salt. Boiling water is physical, because the steam condenses back into water. The test to apply is always the same: has a new substance with different properties been formed, and can the change easily be reversed? If a new substance formed and the change is hard to undo, it is chemical. Notice that a change being hard to reverse is a clue, not proof; the real test is whether new substances have formed.

### subtopic_2 — Particle Models of Reactions
**Assistant:** Why do models use balls and sticks when real atoms are not like that?

**Tutor:** Because a model is a deliberately simplified picture that makes one particular idea easy to see, and for chemical reactions that idea is which atoms are joined to which, and how they are rearranged. Real atoms are not hard balls with colours. Each is a tiny nucleus surrounded by a cloud of electrons, with no sharp edge, and atoms are held together by electrons shared or transferred between them, not by sticks. Atoms are also far too small to see with any ordinary microscope: about ten million carbon atoms side by side would span only a millimetre or two. A model that tried to show all of that would be hard to draw and would hide the main point. The ball-and-stick model keeps exactly what is needed: one ball for each atom, a colour for each element, and a stick for each bond, so you can count the atoms, see the shape of a molecule and follow how the atoms are regrouped from reactants to products. A space-filling model, where balls overlap, gives a better impression of the real shape and size of the molecule but makes the bonds harder to see. Scientists use many different models of the same thing, each chosen for its purpose, and part of learning science is knowing what a model shows and what it leaves out. For example, the models in this lesson cannot show the energy released when magnesium burns, the speed of a reaction, or the fact that sodium chloride is a giant lattice rather than separate pairs. In Grade 10 you will meet more detailed models of the atom and of bonding, which explain why atoms join in the ratios they do.

### subtopic_3 — Word Equations
**Assistant:** Why do we write oxygen as O2 in equations but O inside formulas like CO2?

**Tutor:** Because O2 describes oxygen as an element, the way it actually exists as a gas, while the O inside a formula is just one atom of oxygen that is part of a compound. Oxygen atoms are not stable on their own under ordinary conditions; in the air they are always paired, each pair sharing electrons to form a molecule, O2. So when oxygen gas takes part in a reaction, what actually reacts are O2 molecules, and the equation must show them as O2. Once oxygen atoms have reacted, they are bonded to other atoms instead of to each other: in carbon dioxide, one carbon atom is bonded to two oxygen atoms, written CO2, and in water, two hydrogens are bonded to one oxygen, written H2O. There is no O2 molecule inside a CO2 molecule; the 2 counts oxygen atoms bonded to carbon. The same rule applies to the other elements that form two-atom molecules: hydrogen gas is H2, nitrogen N2, chlorine Cl2, fluorine F2, bromine Br2 and iodine I2. A useful memory aid is that the elements which form pairs are hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen and the halogens of Group 17. Most other elements are written with their symbol alone in equations: metals such as Mg, Fe, Zn and Cu, carbon as C, sulfur as S, and noble gases such as He. So in the burning of carbon, C + O2 → CO2, carbon appears as C because solid carbon is not made of small molecules, and oxygen appears as O2 because the gas is. Getting this right is essential for the next lesson, because balancing an equation depends on counting the atoms in exactly the right particles.

### subtopic_4 — Chemical Equations and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What is the difference between the 2 in 2H2O and the 2 in H2O?

**Tutor:** They count different things, and confusing them is the commonest mistake in writing equations. The small 2 written low after H in H2O is a subscript. It is part of the formula and tells you how many hydrogen atoms are in one water molecule. It is fixed by the nature of water: change it and you have a different substance, as H2O2, hydrogen peroxide, shows. The large 2 written in front, in 2H2O, is a coefficient. It is not part of the formula; it tells you how many water molecules there are. So 2H2O means two separate water molecules, each with two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. To count the atoms, multiply: two molecules times two hydrogens gives four hydrogen atoms, and two molecules times one oxygen gives two oxygen atoms. The coefficient applies to the whole formula after it, including any brackets: 3Ca(OH)2 means three formula units, each with one calcium, two oxygen and two hydrogen, so three calcium, six oxygen and six hydrogen atoms. The distinction is the key to the next two lessons. When an equation is unbalanced, you are allowed to change coefficients, because that only changes how many particles take part, but you are never allowed to change subscripts, because that changes what the substances are. A good habit is to read equations aloud in a way that keeps the two apart: for 2H2O, say two molecules of H2O, not two H two O; and for counting, say coefficient times subscript for each element. With that habit, counting atoms on each side becomes routine.
