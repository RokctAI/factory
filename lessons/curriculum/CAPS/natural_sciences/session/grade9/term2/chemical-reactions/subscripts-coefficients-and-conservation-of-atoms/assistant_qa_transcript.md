### subtopic_1 — Subscripts and Coefficients
**Assistant:** If coefficients can be changed freely, what stops me from putting any numbers in front?

**Tutor:** The requirement that the final equation must be balanced, and that it must use the smallest whole numbers that achieve this. Coefficients can be changed while you are balancing, but they are not arbitrary: only certain combinations make every element match on both sides. For the burning of hydrogen, H2 + O2 → H2O, the only way to make both hydrogen and oxygen match with whole numbers in the smallest possible ratio is 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O. You could also write 4H2 + 2O2 → 4H2O, which is balanced too, because it is simply the same equation doubled; it describes the same reaction happening twice over. But chemists agree to use the simplest whole-number ratio, so the correct answer is the one with 2, 1 and 2. Fractions such as ½O2 are also avoided at this level, because you cannot have half a molecule, and you clear them by multiplying everything by two. The coefficients also carry real meaning: they give the ratio in which the substances react. The equation 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O says that hydrogen and oxygen molecules always react in the ratio 2 to 1, which is why a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen in that ratio explodes most violently, and why a car engine or gas stove has to mix fuel and air in the right proportions to burn cleanly. In Grade 11 you will use these ratios to calculate exact masses of reactants and products. So coefficients are adjustable only in the sense that you search for them; once found, they are fixed by the chemistry, just as firmly as the subscripts are fixed by the identity of each substance.

### subtopic_2 — Counting Atoms in an Equation
**Assistant:** How do I count atoms when an element appears in more than one substance on the same side?

**Tutor:** Count it separately in each term, then add the counts together, and a table makes this very hard to get wrong. Take the burning of ethanol, the alcohol in some fuels and in drinks: C2H5OH + 3O2 → 2CO2 + 3H2O. Notice first that ethanol itself contains hydrogen in two places, five in C2H5 and one in OH, so one molecule has six hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. On the left, oxygen appears in two substances: one atom in the ethanol molecule and three times two, six, in the oxygen molecules, making seven. On the right, oxygen is again in two substances: two times two, four, in the carbon dioxide, and three times one, three, in the water, making seven. Carbon: two on the left, two times one, two, on the right. Hydrogen: six on the left, three times two, six, on the right. Everything matches. The common mistakes are forgetting that an element appears in a second substance, especially oxygen, which is in almost every combustion product, and forgetting to multiply by the coefficient. So the safe routine is: list every element down the side of your table; then go through each term on the left, writing the count of each element it contains as coefficient times subscript; add up each row; and repeat for the right. When balancing, it also helps to leave the elements that appear in several places, usually oxygen and hydrogen, until last, and to balance first the elements that appear in only one substance on each side, because changing a coefficient affects every element in that formula at once.

### subtopic_3 — Conservation of Atoms and Mass
**Assistant:** Does conservation of mass mean the products always weigh the same as what I put in the beaker?

**Tutor:** Only if nothing can enter or leave, which is why the law is stated for a closed system. In a school laboratory most reactions happen in open containers, and gases move in and out freely. When a reaction produces a gas, such as zinc reacting with hydrochloric acid to give hydrogen, or vinegar reacting with bicarbonate of soda to give carbon dioxide, the gas bubbles off into the air and the beaker becomes lighter, even though every atom still exists somewhere. When a reaction takes in a gas from the air, such as magnesium burning or iron rusting with oxygen, the solid becomes heavier, because atoms from outside have been added. In both cases, if you could collect and weigh every gas that left or entered, the total would balance exactly. A simple classroom demonstration shows the difference. Put vinegar in a plastic bottle and some bicarbonate of soda in a balloon stretched over its neck, weigh the whole set-up, then tip the powder in. The balloon inflates with carbon dioxide, but the reading on the scale stays the same, because nothing has escaped. Repeat the experiment in an open beaker and the mass falls as the gas leaves. A reaction that involves only solids and liquids, such as mixing two solutions to make a precipitate, keeps the same mass even in an open beaker, because nothing needs to enter or leave. So the law never fails, but measuring it requires keeping track of every substance. When an exam question describes a change in mass, the expected explanation is almost always a gas that entered from or escaped to the air.

### subtopic_7 — Nothing Is Lost, Nothing Is Gained
**Assistant:** Is mass also conserved in nuclear reactions, like in Koeberg or the Sun?

**Tutor:** Not exactly, and that difference is what makes nuclear energy so powerful, although for chemical reactions the law of conservation of mass holds to far better accuracy than any school balance can measure. In a chemical reaction only the electrons around atoms are rearranged; the nuclei are untouched, so every atom keeps its identity and the total mass stays the same. In a nuclear reaction the nuclei themselves change: in the reactors at Koeberg, uranium nuclei split into smaller nuclei, and in the Sun, hydrogen nuclei fuse into helium. In these reactions a small fraction of the mass, typically less than one percent, is converted into a very large amount of energy, according to Einstein's famous relationship between mass and energy. The products of a nuclear reaction therefore weigh slightly less than the reactants, and the missing mass appears as the energy that heats the water at Koeberg or makes the Sun shine. Scientists now say that it is the total of mass and energy together that is conserved. Strictly speaking, even chemical reactions that release heat lose a tiny amount of mass, but it is so small, around a billionth of the total, that it is undetectable in practice, which is why Lavoisier's law works perfectly in every chemistry laboratory. For Grade 9, the rule is: in chemical reactions atoms are conserved and mass is conserved. You will meet nuclear power at Koeberg in Term 3 and the nuclear fusion that powers stars in Term 4, and Grade 12 Physical Sciences touches on the relationship between mass and energy.
