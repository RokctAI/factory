# Part 1 — Expert

This session sets out a reliable method for balancing chemical equations by inspection, applies it to reactions of increasing difficulty, and shows how to check and present a balanced equation. The anchor examples are the burning of hydrogen, 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O, the burning of methane, CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O, the burning of propane, C3H8 + 5O2 → 3CO2 + 4H2O, and the formation of iron oxide, 4Fe + 3O2 → 2Fe2O3. The governing principle is the law of conservation of mass: atoms are rearranged in a reaction but never created or destroyed, so a correct equation must have the same number of atoms of each element on both sides, achieved by changing coefficients only.

## Subtopic: Why Equations Must Be Balanced

A chemical equation is a statement about atoms. The formulae on the left say which particles react; the formulae on the right say which particles form. If an element has more atoms on one side than the other, the equation claims that atoms have appeared or vanished, and that contradicts the law of conservation of mass.

Take the reaction of hydrogen with oxygen to form water. The skeleton equation, with correct formulae but no coefficients, is H2 + O2 → H2O. Hydrogen: 2 on the left, 2 on the right. Oxygen: 2 on the left, 1 on the right. As written, one oxygen atom has disappeared. The skeleton equation is true about which substances are involved but false about the amounts.

A balanced equation is one in which the number of atoms of each element is the same on both sides. For the burning of hydrogen it is 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O: hydrogen 4 and 4, oxygen 2 and 2. It now says something precise and testable: two molecules of hydrogen react with one molecule of oxygen to make two molecules of water. Those numbers are the reacting ratio, and they are exactly what is observed: hydrogen and oxygen react in a 2 to 1 ratio by volume.

The balanced equation is also the starting point for all the calculations of Grade 10 to 12 chemistry, in which you work out how much product a given amount of reactant can make. A factory making fertiliser or a refinery making petrol uses balanced equations to plan its raw materials. An unbalanced equation gives the wrong answer to every such question.

Two rules hold throughout. Rule one: the formulae must be correct before you start balancing, because balancing cannot fix a wrong formula. Rule two: you balance only by changing coefficients. You may never change a subscript, add a subscript, or insert an atom into a formula.

The questions for this section are with you now: what makes an equation balanced, why it must be balanced, and what may and may not be changed.

## Subtopic: A Step-by-Step Balancing Method

Balancing by inspection works for every equation you will meet in Grades 9 and 10. The method has six steps.

Step one: write the word equation, so that you know exactly which substances react and which form.

Step two: write the skeleton equation with the correct chemical formula for every substance. Remember that hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and chlorine exist as diatomic molecules, H2, O2, N2 and Cl2, while metals and carbon are written as single atoms, Fe, Mg, C.

Step three: draw a stocktake table listing every element, with its count on the left and on the right.

Step four: balance one element at a time by placing a coefficient in front of a formula. Start with an element that appears in only one substance on each side, often a metal or carbon. Leave until last any element that appears in several substances or on its own as an element, which is usually oxygen or hydrogen.

Step five: after every change, recount, because a coefficient multiplies every atom in its formula and may unbalance an element you had already fixed.

Step six: check that every element matches and that the coefficients are the smallest whole numbers possible. If every coefficient can be divided by the same number, divide.

Apply the method to the burning of methane, the gas in many kitchen stoves. Word equation: methane + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water. Skeleton: CH4 + O2 → CO2 + H2O. Stocktake: carbon 1 and 1, already balanced; hydrogen 4 and 2; oxygen 2 and 3. Balance hydrogen by putting 2 in front of H2O: CH4 + O2 → CO2 + 2H2O. Recount: hydrogen 4 and 4; oxygen on the right is now 2 + 2 = 4, while the left has 2. Balance oxygen last by putting 2 in front of O2: CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O. Final check: carbon 1 and 1, hydrogen 4 and 4, oxygen 4 and 4. Balanced, with the smallest whole numbers.

The questions for this section are ready: the order of the steps, which element to balance first, and why you must recount.

## Subtopic: Balancing Harder Equations

The same method handles equations that need larger coefficients. Three examples show the main patterns.

Pattern one: an odd number of oxygen atoms on one side and an even number on the other. Iron rusting to iron(III) oxide: Fe + O2 → Fe2O3. Iron: 1 and 2. Oxygen: 2 and 3. Oxygen comes in pairs on the left but in threes on the right, so find the lowest common multiple, 2 × 3 = 6. Put 3 in front of O2 and 2 in front of Fe2O3 to give six oxygen atoms on each side: Fe + 3O2 → 2Fe2O3. Now iron on the right is 2 × 2 = 4, so put 4 in front of Fe. The balanced equation is 4Fe + 3O2 → 2Fe2O3: iron 4 and 4, oxygen 6 and 6. Aluminium reacting with oxygen follows the same pattern: 4Al + 3O2 → 2Al2O3.

Pattern two: a hydrocarbon fuel burning. Propane, C3H8, is the main gas in the cylinders used for gas braais and heaters. Skeleton: C3H8 + O2 → CO2 + H2O. Carbon first: 3 on the left, so put 3 in front of CO2. Hydrogen next: 8 on the left, so put 4 in front of H2O, since 4 × 2 = 8. Oxygen last: the right now has 3 × 2 = 6 in carbon dioxide plus 4 × 1 = 4 in water, 6 + 4 = 10 oxygen atoms, so put 5 in front of O2, since 5 × 2 = 10. Balanced: C3H8 + 5O2 → 3CO2 + 4H2O.

Pattern three: a compound that breaks down and releases a diatomic gas. Hydrogen peroxide decomposing: H2O2 → H2O + O2. Hydrogen is 2 and 2. Oxygen is 2 on the left and 1 + 2 = 3 on the right. Put 2 in front of H2O2 and 2 in front of H2O: 2H2O2 → 2H2O + O2. Hydrogen 4 and 4; oxygen 4 on the left, 2 + 2 = 4 on the right. Balanced.

When a polyatomic group such as OH or CO3 stays intact from one side to the other, it can be balanced as a single unit. In Ca(OH)2 + HCl → CaCl2 + H2O, chlorine needs 2 in front of HCl, and then hydrogen and oxygen balance with 2 in front of H2O: Ca(OH)2 + 2HCl → CaCl2 + 2H2O.

The questions for this section are waiting: lowest common multiples, fuels that burn, and decomposition.

## Subtopic: Checking, Presenting and the Error Museum

A balanced equation is presented with the reactants on the left, an arrow pointing to the products on the right, a plus sign between substances on each side, and coefficients of 1 left unwritten. Where the question asks for them, state symbols may follow each formula: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas and (aq) aqueous, which means dissolved in water. For example, 2Mg(s) + O2(g) → 2MgO(s).

Always finish with a check written out in the stocktake form. Take 2Na + Cl2 → 2NaCl, sodium burning in chlorine to form table salt. Sodium: 2 and 2. Chlorine: 2 and 2. Balanced. Take N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3: nitrogen 2 and 2, hydrogen 6 and 6. Balanced.

If your coefficients share a common factor, simplify. The equation 4H2 + 2O2 → 4H2O is balanced but not in its simplest form; dividing by 2 gives 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: balancing H2 + O2 → H2O by writing H2O2 on the right; that changes the product to hydrogen peroxide. Two: writing H2 + O → H2O; oxygen gas is O2, and the formula must be correct before balancing. Three: putting a coefficient in the middle of a formula, as in H22O; coefficients go only in front. Four: stopping after one element is balanced without recounting the others. Five: leaving the answer as 4H2 + 2O2 → 4H2O instead of the simplest whole numbers.

Layout for a balancing answer: word equation; skeleton equation with correct formulae; the balanced equation; then a table showing each element on the left and on the right. That layout earns the method marks as well as the answer mark.

The questions for this section are with you now: correct presentation, simplest ratios, and the five exhibits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same skill again, through a fair trade, a recipe, and some harder cases with a final check — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: A Fair Trade

Think of a chemical reaction as a trade at a market. Everything you hand over on the left must turn up again on the right. Not one atom may vanish and not one may appear from nowhere. If the counts do not match, someone has been cheated, and in chemistry that is impossible.

Look at hydrogen burning: H2 + O2 → H2O. Count hydrogen: two and two. Fine. Count oxygen: two on the left but only one on the right. An oxygen has gone missing. That equation is not a fair trade, so it is not balanced.

The fix is to change how many molecules there are, using the big numbers in front. Two hydrogen molecules plus one oxygen molecule make two water molecules: 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O. Now count again: four hydrogens on each side, two oxygens on each side. A fair trade.

And that also tells you something real. Hydrogen and oxygen always join in the ratio two to one. Factories that make fertiliser and fuel use balanced equations to know exactly how much of each raw material to buy.

Two rules never change. First, get the formulae right before you start: oxygen gas is O2, water is H2O, and that is that. Second, only the big numbers may change. The little numbers inside the formulae are locked.

Keep the fair trade in mind and try the questions for this part: spotting the missing atom, and what you are allowed to change.

## Subtopic: The Recipe for Balancing

Balancing is easier with a fixed routine, like following a recipe.

First, write it in words. Second, swap the words for correct formulae. Third, draw your stocktake table: element, left, right. Fourth, fix one element at a time with big numbers, starting with the ones that appear only once on each side. Leave oxygen and hydrogen for last, because they usually turn up in more than one place. Fifth, recount after every change, since a big number multiplies everything in that formula. Sixth, check that it all matches and that you cannot divide every number by the same amount.

Try it on the gas in a kitchen stove. Methane plus oxygen makes carbon dioxide plus water. Formulae: CH4 + O2 → CO2 + H2O.

Carbon: one and one. Done.

Hydrogen: four on the left, two on the right. Put a 2 in front of the water: CH4 + O2 → CO2 + 2H2O. Now hydrogen is four and four.

Oxygen last. On the right there are two in the carbon dioxide and two in the two waters, four. On the left only two. Put a 2 in front of the oxygen: CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O.

Final count: carbon one and one, hydrogen four and four, oxygen four and four. Balanced.

Notice that the recount after each step is what stops mistakes. Fixing one element can quietly break another one, and only counting again catches it.

Hold the recipe in mind and try this part's questions: putting the steps in order and balancing the stove.

## Subtopic: Trickier Ones and Final Checks

Some equations need bigger numbers, but the recipe still works.

Rust first. Iron plus oxygen makes iron oxide: Fe + O2 → Fe2O3. Oxygen comes in twos on the left and threes on the right. What is the smallest number both two and three go into? Six. So use three O2 molecules to get six oxygens, and two Fe2O3 to get six oxygens: Fe + 3O2 → 2Fe2O3. Now the right has four irons, so put 4 in front of the iron: 4Fe + 3O2 → 2Fe2O3. Check: iron four and four, oxygen six and six.

Next, the gas braai. The bottle holds propane, C3H8. Propane plus oxygen makes carbon dioxide plus water. Carbons first: three, so 3CO2. Hydrogens next: eight, so 4H2O. Now count the oxygens on the right: six in the carbon dioxide and four in the water, ten. Ten oxygens on the left means five oxygen molecules. C3H8 + 5O2 → 3CO2 + 4H2O.

Last, hydrogen peroxide, the liquid in some hair bleaches and wound cleaners, slowly breaks down into water and oxygen gas: H2O2 → H2O + O2. Oxygens are two on the left and three on the right. Double the peroxide and double the water: 2H2O2 → 2H2O + O2. Now hydrogen is four and four and oxygen is four and four.

Before you hand in an equation, run three checks.

Check one: are the formulae right? Oxygen, hydrogen, nitrogen and chlorine gases travel in pairs, O2, H2, N2, Cl2. Metals stand alone, Fe, Mg, Na.

Check two: does every element match? Write the table out. For 2Na + Cl2 → 2NaCl, sodium is two and two and chlorine is two and two.

Check three: are the numbers as small as they can be? If you wrote 4H2 + 2O2 → 4H2O, every number divides by two, so the proper answer is 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O.

Watch out for the classic slips: changing water into H2O2 to make oxygen fit, writing O instead of O2 for oxygen gas, squeezing a number into the middle of a formula, and forgetting to recount.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A balanced equation has the same number of each kind of atom on both sides, because atoms are never created or destroyed. Get the formulae right first, then change only the big numbers. Balance one element at a time, leave oxygen and hydrogen for last, recount after every change, and finish with the smallest whole numbers. Grade 10 Physical Sciences uses balanced equations in every chemistry calculation.

Hold the trickier ones and the three checks in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: rust, the braai gas, the bleach and the simplest numbers.
