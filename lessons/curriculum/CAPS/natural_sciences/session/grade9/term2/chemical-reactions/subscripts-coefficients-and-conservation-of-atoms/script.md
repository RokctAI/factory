# Part 1 — Expert

This session distinguishes subscripts from coefficients, sets out how to count every atom on each side of a chemical equation, and explains the principle that makes such counting necessary: in a chemical reaction atoms are rearranged but are neither lost nor gained, so mass is conserved. The anchor examples are 2H2O, with four hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms, the burning of magnesium, in which 2,4 grams of magnesium and 1,6 grams of oxygen form 4,0 grams of magnesium oxide, and the equation N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3. The governing principle is the law of conservation of mass, first stated clearly by Antoine Lavoisier: in a closed system, the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants.

## Subtopic: Subscripts and Coefficients

A chemical equation contains two kinds of number, and they mean different things.

A subscript is the small number written low and to the right of a symbol inside a formula. It tells you how many atoms of that element are in one particle of the substance. In H2O, the subscript 2 means each water molecule contains two hydrogen atoms; oxygen has no subscript, so there is one oxygen atom. In CO2, each molecule has one carbon atom and two oxygen atoms. Subscripts are part of the identity of a substance. They are fixed by nature, and changing one changes the substance: H2O is water, H2O2 is hydrogen peroxide; CO2 is carbon dioxide, CO is carbon monoxide.

A coefficient is the large number written in front of a formula. It tells you how many particles of that substance take part in the reaction: how many molecules, atoms or formula units. In 2H2O, the coefficient 2 means two water molecules. In 3O2, three oxygen molecules. In 4Fe, four iron atoms. When no coefficient is written, it is understood to be 1. A coefficient applies to the whole formula that follows it, including everything inside brackets.

So the two numbers answer different questions. The subscript answers: what is this substance made of? The coefficient answers: how much of this substance is involved? In 3H2SO4, the substance is sulfuric acid, each molecule containing two hydrogen atoms, one sulfur atom and four oxygen atoms, and there are three such molecules.

The rule that follows is absolute and will be used in every equation you ever balance: coefficients may be changed to balance an equation, because that only changes the amounts; subscripts may never be changed, because that would change what the substances are.

The questions for this section are with you now: subscript or coefficient, what each tells you, and which one may be changed.

## Subtopic: Counting Atoms in an Equation

To count the atoms of an element in a term of an equation, multiply the coefficient by the subscript of that element. If the element is inside brackets, multiply by the subscript outside the brackets as well.

Worked counts. In 2H2O: hydrogen 2 × 2 = 4 atoms; oxygen 2 × 1, two atoms. In 3CO2: carbon 3 atoms; oxygen 3 × 2 = 6 atoms. In 2Ca(OH)2: calcium 2 atoms; oxygen 2 × 2 = 4 atoms; hydrogen 2 × 2 = 4 atoms. In 2Fe2O3: iron 2 × 2 = 4 atoms; oxygen 2 × 3 = 6 atoms.

When a side of the equation has more than one substance, count the element in each term and add. In CO2 + 2H2O, oxygen appears in both: two oxygen atoms in CO2 and two in 2H2O, a total of four.

The reliable method is a stocktake table with three columns: element, number of atoms on the left, number of atoms on the right. Take the equation for the burning of methane, CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O. Carbon: left 1, right 1. Hydrogen: left 4, right 2 × 2 = 4. Oxygen: left 2 × 2 = 4, right 2 + 2 = 4. Every element matches.

Take N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3, the reaction used to make ammonia for fertilisers. Nitrogen: left 2, right 2 × 1, two. Hydrogen: left 3 × 2 = 6, right 2 × 3 = 6. Every element matches.

Now take 4Fe + 3O2 → 2Fe2O3, iron reacting with oxygen. Iron: left 4, right 2 × 2 = 4. Oxygen: left 3 × 2 = 6, right 2 × 3 = 6. Every element matches.

For marking, a counting answer must show each element separately, show the multiplication of coefficient and subscript, and give the totals on both sides.

Take this section's questions now: coefficient times subscript, counting with brackets, and a stocktake table for a whole equation.

## Subtopic: Conservation of Atoms and Mass

Why must the stocktake match? Because in a chemical reaction the atoms of the reactants are rearranged to form the products, but no atom is created, destroyed or changed into an atom of another element. Every atom present at the start is still present at the end, just bonded differently. This is the conservation of atoms.

Since every atom has a fixed mass, the conservation of atoms means the conservation of mass. The French chemist Antoine Lavoisier showed this in the 1780s by carrying out reactions in sealed containers and weighing them very carefully before and after: the total mass never changed. The law of conservation of mass states that in a closed system the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants.

Numbers make it concrete. When 2,4 grams of magnesium burn completely, they combine with 1,6 grams of oxygen, and exactly 2,4 + 1,6 = 4,0 grams of magnesium oxide form. When 4 grams of hydrogen react with 32 grams of oxygen, 4 + 32 = 36 grams of water form. When 16 grams of methane burn in 64 grams of oxygen, 16 + 64 = 80 grams of reactants give 44 grams of carbon dioxide and 36 grams of water, and 44 + 36 = 80 grams of products.

Some everyday observations seem to break the law, and explaining them is a classic test question. Magnesium ribbon burnt in an open crucible gets heavier. This is because oxygen from the air, which was not weighed at the start, has combined with it. A log burning in a fire gets lighter. This is because carbon dioxide and water vapour, products of the burning, escape into the air and are not weighed at the end. In both cases the system was open. If the same reactions are carried out in a sealed container, so that nothing can enter or leave, the total mass stays exactly the same. The same holds for a precipitate reaction in a sealed flask on a balance: two clear solutions are mixed, a solid forms, and the reading on the balance does not change.

## Subtopic: Checking Whether an Equation Is Balanced and the Error Museum

An equation is balanced when the number of atoms of each element is the same on both sides. Only a balanced equation obeys the law of conservation of mass, so only a balanced equation correctly describes a reaction. Checking is done with the stocktake table.

Check H2 + O2 → H2O. Hydrogen: left 2, right 2. Oxygen: left 2, right 1. Oxygen does not match, so the equation is not balanced: it seems to say an oxygen atom has vanished. The balanced form is 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O. Check it: hydrogen left 2 × 2 = 4, right 2 × 2 = 4; oxygen left 2, right 2 × 1, two. Balanced.

Check Mg + O2 → MgO. Magnesium: 1 and 1. Oxygen: 2 and 1. Not balanced. The balanced form is 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO: magnesium 2 and 2, oxygen 2 and 2.

Check C + O2 → CO2. Carbon 1 and 1, oxygen 2 and 2. Balanced as it stands.

Notice that balancing was done only by changing coefficients. The tempting shortcut of changing H2O to H2O2 so that the oxygen matches would balance the atoms on paper but describe a different product, hydrogen peroxide, which is not what forms. The next lesson sets out a step-by-step method for balancing.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: the 2 in H2O and the 2 in 2H2O mean the same; the first counts atoms in a molecule, the second counts molecules. Two: in 2H2O there are three hydrogen atoms; there are 2 × 2 = 4. Three: a subscript can be changed to balance an equation; only coefficients may be changed. Four: burning makes things lose mass, so mass is not conserved; the mass escapes as gases, and in a closed system it is conserved. Five: a coefficient multiplies only the first element of the formula; it multiplies every atom in the formula.

Layout for a balance check: a table of element, left count, right count, then the verdict, balanced or not. Layout for a mass question: mass of reactants equals mass of products, with the substitution and the answer in grams. Equations are then checked exactly as the memorandum expects.

The questions for this section are ready: checking given equations, the closed-system argument, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through little numbers and big numbers, a shop stocktake, and the rule that nothing is lost and nothing is gained — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Little Numbers and Big Numbers

An equation has two sizes of number, and they do completely different jobs.

The little number, low down inside a formula, is part of the recipe for one particle. In H2O, the little 2 says: every water molecule has two hydrogens stuck to one oxygen. That is what water is. You cannot change it. Change it to H2O2 and you have hydrogen peroxide, a bleach. Change CO2 to CO and you have a deadly gas. The little numbers are the substance's identity card.

The big number, in front of a formula, just says how many. 2H2O is two water molecules. 3O2 is three oxygen molecules. 5Fe is five iron atoms. It is like ordering at a spaza shop: the little numbers describe what one item is, a loaf with so many slices, and the big number says how many loaves you want. Ordering three loaves does not change what a loaf is.

So when you look at 3H2SO4, read it as: three molecules of sulfuric acid, and each one has two hydrogens, one sulfur and four oxygens.

And here is the most important rule of the next few lessons. To fix an equation you may change the big numbers, how many, as much as you like. You may never touch the little numbers, what it is.

If there is no big number, it means one, just like a formula with no little number after a symbol means one atom.

Keep the little and big numbers in mind and try the questions for this part: which one is the identity card, which one says how many, and which one you may change.

## Subtopic: The Stocktake

At the end of the month a shop does a stocktake: it counts everything on the shelves. A chemist does a stocktake on an equation, counting every kind of atom on the left and on the right.

The counting trick is: big number times little number. 2H2O: hydrogens, two molecules times two each, four. Oxygens, two molecules times one each, two. 3CO2: carbons, three. Oxygens, three times two, six. If there are brackets, multiply by the number outside them too: 2Ca(OH)2 has two calciums, four oxygens and four hydrogens.

If an element shows up in two places on the same side, add them. In CO2 + 2H2O, oxygen is in both: two in the carbon dioxide and two in the waters, four altogether.

Now a whole stocktake. Gas stove: CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O. Make three columns: element, left, right. Carbon: one and one. Hydrogen: four on the left, and on the right two waters times two, four. Oxygen: two molecules times two, four on the left; on the right two in carbon dioxide plus two in the waters, four. Every row matches. The stocktake balances.

Try a broken one: H2 + O2 → H2O. Hydrogen: two and two. Oxygen: two on the left, only one on the right. One oxygen has gone missing on paper. That equation is not balanced. Put a big 2 in front of the water and another in front of the hydrogen, 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O, and count again: four hydrogens each side, two oxygens each side. Fixed, and without touching a single little number.

Hold the stocktake in mind and try this part's questions: big times little, adding across a side, and spotting the row that does not match.

## Subtopic: Nothing Is Lost, Nothing Is Gained

Why should the stocktake match at all? Because atoms are never made and never destroyed in a chemical reaction. They only swap partners. Every atom that goes in comes out, just joined differently. And because every atom has its own mass, the total mass going in equals the total mass coming out. Weigh 2,4 grams of magnesium and let it burn with 1,6 grams of oxygen, and you get 4,0 grams of magnesium oxide, not a crumb more or less.

But wait. Burn a log and you are left with a small pile of ash. Did mass disappear? No: most of it floated off as invisible gases, carbon dioxide and water vapour, up the chimney. Burn magnesium ribbon in an open dish and the white powder weighs more than the ribbon did. Did mass appear from nowhere? No: oxygen came in from the air and joined on, and nobody weighed that oxygen at the start.

The trick is to close the box. Do the reaction in a sealed container, so nothing can sneak in or out, put it on a scale, and the reading does not change from start to finish. A French scientist, Lavoisier, did exactly that more than 200 years ago and turned it into a law: in a closed system, the mass of the products equals the mass of the reactants.

So if a question says 16 grams of methane burn in 64 grams of oxygen and give 44 grams of carbon dioxide, how much water? Mass in: 16 + 64 = 80 grams. Mass out must be 80 grams too, so water is 80 − 44 = 36 grams.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Little numbers, subscripts, say what a substance is and never change. Big numbers, coefficients, say how many and may change. Count atoms with big times little, multiplying brackets too. Atoms are rearranged, never created or destroyed, so mass is conserved in a closed system. An equation is balanced when every element's count matches on both sides. Grade 10 Physical Sciences builds all its calculations on this law.

Hold the closed box in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: missing masses, the log and the magnesium, and why the stocktake must match.
