### subtopic_1 — Why Equations Must Be Balanced
**Assistant:** Does a balanced equation mean the reaction always happens exactly in those numbers of molecules?

**Tutor:** It gives the ratio in which the substances react, not the actual number of molecules in your test tube. A real sample contains an enormous number of particles. Even a single drop of water holds well over a thousand billion billion molecules. The equation 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O tells you that, however many molecules there are, hydrogen and oxygen react in the ratio of two to one and form as many water molecules as there were hydrogen molecules. If you mix six hydrogen molecules with three oxygen molecules, six water molecules form. If you mix them in the wrong ratio, say six hydrogen molecules with five oxygen molecules, only three of the oxygen molecules can react and two are left over, unused. This idea of a substance that is used up first and one that is left over is called the limiting reactant, and you will calculate it in Grade 11. It matters in everyday life too. A gas stove or a car engine is designed to mix fuel and air in close to the right ratio. With too little air, the fuel cannot burn completely and forms poisonous carbon monoxide and black soot instead of carbon dioxide, which is why a paraffin heater or a braai should never be used in a closed room. So the coefficients are best read as a recipe: two parts of this to one part of that, multiplied up to whatever amount you actually have.

### subtopic_2 — A Step-by-Step Balancing Method
**Assistant:** What if I balance an element and then a later step unbalances it again?

**Tutor:** That happens often, and it is exactly why the method says recount after every change. When you place a coefficient in front of a formula, it multiplies every atom in that formula, so it can change the count of an element you had already fixed. The way to keep this to a minimum is the order in which you balance. Start with elements that appear in only one substance on each side, because changing their coefficient affects fewer other counts, and leave until last any element that appears in several substances or as a free element such as O2 or H2. Free elements are the most convenient to finish with, because changing the coefficient of O2 alters only oxygen and nothing else. Look at the burning of ethane, C2H6, a gas found with natural gas. Carbon first: C2H6 + O2 → 2CO2 + H2O. Hydrogen next: six on the left, so 3H2O. Now oxygen on the right is 2 × 2 = 4 plus 3, which is seven, an odd number, while oxygen on the left must come in pairs. When that happens, double everything you have so far: 2C2H6 + O2 → 4CO2 + 6H2O. Now the right has 8 + 6 = 14 oxygen atoms, so put 7 in front of O2. The final equation is 2C2H6 + 7O2 → 4CO2 + 6H2O. Carbon 4 and 4, hydrogen 12 and 12, oxygen 14 and 14. If you ever get stuck going round in circles, start again from the skeleton equation with a fresh table rather than patching the old one.

### subtopic_3 — Balancing Harder Equations
**Assistant:** Can I use a fraction like three and a half in front of oxygen?

**Tutor:** Some textbooks and some university courses do allow fractions as a temporary step, but the final answer at school level must use whole numbers. A coefficient counts particles, and you cannot have half a molecule of oxygen. Using a fraction can be a handy shortcut while you work. In the ethane example, after balancing carbon and hydrogen you need seven oxygen atoms on the left, which would be three and a half O2 molecules. You can write that down as a step, and then multiply every coefficient by two to clear the fraction, which gives 2C2H6 + 7O2 → 4CO2 + 6H2O, the same answer as doubling everything earlier. Either route is fine as long as the answer you present has whole numbers in the smallest possible ratio. A good habit is to check your final line for two things: are all coefficients whole numbers, and is there no number, other than one, that divides every coefficient exactly? If both answers are yes, your equation is in its accepted form. Note that you do not need every coefficient to be different from one; in CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O, methane and carbon dioxide have coefficients of one, which are simply left unwritten. Examiners mark an answer with fractions or with a common factor as not fully correct, so the final check matters.

### subtopic_4 — Checking, Presenting and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** When do I have to include state symbols, and how do I know which one to use?

**Tutor:** Include them when the question asks for them, or when it asks for a balanced equation with phases or states; otherwise they are optional at Grade 9, but they are good practice and you will need them regularly from Grade 10. Choose the symbol by asking what the substance is like at the conditions of the reaction. Use (s) for a solid, such as a metal like magnesium or iron, a solid oxide like magnesium oxide, or a precipitate that forms in a solution. Use (l) for a pure liquid, mainly water when it forms as a liquid, and liquid mercury or bromine. Use (g) for a gas, such as oxygen, hydrogen, carbon dioxide or water vapour when it forms in a flame, and remember that in a combustion reaction the water formed is usually a gas because of the heat. Use (aq), short for aqueous, for a substance dissolved in water, such as hydrochloric acid, sodium chloride solution or sodium hydroxide solution. For example, magnesium reacting with dilute hydrochloric acid is written Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g): the metal is a solid, the acid and the salt are dissolved in water, and hydrogen bubbles off as a gas. State symbols never affect the balancing; they only add information about the physical form of each substance, which helps explain observations such as bubbling or a solid appearing.
