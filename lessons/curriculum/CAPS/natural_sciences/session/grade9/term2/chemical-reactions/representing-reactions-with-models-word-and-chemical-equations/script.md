# Part 1 — Expert

This session sets out how chemical reactions are represented: what changes and what stays the same in a reaction, how particle models show the regrouping of atoms, how word equations name the reactants and products, and how chemical equations use symbols and formulas to show the same reaction exactly. The anchor example is the burning of magnesium, magnesium plus oxygen gives magnesium oxide, written in symbols as 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO, with methane burning in a gas stove and the reaction of hydrogen with oxygen as further examples. The governing principle is that in a chemical reaction atoms are rearranged into new substances, but no atoms are created or destroyed.

## Subtopic: What Happens in a Chemical Reaction

A chemical reaction is a process in which one or more substances are changed into one or more new substances with different properties. The substances present at the start are the reactants; the new substances formed are the products. When magnesium ribbon burns in air, the reactants are the shiny grey metal magnesium and the oxygen gas of the air, and the product is magnesium oxide, a white powder that is nothing like either reactant.

At the level of particles, a reaction involves breaking the chemical bonds that hold the atoms together in the reactants and forming new bonds to make the products. The atoms themselves are not changed: every magnesium atom and every oxygen atom present at the start is still present at the end, but they are now bonded in a new arrangement. This is the key idea of the whole topic, and the next two lessons build on it.

Several observations suggest that a chemical reaction has taken place. A gas may be given off, seen as bubbles or fizzing, as when vinegar is poured onto bicarbonate of soda. The colour may change, as when the blue of copper sulfate solution fades as iron reacts with it. A solid may form when two solutions are mixed, called a precipitate. Energy may be released or absorbed, as heat, light or sound: burning releases heat and light, while some reactions make the container feel cold. And the change is usually hard to reverse.

These signs distinguish a chemical change from a physical change. In a physical change, such as melting ice, dissolving sugar or boiling water, the substance changes state or form but no new substance is made, the particles are not regrouped, and the change is usually easy to reverse. Water is still water whether solid, liquid or gas. When methane burns, however, carbon dioxide and water are made from methane and oxygen, and they cannot easily be turned back.

Chemical reactions are everywhere: in the cells of your body as respiration, in a leaf as photosynthesis, in the engine of a taxi as fuel burns, in the rusting of a gate and in the baking of bread. Representing them precisely is the language of chemistry.

The questions for this section are with you now: reactants and products, the signs of a chemical reaction, and chemical against physical change.

## Subtopic: Particle Models of Reactions

A particle model represents atoms as balls and shows how they are joined. Each element is given a colour by convention, used in most textbooks and model kits: carbon black, hydrogen white, oxygen red, nitrogen blue, chlorine green and sulfur yellow. In a ball-and-stick model the balls are joined by sticks representing bonds; in a space-filling model the balls are pressed together. Either way, the model shows how many atoms of each element are in each particle and how they are grouped.

Take the reaction of hydrogen with oxygen to form water. Hydrogen gas is made of H2 molecules, two white balls joined. Oxygen gas is made of O2 molecules, two red balls joined. A water molecule is one red ball with two white balls attached. If we draw two hydrogen molecules and one oxygen molecule on the left and two water molecules on the right, we can count the balls on each side: four white and two red before, four white and two red after. The model shows clearly that the atoms have been regrouped, the pairs of hydrogen and the pair of oxygen pulled apart and reassembled into water, without any ball appearing or vanishing.

Now methane burning in oxygen. A methane molecule, CH4, is a black ball with four white balls. Two oxygen molecules, each two red balls, react with it. The products are one carbon dioxide molecule, a black ball between two red balls, and two water molecules. Count the balls: one black, four white and four red on each side. The model makes the meaning of the equation visible.

Models have limits. Real atoms are not coloured, are not hard spheres, and are bonded by shared or transferred electrons rather than sticks; sizes in a kit are only roughly in proportion. And a model of separate molecules does not suit giant structures such as sodium chloride or magnesium oxide, which are better drawn as a small section of a regular pattern. But for showing which atoms join to which and checking that every atom is accounted for, models are the clearest tool.

For marking, a model drawing must use a key showing which colour or shading is which element, must draw each molecule with the correct number and arrangement of atoms, and must show the same number of each kind of atom on both sides.

## Subtopic: Word Equations

A word equation names the reactants and the products of a reaction in words. The reactants are written on the left, the products on the right, and they are separated by an arrow. The plus sign between reactants means and, or reacts with; the plus sign between products means and. The arrow means produces, forms or gives, and points from reactants to products, showing the direction of the change.

So the burning of magnesium is written: magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide, read as magnesium reacts with oxygen to produce magnesium oxide. The burning of methane in a gas stove is: methane + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water. The reaction of iron with sulfur when heated is: iron + sulfur → iron sulfide. The reaction of hydrochloric acid with sodium hydroxide is: hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide → sodium chloride + water. Heating limestone in a kiln to make quicklime for cement is: calcium carbonate → calcium oxide + carbon dioxide, a reaction with a single reactant breaking down, called decomposition.

Two reactions from Life Sciences use the same form. Photosynthesis in green leaves: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen, in the presence of light energy and chlorophyll, which are written above the arrow because they are conditions, not reactants. Cellular respiration in mitochondria: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy.

A word equation must name the actual substances, using correct chemical names. It does not show how many particles take part, or their formulas, so it cannot show whether atoms are conserved. Conditions such as heat, light, a catalyst or electricity are written above or below the arrow. Writing water as H2O or adding numbers turns a word equation into something else; keep it in words.

This section's questions are available now: reading the plus sign and the arrow, writing a word equation from a description, and identifying reactants, products and conditions.

## Subtopic: Chemical Equations and the Error Museum

A chemical equation represents the same reaction using symbols and formulas, and it shows how many particles of each substance take part. For the burning of magnesium: 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO. The symbols say which substances: Mg is magnesium, O2 is oxygen gas, MgO is magnesium oxide. The large numbers in front, called coefficients, say how many particles: two magnesium atoms react with one oxygen molecule to give two formula units of magnesium oxide. A coefficient of one is not written. The next lessons explain coefficients and balancing in detail; here the point is to read an equation correctly.

More examples. Carbon burning in plenty of oxygen: C + O2 → CO2. Hydrogen and oxygen forming water: 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O. Methane burning: CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O. Iron and sulfur: Fe + S → FeS. In each, you can check with the particle models that every atom on the left appears on the right.

State symbols are often added in brackets after each formula to show the physical state: (s) for solid, (l) for liquid, (g) for gas, and (aq) for aqueous, meaning dissolved in water. So the burning of magnesium is 2Mg(s) + O2(g) → 2MgO(s), and the reaction of zinc with hydrochloric acid is Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g), which tells you at a glance that a gas is given off.

Moving between the three representations is the skill being examined. From a word equation, replace each name with its formula, remembering that hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and chlorine gases are written H2, O2, N2 and Cl2. From a chemical equation, name each formula. From a model, write the formula of each particle and count how many of each.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: the arrow is an equals sign; it means produces and shows a direction, from reactants to products. Two: the reactants are written on the right; they are on the left, and the products on the right. Three: dissolving sugar in tea is a chemical reaction; it is a physical change, because no new substance forms. Four: in a reaction the atoms change into different atoms; the atoms are only regrouped, and every element on the left appears on the right. Five: oxygen gas is written O in an equation; it exists as molecules and is written O2.

Layout for a representation question: the word equation, then the chemical equation with formulas, then a model or a count of atoms of each element on each side. Reactions are then represented exactly as the memorandum expects.

The questions for this section are ready: reading coefficients and state symbols, converting words to symbols, and the five exhibits of the museum.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ideas again, through old Lego made into new models, sentences with arrows, and chemistry shorthand — plain words, same facts.

## Subtopic: Old Lego, New Models

Think of atoms as Lego blocks of different colours. A substance is a model built from those blocks. A chemical reaction is what happens when you pull some models apart and use the same blocks to build new models. No new blocks arrive, and no blocks are thrown away. You just end up with different things built from the same pieces.

The models you start with are the reactants. The new models you end up with are the products. When you burn magnesium ribbon, a shiny grey metal and the oxygen in the air are the reactants, and the white powder left behind, magnesium oxide, is the product. It is nothing like what you started with.

How can you tell that new models have been built, and not just the old ones moved around? Look for clues. Bubbles or fizzing, like vinegar on bicarbonate of soda: a gas is being made. A colour change. A solid appearing out of two clear liquids. Heat, light or sound, like the bright white flash of burning magnesium or the warmth of a gas flame. Or a container that suddenly feels cold. And usually you cannot easily undo it: you cannot turn ash back into wood.

Compare that with a physical change. Melt an ice block and it is still water. Dissolve sugar in tea and it is still sugar, just spread out; let the tea dry and the sugar is still there. Nothing was pulled apart and rebuilt. Same Lego model, just in a different state or place.

Chemical reactions are happening all around you. In your cells, food is burnt for energy. In leaves, sunlight builds sugar. In a taxi engine, petrol burns. On a gate, iron slowly rusts. In the oven, a cake rises.

Keep the Lego blocks in mind and try the questions for this part: reactants and products, the clues of a reaction, and chemical against physical change.

## Subtopic: Sentences With Arrows

Chemists write reactions as short sentences with two special signs. The plus sign means and. The arrow means makes, or gives. The things before the arrow are what you start with; the things after the arrow are what you end up with.

So the sentence magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide reads: magnesium and oxygen make magnesium oxide. Methane + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water reads: methane and oxygen make carbon dioxide and water. That is what happens on a gas stove: the blue flame is methane or a similar gas joining oxygen from the air.

These sentences are called word equations, because they use only the names of the substances. Here are a few more to read out loud. Iron + sulfur → iron sulfide. Calcium carbonate → calcium oxide + carbon dioxide: that one is limestone being heated in a kiln to make lime for cement, one thing breaking into two. And two you know from your body and from plants. In a leaf: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen, using light. In your cells: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy.

Notice that the light in photosynthesis is not on either side. It is something the reaction needs, a condition, so it is written on top of the arrow, the way heat is written on top for the limestone kiln.

The arrow is not an equals sign. It points one way, from start to finish, like the arrow on a one-way street. And a word equation only uses names. As soon as you write H2O instead of water, you are writing in a different language, the chemistry shorthand of the next part.

Hold the sentences in mind and try this part's questions: what the plus and the arrow mean, which side is which, and where conditions go.

## Subtopic: Writing It in Chemistry Shorthand

The shortest way to write a reaction swaps every name for its symbol or formula. Magnesium becomes Mg. Oxygen becomes O2, because oxygen in the air comes in pairs of atoms. Magnesium oxide becomes MgO. Then big numbers in front say how many of each take part: 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO, which reads: two magnesium atoms and one oxygen molecule make two magnesium oxide.

Here is the clever bit. Count the Lego blocks on each side. Left: two magnesium, two oxygen. Right: two magnesium oxides, so two magnesium and two oxygen. Same blocks, new models. A chemical equation lets you check that nothing appeared and nothing vanished, which a word equation cannot do.

Try methane on the gas stove: CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O. Left: one carbon, four hydrogen, four oxygen. Right: one carbon in carbon dioxide, two oxygen there plus two more in the two waters, so four oxygen, and four hydrogen in the two waters. Everything matches.

Sometimes little letters in brackets are added after each formula to tell you what state it is in: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) dissolved in water. Zinc dropped into hydrochloric acid: Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g). That little (g) after the hydrogen tells you to expect bubbles.

Remember the gases that come in pairs: hydrogen H2, oxygen O2, nitrogen N2, chlorine Cl2. Writing just O for oxygen gas is a common way to lose a mark.

Pocket summary of the lesson. A reaction turns reactants into new products by breaking and making bonds; atoms are rearranged, never created or destroyed. Clues: gas, colour change, a new solid, energy change. Three ways to show it: particle models with a colour key, word equations with plus and arrow, and chemical equations with formulas, coefficients and state symbols. Grade 10 Physical Sciences writes equations in every chapter.

Hold the shorthand in mind and take the final questions of the lesson: turning words into formulas, counting blocks on each side, and reading state symbols.
