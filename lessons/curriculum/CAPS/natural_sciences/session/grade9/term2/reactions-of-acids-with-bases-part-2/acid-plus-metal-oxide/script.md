# Part 1 — Expert

This session sets out the general reaction of an acid with a metal oxide, works through balanced examples, describes the laboratory preparation of copper sulfate crystals from copper oxide and sulfuric acid, and shows how this reaction is used in industry. The anchor examples are CuO + H2SO4 → CuSO4 + H2O, in which black copper(II) oxide dissolves to give a blue solution; MgO + 2HCl → MgCl2 + H2O; and Fe2O3 + 6HCl → 2FeCl3 + 3H2O, the removal of rust from steel. The governing principle is that a metal oxide is a base, so it neutralises an acid to form a salt and water, with the metal from the oxide and the non-metal part from the acid making up the salt.

## Subtopic: The General Reaction: Acid + Metal Oxide → Salt + Water

Metal oxides are bases, so they react with acids in a neutralisation. The general word equation is: acid + metal oxide → salt + water.

The particle picture: the oxide ion, O2−, from the metal oxide takes two hydrogen ions, H+, from the acid, forming a water molecule. The metal ions from the oxide and the remaining ions from the acid make up the salt, which stays dissolved in the water.

The salt is named in the usual way. The first part comes from the metal in the oxide; the second part comes from the acid: hydrochloric acid gives chlorides, sulfuric acid gives sulfates and nitric acid gives nitrates.

Word equation examples: copper oxide + sulfuric acid → copper sulfate + water. Magnesium oxide + hydrochloric acid → magnesium chloride + water. Zinc oxide + hydrochloric acid → zinc chloride + water. Calcium oxide + nitric acid → calcium nitrate + water.

Unlike the reaction of an acid with a reactive metal, no hydrogen gas is produced, and unlike the reaction with a carbonate, no carbon dioxide is produced. There is no fizzing. The evidence that a reaction has happened is that the solid oxide disappears, the solution may change colour, the indicator moves towards neutral and the mixture becomes slightly warmer.

Many metal oxides do not dissolve in water at all, yet they dissolve readily in acids. This is a useful test: copper oxide stirred into water stays as a black powder, but stirred into warm dilute acid it disappears. That difference shows that the oxide is reacting with the acid, not just dissolving.

It helps to place this reaction alongside the other reactions of acids you are meeting. Acid + metal hydroxide → salt + water, as in HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H2O. Acid + metal oxide → salt + water, the subject of this lesson. Acid + metal carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide, which fizzes. Acid + reactive metal → salt + hydrogen, which also fizzes. The first three are neutralisations, because the second reactant is a base. The fourth is not a neutralisation, because a metal is not a base, but it still produces a salt.

Following the reaction with universal indicator shows the neutralisation clearly. A few drops of indicator in the dilute acid give a red colour. As magnesium oxide powder is added and stirred, the colour moves through orange and yellow towards green as the acid is used up.

## Subtopic: Balanced Examples

Balancing follows the method you know: correct formulae first, then coefficients only.

Copper(II) oxide and sulfuric acid: CuO + H2SO4 → CuSO4 + H2O. Copper 1 and 1; sulfur 1 and 1; hydrogen 2 and 2; oxygen 1 + 4 = 5 on the left and 4 + 1 = 5 on the right. Balanced as written.

Magnesium oxide and hydrochloric acid: skeleton MgO + HCl → MgCl2 + H2O. Chlorine is 1 on the left and 2 on the right, so put 2 in front of HCl: MgO + 2HCl → MgCl2 + H2O. Check: magnesium 1 and 1; oxygen 1 and 1; hydrogen 2 and 2; chlorine 2 and 2. Balanced.

Magnesium oxide and sulfuric acid: MgO + H2SO4 → MgSO4 + H2O, balanced as written, since the sulfate ion has a 2− charge matching the magnesium ion's 2+.

Copper(II) oxide and hydrochloric acid: CuO + 2HCl → CuCl2 + H2O, giving a green-blue solution of copper chloride.

Sodium oxide and hydrochloric acid: Na2O + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H2O. Sodium needs 2 on the right, which then needs 2 chlorine atoms on the left.

Iron(III) oxide and hydrochloric acid, a harder one: skeleton Fe2O3 + HCl → FeCl3 + H2O. Iron: 2 on the left, so 2FeCl3, giving 6 chlorine atoms on the right, so 6HCl. Hydrogen is now 6 on the left, so 3H2O. Oxygen: 3 on the left and 3 on the right. Fe2O3 + 6HCl → 2FeCl3 + 3H2O.

A useful pattern: a metal ion with a 2+ charge, such as Mg, Ca, Cu or Zn, combined with hydrochloric acid always needs 2HCl, while with sulfuric acid it needs only one H2SO4.

A good check on any salt formula is the charges. Magnesium, calcium, copper and zinc ions are 2+; sodium and potassium ions are 1+; iron in rust is 3+. Chloride and nitrate ions are 1−; sulfate is 2−. The salt's formula must make the charges add to zero: MgCl2, since 2+ and two 1− cancel; CuSO4, since 2+ and 2− cancel; FeCl3, since 3+ and three 1− cancel; Na2SO4, since two 1+ and 2− cancel. Once the salt formula is right, the coefficients follow by the stocktake.

## Subtopic: Preparing Copper Sulfate Crystals

The preparation of a salt from an insoluble metal oxide is a standard practical.

Step one: pour about 25 millilitres of dilute sulfuric acid into a beaker and warm it gently over a Bunsen burner or on a hot plate, without boiling. Warming speeds up the reaction.

Step two: add black copper(II) oxide powder a spatula at a time, stirring after each addition. The powder disappears and the solution turns blue. Keep adding until some black powder remains undissolved even after stirring. This excess shows that all the acid has been used up, so the solution contains no leftover acid.

Step three: filter the warm mixture to remove the excess copper oxide. The filtrate is a clear blue solution of copper sulfate; the black residue stays in the filter paper.

Step four: pour the filtrate into an evaporating basin and heat it gently to evaporate some of the water, until the solution is concentrated. A useful test is to dip in a glass rod and see whether small crystals form on it as it cools.

Step five: leave the concentrated solution to cool slowly, and blue crystals of hydrated copper sulfate form. Pour off the remaining liquid and dry the crystals between filter papers.

The reasons for each step are examination favourites. The oxide is added in excess to make sure all the acid reacts. Filtering removes the unreacted solid. Evaporating concentrates the solution. Cooling slowly gives larger crystals. The solution is not boiled dry, because that would destroy the crystal shape and remove the water that is part of the blue crystals.

Safety: wear goggles, because warm acid can spit; handle copper compounds carefully, as they are harmful if swallowed; and wash hands after the experiment.

A typical results description reads: the colourless acid turned pale blue as the first copper oxide dissolved, then deep blue; after about four spatulas, black powder remained even after stirring; the filtrate was a clear blue solution; on cooling, blue diamond-shaped crystals formed. Note that the crystals are a different shape and colour from the starting powder, which is further evidence that a new substance was made.

The same method makes other salts. Magnesium oxide and dilute sulfuric acid give colourless magnesium sulfate crystals, Epsom salts. Zinc oxide and dilute sulfuric acid give colourless zinc sulfate crystals.

## Subtopic: Industrial Uses and the Error Museum

Removing rust and scale from steel, called pickling, uses this reaction on a large scale. Before steel sheet is galvanised with zinc or painted, it is dipped into hydrochloric or sulfuric acid, which dissolves the iron oxide on the surface, Fe2O3 + 6HCl → 2FeCl3 + 3H2O, leaving clean metal for the coating to stick to. Household rust removers often contain a weak acid that works the same way.

Copper sulfate made in this kind of reaction is used as a fungicide in farming, for example in Bordeaux mixture sprayed on vineyards in the Western Cape, as an algicide in water, and in electroplating copper.

The processing of ores also relies on it. Copper and zinc ores that have been roasted to oxides are dissolved in sulfuric acid, and the metals are then recovered from the solution, in plants in South Africa and elsewhere.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: acid + metal oxide → salt + hydrogen; the second product is water, and hydrogen comes only from acid and metal. Two: the reaction fizzes; there is no gas. Three: MgO + HCl → MgCl2 + H2O is balanced; it needs 2HCl. Four: copper oxide dissolves in water to make the blue solution; it only reacts with the acid. Five: boil the copper sulfate solution dry to get crystals; evaporate partly and let it cool slowly.

Layout for an acid and metal oxide answer: general word equation; specific word equation with the salt correctly named; balanced chemical equation with a stocktake; observations, such as the black solid disappearing and the solution turning blue; and, for a preparation, the steps with reasons.

Medicine uses the reaction too. Magnesium oxide is one of the active ingredients in some antacids: it neutralises excess stomach acid, MgO + 2HCl → MgCl2 + H2O, forming magnesium chloride, which is harmless in small amounts. Zinc oxide, in sunscreen and nappy rash creams, is chosen partly because it is a mild base that does not irritate skin.

## Subtopic: The Vanishing Black Powder

Remember that metal oxides are bases. So when a metal oxide meets an acid, they cancel each other out, just like any acid and base. Out come a salt and water. Acid plus metal oxide gives salt plus water.

Here is the classic demo. Copper oxide is a black powder. Stir it into water and nothing happens: it just sits there, black. Now stir it into warm dilute sulfuric acid. The black powder vanishes and the liquid turns a clear, bright blue. That blue is copper sulfate, the salt.

In words: copper oxide plus sulfuric acid gives copper sulfate plus water.

There is no fizzing this time. No hydrogen and no carbon dioxide. The clues that something happened are the powder disappearing, the colour change and a little warmth.

To name the salt: metal first, then the acid's part. Copper oxide with sulfuric acid: copper sulfate. Magnesium oxide with hydrochloric acid: magnesium chloride. Zinc oxide with nitric acid: zinc nitrate.

Keep the family of acid reactions straight. Acid plus hydroxide: salt plus water. Acid plus oxide: salt plus water. Acid plus carbonate: salt plus water plus carbon dioxide, with fizzing. Acid plus metal: salt plus hydrogen, with fizzing. Only the carbonate and the metal fizz.

## Subtopic: A Balancing Workout

Now let us put numbers on it.

Copper oxide and sulfuric acid: CuO + H2SO4 → CuSO4 + H2O. Count: one copper, one sulfur, two hydrogens and five oxygens on each side. Already balanced. Easy.

Magnesium oxide and hydrochloric acid: MgO + HCl → MgCl2 + H2O. The salt has two chlorines, but the left only has one. Put a 2 in front of HCl: MgO + 2HCl → MgCl2 + H2O. Now two hydrogens and two chlorines each side. Balanced.

The tough one: rust and hydrochloric acid. Fe2O3 + HCl → FeCl3 + H2O. Two irons on the left, so two FeCl3 on the right. That is six chlorines, so six HCl. Six hydrogens means three waters. Check oxygen: three each side. Fe2O3 + 6HCl → 2FeCl3 + 3H2O.

A handy shortcut: magnesium, calcium, copper and zinc oxides need 2HCl, but only one H2SO4.

Quick check for any salt: make the charges cancel. Magnesium is 2+ and chloride is 1−, so it takes two chlorides: MgCl2. Copper is 2+ and sulfate is 2−, so one each: CuSO4. Get the salt right first, then count.

## Subtopic: The Crystal Recipe

You can make your own blue crystals.

Warm some dilute sulfuric acid. Add black copper oxide a spoonful at a time, stirring, until some black powder is left over no matter how much you stir. That leftover powder proves all the acid is used up.

Filter out the leftover powder. You now have a clear blue solution.

Heat it gently in an evaporating dish until about half the water has gone. Then leave it to cool slowly. Blue crystals grow. Do not boil it dry, or you get a crusty powder instead of crystals.

The same reaction cleans steel. Factories dip rusty steel in acid, the rust dissolves, and the clean steel can be painted or galvanised. Farmers spray copper sulfate on vines to stop fungus.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Acid plus metal oxide gives salt plus water, because metal oxides are bases. CuO + H2SO4 → CuSO4 + H2O makes a blue solution. MgO + 2HCl → MgCl2 + H2O. Fe2O3 + 6HCl → 2FeCl3 + 3H2O removes rust. No fizzing. To make crystals: add oxide in excess, filter, evaporate partly, cool slowly. Grade 10 to 12 chemistry builds on preparing salts in this way.

Swap the ingredients and you get other crystals. Magnesium oxide with sulfuric acid gives Epsom salts, the white crystals some people put in a bath. Zinc oxide with sulfuric acid gives zinc sulfate.
