### subtopic_1 — The General Reaction: Acid + Metal Oxide → Salt + Water
**Assistant:** Why does copper oxide dissolve in acid but not in water?

**Tutor:** Because in acid it is not simply dissolving; it is reacting to form a new substance that is soluble. Copper oxide is an ionic solid made of copper ions and oxide ions held together very strongly in a lattice. Water molecules are not able to pull these ions apart, so the black powder stays undissolved in water. An acid, however, supplies hydrogen ions, and these react with the oxide ions, removing them from the lattice as water molecules. As the oxide ions are removed, the copper ions are released into the solution, where they are surrounded by water molecules, and it is these surrounded copper ions that give the solution its blue colour. The negative ions from the acid, sulfate ions from sulfuric acid, stay in solution alongside them. So the substance in the beaker at the end is copper sulfate, which is very soluble in water, not copper oxide. You can show this is a chemical change by evaporating the blue solution: you get blue crystals of copper sulfate, not black copper oxide back again. The same idea explains why rust dissolves in acid but not in water, and why limestone is attacked by acid rain but not by pure water. Warming the acid helps, because the hydrogen ions then collide with the surface of the oxide more often and with more energy, so the reaction is faster.

### subtopic_2 — Balanced Examples
**Assistant:** Why do we add the copper oxide in excess instead of measuring the exact amount?

**Tutor:** Because adding excess solid is a simple, reliable way to make sure all the acid has been used up, without needing to measure anything precisely. If you added too little copper oxide, some sulfuric acid would remain in the solution. When you then evaporated it, the acid would become more concentrated, which is dangerous, and the crystals would be contaminated and wet with acid. If you add copper oxide until some stays undissolved, you know there is no acid left for it to react with, so the solution contains only copper sulfate and water. The leftover solid is easy to remove by filtering, since copper oxide is insoluble. This method works whenever the base is an insoluble solid, such as copper oxide, magnesium oxide, zinc oxide or a metal carbonate. When the base is a soluble alkali, such as sodium hydroxide, you cannot use the excess method, because extra sodium hydroxide would dissolve and could not be filtered out. In that case, you have to add exactly the right amount, which is found with an indicator or by a titration, and then repeat the reaction without indicator so the salt is pure. You will practise titrations in Grade 12. The same thinking about excess reactants and how to remove them is used in industry and in pharmaceutical manufacture.

### subtopic_3 — Preparing Copper Sulfate Crystals
**Assistant:** Is the blue colour from copper itself, or from the sulfate?

**Tutor:** It comes from the copper ions, more precisely from copper ions surrounded by water molecules. Sulfate ions are colourless: sodium sulfate and magnesium sulfate solutions are clear and colourless. Copper ions in water, however, absorb light from the red and orange end of the spectrum, so the light that passes through looks blue. That is why many copper compounds dissolved in water look blue or blue-green, whatever the acid: copper sulfate is blue, copper nitrate is blue, and copper chloride is green-blue because the chloride ions also attach to the copper and shift the colour slightly. The colour also depends on water being attached. Blue copper sulfate crystals contain water molecules locked into the crystal, five for every copper sulfate unit, and they are called hydrated copper sulfate. If you heat the blue crystals strongly, the water is driven off and a white powder, anhydrous copper sulfate, remains. Add a few drops of water to the white powder and it turns blue again and becomes warm. This is a standard test for water. The colour of transition metal ions is a big topic in later chemistry, and it explains many colours you see, from the green patina on copper roofs to the colours of gemstones, such as the red of rubies, caused by small amounts of chromium.

### subtopic_4 — Industrial Uses and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** If acids remove rust, why not just use acid to clean my rusty bicycle?

**Tutor:** You can use a mild acid with care, but strong acids would create more problems than they solve. Acids do dissolve rust, because rust is mainly iron oxide, a base, and acid plus metal oxide gives salt plus water. The difficulty is that the acid does not stop at the rust. Once the rust is gone, the acid reacts with the iron underneath as well, iron plus acid gives an iron salt and hydrogen, so the metal is eaten away and weakened. Bare iron that has been cleaned with acid also rusts very quickly afterwards unless it is rinsed, dried and protected at once. That is why factories that pickle steel use carefully controlled concentrations and times, rinse the steel thoroughly, and coat it immediately with zinc or paint. At home, commercial rust removers are made with weaker acids, such as phosphoric acid or oxalic acid, and often form a thin protective layer on the iron. Vinegar or a paste of lemon juice can loosen light rust on small tools if they are rinsed, dried and oiled afterwards. Strong acids such as pool acid should not be used on a bicycle: the fumes are harmful, the acid attacks the chain and other parts, and splashes can burn skin and eyes. Prevention, keeping the bicycle dry, oiled and painted, is far better than cure.
