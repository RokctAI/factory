# Part 1 — Expert

This session applies acid-base chemistry to the environment and to farming. It explains how burning fuels produces acid rain, describes the damage acid rain causes, explains why many soils become acidic and how farmers use limestone and other forms of lime to neutralise them, and reviews ways of reducing acid rain at its source. The anchor facts are that clean rain has a pH of about 5,6 because of dissolved carbon dioxide, that acid rain is rain below that pH and can reach about pH 4, that sulfur dioxide forms by S + O2 → SO2 when coal burns, and that limestone neutralises acid, for example CaCO3 + H2SO4 → CaSO4 + H2O + CO2. The governing principle is that acidic non-metal oxides from burning fuels make rain acidic, and that bases such as calcium carbonate neutralise that acidity.

## Subtopic: How Acid Rain Forms

Even unpolluted rain is slightly acidic. As raindrops fall, they dissolve some carbon dioxide from the air, forming a weak acid, carbonic acid: CO2 + H2O → H2CO3. This gives clean rain a pH of about 5,6. Acid rain is the name for rain, snow, mist or dust with a pH lower than this, made more acidic by pollution. In badly polluted areas the pH of rain can fall to about 4, which is roughly 10 × 10 = 100 times more acidic than pH 6.

The two main culprits are sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides.

Sulfur dioxide comes mainly from burning coal, which contains sulfur, about one percent in typical South African coal. When coal burns, the sulfur combines with oxygen: S + O2 → SO2. Other sources are the smelting of metal ores that contain sulfur, oil refining, and diesel engines. In the air, sulfur dioxide reacts with more oxygen and with water to form sulfuric acid, overall 2SO2 + O2 + 2H2O → 2H2SO4.

Nitrogen oxides come from the very high temperatures inside vehicle engines, power station boilers and lightning, where nitrogen and oxygen from the air combine: N2 + O2 → 2NO, followed by 2NO + O2 → 2NO2. Nitrogen dioxide dissolves in water and reacts with oxygen to form nitric acid, HNO3.

South Africa produces most of its electricity in coal-fired power stations, a large number of which are clustered on the Mpumalanga Highveld, together with the Sasol plant at Secunda and many smelters. Measurements have shown this area to have some of the highest levels of nitrogen dioxide and sulfur dioxide pollution in the world, and the government has declared it a Highveld Priority Area for air quality. Because winds carry pollutants hundreds of kilometres before rain washes them out, acid rain can fall far from its source, including over Gauteng, the Free State and neighbouring countries.

Scientists measure acid rain by collecting rainwater at monitoring stations in clean containers, measuring its pH with a meter, and analysing it for sulfate and nitrate ions, which reveal how much of the acidity comes from each gas. Air quality stations also measure the concentrations of sulfur dioxide and nitrogen dioxide in the air every hour. Satellites can map nitrogen dioxide from space, and those maps show a bright patch over the Highveld.

Acid deposition can also be dry. On days without rain, particles and gases carrying sulfate and nitrate settle directly onto surfaces, and the next rain or dew turns them into acid on the spot. The total effect, wet and dry together, is called acid deposition.

## Subtopic: The Effects of Acid Rain

Acid rain causes damage wherever it falls.

Buildings and monuments. Limestone and marble are both forms of calcium carbonate, which is a base and reacts with acids: CaCO3 + H2SO4 → CaSO4 + H2O + CO2. The calcium sulfate formed is softer and slightly soluble and is washed away, so carved details on statues, gravestones and old buildings are gradually worn smooth. Concrete and mortar, which contain calcium compounds, are also attacked.

Metals. Acid rain speeds up the corrosion of iron and steel bridges, railway lines, roofs, cars and pylons, increasing the cost of maintenance.

Soils and plants. Acid rain washes essential nutrients such as calcium, magnesium and potassium out of the soil, and releases aluminium ions that are toxic to plant roots. Leaves can be damaged directly. Forests in parts of Europe and North America were badly damaged in the late twentieth century, and the timber plantations of Mpumalanga are potentially at risk.

Water and living things. Lakes and rivers become more acidic, and fish and other animals cannot survive when the pH falls below about 5. Aluminium washed from the soil adds to the harm, damaging the gills of fish.

Human health. The sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides that cause acid rain, and the fine particles that form from them, irritate the lungs, worsen asthma and bronchitis, and are linked to many premature deaths each year in the Highveld region.

A simple laboratory model of the damage uses marble chips in dilute acid. Place a few chips in each of three beakers, containing dilute hydrochloric acid, vinegar and distilled water. The chips in the hydrochloric acid fizz steadily as carbon dioxide is released; those in the vinegar fizz slowly; those in distilled water do not react. Weighing the dried chips after a few days shows the greatest loss of mass in the strongest acid. A burning splint placed in the beaker mouth goes out, and the gas turns limewater milky, confirming that it is carbon dioxide.

## Subtopic: Using Limestone to Reduce Soil Acidity

Many South African soils are naturally acidic, especially in high-rainfall areas such as parts of KwaZulu-Natal, Mpumalanga, the Eastern Cape and the Western Cape, where rain has leached out the basic minerals over a very long time. Soils also become more acidic from acid rain, from the long-term use of some nitrogen fertilisers, and from removing crops year after year.

Most crops, such as maize, wheat, potatoes and vegetables, grow best in soil with a pH of about 6 to 7. In acidic soil, below about pH 5,5, plants struggle to take up nutrients such as phosphorus, aluminium becomes toxic to roots, and yields fall sharply.

The remedy is liming: adding a base to neutralise the acid in the soil. Farmers and gardeners use several forms of lime. Agricultural lime is finely crushed limestone, calcium carbonate, or dolomitic lime, which also contains magnesium carbonate and supplies magnesium. It reacts slowly and safely: CaCO3 + 2HNO3 → Ca(NO3)2 + H2O + CO2, or with sulfuric acid CaCO3 + H2SO4 → CaSO4 + H2O + CO2. Slaked lime, calcium hydroxide, acts faster: Ca(OH)2 + H2SO4 → CaSO4 + 2H2O. Quicklime, calcium oxide, is the fastest but is caustic and dangerous to handle.

Good practice: test the soil pH first, with an indicator kit or by sending a sample to a laboratory; calculate the amount of lime needed; spread finely ground lime evenly and work it into the soil, ideally several months before planting, because limestone reacts slowly; and retest after a season or two. Too much lime can raise the pH above 7 and cause other nutrient problems, so the amount matters.

Lakes and dams affected by acid rain can also be treated by adding powdered limestone, and acid mine drainage is neutralised with lime before it is released.

Home gardeners can test soil easily. Mix a teaspoon of soil with distilled water in a jar, shake, let it settle, and test the clear liquid with universal indicator paper or a garden pH kit. A result of orange or yellow suggests acidic soil that may need lime; green suggests neutral soil. The test is rough, but it shows the principle the soil laboratories use with more precise equipment.

## Subtopic: Reducing Acid Rain and the Error Museum

Treating the effects of acid rain helps, but reducing the emissions is better.

Flue gas desulfurisation. The waste gases from a power station boiler are sprayed with a slurry of crushed limestone before they reach the chimney. The sulfur dioxide, an acidic oxide, reacts with the base: CaCO3 + SO2 → CaSO3 + CO2, and further oxidation turns the product into calcium sulfate, gypsum, which can be sold for plasterboard. This removes up to about 90 percent or more of the sulfur dioxide. The new Kusile power station in Mpumalanga is the first in South Africa built with this technology.

Other measures: washing coal to remove some sulfur before burning; using low-sulfur fuels, such as the cleaner petrol and diesel now required in South Africa; catalytic converters in vehicle exhausts to reduce nitrogen oxides; saving electricity; and generating more electricity from renewable sources such as solar and wind, which release no sulfur dioxide.

The error museum for this lesson has five exhibits. One: all rain is neutral, pH 7; clean rain is about 5,6 because of dissolved carbon dioxide. Two: acid rain is caused by carbon dioxide; the main causes are sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides. Three: farmers add acid to acidic soil; they add a base, lime. Four: limestone and marble resist acid; they are calcium carbonate, a base, and react with it. Five: acid rain only affects the area where the pollution is produced; winds carry pollutants hundreds of kilometres.

Layout for an acid rain answer: the source of the pollutant; the equation for its formation; how it forms an acid in rain; at least two effects, each with an explanation; and a solution with the chemistry behind it, such as neutralisation by limestone.

Countries that acted on acid rain have seen results. In Europe and North America, laws from the 1980s onwards forced power stations to fit scrubbers and switch to low-sulfur fuels, and sulfur dioxide emissions fell by more than three quarters. Lakes and forests have slowly begun to recover, showing that the problem can be reversed when emissions are cut.

## Subtopic: Sour Rain

Rain is never perfectly pure. As it falls, it picks up a little carbon dioxide from the air, which makes it very slightly acidic, about pH 5,6. That is normal.

Acid rain is rain that has become much more acidic because of pollution. It can drop to pH 4, which is about a hundred times more acidic than pH 6.

Where does the extra acid come from? From burning things. Coal has a little sulfur in it. When coal burns in a power station, the sulfur burns too and makes sulfur dioxide. Very hot engines and furnaces also make nitrogen oxides out of the air itself. These are non-metal oxides, and remember: non-metal oxides make acids. In the clouds they turn into sulfuric acid and nitric acid, and down they come with the rain.

Mpumalanga has many coal power stations close together, plus a huge fuel factory at Secunda, so its air is among the most polluted in the world for these gases. And the wind carries them far, so the acid can fall hundreds of kilometres away.

It does not even need to rain. On dry days, tiny particles carrying the acid-making gases settle on roofs, leaves and statues, and the next dew turns them into acid right there.

## Subtopic: What Acid Rain Eats

Acid rain is a slow, quiet vandal.

It eats stone. Marble and limestone are calcium carbonate, which is a base. Acid reacts with it, fizzing out carbon dioxide and leaving a soft, crumbly salt that washes away. Old statues and gravestones lose their faces and letters.

It eats metal. Bridges, rails, roofs and cars rust faster.

It starves plants. It washes good nutrients like calcium and magnesium out of the soil, and sets free aluminium, which poisons roots.

It kills fish. Lakes that get too acidic, below about pH 5, cannot support fish.

And the gases that cause it hurt people's lungs, making asthma worse.

You can copy the damage in a classroom. Drop marble chips into dilute acid and they fizz as carbon dioxide escapes. Drop them into plain water and nothing happens. After a few days, the chips in acid weigh less. That is acid rain on a statue, speeded up.

## Subtopic: Lime to the Rescue

Lots of soil in the wetter parts of South Africa is naturally sour, or acidic. Years of rain wash out the minerals that keep it neutral, and acid rain and some fertilisers make it worse. Most crops, like maize and vegetables, want a pH of about 6 to 7.

The fix is a base. Farmers spread crushed limestone, called agricultural lime, which is calcium carbonate. It cancels the acid in the soil, slowly and safely, and the pH climbs back up. They test the soil first, spread the right amount, and do it months before planting, because limestone works slowly. Slaked lime works faster.

The same trick can clean the smoke. At a power station with a scrubber, the smoke is sprayed with wet limestone before it goes up the chimney. The sulfur dioxide reacts with the limestone and is trapped. Kusile power station does this.

Other ways to cut acid rain: cleaner fuels, catalytic converters on cars, saving electricity, and more solar and wind power.

Pocket summary of the lesson. Clean rain is about pH 5,6; acid rain is lower. Burning coal and fuel releases sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides, which form sulfuric and nitric acid in rain. Acid rain damages limestone and marble, metals, soil, plants, lakes and lungs. Limestone, a base, neutralises acidic soil and can scrub sulfur dioxide from smoke. Grade 10 to 12 chemistry and geography build on this.
