### subtopic_1 — How Acid Rain Forms
**Assistant:** If carbon dioxide makes rain acidic, why is it not counted as a cause of acid rain?

**Tutor:** Because carbon dioxide only makes rain slightly acidic, and that level is natural. Carbonic acid is a weak acid, and carbon dioxide does not dissolve very much in water, so even with today's raised carbon dioxide levels, rain in equilibrium with the air only reaches a pH of about 5,6. Plants, soils, lakes and buildings have existed with rain of that pH for as long as there has been rain. Sulfuric and nitric acids are strong acids, and even small amounts of them push the pH of rain far lower, to 4 or below, which is the level that damages forests, lakes and buildings. That is why the definition of acid rain is rain below about pH 5,6, and why the main causes are sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides. Carbon dioxide does cause a different and very serious problem. As its concentration in the atmosphere rises from burning fossil fuels, more of it dissolves in the oceans, slowly lowering their pH, a process called ocean acidification, which makes it harder for corals, shellfish and some plankton to build their shells and skeletons from calcium carbonate. And carbon dioxide is the main gas behind the enhanced greenhouse effect and global warming, which you will study in Term 4. So it is important, just not the cause of acid rain itself.

### subtopic_2 — The Effects of Acid Rain
**Assistant:** Why does acid rain damage some lakes much more than others?

**Tutor:** It depends on the rocks and soils around the lake, which determine how much natural base the water contains to neutralise the acid. Lakes and rivers in areas with limestone, dolomite or other rocks rich in calcium carbonate have dissolved carbonate and hydrogencarbonate ions in their water. These bases react with the acid that falls into the water and keep the pH fairly steady, an effect called buffering. Such lakes can receive a lot of acid rain before their pH falls noticeably. Lakes in areas with granite or sandstone, which contain very little calcium carbonate, have soft water with almost no buffering, so even a modest amount of acid rain lowers their pH quickly. That is why the worst damage to lakes in Scandinavia and Canada occurred in regions of granite rock. The same principle explains why some soils are acidified quickly and others resist. It also suggests a remedy: adding powdered limestone to an acidified lake restores its buffering and raises the pH, which has been done in Sweden on a large scale, though it has to be repeated as long as the acid rain continues. The lasting solution is always to reduce emissions. In South Africa, monitoring of dams and rivers on the Highveld forms part of the national effort to understand the effects of the region's air pollution.

### subtopic_3 — Using Limestone to Reduce Soil Acidity
**Assistant:** Can a farmer add too much lime?

**Tutor:** Yes, and over-liming causes its own problems, which is why soil testing comes first. If lime raises the soil pH much above 7, several plant nutrients, including iron, manganese, zinc and phosphorus, become less available to plants because they form compounds that do not dissolve. Crops may then show yellowing leaves and poor growth even though the nutrients are present in the soil. Some crops are especially sensitive. Potatoes, for example, are more prone to a skin disease called scab in soil with a high pH, and tea and blueberries prefer acidic soils. So the aim is not maximum lime but the right amount to reach the target pH for the crop, usually about 6 to 6,5 for most field crops and vegetables. Soil laboratories measure the pH and also how strongly the soil resists a change in pH, which depends on its clay and organic matter content, and from that they recommend how many tonnes of lime to apply per hectare. A clay soil needs more lime than a sandy soil to change its pH by the same amount. Farmers also choose the type of lime: calcitic lime supplies calcium, while dolomitic lime also supplies magnesium if the soil lacks it. Because over-liming is slow to correct, the safest approach is to apply lime in measured amounts and retest every few years.

### subtopic_4 — Reducing Acid Rain and the Error Museum
**Assistant:** What happens to the gypsum made in power station scrubbers?

**Tutor:** It can be a useful product rather than a waste. In a scrubber, crushed limestone mixed with water is sprayed into the flue gases. The sulfur dioxide reacts with the calcium carbonate to form calcium sulfite and carbon dioxide, CaCO3 + SO2 → CaSO3 + CO2, and air blown through the slurry then oxidises the calcium sulfite to calcium sulfate. When this calcium sulfate crystallises with water, it is gypsum, the same mineral used to make plaster of Paris, plasterboard for ceilings and walls, and cement, where it controls how quickly the cement sets. Farmers also use gypsum to improve some soils. In countries with many scrubbers, much of the plasterboard used in buildings is made from this synthetic gypsum. The challenge is scale: a large coal power station with a scrubber needs well over a million tonnes of limestone a year and produces a similar mass of gypsum, so a market or a safe disposal site must be found, and the limestone must be mined and transported, which has its own environmental costs. The scrubbing reaction also produces carbon dioxide, so it reduces acid rain while adding slightly to greenhouse gas emissions. Engineers weigh these trade-offs. It is a clear example of a chemical reaction you have learnt in Grade 9, an acidic oxide reacting with a basic carbonate, applied at an industrial scale.
