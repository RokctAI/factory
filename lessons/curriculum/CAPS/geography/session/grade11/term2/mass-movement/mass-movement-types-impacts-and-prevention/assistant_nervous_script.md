Hey, it's Bianca — mass movement is the last geomorphology topic, and it can feel like six disasters, one tragedy and twenty prevention measures all talking at once. Breathe. The whole thing hangs on one tug-of-war and one ladder.

The tug-of-war: gravity pulls the slope down; friction, particle grip and plant roots hold it up. Mass movement is the moment the rope changes hands — material moving downslope under gravity, with NO river, glacier or wind carrying it. That definition alone is two marks; write both halves. And remember water's double game from the sandcastle: damp glues, soaked destroys. Nearly every failure follows heavy rain.

The ladder is the six types, slowest to fastest, water rising as you climb: creep, solifluction, landslide, slump, rock fall, mud flow. Attach one picture to each — leaning fence posts, cold porridge over frozen ground, the slab off the flatbed, the spoon-scar with the back-tilted block, the dropping cliff block, the racing river of setting concrete — and any photo question sorts itself in seconds.

For the case study, carry April 2022 Durban with three numbers and one sentence: over 300 millimetres in 24 hours, more than 400 deaths, billions in damage — and the sentence: same rain, unequal risk, because the poorest homes stood on the steepest, least engineered slopes. That sentence is the difference between retelling and understanding.

Prevention never needs memorising, because every measure is a cause reversed: too steep — regrade, terrace, gabions; too wet — drains and fixed pipes; too bare — plant it; badly placed — GIS maps, warnings, and homes off the red zones. Walk the causes and the answer writes itself.

One tug-of-war, one six-rung ladder, three numbers, one reversal trick. That is the entire topic — and the term. You've got this!
