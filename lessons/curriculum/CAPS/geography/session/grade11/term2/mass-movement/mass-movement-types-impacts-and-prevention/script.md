# Part 1 — Expert

The slopes session ended with a warning about building at the foot of an active talus. This session is that warning expanded into a topic: MASS MOVEMENT, the downslope transfer of rock, soil and debris under gravity. Four pieces: the concept itself and the factors that prime a slope to move; the six kinds of movement classified by speed and water content; the impacts on people and the environment, with the KwaZulu-Natal floods of April 2022 as the case study no South African class can skip; and the strategies that prevent or minimise the damage.

## Subtopic: What Moves a Slope

MASS MOVEMENT — also called mass wasting — is the movement of weathered material downslope under the direct pull of GRAVITY, without requiring a transporting agent such as a river, glacier or wind. That definition carries two marks on its own: gravity as the driver, and no transporting medium. The material may creep a millimetre a year or fall at 200 kilometres per hour; both are mass movement.

Whether a slope moves is a contest between DRIVING forces and RESISTING forces. Gravity's downslope pull grows with the SLOPE ANGLE — recall the angle of repose from the slopes lesson: loose material standing near its limit needs only a nudge. Resistance comes from friction, from the interlocking of particles, from plant roots binding the soil, and from dry cohesion. Anything that increases the drive or cuts the resistance pushes the slope toward failure.

The great destabiliser is WATER. A little moisture binds sand — a sandcastle needs damp sand — but saturation is fatal: water fills the pores, pushes particles apart, adds enormous weight, and lubricates potential slide surfaces such as the junction between soil and bedrock. Most mass movement disasters follow heavy or prolonged rain. The other triggers: UNDERCUTTING of the slope base by rivers, waves or road cuttings, which removes support exactly where it matters most; EARTHQUAKES and blasting, which shake material loose; FREEZE-THAW in cold mountains, prying rock from cliffs; and the human contributions — stripping vegetation whose roots held the soil, loading slopes with buildings and waste dumps, leaking water into them from pipes and pit latrines, and cutting steep, unsupported faces for roads and platforms.

Take this section's questions now: the definition with its two essential clauses, driving forces against resisting forces, and water's double role explained.

## Subtopic: The Six Kinds of Movement

CAPS names six, and they order themselves neatly from slowest and driest to fastest and wettest — classify by SPEED and WATER and each becomes recognisable on sight.

SOIL CREEP is the slowest: the whole soil blanket migrating downslope at millimetres per year. Each wetting-drying or heating-cooling cycle lifts soil particles at right angles to the slope and lets them settle vertically, a fraction downhill of where they started. Too slow to watch, it shows itself in the evidence: tilted fence posts and gravestones, curved tree trunks, small step-like TERRACETTES across grassy hillsides, and soil piled against the upslope side of walls. SOLIFLUCTION is creep's cold, wet cousin: in mountains where the subsoil freezes, the thawed, waterlogged surface layer flows slowly over the frozen layer beneath, hanging lobes like thick porridge down the high Drakensberg and Lesotho slopes.

LANDSLIDES are rapid: a coherent mass of rock or soil sliding along a defined plane, usually after saturation, often where the slide plane parallels the slope — a road cutting through tilted strata is a classic setting. The SLUMP is a special slide with ROTATION: the mass moves along a curved, spoon-shaped surface, leaving a crescent scar above and a tilted-back block below, common in thick soils and embankments. ROCK FALLS are the free-fall member: blocks pried loose from cliffs by freeze-thaw or heat expansion drop, bounce and shatter, feeding the talus below — the reason mountain-pass road signs warn of falling rocks. Fastest and wettest are MUD FLOWS: soil and debris so saturated they behave as a fluid, racing down valleys and gullies at frightening speed and setting like concrete when they stop. On deforested or burnt slopes after cloudbursts, mud flows are the deadliest of the six.

Hold the ladder: creep, solifluction — slow; landslide, slump — rapid sliding; rock fall — free-fall; mud flow — fluid. Speed rises, and from slide to flow, water content rises with it.

## Subtopic: Impacts — KwaZulu-Natal and Beyond

Mass movement's impacts on the ENVIRONMENT begin with the land itself: slopes stripped to bedrock, fertile topsoil buried under sterile debris, rivers choked with sediment that muddies water, smothers aquatic life and silts up dams. Scars open the way for gully erosion, and repeated failures can strip whole hillsides of the soil that took millennia to form.

The impacts on PEOPLE range from nuisance to catastrophe. Creep quietly tilts poles, cracks walls and bends railway lines — a slow tax on infrastructure. Rock falls close mountain passes and menace traffic. Slides and mud flows destroy in seconds what took decades to build: houses, roads, bridges, water and power lines. The costs multiply where poverty pushes settlement onto exactly the most dangerous ground — steep, unstable slopes that formal planning rejected, occupied by informal housing with no engineering, where cut platforms, leaking water and stripped vegetation prime the very slopes people sleep on.

South Africa's defining case study is the KWAZULU-NATAL FLOOD DISASTER of April 2022. Over 300 millimetres of rain fell in 24 hours across parts of the Durban metro — several months' worth in a day. Saturated hillsides failed in hundreds of places at once: landslides and mud flows tore through formal and informal settlements alike, more than 400 people died, thousands of homes were destroyed, and roads, rail, the harbour and water systems suffered billions of rands in damage. The disaster braided together every thread of this topic: extreme rainfall as trigger, steep coastal hills as setting, and vulnerable settlement on unstable slopes as the multiplier that turned a natural process into a national catastrophe. Smaller recurring examples complete the picture: rock falls that forced the rebuilding of Chapman's Peak Drive, slumping cuttings on the N2, and creep damage in hillside suburbs across the country.

## Subtopic: Prevention and Minimisation

Strategies divide into engineering the slope, managing the water, protecting the surface, and planning where people live — most real defences combine all four.

ENGINEERING THE SLOPE: cut unstable slopes back to a gentler angle below their failure threshold; TERRACE long slopes into steps that break the momentum of moving material; build RETAINING WALLS and GABIONS — wire baskets packed with rock — to hold the toe of a slope where support was removed; anchor unstable rock with bolts driven into sound bedrock; and drape steel MESH and CATCH FENCES over cliff faces to hold falling rock. Chapman's Peak Drive shows the full engineering catalogue: catch fences, anchored mesh and a half-tunnel roof that lets rock falls pass overhead into the sea.

MANAGING THE WATER, because water causes most failures: intercept runoff above the slope with cut-off drains; lay subsurface drains that bleed water out of the slope before pore pressures build; seal cracks and fix leaking pipes; and never discharge stormwater onto a slope face.

PROTECTING THE SURFACE: keep and restore VEGETATION — roots bind soil, plants intercept rain, and transpiration dries the slope; re-grass and plant burnt or cleared slopes immediately; ban overgrazing and uncontrolled clearing on steep land.

PLANNING: map hazard zones with the GIS tools of the previous session — overlay slope angle, soil, drainage and rainfall — and keep housing, schools and clinics off high-risk slopes; enforce building regulations on cuttings and platforms; install early-warning rain gauges that trigger evacuation when thresholds are passed, as Durban has moved to do since 2022; and educate communities on the warning signs — new cracks, tilting poles, springs appearing on a slope, doors jamming. Where settlements already occupy dangerous slopes, the honest strategies are upgrading — drainage, retaining structures, safe platforms — and, in the worst cases, relocation with dignity.

## Subtopic: Why Slopes Let Go

Every slope on earth is holding a tug-of-war. On one side pulls gravity, forever trying to drag everything downhill. On the other side pull the slope's defenders: friction, the interlocking grip of particles, and plant roots stitching the soil together like the threads in fabric. As long as the defenders win, the hillside stands. Mass movement is simply the moment the rope changes hands — and the definition to write is: material moving downslope under gravity, WITHOUT a river, glacier or wind carrying it. Gravity alone.

The match-fixer is water, and it plays for both teams. Squeeze a handful of damp sand: it holds together — perfect sandcastle. That little bit of water glues the grains. Now pour a bucket over your castle: it slumps into soup. Too much water forces the grains apart, adds massive weight, and greases every surface the soil could slide on. That is why almost every slope disaster arrives during or just after heavy rain — the slope that stood for fifty dry years fails in one soaked night.

The other ways to lose the tug-of-war are easy to picture: saw at the rope's anchor — a river or a road cutting undercutting the slope's toe; shake the players — an earthquake; or send off the defenders — strip the vegetation, and the roots that held the soil are gone. People manage all three at once on the same hillside: cut a platform, strip the grass, leak water from a pipe. The slope keeps the score.

## Subtopic: The Six Speeds of Falling Ground

Line the six movements up like gears, slowest to fastest, and add water as you go. Gear one: SOIL CREEP. The whole hillside skin slides downhill slower than fingernails grow. You never see it move — you see its paperwork: fence posts leaning drunk, tree trunks curved like bananas as they keep growing upright while their feet slip, little grass steps across the slope, soil heaped behind the top side of a wall. Gear two: SOLIFLUCTION — creep in freezing mountains. The deep soil is frozen solid; the surface thaws into cold porridge and oozes over it in slow lobes, the high Drakensberg's speciality.

Gear three and four are the sliders. A LANDSLIDE is a slab of hillside letting go all at once and sliding down a plane, like a loaded tow truck losing its bakkie off the flatbed — one mass, one slippery surface, usually after soaking rain. A SLUMP is the slide with a twist: the surface it moves on is curved like a spoon, so the block ROTATES as it drops, leaving a bitten crescent scar above and a block tilted backwards below — look for slumps in road embankments after wet winters.

Gear five: ROCK FALL — no sliding at all, just free-fall. Frost levers a block off a cliff and it drops, bounces and shatters, which is why mountain passes hang steel nets above the road. And gear six, the killer: the MUD FLOW. Soak soil past all limits and it stops being ground and becomes a fluid — a river of porridge and boulders doing highway speeds down a valley, then setting like concrete around whatever it caught. Slow and dry at gear one, fast and fluid at gear six: speed up, water up. Recite the ladder — creep, solifluction, landslide, slump, rock fall, mud flow — and every photograph in the exam sorts itself.

## Subtopic: April 2022 and How to Fight Back

Now the case study every South African answer should carry. April 2022, Durban and the KwaZulu-Natal coast: over 300 millimetres of rain in 24 hours — months of rain in a day. The hills of the metro, steep and soaked, failed in hundreds of places in one night. Landslides and mud flows cut through suburbs and informal settlements, more than 400 people died, thousands of homes vanished, and roads, rail and water systems took billions of rands of damage. And the tragedy had a geography: the poorest households, pushed onto the steepest, least stable land, with cut platforms, no drainage and no engineering, stood exactly where the hills gave way. Same rain, unequal risk — write that sentence and you have understood the disaster, not just described it.

Fighting back is four moves, and each is easy to picture. Move one, ENGINEER: shave steep cuts gentler, step long slopes into terraces, and hold slope toes with retaining walls and gabions — those wire cages of rock you see beside highways. Chapman's Peak Drive is the full showroom: nets on the cliff, anchored mesh, and a half-tunnel that lets falling rock fly over the road into the sea. Move two, DRAIN: water causes most failures, so catch runoff above the slope, bleed water out of it with buried drains, fix leaking pipes, and never point a stormwater outlet at a slope face. Move three, PLANT: roots are free engineering — grass and trees stitch the soil, drink its water and break the rain's fall; re-green every burnt or cleared slope fast. Move four, PLAN: use the GIS from last lesson to overlay slope, soil and rainfall and map the danger zones; keep homes, schools and clinics off them; set rain gauges to trigger evacuations — as Durban now does — and teach communities the warning signs: fresh cracks, leaning poles, doors that suddenly jam, water seeping from a hillside that was dry.
