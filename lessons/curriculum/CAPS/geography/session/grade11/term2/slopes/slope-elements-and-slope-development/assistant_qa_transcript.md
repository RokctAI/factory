**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the diagram labels keep tangling because every element seems to have three names — scarp, cliff, free face; talus, scree, debris slope. Which names does the marker actually want?

**Tutor:** The memorandum accepts the synonyms, but the safest habit is to lead with the CAPS term and put one alternative in reserve — and, more importantly, to understand why the names double up, because that understanding is itself examinable. The steep bare-rock element is the CLIFF; scarp emphasises that it truncates the rock structure, and FREE FACE describes its behaviour — rock loosened from it falls free immediately, leaving the face bare and active. If a question asks why it is called the free face, that behavioural definition is the answer, not the synonym list. The straight slope below is the TALUS or DEBRIS SLOPE; scree strictly refers to the loose angular fragments themselves, while talus names the slope they build — a distinction worth one precise sentence if the question probes it. The top and bottom elements are stable: CREST for the convex summit, PEDIMENT for the concave rock apron at the base. So label a diagram: crest, cliff, talus slope, pediment — and if you have time, add the parenthetical alternatives in your written answer as words, for instance by writing that the cliff is also known as the free face because material weathered from it falls away at once. Four elements, one leading name each, one behaviour-based explanation held ready.

***

**Bianca:** Next: why is the talus slope always straight? The cliff is vertical and the pediment curves, so a straight line in the middle feels too neat to be natural. Where does the perfectly constant angle come from?

**Tutor:** It comes from a genuine piece of physics, and once you see it you can demonstrate it on any heap of sand. Loose fragments — no cement, no roots binding them — can stand no steeper than the angle at which friction between the particles balances gravity. That threshold is the ANGLE OF REPOSE, and for broken rock it sits between roughly 25 and 35 degrees, depending on how large and angular the fragments are. Now watch the slope regulate itself. The cliff above keeps dropping fresh debris onto the pile. Wherever the pile locally steepens beyond the repose angle, the surface layer simply slides — small avalanches of stone — until the slope is back at the threshold. Wherever it is gentler, new debris accumulates until the threshold is reached. The slope is therefore constantly corrected toward one uniform angle along its whole length, and a surface held at one constant angle IS a straight line — a rectilinear profile. That is why every healthy talus from the Drakensberg to a mine dump shows the same clean straight ramp: the mine dump is the proof, because tipped waste rock, poured with no design at all, settles at exactly the same kind of angle. One extra observational detail earns refinement marks: the biggest blocks roll furthest and rest near the bottom of the talus while finer material stays high, so the slope sorts its own material by size.

**Bianca:** Third: parallel retreat says the slope keeps its exact shape forever while moving backwards. That sounds too tidy — surely slopes must wear down and flatten eventually? Which story is actually true?

**Tutor:** Both are true, in different places — and knowing WHICH conditions produce which story is precisely the evaluation skill the top questions test. The declining-slope account fits humid environments with deep soils and thick vegetation: there, weathering attacks the whole profile under its soil blanket, creep moves material on every part of the slope, no bare rock face survives, and the profile as a whole lowers and rounds — down wasting, convex-concave hills, the smooth green landscapes of well-watered country. The parallel-retreat account fits South Africa's conditions almost perfectly, for three reasons. First, resistant caprock: a dolerite sill or quartzite band holds the cliff element rigid, so the profile cannot round from the top. Second, semi-arid climate: sparse vegetation and episodic violent rain strip loose material efficiently from the slope base, keeping the lower elements clean rather than letting debris bury and soften the cliff. Third, the evidence of the landscapes themselves: the Karoo's profiles stand sharp on erosion surfaces tens of millions of years old, and Lester King read parallel retreat straight off them. So structure the answer as conditions and consequences: deep soil and humidity favour decline; caprock, aridity and sparse cover favour retreat; South Africa has the second set, therefore our slopes walk backward rather than slumping. Naming both models and then choosing with reasons is what turns a description into a distinction-level argument.

**Bianca:** Last one: the GIS section feels bolted on — rocks and pixels in one paper. How do we bring resolution and raster-versus-vector into a slopes answer without it sounding like two different subjects?

**Tutor:** Bind them with one scenario and the join disappears — and scenarios are exactly how the exam asks GIS anyway. Take a real task: the provincial agriculture department must find every slope in a district too steep to plough safely, because ploughing steep land invites the soil erosion and mass movement problems this term keeps meeting. Walk the data through the system. Start with a satellite image: SPATIAL resolution decides whether a 10-metre pixel can even see the gully networks and small fields involved, and SPECTRAL resolution — the infrared bands — separates bare eroding soil from healthy veld, mapping the damage. Then the elevation data: a digital elevation model is RASTER — a grid in which every cell carries a height — because altitude varies continuously, and from that grid the GIS computes a slope-angle map for the whole district in seconds. Then the human features: farm boundaries as AREA polygons, roads and rivers as LINES, boreholes as POINTS — vector, because these are discrete objects with edges — each carrying ATTRIBUTE tables of owner, surface, yield. Finally the overlay, which is the whole point of a GIS: slope grid over soil type over land use, and the output is a map of high-risk fields, each one identifiable by its attributes. Tell that single story and you have used spatial and spectral resolution, raster, vector, all four data types and overlay analysis — every GIS term in the syllabus — inside one slopes answer that sounds like one subject, because it is.
