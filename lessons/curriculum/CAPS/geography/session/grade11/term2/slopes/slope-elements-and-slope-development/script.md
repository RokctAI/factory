# Part 1 — Expert

Every landform of this term — mesa, cuesta, dome — has been described by its slopes, and this session studies the slope itself as the unit of landscape. Four pieces: South Africa's topography as the stage on which all our slopes stand; the types of slopes and the four classic slope elements from crest to pediment; the characteristics of each element and the concept of slope retreat that links them; and the GIS toolkit — resolution, data types, raster and vector — through which modern geographers capture it all.

## Subtopic: South Africa's Topography — the Stage

South Africa's surface organises itself into three great units. The INTERIOR PLATEAU fills the heart of the country: a high, largely flat erosion surface standing mostly between 1 200 and 1 800 metres, taking in the Highveld, the Free State plains and the Karoo basin. Around it runs the GREAT ESCARPMENT, the near-continuous rim where the plateau breaks off toward the coast — reaching its climax in the KwaZulu-Natal Drakensberg above 3 000 metres, and continuing as the Nuweveldberge, Roggeveldberge and the escarpments of Mpumalanga and Limpopo. Between escarpment and sea lies the MARGINAL ZONE or coastal belt: the folded Cape ranges in the south-west, the terraced Great Karoo margins, and the subtropical lowlands of the east coast.

This three-part build explains why slopes dominate South African geography. The plateau supplies gentle surfaces; the escarpment supplies the steepest slopes in the country, dropping one to two kilometres in a few horizontal kilometres; and the marginal zone is a staircase of terraces and ranges between the two. Rivers plunging off the plateau cut ravines and waterfalls — the Tugela leaps nearly 950 metres off the Drakensberg rim in one of the world's tallest falls — and every road from coast to interior must climb the stairs through passes.

Slopes are classified first by shape. A CONVEX slope bulges outward, steepening downhill, like the shoulder of a dome. A CONCAVE slope hollows inward, gentlest at the bottom, like the inside of a saucer. A RECTILINEAR or straight slope holds one constant angle, like a plank. Most real hillsides are COMPOUND — combinations stacked downhill, and in South Africa overwhelmingly in one particular order, which is the next section's subject.

Take this section's questions now: plateau, escarpment and marginal zone placed and characterised, the Drakensberg named as the escarpment's climax, and the four slope shapes distinguished.

## Subtopic: The Four Slope Elements

Cut a section through a classic South African hillside — a Karoo koppie, a Drakensberg spur — and four elements appear in fixed order from the top down. This four-part profile is the examination's favourite diagram, described here from summit to plain.

At the top, the CREST, also called the summit or waxing slope: gently CONVEX, mostly bare of loose material or thinly covered, where weathering slowly rounds the hilltop. Below it, wherever a resistant rock band outcrops, comes the CLIFF — the scarp or FREE FACE: the steepest element, often near-vertical, made of exposed bare bedrock. It is called the free face because rock freed from it by weathering falls away immediately rather than lying where it was loosened; the cliff is the hillside's active supplier of debris.

That debris lands on the third element: the TALUS or DEBRIS SLOPE, also called scree. It is rectilinear — straight — because loose rock settles at its angle of repose, the steepest angle at which fragments can rest, generally between about 25 and 35 degrees. Coarser blocks come to rest higher, finer material lower. The talus is a slope of TRANSPORT: material pauses here on its journey from cliff to plain.

At the base lies the PEDIMENT: a gently CONCAVE surface, usually under about 5 degrees, cut across bedrock and thinly veneered with sediment, sweeping away from the hill to merge with the plain or the next drainage line. Water leaving the talus spreads across the pediment as sheetwash, ferrying fines outward. Learn the four as a chant with their shapes attached: crest convex, cliff vertical, talus straight, pediment concave. Not every slope carries all four — no resistant band, no cliff, as on the smooth hills of soft-rock country — but where the full set occurs, that is the order, and no other.

This section's questions are ready: the four elements named in order with their shapes, the free face explained, and the angle of repose attached to the talus.

## Subtopic: Slope Retreat — How the Profile Moves

The four elements are not just a static picture; they are a machine, and the machine's motion is SLOPE RETREAT. Weathering attacks the cliff; the cliff sheds rock and retreats backward; the talus below receives the debris; and the pediment extends itself at the retreating slope's expense. Crucially, in the classic South African account — Lester King's parallel retreat, met already in the Karoo lesson — the slope keeps its PROPORTIONS as it retreats: the cliff stays steep, the talus holds its angle of repose, the pediment stays gentle; the whole profile simply relocates backward, leaving a wider pediment behind.

Follow the consequences over deep time. As opposing slopes retreat away from each other, uplands are consumed from their edges, pediments from neighbouring valleys meet, and the merged surface becomes a PEDIPLAIN — with the isolated survivors standing as koppies and inselbergs. This is why the Karoo looks the way it does, and why the terminology of this session and the horizontal-strata session interlock: mesa and butte are stages of slope retreat wearing a cap; pediment and pediplain are what retreat leaves behind.

Contrast the alternative account for balance. In humid regions with deep soils, many geomorphologists describe slopes DECLINING through time — the profile as a whole flattening and rounding, cliff elements decaying into convex-concave curves — down wasting rather than back wasting. South Africa's semi-arid climates, resistant caprocks and sparse vegetation favour parallel retreat, which is why our landscapes keep sharp profiles for tens of millions of years. An answer that names both models, then explains why the local conditions choose retreat, is a distinction-level answer.

Slopes also matter to people, and the exam asks it directly: settlement, ploughing and roads keep to gentle crests and pediments; the cliff and steep talus zones carry rockfall and erosion risk; overgrazing on fragile pediments strips the veneer and gullies the surface; and building at the foot of an active talus invites what the next lesson calls mass movement.

Work through the questions for this section: the retreat cycle traced element by element, parallel retreat contrasted with decline, and pediplain and inselberg tied into the story.

## Subtopic: GIS — Resolution, Data Types, Raster and Vector

CAPS pairs this topic with the geographer's digital toolkit, because slopes today are measured and mapped by computer. A Geographic Information System stores the world as LAYERS — relief, rivers, roads, land use — that can be overlaid and analysed together.

Two kinds of RESOLUTION govern what a satellite image can show. SPATIAL RESOLUTION is the size of the ground area represented by one pixel: an image of 10-metre resolution shows objects down to roughly 10 metres across — fields and roads but not cars. The finer the resolution, the smaller the detail. SPECTRAL RESOLUTION is the number and narrowness of the wavelength bands a sensor records — visible light, near-infrared, thermal and beyond. More, narrower bands let analysts tell apart healthy veld, drought-stressed crops, bare soil and water, because each surface reflects the bands differently.

GIS data itself comes in two structures. VECTOR data builds the world from geometry: POINTS for objects located by a single position — a borehole, a trig beacon; LINES for linear features — rivers, roads, contours; and AREAS or polygons for features with extent — a farm, a dam, a drought-declared district. Attached to each object is ATTRIBUTE data, the table of facts about it: the borehole's depth and yield, the road's surface, the district's rainfall. RASTER data instead lays a grid of equal cells over the land and gives every cell a value — elevation in a digital elevation model, reflectance in a satellite image. Raster suits continuously varying phenomena like altitude and rainfall; vector suits discrete objects with edges.

Put the toolkit onto this session's subject and it earns its keep: a digital elevation model — raster — yields a slope-angle map at the press of a button; contours and rivers are vector lines over it; and an analyst overlaying slope, soil and rainfall layers can map every hillside too steep to plough or ripe for erosion, which is exactly how provincial agriculture departments target soil conservation.

The final questions of this part are in front of you: spatial against spectral resolution, point, line, area and attribute data sorted, and raster against vector with the right use for each.

## Subtopic: The Country Is a Three-Step Staircase

Model South Africa in your kitchen: put a thick book flat on the table — that is the interior plateau, high and flat, Johannesburg on top at about 1 700 metres. The book's edges — that sudden drop on every side — are the Great Escarpment, and where the drop is highest and sharpest, above 3 000 metres, you are looking at the Drakensberg. The strip of table between book and table-edge is the coastal zone, wrinkled in the south-west where the Cape folded mountains crumple it. Plateau, edge, coast: every South African journey inland climbs this staircase, and every river leaving the plateau must jump it — the Tugela does so in a single 950-metre plunge.

Now zoom from the country to a single hillside, and learn the four shapes slopes can take by feeling them. A convex slope is the top of your shoulder — it starts gentle and steepens as it rolls over. A concave slope is the inside of a soup spoon — steep rim, flattening to the bottom. A rectilinear slope is a plank leaned against a wall — one angle all the way. And a compound slope is what real hills do: several shapes stacked in sequence downhill.

South African hills stack them in one famous order, and you have seen it a thousand times without naming it. Next long drive, watch any Karoo koppie: a rounded top, then a rock cliff, then a straight ramp of broken stone, then a long gentle apron running out to the plain. Four shapes, top to bottom, every time. Naming those four is the next section — and a guaranteed exam diagram.

Well done — this section's questions are with you: the book on the table, the staircase, and the four shapes felt on shoulder, spoon and plank.

## Subtopic: Crest, Cliff, Chute and Apron

Give the four elements everyday jobs and they never scramble again. The CREST is the hill's rounded scalp — convex, smooth, slowly balding as weathering loosens its grains. The CLIFF is the face of bare rock below it — the steepest part, and the hill's factory: this is where solid rock is broken off and dropped. Geographers call it the free face because whatever breaks free falls at once — nothing stays to soften it, so it stays sheer.

Below the factory hangs the TALUS — the rubble chute. Every block the cliff drops lands here, and the pile organises itself at the steepest angle loose stone can hold, roughly 25 to 35 degrees, the same angle a pile of builder's sand settles at no matter how you pour it. That is the angle of repose, and it is why the talus is dead straight: tip more rubble on a full chute and it slides until the slope is back at its resting angle. Big blocks stop high up; small stones trickle to the bottom.

Last comes the PEDIMENT — the apron. A gently curved rock floor, under about 5 degrees, sweeping from the foot of the rubble out to the flat plain. Rainwater fans across it in sheets, carrying the finest grit away. So the hillside is a production line: the scalp weathers, the factory face drops rock, the chute carries it down, the apron spreads it out. Say the chant — crest convex, cliff vertical, talus straight, pediment concave — and the diagram labels itself. One warning for the exam: a hill without a hard rock band has no factory, so no cliff and no chute — just scalp and apron in a smooth curve. The full four-part profile needs resistant rock to make the cliff.

Great progress — your questions for this part are ready: the four jobs, the sand-pile angle, and which element goes missing on soft-rock hills.

## Subtopic: The Hill That Walks and the Map Made of Lego

Here is the strangest fact in the topic: the hillside production line makes the hill WALK. The cliff face drops rock and, in doing so, retreats a fraction backward. The chute follows its factory. The apron extends forward into the space they vacated. And — this is the exam point — the profile does not slump or soften as it goes: cliff stays sheer, chute stays at its resting angle, apron stays gentle. The whole staircase simply shifts backward through the landscape, like a stage set on rollers. That is parallel slope retreat. Run it for fifty million years: hills walk away from each other, their aprons meet and merge into one vast floor — the pediplain — and the last un-eaten remnants stand on it as koppies. The Karoo, again: the flat is the merged aprons, and every flat-topped hill is a piece of old plateau still walking.

Now for the digital half, because CAPS wants you fluent in the tools that map all this. Every satellite image is a mosaic of pixels, and SPATIAL resolution is simply the size of the ground each pixel covers. Zoom a phone photo until it blocks up: a 10-metre pixel sees your school but not your bakkie. SPECTRAL resolution is how many colours beyond colours the sensor sees — infrared bands that make sick crops glow differently from healthy veld, which is how drought maps are made.

Then the two ways a computer stores a map — and think Lego against graph paper. VECTOR is Lego: crisp separate objects — a POINT for a borehole, a LINE for a river or road, an AREA for a farm or district — each with a table of ATTRIBUTE facts clipped to it, like the borehole's depth or the district's rainfall. RASTER is graph paper: a grid laid over the land, every square holding one value — height, rainfall, brightness. Sharp-edged things want Lego; smoothly varying things like altitude want graph paper. And the payoff for slopes: feed a raster elevation grid into the GIS, and it calculates the steepness of every hillside in the province in seconds — printing exactly the map a soil conservation officer, a road engineer or a farm planner needs.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — the walking hill, the merged aprons, pixels and their sizes, and Lego against graph paper. Land these and both the oldest landscapes and the newest tools in the syllabus belong to you.
