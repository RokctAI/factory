**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: the free face. Everyone can point at the cliff in the diagram, but the name itself keeps costing marks — what exactly is free about it, and what should a definition-answer contain?

**Tutor:** The freedom belongs to the rock that leaves, and a full answer captures that in three linked statements. Statement one, position and material: the free face is the steepest of the four slope elements, an outcrop of bare, solid bedrock standing wherever a resistant band crops out below the crest — no soil, no debris blanket. Statement two, the name: it is called free because whatever weathering loosens from it — a frost-wedged block, a slab split along a joint — falls away IMMEDIATELY under gravity. The face cannot accumulate a protective cover of its own rubble; everything it produces is delivered instantly to the slope below. Statement three, the consequence, which is where the marks concentrate: because nothing ever lies on it to blunt it, the face stays sheer and remains the hillside's active supplier — the element that manufactures the debris the talus receives. Contrast makes it vivid: the talus below is built OF loose material and so must obey the angle of repose, while the free face is built of solid rock and so can stand near-vertical. If you write bare bedrock, instant loss of loosened rock, stays sheer and supplies the talus — in that order — the definition is complete and the logic of the whole four-element profile comes free with it.

**Bianca:** Next one: why is the talus so wonderfully straight? Every photo shows that perfect ruler-edge ramp of rubble, and nobody in class can say why nature draws a straight line there of all places.

**Tutor:** Because the talus is not carved — it is POURED, and poured granular material has only one shape available to it. Every fragment the free face drops lands on the pile below, and loose fragments can stack no steeper than their angle of repose — the maximum slope at which friction and interlock can hold them, roughly 25 to 35 degrees for coarse rock rubble. The pile regulates itself with beautiful simplicity: wherever the local slope creeps past the resting angle, the next knock or rainstorm sends fragments sliding until the angle is restored; wherever the slope is gentler than the resting angle, arriving debris steepens it again. Push the system from both sides for long enough and the whole surface converges on ONE angle — and a surface holding a single constant angle is, by definition, a straight line in profile. That is why any dumped granular material — a tipped load of gravel, grain in a silo — builds the same ruler-edged cone, and why the talus is described as rectilinear. Add the sorting detail for extra credit: large blocks, carrying more momentum, come to rest high on the slope, while finer chips filter to the base — so the ramp is graded by size as well as ruled straight. The straight line is not a coincidence; it is the physics of loose rock signing its name.

**Bianca:** Third: parallel retreat against slope decline — when a question asks us to compare them, what is the cleanest structure, and whose side is South Africa on?

**Tutor:** Compare them on three axes — what moves, what the profile looks like through time, and where each model rules — and then commit to the local verdict with reasons. Axis one, the motion: in parallel retreat the slope migrates BACKWARD while keeping its proportions — the cliff stays steep, the talus keeps its resting angle, the pediment stays gentle, and the whole staircase relocates like scenery on rollers, leaving an ever-wider pediment behind. In decline, the slope stays roughly where it is but LOSES its proportions: the summit lowers, the cliff decays, and the profile softens into a smooth convex-concave curve. Axis two, the long-term product: retreat manufactures pediplains studded with koppies and inselbergs — sharp-edged survivors on a vast flat; decline manufactures rounded, subdued hill country with no cliffs at all. Axis three, the conditions: retreat thrives where resistant caprocks hold summits rigid, climates are semi-arid, and vegetation is too sparse to anchor deep soils; decline thrives in humid regions where deep weathering and thick plant cover soften everything from above. Then the verdict: South Africa's interior — dry, capped by dolerite and hard sandstones, thinly vegetated — sits squarely in retreat territory, which is why Lester King could build the parallel-retreat model on these landscapes and why our koppies keep knife-edged profiles for tens of millions of years. Name both models, run the three axes, argue the local conditions: that structure reads like a distinction.

**Bianca:** Last one: the GIS section feels bolted on — rocks and pixels in one lesson. If the exam links them, how do we show the connection instead of answering two separate questions?

**Tutor:** Show the connection by making the slope the CLIENT and the GIS the contractor, and walk one job from data to decision. Start with the data layers: relief arrives as a raster digital elevation model — a chessboard of cells, each holding a height, the right structure for a continuously varying surface; rivers, contours, roads and farm boundaries arrive as vector — points, lines and polygons, each dragging its table of attributes. First deliverable: ask the GIS to differentiate the elevation model and it returns a SLOPE-ANGLE map of the whole district in seconds — every hillside classified from flat to precipitous, which is this lesson's four elements translated into degrees. Second deliverable, the overlay: stack the slope map with soil and rainfall layers, and the combinations light up the problems — steep plus erodible plus high rainfall marks the hillsides that must never be ploughed; a gentle pediment below an active talus marks ground to keep buildings off; a road crossing the 30-degree class marks a cutting that needs inspection before the wet season. Third, the resolution caveat that earns the final mark: the analysis is only as fine as the pixels — a 30-metre model averages away a farm track's cutting, so choose finer data for site-scale questions and coarser data for provincial planning. Data structures chosen for the phenomenon, a derived slope map, an overlay that answers a real question, and a resolution check: tell that story once and the rocks and the pixels become one subject, which is exactly the point the syllabus is making.
