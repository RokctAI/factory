**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: everyone can point at the steep side of the diagram, but when the question asks us to EXPLAIN why the scarp is steeper than the dip slope, our paragraphs keep coming back marked as incomplete. What is the complete explanation?

**Tutor:** The complete explanation compares what the two slopes are MADE of, and most answers stop before they get there. Start with the dip slope: it is not a slope cut into the rock at all — it IS the rock, the exposed upper surface of the resistant layer with its soft cover stripped away. One continuous sheet of hard material, lying at whatever angle the strata lean. Its steepness is therefore fixed by the geology: it cannot be steeper than the dip, because it is the dip made visible. Now the scarp: no protective sheet exists there. The scarp is a cut through the whole stack, and the cut exposes weak layers directly beneath the resistant edge. Those weak layers weather and wash away quickly, eating back underneath the hard band, which is left overhanging until it breaks off along its joints — so the face is kept steep, often cliffed at the very top, by continuous undermining. Write both halves and link them: gentle because it follows the protected surface of a single resistant layer at the angle of dip; steep because it cuts across the strata, where erosion of the exposed weak layers undermines the resistant edge into a cliff. Surface versus cut edge — the contrast is the whole answer.

**Bianca:** Next one: the angle boundaries keep worrying people. One book says cuestas go up to 20 degrees, another says 25, and someone found 30. If we quote the wrong number, do we lose the marks?

**Tutor:** You lose marks only if you present the numbers as laws and get the LOGIC wrong — so present them the way geographers actually hold them: as conventions marking zones on a continuous scale. Anchor the logic first, because it is untouchable: as the dip steepens, the dip slope's angle climbs closer and closer to the scarp's, so the ridge narrows and its two faces grow more equal, and at near-vertical the difference disappears entirely. That reasoning earns its marks with any figures attached. Then attach the conventional figures with a softening word — up to about 25 degrees for a cuesta, roughly 25 to 45 for a homoclinal ridge, beyond about 45 for a hogsback. The words about and roughly are not weakness; they are accuracy, because the boundaries genuinely are approximate. What must never be approximate is the ORDER — the hogsback always carries the steepest dip — and the examples: cuesta country on the sandstone flanks of the Soutpansberg, the Magaliesberg as the homoclinal showpiece, and the crest above the village of Hogsback wearing the family name. Exact logic, approximate numbers, named places — that combination reads as understanding rather than memorisation, which is precisely what earns the higher levels.

**Bianca:** Third: basins and domes. The inward-outward directions flip between them and half the class swaps them under pressure. Is there a foolproof way to get them right without a memorised list?

**Tutor:** There is, and it takes one rule plus one sketch. The rule: a dip slope always faces the direction in which its layer descends, and the scarp faces the opposite way. That rule never changes — only the geometry around it does. So in the exam, do not reach for a remembered pair; ask the single question, which way do the layers go DOWN? For a basin, sketch a quick curve sagging to a low point in the middle. Layers descend toward the centre, so dip slopes face inward — and the scarps must therefore ring the structure facing outward, like the cut rims of stacked dishes. For a dome, sketch the arch. Layers descend away from the crown on every side, so dip slopes face outward — and the scarps face inward, glaring at each other across the core that erosion has hollowed out. Ten seconds of sketching replaces a memory that flips when you are nervous. Then collect the two applications that ride along: in the basin, rain entering a tilted aquifer at the rim slides down the dip and gathers under pressure beneath the centre, which is why artesian boreholes there can flow unaided; and for the dome, cite the Vredefort Dome, where an ancient impact arched the strata and erosion has carved the upturned layers into concentric rings — recognised as a World Heritage Site.

**Bianca:** Last one: if a mapwork question hands us an unnamed ridge on the contour sheet, what is the quickest reliable routine for classifying it and stating the dip direction?

**Tutor:** Run a three-move routine and write each move down, because the method itself carries marks. Move one, LOOK: sweep your eye across the ridge at right angles to the crest and compare contour spacing on the two flanks. Packed on one side, spacious on the other: an asymmetrical ridge, so cuesta or homoclinal. Packed tight on both sides of a thin crest: hogsback. Ridges in concentric rings: basin or dome, then apply the sketch rule to decide which. Move two, CALCULATE: put numbers on the asymmetry with a gradient per side between the same two heights. Take a crest at 900 metres with the 750-metre contour lying 300 metres away to the east but 3 000 metres away to the west: east gives 150 over 300, which is 1 in 2; west gives 150 over 3 000, which is 1 in 20. Show both calculations in full — the working is where the marks live. Move three, CONCLUDE: the steep side is the scarp, the gentle side is the dip slope, and the strata dip toward the gentle side — westward in that example. How unequal the two figures are settles the class: strongly unequal, cuesta or homoclinal ridge; nearly equal and both steep, hogsback. If the question then asks for interpretation, read the human evidence off the same sheet: cultivated land on the dip side, the road and town in the parallel valley, and the crossing squeezed through the poort where the river breaks the crest — the way road and dam share the river's gap at Olifantsnek. Look, calculate, conclude — the ridge cannot hide from that routine.
