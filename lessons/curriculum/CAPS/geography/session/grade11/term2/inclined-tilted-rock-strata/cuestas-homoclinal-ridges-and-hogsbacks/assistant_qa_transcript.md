**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: on the diagram everyone can point at the steep side and the gentle side, but in the exam they ask WHY the scarp slope is steeper than the dip slope, and our answers keep coming back half-marked. What is the full reason?

**Tutor:** The full reason is about what each slope is made of, not just how it leans — and that is the half most answers leave out. The dip slope is the exposed upper SURFACE of the resistant layer itself: erosion has stripped the soft cover off it, and what remains is one continuous sheet of hard rock lying at whatever angle the strata dip. Its gradient is therefore set by the geology — it can be no steeper than the dip, because it IS the dip. The scarp slope has no such sheet protecting it. It is a cut edge, a cross-section through the whole stack: the resistant layer truncated at the top, soft layers exposed beneath it. The soft layers weather and wash out quickly, undermining the hard band above, which breaks away along its joints and holds a near-vertical face — exactly the cap-and-undermine mechanism from the horizontal-strata lesson, tilted. So write both halves: the dip slope is gentle because it follows the protected surface of one resistant layer at the angle of dip; the scarp slope is steep because it cuts across the strata, where rapid erosion of the exposed soft layers undermines the resistant band into a cliff. Surface versus cut edge — that contrast is the mark.

***

**Bianca:** Next one: the dip-angle boundaries — 25 degrees, 45 degrees — do we lose marks if we treat them as exact? One textbook says 20, another says 30, and the class is confused about what to write.

**Tutor:** The examiners know the textbooks disagree, and the memorandum is written to reward the PRINCIPLE with the numbers as approximations. Present it exactly that way. The principle: as dip increases, the ridge narrows and its two slopes become more equal, because a steeper dip slope grows closer and closer in angle to the scarp until the asymmetry disappears entirely. The conventional bands: dips up to ROUGHLY 25 degrees give a cuesta, clearly lopsided, dip slope gentle enough for farming and roads; dips of roughly 25 to 45 give a homoclinal ridge, still asymmetrical but sharper and narrower; beyond roughly 45 degrees, a hogsback, narrow and near-symmetrical because both faces now cut steeply across the strata. Use the words about, roughly or approximately with every figure, and you are safe with any marker. What you must NOT be vague about is the ORDER and the reasoning — a hogsback is always the steepest-dipping of the three, and the loss of asymmetry always has the same cause. And anchor each with its example: any Karoo-edge cuesta country, the Magaliesberg for the homoclinal ridge, the ridge above the village of Hogsback for the hogsback. Approximate numbers, exact logic, named examples: that combination cannot be marked down.

**Bianca:** Third: basins and domes wreck people in the exam because the dip and scarp directions swap. Is there a way to work them out on the spot instead of memorising inward-outward pairs that flip under pressure?

**Tutor:** Derive, never memorise — the derivation takes five seconds and cannot flip. One rule generates everything: the DIP SLOPE ALWAYS FACES THE WAY THE LAYERS LEAN DOWNWARD, and the scarp faces opposite. So the only question to ask in the exam is: which way do the layers dip? In a basin, the strata sag toward the centre — picture one soup bowl in your hands; its smooth inner surface slopes down toward the middle. Down-toward-centre means dip slopes face INWARD; therefore scarps face OUTWARD, ringing the basin like the cut rims of the stacked bowls. In a dome, the strata arch over the centre and lean down away from it — a blister; its surface slopes outward on every side. Down-away-from-centre means dip slopes face OUTWARD; therefore scarps face INWARD, staring at each other across the eroded middle. Notice you never memorised a pair — you asked where the rock leans and read off the answer. Practise the derivation twice tonight on a sketch of each, once for the Vredefort Dome and once for an artesian basin, and add the artesian bonus while you are there: in the basin, water entering the tilted aquifer at the rim runs down the dip to the centre and sits under pressure, which is why the centre boreholes can flow without pumps.

**Bianca:** Last one: for the mapwork, if the exam gives us an unlabelled ridge on a 1:50 000 sheet, what is the fastest reliable routine to identify which type it is and which way the rock dips?

**Tutor:** Use a three-step routine, and write each step down as you do it, because the steps themselves carry method marks. Step one: SPACING. Run your eye across the ridge perpendicular to its crest and compare the contour spacing on the two sides. Tightly crowded one side and clearly wider the other: an asymmetrical ridge — a cuesta or homoclinal ridge. Crowded on both sides of a narrow crest: a hogsback. Concentric rings of ridges: think basin or dome, then apply the derivation rule for which is which. Step two: NUMBERS. Confirm asymmetry with a gradient on each side between the same two contours. Say the crest area stands at 1 500 metres and the 1 300-metre contour lies 400 metres away to the north but 2 000 metres away to the south: north gives 200 over 400, 1 in 2; south gives 200 over 2 000, 1 in 10. The steep side is the scarp; the gentle side is the dip slope. Step three: CONCLUDE with direction. The strata dip toward the gentle side — in that example, southward — and the ridge classifies by how unequal the two figures are: strongly unequal, cuesta or homoclinal ridge; nearly equal and both steep, hogsback. Then, if asked for interpretation, read the human layer off the same map: cultivated lands and plantations on the dip side, the road and railway in the parallel valley, and the pass where a river cuts a poort through the crest. Spacing, numbers, direction — three steps, and the ridge has nowhere to hide.
