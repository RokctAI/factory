Hey, it's Bianca — tilted strata week, and I know the words cuesta, homoclinal and hogsback sound like a spelling test designed to hurt you. Breathe. The entire topic is one lever-arch file with a book shoved under one end.

Look at that tilted file and you have both slopes. The top cover is a smooth ramp — the dip slope, riding down the surface of one strong layer. The end of the file shows all the page-edges cut off — the scarp slope, steep because it slices across every layer. Four words hold it forever: dip follows, scarp cuts. Every diagram in the exam is labelled with those four words.

The three ridge names are just one dial. Tilt gently, up to about 25 degrees: cuesta — wheelchair ramp with a cliff at the back. Tilt to between about 25 and 45: homoclinal ridge — think Magaliesberg. Tilt past about 45 and the ramp disappears, both sides steep and spiky: hogsback. Say "about" with the numbers, keep the order right, and quote the trend: steeper dip, narrower ridge, more equal slopes.

Basins and domes are the one place people panic, so don't memorise them — derive them. The dip slope always faces the way the layers lean down. Bowls sag toward the middle: dips in, scarps out, artesian water under the centre. Blisters lean away from the middle: dips out, scarps in — that is Vredefort. Five seconds of thinking beats a memorised pair that flips under pressure.

On the map, your routine is spacing, numbers, direction: compare contour crowding on the two sides, do a quick gradient for each, and state that the rock dips toward the gentle side. Show the working; the working is where the marks sit.

One file, one dial, one derivation rule, one three-step map routine. That is the whole topic. You've got this!
