# Part 1 — Expert

The previous session read landscapes where the rock layers lie flat. Tilt those same layers — by crustal movements, by folding, by the sagging or arching of a basin — and a completely different family of landforms appears. Four pieces this session: what tilted strata are and the two unequal slopes every tilted ridge must have; the three ridge types classified by dip angle — cuesta, homoclinal ridge and hogsback; the circular arrangements called cuesta basins and cuesta domes; and the contour signatures and calculations that carry the mapwork marks.

## Subtopic: Tilted Strata, Dip Slopes and Scarp Slopes

Strata are laid down horizontally, but they rarely stay that way. Compression from moving plates folds them; uplift raises one side of a region more than the other; the weight of sediment sags a basin's centre. The result is INCLINED or TILTED strata — layers that meet the surface at an angle. The angle between a tilted layer and the horizontal is the DIP, and the direction the layer slopes down toward is the dip direction.

Give erosion a stack of tilted layers of unequal strength and differential erosion goes to work exactly as before: soft layers are excavated into valleys, resistant layers survive as ridges. But a ridge carved from tilted rock is fundamentally ASYMMETRICAL — its two sides are built by different rules.

The gentler side is the DIP SLOPE: it runs down the tilted upper surface of the resistant layer itself, so its angle matches the dip of the rock. Walk down a dip slope and the same hard layer stays underfoot the whole way. The steeper side is the SCARP SLOPE: it cuts ACROSS the edges of the strata, exposing the broken ends of layer after layer — hard band, soft band, hard band — so it is steep, often cliffed near the top where the resistant layer is truncated, with debris below. One landform, two slopes with two different relationships to the rock: the dip slope FOLLOWS a layer, the scarp slope CUTS the layers. Every mark in this topic hangs on that distinction.

Take this section's questions now: how strata come to be tilted, dip defined as the angle with the horizontal, and the dip slope and scarp slope distinguished by their relationship to the layers.

## Subtopic: Cuesta, Homoclinal Ridge and Hogsback

The three ridge types are one landform at three dip angles — tilt the layers further and the name changes.

Dip the strata gently, up to about 25 degrees, and the result is a CUESTA: a markedly asymmetrical ridge with a long, gentle dip slope you could farm or drive up, and a short, steep scarp slope facing the opposite way. Cuestas often run for tens of kilometres, because a resistant layer outcrops along a long line.

Steepen the dip to between about 25 and 45 degrees and the ridge is a HOMOCLINAL RIDGE: still clearly asymmetrical — the scarp slope is still the steeper — but the dip slope is now too steep to plough, and the ridge stands narrower and sharper. South Africa's textbook example is the MAGALIESBERG, the long quartzite ridge running past Pretoria and Rustenburg, its resistant layers dipping steeply away toward the north.

Push the dip beyond about 45 degrees — layers standing nearly on end — and asymmetry disappears: both sides now cut across strata at similar steep angles, and the resistant layer forms a narrow, jagged, near-symmetrical crest called a HOGSBACK, named for its resemblance to the bristly spine of a wild hog. The mountain scenery above the Eastern Cape village of Hogsback carries the name into the atlas.

Hold the classification as a dial: below 25 degrees, cuesta; 25 to 45, homoclinal ridge; beyond 45, hogsback. The boundaries are conventions, not laws of nature — examiners want the principle that increasing dip narrows the ridge and evens up its two slopes, with the numbers quoted as approximations. And note what all three share: a resistant layer supplying the crest, soft layers eroded into the flanking valleys, and — until the dip passes 45 — a steeper scarp side and gentler dip side.

This section's questions are ready: the three ridges placed on the dip dial, the Magaliesberg and Hogsback named, and the reason a hogsback loses its asymmetry.

## Subtopic: Cuesta Basins and Cuesta Domes

Tilted strata need not tilt in one direction only. Bend the layers into a giant saucer or a giant blister, and the cuesta principle wraps into circles.

In a CUESTA BASIN, the strata sag downward toward a centre, like nested mixing bowls. Erosion strips the soft layers and leaves the resistant ones as concentric ridges RINGING the basin. Work out the slopes: each layer dips down toward the basin's centre, so every dip slope faces INWARD toward the middle, and every scarp slope faces OUTWARD, away from the basin. Concentric cuesta rings, scarps out, dips in. Artesian water is the classic bonus: rain entering a tilted aquifer along the basin's rim flows down the dip and collects under pressure beneath the centre, so boreholes there may flow without pumping.

A CUESTA DOME is the saucer flipped: strata arched upward over a centre, like blister layers over a swelling. Erosion usually breaches the crest of the arch, gutting the soft layers from the middle, and the resistant layers again stand as concentric ridges — but the slopes reverse. Each layer dips down AWAY from the centre, so dip slopes face OUTWARD and scarp slopes face INWARD, glaring at each other across the eroded core. South Africa holds a world-famous approximation: the ring structure of the VREDEFORT DOME south-west of Johannesburg, where an ancient impact arched the strata and erosion has etched the upturned layers into concentric ridges — a World Heritage Site.

The memory anchor: in BOTH structures the dip slope always faces the way the rock dips — inward in a basin, outward on a dome — and the scarp faces the opposite way. Derive it from the geometry each time rather than memorising the pairs, and the exam sketch cannot trap you.

Work through the questions for this section: basin and dome geometry, the facing directions of dip and scarp slopes in each, and artesian water and the Vredefort Dome as the applications.

## Subtopic: Reading Tilted Country on the Map

On the 1:50 000 sheet, tilted-strata country announces itself through ASYMMETRY. A cuesta or homoclinal ridge shows contours tightly crowded along one side of the crest line — the scarp slope — and noticeably wider apart on the other — the dip slope. A hogsback shows tight crowding on BOTH sides of a narrow crest. Ranges of parallel asymmetrical ridges with straight valleys between them are the map signature of a whole tilted sequence; ring-shaped contour patterns hint at a basin or dome.

Confirm the eye's impression with GRADIENT, calculated on each side of the same ridge. Suppose the crest stands at 1 500 metres and the 1 300-metre contour lies 400 metres away on the north side but 2 000 metres away on the south. North side: 200 metres over 400 metres, a gradient of 1 in 2 — precipitous, the scarp. South side: 200 metres over 2 000 metres, 1 in 10 — a moderate climb, the dip slope. The asymmetry is now numerical, and the dip direction follows: the strata dip toward the gentle side, here the south.

A CROSS-SECTION drawn perpendicular to the crest makes the shape unmistakable: a right-angled-triangle profile, cliff on one side, ramp on the other. Choose the vertical scale before drawing and state the VERTICAL EXAGGERATION: with a vertical scale of 1 centimetre to 100 metres — 1:10 000 — against the 1:50 000 horizontal, exaggeration is 50 000 divided by 10 000, which is 5 times, a sensible figure that dramatises the asymmetry without turning the ridge into a fantasy spike.

Finally, human geography reads straight off the structure. Roads and railways follow the strike valleys between ridges; passes cross where rivers have cut poorts and kloofs through the resistant crest — the Magaliesberg's Hartbeespoort gap carries road, and dam. Dip slopes carry farms, plantations and settlements; scarp faces stay wild, holding protea veld and vulture colonies. Where a question asks why the towns stand in lines, the answer is the rock's.

The final questions of this part are in front of you: asymmetric contour spacing interpreted, the two gradients calculated and compared, and land use assigned to dip slope, scarp and valley.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same ridges with a lever-arch file, a dinner plate stack and a hedgehog — same landforms, same marks.

## Subtopic: The File That Slipped Over

Take a lever-arch file full of paper and lay it flat on the desk: that was the previous session — flat layers, flat-topped hills. Now slide a book under one end so the whole file tilts. Nothing about the pages changed — only their angle. That is tilted strata, and the angle the pages now make with the desktop is the dip.

Here is the whole topic in one look at that tilted file. Run your hand down the TOP COVER from the high end to the desk: a long, smooth, gentle ramp — that is the dip slope, riding on the surface of one strong layer all the way down. Now look at the high END of the file, where you see the edges of all the pages stacked up and cut off: a short, steep face — that is the scarp slope, slicing across the ends of the layers. Every tilted ridge on earth has exactly these two sides: a ramp that follows one layer, and a cliff that cuts through all of them. Ramp gentle, cliff steep. Say "dip follows, scarp cuts" and you can label any diagram they print.

Why does the ridge exist at all? Because the pages are not all paper — some are cardboard. Rain and rivers chew out the paper layers into valleys and leave the cardboard standing as ridges. Same differential erosion as the Karoo, just tilted.

Well done — this section's questions are with you: the tilted file, the ramp and the cliff, and which one follows the layer.

## Subtopic: Turning the Dial from Ramp to Hedgehog

Now tilt the file further, in stages, and watch the ridge change its name. Gentle tilt, up to about 25 degrees: the top cover is still an easy ramp you could cycle up, the end face short and steep. That comfortable, lopsided ridge is a CUESTA. Think of it as a wheelchair ramp with a cliff at the back.

Crank the dial to somewhere between 25 and 45 degrees. The ramp is now too steep to cycle — you would scramble up it — but the two sides are still clearly unequal, the end face still steeper. That sharper, narrower ridge is a HOMOCLINAL RIDGE, and you have seen one if you have ever driven past Pretoria: the Magaliesberg is a quartzite file tilted to exactly this setting, running across the landscape for over a hundred kilometres.

Now slam the dial past 45 degrees, standing the pages nearly upright. Something important happens: the ramp disappears. When layers stand on end, BOTH sides of the ridge are cutting across page-edges, both are steep, and the lopsidedness is gone. What remains is a narrow, spiky, symmetrical crest like the bristles along a hedgehog's back — a HOGSBACK. There is a village in the Eastern Cape named for the ridge above it.

The dial is the whole classification: soft tilt, cuesta; middle tilt, homoclinal ridge; extreme tilt, hogsback. And one sentence explains the trend for the essay: the steeper the dip, the narrower the ridge and the more equal its two slopes, until at near-vertical the difference between them vanishes.

Great progress — your questions for this part are ready: the three dial settings, the Magaliesberg placed, and why the hogsback has no gentle side.

## Subtopic: Soup Bowls, Blisters and Where the Roads Go

Layers do not always tilt one way like a file — sometimes they bend into a circle. Stack four soup bowls inside each other: every bowl slopes down toward the middle. Erode the stack from above and the rims of the strong bowls stand up as rings — a CUESTA BASIN. Check the slopes with the bowls in your hands: each bowl's smooth inside slopes toward the centre, so the gentle dip slopes face INWARD; the cut rims face outward, so the scarps glare OUT of the basin. Bonus fact worth a mark: rain soaking into a tilted layer at the basin's rim slides down inside the layer to the middle, where it sits under pressure — drill there and the water can rise up the borehole on its own. That is artesian water.

Now flip the stack upside down into a pile of blisters: every layer slopes down AWAY from the centre. Erosion pops the top of the blister and guts the middle, leaving the strong layers as rings again — a CUESTA DOME — but with the slopes reversed: dip slopes face OUTWARD, scarps face INWARD at each other across the hollowed core. South Africa's famous one is the Vredefort Dome, where an ancient impact blistered the layers and erosion has carved them into rings you can see from the air. Never memorise which way the slopes face — pick up the bowl or the blister in your head and read it off: the dip slope always faces the way the layers lean.

Last stop, the map and the people. On contours, one crowded side and one spaced side along a ridge is a cuesta or homoclinal ridge shouting its asymmetry; crowding on both sides of a thin crest is a hogsback; rings of ridges are a basin or dome. And humans obey the rock: farms and plantations climb the gentle dip slopes, roads and towns line up in the soft valleys between ridges, and traffic crosses the ranges only where a river has sawn a gap — which is why the highway, the railway and the dam all squeeze through the same poort in the Magaliesberg.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — bowls against blisters, the slopes read off each, and the map's crowded and spaced sides decoded. Get them right and every tilted ridge in the country introduces itself by name as you drive past.
