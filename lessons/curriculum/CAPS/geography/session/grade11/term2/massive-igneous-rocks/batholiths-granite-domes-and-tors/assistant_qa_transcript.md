**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: sills and dykes both turned up in the horizontal-rocks lesson AND in this one, and now the class is unsure — are they layered rocks or massive rocks? Which topic do we answer them under?

**Tutor:** Both, and understanding why clears up the whole framework. The sill and the dyke are IGNEOUS INTRUSIONS — massive rock by composition, born as magma, so they belong to this topic whenever the question asks what they ARE, how they formed, or asks you to identify intrusions on a diagram. But once a sill has been injected between horizontal Karoo strata, it becomes, functionally, one more layer in the stack — the hardest layer — and from that moment it plays the cap-rock role in the layered-landscape story: protecting mesas and buttes, forming waterfall lips and cliff bands. So the rule for the exam is to answer by FUNCTION. Question on intrusion types, concordant versus discordant, or the Bushveld Complex: treat the sill as an igneous body and describe its emplacement between strata. Question on Karoo landforms, scarp retreat or capped hills: treat the dolerite sill as the resistant layer and get on with the differential-erosion story. The dyke plays the same double game — igneous intrusion by origin, but in the landscape it behaves as a resistant wall cutting the grain of the country. One rock, two chapters, and the question's wording tells you which chapter to open.

**Bianca:** Next: laccolith and lopolith — the names are nearly identical and the class swaps them constantly in tests. Is there a hook that locks each one to its shape?

**Tutor:** Lock them with the middle syllables and with what each one does to its neighbours. LACColith — think of the arched CEILING: it is the intrusion that pushed UP, doming the overlying strata into a blister, mushroom-shaped, flat floor and arched roof. LOPolith — hear the word DROP in it: it is the intrusion that sagged DOWN, a saucer whose enormous weight bowed the strata beneath it. Then attach the anchors that turn a memory trick into geography. The lopolith's anchor is unmissable because it is ours: the Bushveld Igneous Complex north of Pretoria, the largest lopolith on earth, and the reason South Africa holds most of the world's platinum — its layered saucer edges are mined in an arc through Rustenburg and beyond. The laccolith has no equally famous local giant, so anchor it structurally: small enough to hide under one hill, concordant, and discovered as a dome-shaped bump in otherwise flat country. Final check for the exam sketch: both are concordant — they respect the layering — so if the diagram shows the body CUTTING across strata, it is neither of them; it is a dyke if it is a wall, or a batholith if it is the vast bottomless mass. Ceiling up, saucer down, both between the layers.

**Bianca:** Third: exfoliation confuses us because Grade 10 taught it as the sun heating and cooling the rock surface. Now this lesson says it comes from pressure release. Which cause do we write in Grade 11?

**Tutor:** Write both, in the right order, because they operate together and the marker wants the deeper cause leading. The primary, structural cause is PRESSURE RELEASE — unloading. The granite crystallised kilometres down, compressed by the mass of rock above it; erosion stripped that overburden over millions of years; the de-pressurised granite expanded outward, and the expansion opened curved sheet joints parallel to the ground surface. Without those joints there is nothing to peel — unloading writes the lines the mountain will later tear along. Then the surface processes exploit the joints, and this is where the Grade 10 story slots in: daily heating and cooling flexes the outer sheets, water enters the joints and expands when it freezes on cold nights or works chemically on the minerals, plant roots find the cracks — and shell after shell loosens and slides away. So structure your answer in two acts: act one, unloading creates the sheet joints; act two, temperature change and water pry the sheets off, which is the exfoliation seen at the surface. Then close with the signature consequence: because the sheets are curved, every peeling leaves a rounder surface, so the dome perfects its own shape as it erodes — the sentence that turns a description into an explanation and usually carries the final mark.

**Bianca:** Last one: if a tor forms underground first, how could anyone ever have known that? A learner in our group refuses to believe rocks were rounding off below the surface where nobody can see them.

**Tutor:** A healthy objection — and geography can answer it with visible evidence, which is what makes the two-stage theory science rather than storytelling. First piece of evidence: road cuttings and quarries through granite country routinely slice open the process in mid-operation. Look at a fresh cutting near any granite koppie and you can see rounded boulders still SITTING IN the reddish, crumbly grus, underground, never yet exposed — corestones in their packaging, caught before the stripping stage. The Cape and Limpopo granite belts show this on many roadsides. Second: the stacking itself. Tor boulders sit one upon another in neat vertical alignments, matching the original rectangular joint pattern of the rock mass; boulders that had rolled or been dumped by water would lie jumbled, not stacked in columns. The architecture of a tor is inherited from joints that can only be organised underground. Third: the material between and beneath tors is precisely the grus the theory requires — rotted granite you can crumble in your fingers, chemically decayed in a way that needs long contact with water in enclosed cracks, not open-air conditions. Deep rot demands burial; the neat stacks demand inherited joints; the road cuttings show the intermediate stage frozen in place. Present those three observations and the two-stage theory — rot first underground, strip second — stops being a claim to memorise and becomes a conclusion your learner can defend.
