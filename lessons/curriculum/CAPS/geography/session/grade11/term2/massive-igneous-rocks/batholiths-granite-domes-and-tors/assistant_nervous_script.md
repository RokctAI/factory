Hey, it's Bianca — massive igneous rocks, and suddenly there are five Greek-sounding intrusion names plus domes, tors, corestones and grus. Deep breath. The whole topic is a bakery, an onion and a box of sugar cubes.

The bakery sorts the intrusions. Sack of dough with no bottom: batholith. Bun that puffed the ceiling up: laccolith. Heavy saucer that sagged its floor: lopolith — and ours is the Bushveld Complex, the biggest on earth, full of platinum. Dough up a crack: dyke, a wall. Dough between layers: sill, a shelf. Then one test sorts them for any diagram: lies WITH the layers, concordant; CUTS the layers, discordant. Practise saying the five shapes once and the identification question is banked.

The onion is the dome. Granite squashed under kilometres of rock gets unloaded when erosion strips the cover; it swells, cracks in curved shells parallel to the surface, and the shells peel — exfoliation. Key sentence, worth a mark every time: each peeled shell leaves a rounder surface, so the dome polishes itself. Think Paarl Mountain shining after rain.

The sugar cubes are the tors. Buried granite is cracked into blocks; water creeps along the joints and rots each block from the corners in — corners get hit from three sides, so everything rounds off into corestones sitting in crumbly grus. Then erosion strips the grus and the survivors stand stacked. Order matters: rot first, underground; strip second. And the finishing rule: wide joint spacing makes domes, close spacing makes tors — same granite, two landforms.

Bakery, onion, sugar cubes, and the concordant-cuts test. Four small pictures and the entire topic is in your pocket. You've got this!
