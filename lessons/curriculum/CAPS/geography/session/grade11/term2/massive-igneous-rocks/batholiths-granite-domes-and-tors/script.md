# Part 1 — Expert

Layered rocks, flat or tilted, are only part of South Africa's foundation. Vast bodies of the country are built instead of MASSIVE igneous rock — molten magma that pushed up into the crust, cooled underground into structureless masses, and now stands exposed as some of the most distinctive scenery in the country. Four pieces this session: the intrusion family from batholith to sill, identified by shape and relationship to the surrounding layers; how erosion converts each intrusion into a surface landform; granite domes and the exfoliation that peels them; and tors, the balanced boulder stacks born underground.

## Subtopic: The Intrusion Family — Batholith to Sill

When magma rises into the crust but fails to reach the surface, it cools slowly underground into INTRUSIVE igneous rock — typically granite or dolerite, coarse-grained because slow cooling grows large crystals. Intrusions are classified by SIZE, SHAPE and, critically, their relationship to the layers of the surrounding rock, called the country rock. An intrusion that respects the layering and squeezes BETWEEN strata is CONCORDANT; one that cuts ACROSS the layering is DISCORDANT.

The BATHOLITH is the giant of the family: an enormous, deep-seated, dome-topped mass of granite, tens to hundreds of kilometres across, discordant, with no known floor. The Cape Granite that anchors Paarl Mountain and the boulders of Boulders Beach belongs to batholiths that cooled beneath the Cape more than 500 million years ago. The LACCOLITH is a smaller pocket of the same idea, but concordant: magma injected between strata that arched the overlying layers upward into a blister, giving a mushroom shape with a flat floor and domed roof.

The LOPOLITH is the laccolith's mirror: a huge concordant, saucer-shaped intrusion whose weight sagged the strata beneath it downward. South Africa owns the world's largest — the BUSHVELD IGNEOUS COMPLEX north of Pretoria, source of most of the planet's platinum. Finally the sheets. A DYKE is a discordant wall of magma frozen in a vertical or near-vertical crack, cutting across the strata; a SILL is its concordant twin, a near-horizontal sheet squeezed between strata — the Karoo dolerite sills that cap the mesas of the previous sessions are the country's everyday example.

Take this section's questions now: concordant separated from discordant, each of the five intrusions identified by shape, and the Bushveld Complex and Karoo sills placed.

## Subtopic: From Buried Intrusion to Standing Landform

Every intrusion is born invisible. It becomes scenery through one two-step process: the cover is stripped, and the intrusion resists. Granite and dolerite are generally harder than the sedimentary country rock around them, so once erosion exposes them, differential erosion lets them stand while the surroundings waste away.

Each family member surfaces with its own signature. An exposed BATHOLITH, immense and structureless, weathers into massive rounded granite mountains and dome landscapes — the Paarl pluton's three great domes above the Berg River valley are the Western Cape's showpiece. An exposed LACCOLITH appears first as a rounded hill where the arched strata above it wear thin, then as a granite-cored dome mountain once the cover is gone. The LOPOLITH, saucer-down and enormous, rarely makes a single dramatic peak; the Bushveld Complex instead floors a broad basin-like region rimmed by higher ground — and announces itself economically, through the platinum, chrome and vanadium mines that follow its layered edges around the Pilanesberg.

The sheets produce the sharpest contrasts. A resistant DYKE, cutting vertically across softer rock, erodes into a long, narrow, wall-like ridge marching across country — or, where the dyke happens to be softer than its surroundings, into a straight trench. A resistant SILL emerges as a horizontal cliff band partway up a valley side, as a hard cap on a mesa or butte, or as the lip of a waterfall: rivers crossing a sill edge fall over it, as the Tugela does off the basalt-and-dolerite rim of the Drakensberg escarpment. On the map, dykes betray themselves as dead-straight dark ridges ignoring the drainage; sills as contour-hugging cliff lines that stay at one level.

This section's questions are ready: the exposure process in two steps, each intrusion matched to its surface landform, and dyke and sill signatures told apart on a map.

## Subtopic: Granite Domes and Exfoliation

Why do exposed granite masses form smooth, bare, rounded DOMES — Paarl Rock, the domes of the Matopos in Zimbabwe, the heads of the Cape batholiths? The answer is a weathering process that granite practically invents for itself.

Granite that crystallised kilometres down did so under the crushing PRESSURE of the rock above. As erosion strips that overburden away, the pressure on the granite drops, and the rock expands slightly — it literally unloads. The expansion cracks the granite in curved sheets parallel to the land surface, called SHEET JOINTS or unloading joints, wrapped around the mass like the layers of an onion. This is PRESSURE RELEASE, also called unloading — a physical weathering process triggered not by temperature but by the removal of weight.

Weathering then exploits the sheet joints. Water enters them; heating and cooling flex them; and the curved outer sheets loosen and peel away slab by slab, sliding off the dome's shoulders. The process is EXFOLIATION — the same word used for skin — and each shed sheet leaves a fresh, smoother, MORE rounded surface beneath. The dome therefore renews its own curvature as it wastes: exfoliation does not destroy the dome shape, it perfects it. Broken slabs collect around the base as curved plates of granite rubble.

The resulting landform — a bare, smooth, rounded granite summit rising abruptly from the plain — is an EXFOLIATION DOME, and a large isolated one standing over a pediplain is a classic BORNHARDT, a term coined in Africa. Paarl Mountain's domes, gleaming after rain, are the textbook South African case; the whalebacked granite hills of the Matopos carry the same story across the border.

Work through the questions for this section: pressure release explained as unloading, sheet joints and exfoliation connected, and the dome placed as a self-rounding landform with named examples.

## Subtopic: Tors — Boulder Stacks Born Underground

A TOR is a small, castle-like stack of rounded granite boulders perched on a hill crest or slope, looking for all the world as if giants piled them by hand. The giants were chemistry and time, and most of the work happened before the boulders ever saw daylight.

Granite masses are cut by JOINTS — sets of natural cracks, roughly vertical and horizontal, dividing the rock into rectangular blocks like brickwork. While the granite still lies buried beneath soil and weathered rock, water seeps along these joints and chemical weathering rots the rock outward from every crack. Corners of each block, attacked from three sides at once, decay fastest; edges, attacked from two, decay next; flat faces slowest. Underground, every angular block is therefore rounding into a smooth CORESTONE wrapped in rotten, crumbly granite called GRUS. Where joints are closely spaced, the blocks are small and may rot away entirely; where joints are widely spaced, large corestones survive.

Step two is exhumation. Erosion — sheetwash, rivers, creep — strips away the soft grus and exposes the survivors: piles of rounded corestones stacked in their original brickwork positions, now freestanding as a tor. The deep weathering happened in warmer, wetter periods; the stripping often in drier ones — a tor records two climates in one landform. That is the two-stage theory of tor formation, and the sequence matters for marks: joint-guided deep chemical weathering FIRST, underground; stripping and exposure SECOND.

South Africa's granite country is rich in them: the bouldered koppies of the Johannesburg Dome, the stacked rocks of the Magoebaskloof and Limpopo granites, the corestone fields around Paarl and Darling — and across the border the Matopos balance whole hillsides of them. Domes and tors often share a hill: the dome where joints were sparse, tors where they were close. One granite, two spacings, two landforms.

The final questions of this part are in front of you: joints, corestones and grus in the underground stage, exhumation in the second, and the joint-spacing rule that decides dome or tor.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same rocks with a bakery, an onion and a sugar cube experiment — same landforms, same marks.

## Subtopic: The Dough That Never Reached the Surface

Think of magma as dough rising in the earth's oven. Sometimes it erupts — that is a volcano, last year's story. This session is about the batches that never made it out: they pushed up into the crust, got stuck between and across the old rock layers, and slowly baked solid underground. Each stuck batch has a name, and the names are just shapes.

The batholith is the whole sack of dough: a monster of granite, kilometres deep, so big no one has found its bottom, shouldering aside every layer it meets. The laccolith is a bun that squeezed between two layers and puffed the ceiling upward into a blister — flat floor, domed roof. The lopolith is the opposite: a batch so heavy it sagged its floor into a saucer — and our Bushveld Complex is the biggest saucer on earth, filled with platinum instead of custard. Then the thin pours: dough forced up a vertical crack sets into a dyke, a buried wall cutting across the layers; dough forced sideways between two layers sets into a sill, a buried shelf lying with the layers.

Two words sort all five for the exam: a shape that lies WITH the layers — bun, saucer, shelf — is concordant; a shape that CUTS the layers — sack, wall — is discordant. Lies with, or cuts across. That single test answers the identification question every time.

Well done — this section's questions are with you: the five shapes, the bakery names behind them, and the concordant-discordant test.

## Subtopic: Peeling the Onion Mountain

Now bring the buried granite to the surface and watch what the sky does to it. Granite set solid under kilometres of rock pressing down on it, like a passenger squashed under a fully loaded taxi. Erosion then unloads the taxi — it strips the covering rock away, and the granite, finally free of the weight, breathes out and swells ever so slightly.

A rock that swells must crack, and granite cracks in a very particular way: in curved shells parallel to its surface, layer under layer, like an onion. Sun, night cold and rainwater then work those onion-shell cracks until the outer shell loosens and slides off in great slabs. That peeling is exfoliation — the same word the skincare adverts use, and it means exactly the same thing: shedding the outer layer to reveal a smoother one beneath.

Here is the beautiful part: every shell that peels off leaves the mountain MORE rounded than before, because curved shells can only shed into curves. So the granite does not crumble into rubble like other rocks — it polishes itself into a smooth, bald, gleaming dome. That is why Paarl Mountain's great domes shine above the winelands after rain, and why the Matopos across the border look like a pod of stone whales surfacing through the plain. Whenever you see a bare rounded rock summit, read the story backwards: buried granite, unloaded, cracked in onion shells, peeling itself rounder century after century.

Great progress — your questions for this part are ready: the squashed passenger, the onion shells, and why peeling makes the dome rounder instead of destroying it.

## Subtopic: The Sugar-Cube Trick Behind Every Tor

Those impossible boulder stacks on granite koppies — round rocks balanced three and four high, as if packed by giants? You can build the whole explanation with sugar cubes and tea.

Stack sugar cubes into a block, three by three. Now imagine tea seeping along every gap between the cubes. Each cube gets attacked from its outside inward — but not evenly. A CORNER is hit from three directions at once, an edge from two, a flat face from only one. So corners melt first, edges next, faces last — and every cube rounds itself off into a ball. Granite underground does exactly this. The rock is naturally cracked into rectangular blocks by joints; groundwater creeps along the joints for tens of thousands of years, rotting the rock from every crack inward; and each buried block rounds into a hard ball — a corestone — sitting in its own crumbly rot, called grus, like a marble in wet sand.

Step two: the great unveiling. Rain, rivers and slopewash strip away the soft grus, and the hard corestones are left behind — still stacked in the positions the joints gave them, but now standing free in the air. That is a tor: not built up by anything, but LEFT BEHIND, a stack of survivors revealed when their rotten packaging washed away. The order is the mark-earner: rot first, underground; strip second, in the open.

One last rule ties the whole granite family together. Where the joints are far apart, the blocks are huge and weathering can only polish the mass into a dome. Where the joints are close together, the blocks are small enough to round into corestones and stack into tors. Same granite, same processes — the spacing of the cracks decides whether the hill becomes a whale's back or a giant's game of stacking stones. You can see both on one koppie outside Polokwane or Paarl.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — the sugar-cube corners, the two stages in order, and the joint-spacing rule. Answer them and no pile of balanced boulders will ever look like an accident again.
