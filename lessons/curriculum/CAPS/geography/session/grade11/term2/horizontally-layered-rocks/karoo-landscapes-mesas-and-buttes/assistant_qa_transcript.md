**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: in the test half of us called a flat-topped hill a mesa and the other half called the same hill a butte, and we still argue about it. Is there an actual measurable line between them?

**Tutor:** There is, and it is a proportion, not an absolute size — which is why arguments break out when people judge by eye. The working rule the DBE uses: a MESA is wider than it is high — a broad table whose flat summit is the dominant impression; a BUTTE is higher than it is wide — a tower or stool whose small flat cap is almost an afterthought on top of steep sides. So the test is a comparison of the summit width against the height of the landform above the plain. In an exam sketch or photograph, do that comparison explicitly in your answer: state that the flat summit is clearly broader than the hill is tall, therefore mesa; or that the hill is a tall, narrow pillar with only a small flat remnant on top, therefore butte. There is one more clue that settles borderline cases: position in the sequence. A mesa is the earlier, larger fragment freshly separated from the plateau; a butte is the same body later in its life, whittled down by continued scarp retreat. If the question shows several hills together, the biggest flat-topped ones are the mesas and the small tower-like ones the buttes — and if the cap is gone entirely and the top has rounded to a point, it has left the argument altogether and become a conical hill.

**Bianca:** Next one: why do the Karoo's hills keep those sharp cliff edges for millions of years? Everything else in nature wears smooth and round — river stones, old mountains. Why not these?

**Tutor:** Because the cap changes the rules of the game. On a hill made of uniform rock, weathering attacks the whole surface at once, the summit is lowered along with everything else, and the profile does indeed soften and round with age — that is down wasting, the ice-cream-in-the-sun picture. But a Karoo hill is not uniform: it wears a plate of dolerite that weathers perhaps ten or more times slower than the shale it protects. The summit therefore cannot be lowered — the cap holds it rigid and flat. All the erosive work is forced to the one place the soft rock is exposed: the slope face below the cap's edge. Frost levers blocks off the cliff, storms gut the shale beneath it, the undermined cap breaks off in blocks, and the entire face is rebuilt a little further back at the same steep angle. The hill's only possible response to erosion is sideways — back wasting, parallel retreat — so it shrinks in plan while keeping its cliff-edged profile to the very end. That insight, incidentally, is one of South Africa's exports to world geomorphology: Lester King built the parallel-retreat model on exactly these Karoo landscapes, against the older idea that all slopes must decline and soften with age. The one-line answer: uniform rock wears down; capped rock wears back.

**Bianca:** Third: on the 1:50 000 map I can find the mesas, but the vertical exaggeration calculation keeps falling apart for us. Where does the 1:2 000 suddenly come from when the vertical scale said 1 centimetre to 20 metres?

**Tutor:** That conversion step is exactly where the marks leak, so slow it down into three moves. Move one: understand that vertical exaggeration compares two SCALES, and scales must both be written as ratios before they can be divided — you cannot divide a ratio by a sentence. The horizontal scale is already a ratio: 1:50 000. The vertical scale arrives in words — 1 centimetre represents 20 metres — and must be converted. Move two: the conversion. Both sides of a ratio must be in the SAME unit, so turn 20 metres into centimetres: 20 metres is 2 000 centimetres. The vertical scale is therefore 1:2 000. That is where the 2 000 comes from — nothing mysterious, just metres times 100. Move three: divide. Vertical exaggeration equals the horizontal scale denominator divided by the vertical scale denominator: 50 000 divided by 2 000 equals 25. The section is stretched 25 times taller than reality. Write the answer as a bare number of times — no units, because the units cancelled when you divided. And run the sanity check: the vertical denominator must come out SMALLER than the horizontal one, because a smaller denominator means a larger scale, and stretching the vertical is the entire point of drawing the section. If your exaggeration comes out below 1, you divided the wrong way around — swap and rescue the mark.

**Bianca:** Last one: do we actually need the human side — farming, tourism, all that — for a rocks topic? It feels like padding after all this hard geomorphology.

**Tutor:** It is not padding; it is a guaranteed question, because CAPS frames every landscape topic with the utilisation of that landscape by people, and an answer that stops at the rock misses waiting marks. For horizontally layered landscapes, carry a concrete set. Farming: the wide pediplains carry the Karoo's sheep and wool industry — extensive grazing suited to flat, semi-arid country — and those same open plains and strong sunshine now attract solar energy farms and the country's major astronomical observatories, which need the dry, clear air. Resources: dolerite is quarried and crushed for road-building stone, and the flat Karoo strata carry the fossil record that makes the region scientifically famous. Tourism: the Valley of Desolation outside Graaff-Reinet, the Three Sisters on the N1, the Camdeboo plains — flat-topped scenery is an economic asset that supports whole towns. Hazards and limits, for balance: cliff edges and rockfalls constrain roads and settlement, thin soils and low rainfall keep cultivation marginal, and the same flatness that suits farming makes the veld fragile to overgrazing. Structure it as opportunities and constraints, two or three of each, all named and placed — that is a full-mark human paragraph, and it takes four sentences.
