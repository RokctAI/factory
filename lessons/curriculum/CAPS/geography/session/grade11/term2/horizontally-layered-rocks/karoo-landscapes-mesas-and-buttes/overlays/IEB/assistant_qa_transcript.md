**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First up: two of us drew the same koppie on a field trip and one labelled it a mesa while the other wrote butte, and neither would back down. Is there a proper test we can apply, or is it just vibes?

**Tutor:** There is a proper test, and it rescues you from every argument like that one: it is a test of PROPORTION, never of absolute size. A mesa is broader than it is tall — the flat summit dominates the view, a table standing on the plain. A butte is taller than it is broad — a tower whose small flat crown sits almost as an afterthought on steep sides. So do not ask how big the hill is; ask how the summit width compares with the height above the plain, and say that comparison out loud in your answer: the summit is clearly wider than the hill is high, therefore mesa; or the landform is a narrow pillar carrying only a small flat remnant, therefore butte. When the case is genuinely borderline, use the life story as a tiebreaker. The mesa is the younger, larger fragment recently severed from the plateau; the butte is the same body later in life, narrowed by continued retreat of its own cliffs. Teebus and Koffiebus near Steynsburg make the perfect memory pair — two neighbouring dolerite-capped hills, one still comfortably table-like, its twin trimmed towards the tower stage. And if the flat top has vanished completely and the hill has rounded to a point, both labels are wrong: the cap is gone and you are looking at a conical hill.

***

**Bianca:** Next question: the lesson said Karoo cliffs stay steep for millions of years. But everything old is supposed to wear smooth — old mountain ranges, pebbles in a river. Why do these cliffs get to break the rule?

**Tutor:** Because the rule you are quoting silently assumes the rock is uniform, and a Karoo hill is anything but. In uniform rock, weathering lowers every surface at roughly the same rate, so summits sink, angles ease, and the whole profile rounds with age — down wasting, exactly the fate of those old smooth ranges. Now armour the summit. The dolerite cap weathers many times more slowly than the shale beneath it, so the top simply cannot be lowered: the cap holds it rigid and level. All the erosive energy is diverted to the one exposed weakness, the soft face below the cap's rim. Winter ice levers cap blocks off; summer storms scour out the shale beneath; the undermined edge collapses; and the cliff is rebuilt at its old angle, one step back. The hill therefore shrinks in plan while its profile refuses to age — back wasting instead of down wasting, retreat instead of decline. It is worth knowing that this idea has a South African signature on it: Lester King argued from these very landscapes that slopes can retreat in parallel rather than always flattening, and the Karoo remains the world's exhibit for the claim. The one-line version for your notes: uniform rock wears down; capped rock wears back.

**Bianca:** Third one: in our mapwork practice the vertical exaggeration question fell apart at the same place for nearly everyone — turning one centimetre to twenty-five metres into a ratio. Can you walk that conversion slowly?

**Tutor:** Gladly, because that single conversion is where the method stands or falls. Step one: recognise that vertical exaggeration is one scale divided by another, and you can only divide ratios — so both scales must be in ratio form before anything else happens. The horizontal scale usually arrives ready-made: 1:50 000. The vertical scale arrives as a sentence — one centimetre represents twenty-five metres — and a sentence cannot be divided. Step two: convert the sentence. A ratio needs both sides in the SAME unit, so express twenty-five metres in centimetres: twenty-five times one hundred is two thousand five hundred. The vertical scale is therefore 1:2 500. That is the whole mystery — multiply the metres by one hundred, nothing more. Step three: divide the denominators, horizontal over vertical: 50 000 divided by 2 500 equals 20, so the section is drawn twenty times taller than the real landscape. Write the answer as a plain number of times, with no unit, because the units cancelled in the division. Then apply the sanity check that catches the classic error: the vertical denominator must be the smaller of the two, since the whole purpose of the section is to stretch the vertical. If your answer comes out as a fraction below one, you divided upside down — invert it and you have the mark back.

**Bianca:** Last one: the lesson ended with sheep, telescopes and tourists. Do we really have to carry the human material into an answer about rocks, or can we stay safely in the geomorphology?

**Tutor:** Carry it, always — a landscape answer that never mentions people is only half an answer, because geography as a discipline asks how physical systems and human systems shape each other, and your final exams reward exactly that connection. The trick is to hold a small, concrete, placed set rather than vague talk about the economy. Opportunities first. The pediplains carry extensive stock farming — merino sheep and angora goats thrive on semi-arid flats that would defeat crops. The same dry, cloudless air that starves the farms feeds science: the great optical and radio astronomy sites in the Northern Cape chose the Karoo precisely for its clear skies and radio quiet. Dolerite is quarried and crushed into road stone; the horizontal strata hold a world-renowned fossil record; and the scenery itself is an export — Graaff-Reinet and Nieu-Bethesda live substantially on visitors drawn by the koppies and the Valley of Desolation. Then balance with constraints: rockfall from retreating cliff edges limits where roads and houses can go, thin soils and scarce water keep cultivation marginal, and overgrazing damages the fragile veld faster than it recovers. Give two or three opportunities and two constraints, each named and placed, and you have turned a rock question into a complete geographical answer in about five sentences.
