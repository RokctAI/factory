# Part 1 — Expert

Geomorphology begins where the weather lessons end: with the rock that all that sun, frost and rain has been working on for millions of years. This session reads landscapes built from HORIZONTAL layers of rock — the flat-lying strata that give South Africa the Karoo's tabletop hills and the basalt ramparts of the Drakensberg. Four pieces: the rocks themselves and why layers of different strength matter; the family of landforms from plateau to pointed koppie; the processes of scarp retreat and back wasting that carve them; and the canyon landscapes cut where rivers slice through the layers.

## Subtopic: Horizontal Strata and Differential Erosion

Sedimentary rocks form in layers, called STRATA, laid down one on top of another on old sea floors, lake beds and flood plains. Where the crust has stayed calm — no folding, no tilting — those layers still lie flat today, stacked like a pile of blankets. The interior of South Africa is the world's showpiece: the KAROO SUPERGROUP, thousands of metres of horizontally layered shales, mudstones and sandstones covering some two-thirds of the country.

The key to every landform in this session is that the layers differ in STRENGTH. Shale and mudstone are soft and weather quickly; sandstone is harder; and hardest of all here is DOLERITE — molten rock that squeezed between the Karoo layers about 180 million years ago and cooled into tough horizontal sheets called sills. When weathering and erosion attack a stack of unequal layers, the soft ones are stripped quickly and the hard ones survive, standing proud. This is DIFFERENTIAL EROSION, and it is the sculptor of the entire Karoo.

A resistant layer lying on top of soft layers acts as a CAP ROCK or crown rock: it shields everything beneath it like a hard hat. Wherever a dolerite sill or tough sandstone caps a hill, the hill keeps a dead-flat top and steep sides; wherever the cap has been breached, the soft rock beneath is gutted. So a landscape of horizontal strata develops its signature profile: flat skylines, step-like slopes, and isolated flat-topped hills scattered across wide plains.

One more member completes the family. Where floods of basalt LAVA poured out over the surface — as they did across Lesotho and the high Drakensberg — the stacked flows behave exactly like horizontal strata, building a BASALTIC PLATEAU whose edges retreat as towering walls. Same geometry, igneous materials.

Take this section's questions now: strata and differential erosion defined, the role of the cap rock, and the Karoo and Drakensberg placed as the South African examples.

## Subtopic: From Plateau to Pointed Koppie — the Landform Family

Now name the family, in the order erosion creates it. A PLATEAU is an extensive elevated area with a flat top, protected by a resistant cap, bounded by steep sides. The steep edge of a plateau is an ESCARPMENT — and the Great Escarpment that rings South Africa's interior, crowned by the basalt wall of the Drakensberg, is the continent-scale example.

Erosion attacks a plateau from its edges. Rivers and weathering eat into the escarpment until pieces of the plateau are cut off from the main body. A large detached fragment — still flat-topped, still capped, wider than it is high — is a MESA, from the Spanish for table. The Karoo calls them tafelbergs. Continue the attack and the mesa shrinks. When erosion has whittled it until the flat summit is small — the hill now higher than it is wide — it is a BUTTE. The Three Sisters beside the N1 near Beaufort West are the country's most photographed trio: dolerite-capped buttes standing in file on the plain.

The end of the line comes when the protective cap is finally breached and stripped. The soft layers beneath, defenceless at last, weather into a cone: a CONICAL HILL, or pointed koppie, often with a small hard summit remnant. Beyond that, the hill melts into the plain entirely. So the sequence reads: plateau, mesa, butte, conical hill, plain — one landform family at five ages, all visible in a single Karoo panorama. The slopes of every member show the same four-part profile — a flat cap edge ending in a small cliff, a straight debris slope below it, easing into the gentle plain — details the slopes topic will formalise later in the term.

These landscapes matter to people as more than scenery: the flat plains suit sheep farming and solar farms, dolerite provides crushed road stone, the mesas and valleys carry the Karoo's fossil record, and towns like Graaff-Reinet live on the tourism the koppies bring.

This section's questions are ready: plateau, mesa, butte and conical hill defined by their proportions, the Three Sisters placed, and the five-age sequence in order.

## Subtopic: Scarp Retreat and Back Wasting

How exactly does a plateau become a butte? Watch one edge — one scarp — for a few thousand years.

The cliff face at the top of the slope is attacked by weathering: frost wedges open its joints in the cold Karoo winters, heating and cooling flex it by day, and blocks tumble to the slope below. Rills and gullies claw at the soft shale beneath the cap during thunderstorms, undermining the cliff so that more capped blocks break away. The crucial observation is that the cliff does not become gentler as it erodes — it stays a cliff, keeping roughly the same steep angle, but positioned a little further BACK. The whole slope retreats parallel to itself. This is SCARP RETREAT, also called parallel retreat, and geomorphologist Lester King built the classic account of it on these very South African landscapes.

Erosion that works this way — wearing slopes back sideways while they keep their shape — is called BACK WASTING. Contrast it with DOWN WASTING, where a surface is lowered from the top, flattening and softening the whole profile like ice cream sinking in the sun. Horizontal strata with hard caps favour back wasting: the cap holds the summit rigid, so the only way for the land to yield is for its edges to march inward.

As each scarp retreats it leaves behind a gently sloping rock floor at its foot: a PEDIMENT. When scarps retreating from opposite sides consume most of the upland between them, the pediments meet and merge into a wide, nearly level erosion plain — a PEDIPLAIN — dotted with the surviving mesas, buttes and koppies. That is the Great Karoo in a single sentence: an immense pediplain studded with the flat-topped survivors of a higher, older surface.

Work through the questions for this section: scarp retreat as parallel retreat, back wasting against down wasting, and pediments merging into the pediplain.

## Subtopic: Canyons and the Mapwork of Flat Country

Give the same horizontal layers to a powerful river and the erosion runs vertical instead of lateral. A river cutting down through flat-lying strata slices a deep, steep-walled valley: a CANYON, or gorge. The walls expose the layer-cake in cross-section — hard bands standing out as vertical cliffs, soft bands weathering back into slopes — so the canyon wall descends in giant steps. The classic African example is the FISH RIVER CANYON in southern Namibia, among the largest canyons on earth; South Africa's showpieces include the Blyde River Canyon on the Mpumalanga escarpment and the Oribi Gorge in KwaZulu-Natal. Canyons deepen where uplift of the land rejuvenates the river, giving it fresh cutting power while the resistant walls hold steep.

Now the mapwork, because this topic is examined on the 1:50 000 sheet as often as in the essay. On a contour map, horizontal-strata country is unmistakable. A mesa appears as a tight cluster of closed contours with a WIDE empty summit — the flat top has no contours crossing it — ringed by contours crowded so closely they almost touch, which is the cliff. A butte is the same picture with a tiny summit area. The step-like slope shows as alternating bands of crowded and spaced contours. A canyon appears as paired walls of tightly packed contours with the river's blue line pinched between them.

Two calculations complete the toolkit. A CROSS-SECTION is drawn by laying a paper strip along a line on the map, ticking each contour it crosses, and transferring the ticks to a height grid — flat-topped hills draw themselves as tables. Its VERTICAL EXAGGERATION is the vertical scale divided by the horizontal scale: if the vertical scale is 1 centimetre to 20 metres — that is 1 over 2 000 — and the horizontal scale is 1:50 000, the exaggeration is 50 000 divided by 2 000, which is 25 times. GRADIENT is height difference over horizontal distance in the same units: 200 metres of climb over 1 000 metres of ground is 200 over 1 000, a gradient of 1 in 5 — and on these landscapes the answer announces which slope element the line has crossed.

The final questions of this part are in front of you: the canyon and its named examples, the contour signatures of mesa and butte, and vertical exaggeration and gradient each calculated in full.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same landscape with a birthday cake, a hard hat and a retreating army — same landforms, same marks.

## Subtopic: The Layer Cake with Icing

Picture the Karoo as a giant birthday cake: soft sponge layers stacked flat, and on top a thin, hard layer of icing that has set rock-solid. The sponge is the shale and mudstone — soft, crumbly, easy to carve. The icing is dolerite, old molten rock that pushed in between the layers and cooled hard as a griddle.

Rain, frost and wind are children picking at the cake, and they always eat sponge first. Wherever the icing is unbroken, the cake underneath survives with a perfectly flat top. Wherever the icing has cracked or been picked away, the children gut the sponge in no time. Hard layers protect, soft layers vanish — that is differential erosion, the only rule this whole landscape obeys.

Look at any Karoo horizon and you can see the rule at work: dead-flat skylines where the icing holds, steep drops where the sponge is being eaten, and lonely flat-topped hills standing where a patch of icing still guards its column of cake. Lesotho and the high Drakensberg are the same trick with different icing — layer upon layer of hardened lava stacked into a mighty plateau, its edge standing as the tallest wall in the country.

Well done — this section's questions are with you: the sponge, the icing, and why flat tops mean a hard cap is still on duty.

## Subtopic: Table, Stool, Traffic Cone

The landforms are just the cake at five stages of being eaten, and their names follow their shapes. Start with the whole slab: a plateau — a huge table of land with icing intact and steep edges all round. The children eat inward from the edges, and eventually a chunk gets separated from the main cake. Still flat-topped, still wider than it is tall: that is a mesa — literally the Spanish word for table. Keep eating. The table shrinks until it is taller than it is wide, like a bar stool: that is a butte. Drive the N1 near Beaufort West and three of them stand in a row beside the road — the Three Sisters, each one wearing its dolerite hard hat.

Then comes the fatal moment: the hard hat is finally picked through. With the icing gone, the sponge has no protection, and the weather rounds the hill into a traffic cone — a conical hill, the pointed koppie of every Karoo postcard. After that the cone slumps into the plain and the story ends.

So recite the five ages like a countdown: table-land, table, stool, cone, flat. Plateau, mesa, butte, conical hill, plain. One family photographed at five ages — and in the Karoo you can often see all five generations standing in the same view, which is why geographers cross the world to look at our sheep country.

Great progress — your questions for this part are ready: the shapes matched to the names, the hard hat's role, and the countdown in order.

## Subtopic: The Cliff That Marches Backwards

How does the eating actually happen? Not the way you might guess. The hills do not slowly slump into rounder, softer versions of themselves — they keep their sharp cliff edges to the very end. The cliff simply moves BACKWARDS, step by step, like a soldier retreating without ever turning around.

Here is the mechanism in one winter. Karoo nights drop below freezing; water in the cliff's cracks freezes, swells and levers off blocks, which crash onto the slope below. Summer thunderstorms then claw at the soft sponge under the cap, undermining the cliff so the next block falls. Result: the cliff face is rebuilt a metre further back, at the same steepness as before. Repeat for a million years and the whole scarp has marched kilometres backwards while staying a scarp the whole time. That is scarp retreat, and wearing a landscape back sideways like this is back wasting. The opposite — grinding a surface down from the top until everything goes low and round — is down wasting, and the hard caps make sure the Karoo mostly refuses to do it.

Every retreating cliff leaves something behind: a smooth, gently sloping rock apron at its foot, called a pediment — think of the cleared floor a retreating army leaves behind it. When cliffs retreating from opposite sides have eaten a whole upland, their aprons meet and merge into one vast plain — a pediplain — with only the last mesas and buttes still standing like abandoned watchtowers. And when a big river joins the battle it cuts straight DOWN instead of sideways, slicing a canyon whose stepped walls show every layer of the cake — the Fish River Canyon in Namibia is the giant, and the Blyde River Canyon is our own showpiece.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — the backwards-marching cliff, back wasting against down wasting, and the aprons that merge into the great plain. Answer them and you can read a hundred million years of history off any flat-topped hill you pass.
