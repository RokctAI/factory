Hey, it's Bianca — Africa's weather looks like a lot of moving parts: the ITCZ, three anticyclones, two ocean currents, synoptic charts, El Niño. Breathe. Every one of those parts is running the same two-word engine: rising air rains, sinking air doesn't.

Say that engine out loud and then hang the map on it. Equator — air converges and rises, so the Congo is wet all year. Thirty degrees — air subsides, so the Sahara, Kalahari and Namib are dry. Everything between those belts gets rain only when the rising zone, the ITCZ, migrates over it — south to us in January, north to the Sahel in July. That is three-quarters of the topic in one breath.

The South African corner is two pictures. Picture one: the Kalahari High is a lid over the plateau — clamped below the escarpment in winter, so the Highveld is dry; lifted above it in summer, so the thunderstorms come. Picture two: two buckets — the warm Agulhas water making Durban's air moist and unstable, the cold Benguela making the west coast air dry and stable. Lid and buckets. That is every "explain the climate of..." question sorted.

For the synoptic chart, protect your marks with the reading order: temperature top left, dew point below it, cloud in the circle, wind FROM the shaft, feathers for speed. Five reads, same order every time, and then say which pressure cell the station sits under. Write the order on the inside cover of your file and rehearse it on the chart in every newspaper you see this week.

One engine, one migrating rain belt, a lid and two buckets, five reads on the chart. That is the whole topic — everything else is place names. You've got this!
