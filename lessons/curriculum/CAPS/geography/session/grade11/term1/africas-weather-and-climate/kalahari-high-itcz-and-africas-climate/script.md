# Part 1 — Expert

Global circulation built the machine; this session points it at one continent. Africa sits astride the equator, so it carries the equatorial low, both subtropical high belts and two great ocean systems all at once. Four pieces assemble the continent's weather: subsidence and convergence as the master switch for rain; the Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone that drags the rainy season up and down the map; the Kalahari High and the two ocean currents that decide which South African coast is green and which is desert; and the synoptic chart and satellite image on which all of it is read.

## Subtopic: Subsidence, Convergence and the Rain Switch

Whether a place in Africa is wet or dry comes down to one question: is the air above it rising or sinking?

CONVERGENCE means surface winds flowing toward each other or toward a low-pressure cell. Converging air has nowhere to go but UP. Rising air expands in the thinner air aloft and cools adiabatically; its water vapour condenses into towering cumulus and cumulonimbus cloud, and convectional rain follows. Wherever convergence dominates, the map turns green: the Congo Basin, coastal West Africa, the summer interior of southern Africa.

SUBSIDENCE is the reverse: air sinking from aloft, spreading outward at the surface as DIVERGENCE. Sinking air is compressed and warms adiabatically, its relative humidity falls, cloud evaporates and rain becomes almost impossible. Subsidence also builds a TEMPERATURE INVERSION — a layer where temperature rises with height — which sits like a ceiling over the surface air and stops thermals from climbing high enough to make cloud. Wherever subsidence dominates, the map turns brown: the Sahara under the northern subtropical high, the Kalahari and Namib under the southern one.

So Africa's climate map is not a mystery of many causes; it is one switch thrown two ways. Equatorial latitudes sit under permanent convergence and stay wet all year. The latitudes near 30 degrees north and south sit under permanent subsidence and carry the deserts. The lands in between get their rain only when the rising side of the machine visits them — which is the next section.

The questions for this section are ready: convergence and subsidence each traced to their weather, the inversion explained as a lid, and Africa's wet and dry belts placed at their latitudes.

## Subtopic: The ITCZ — the Rain Belt That Migrates

The INTER-TROPICAL CONVERGENCE ZONE, the ITCZ, is the low-pressure trough where the south-east trade winds of our hemisphere meet the north-east trades of the northern hemisphere. Converging trades force air upward on a grand scale, so the ITCZ appears on satellite images as a broken band of massive thunderstorm cloud wrapped around the tropics.

The ITCZ is not fixed. It follows the overhead sun, lagging about a month behind it, because the trades converge on the belt of greatest surface heating. In January the overhead sun stands south of the equator, so the ITCZ lies across southern Africa — roughly over Angola, Zambia and Zimbabwe, brushing the far north of South Africa — and this is the heart of the southern summer rainy season. By July the sun and the ITCZ have moved far north of the equator, to about 15 to 20 degrees north across the Sahel, watering Senegal, Mali, Niger and northern Nigeria while southern Africa waits in dry-season sunshine.

That single migration writes the rainfall calendar of the whole continent. Places near the equator, such as the Congo Basin and coastal Kenya, are crossed by the ITCZ TWICE a year — once going north, once coming south — and record a DOUBLE RAINFALL MAXIMUM. Places nearer the tropics, such as the Sahel or Zimbabwe, are reached only once, at the belt's furthest excursion, and record a SINGLE summer maximum with a long dry winter. And because the belt only grazes their latitude, a small shortfall in its journey — a season when it stalls or arrives weakly — is the difference between harvest and drought across the Sahel and southern Africa alike.

Take this section's questions now: the ITCZ defined as converging trades, its January and July positions placed on the map, and the double and single rainfall maxima tied to the number of annual crossings.

## Subtopic: The Kalahari High and the Oceans on Either Side

Now the South African instalment. Three semi-permanent anticyclones ring the country — the South Atlantic High off the west coast, the South Indian High off the east coast, and the KALAHARI HIGH over the interior plateau. The Kalahari High is the continental branch of the subtropical high-pressure belt, and its seasonal behaviour runs our rainfall.

In WINTER the Kalahari High is strong and sits firmly over the plateau. Subsiding air builds a powerful inversion that lies BELOW the level of the escarpment, so moist ocean air cannot climb onto the plateau. The result is the classic Highveld winter: cloudless, dry, cold nights, no rain. In SUMMER intense surface heating weakens the high, the inversion lifts ABOVE the escarpment, and moist air from the Indian Ocean floods onto the plateau, feeding the afternoon thunderstorms of Gauteng, Mpumalanga and the Free State. One anticyclone breathing in and out gives the interior its dry winters and wet summers.

The oceans finish the picture. Along the west coast runs the BENGUELA CURRENT — cold water drawn up from the depths by upwelling. Air crossing cold water is chilled from below, picks up little moisture and becomes stable; it produces fog where it drifts ashore, but almost never rain. That is why the Namib Desert runs down the west coast and why Port Nolloth stays dry and cool. Along the east coast runs the warm MOZAMBIQUE-AGULHAS CURRENT system. Air crossing warm water is heated from below, evaporates moisture freely and turns unstable, ready to rise and rain. That is why Durban, at the same latitude as Port Nolloth, is humid, green and subtropical. Same latitude, opposite coasts, opposite currents, opposite climates.

Work through the questions for this section: the winter and summer behaviour of the Kalahari High with its inversion, and the Benguela and Mozambique-Agulhas currents each linked to the coast they create.

## Subtopic: Synoptic Charts, Satellites and El Niño

A SYNOPTIC WEATHER MAP is a snapshot of atmospheric conditions over a region at one moment, and the South African Weather Service chart is an examination fixture. Read it in a fixed order. First the ISOBARS, drawn at four-hectopascal intervals: closed cells labelled H are anticyclones bringing subsidence and clear weather; cells labelled L are lows bringing uplift and rain; tight isobar spacing means strong wind. Find the three highs — South Atlantic, South Indian, Kalahari — and any COLD FRONT, drawn as a line of triangles pointing in the direction of movement, sweeping toward the Cape from the south-west with rain, a wind shift and a temperature drop behind it.

Then the STATION MODEL, the small cluster of symbols at each weather station: air temperature at upper left, dew-point temperature at lower left, a circle shaded for cloud cover, and a wind shaft with feathers flying FROM the wind's direction, each full feather worth ten knots. Practise until the model reads as a sentence — Kimberley, 32 degrees, dew point 8, clear skies, light north-westerly.

Remote sensing extends the chart from space. Weather satellites deliver visible and infrared images on which the ITCZ shows as its band of cloud, a cold front as a curved cloud bar, and the clear eye of subsidence over the Kalahari as no cloud at all. A GIS then layers rainfall records, dam levels, vegetation condition and temperature over one another, letting forecasters and farmers see drought developing district by district.

One more driver deserves basic knowledge, though it is not for examination: EL NIÑO and LA NIÑA. In an El Niño year the eastern Pacific Ocean turns unusually warm and the global circulation shifts; southern Africa usually receives BELOW-average summer rain, and the 2015-16 El Niño brought one of our worst droughts. In a La Niña year the same ocean turns cool and our summers usually turn wetter, as the flooded summers of 2021 and 2022 showed.

The final questions of this part are in front of you: highs, lows, fronts and isobars read from the chart, the station model decoded, and satellite images matched to subsidence and convergence.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole continent with a pot lid, a garden sprinkler and two buckets of water — same weather, same answers.

## Subtopic: The Lid and the Chimney

Everything in this topic is one kitchen picture. A pot boiling with the lid OFF is convergence: heat from below, air rushing in and shooting up, steam towering into the room — that is cloud and rain. A pot with the lid ON is subsidence: the sinking air of a high presses down like a lid, nothing can rise, nothing can rain.

Africa is a stove with the burner set at the equator. Over the Congo Basin the burner roars and the lid is off — air converges, climbs, and it rains nearly every afternoon, all year. Near 30 degrees north and south the machine turns over: air that rose at the equator comes back DOWN, and coming-down air is a lid. Under the northern lid sits the Sahara. Under the southern one sit the Kalahari and the Namib. Wet chimney in the middle, dry lids on either side — that is the continent's climate map in one sentence.

The lid even has a name you can use for marks: a temperature inversion. Normally air gets colder as you go up; under a subsiding high there is a layer where it gets WARMER as you go up instead. Warm above cold is upside-down and totally stable — a thermal rising off the hot ground hits that warm layer, finds itself cooler and heavier than its surroundings, and stops dead. No rising, no cloud, no rain. That is why a high-pressure day is blue from horizon to horizon.

Well done — this section's questions are with you: the lid, the chimney, and the inversion that stops the pot from boiling over.

## Subtopic: The Sprinkler That Follows the Sun

Now put a garden sprinkler on the equator — a band of storms called the ITCZ, where the trade winds of the two hemispheres crash together and shove the air upward. Wherever that band sits, it is pouring.

The trick is that the sprinkler is mounted on a rail that runs north-south, and it slides along the rail chasing the overhead sun, about a month behind it. In our summer — January — it slides south, and the storms hose down Angola, Zambia, Zimbabwe and the north of our country. In July it slides far north and waters the Sahel on the southern edge of the Sahara, while our side of the continent stands dry in the winter sun.

Count how many times the sprinkler passes over your house each year and you know your rainfall calendar. Live right next to the rail's midpoint — the Congo Basin — and it passes twice, once heading north and once heading south: two rainy seasons a year. Live near the end of the rail — Zimbabwe, or the Sahel — and it barely reaches you once before turning back: one rainy season, and a long dry wait. And if one year the sprinkler stops short of your district, that is not a curiosity; that is a failed harvest. Most droughts in the tropics are simply the sprinkler not arriving.

Great progress — your questions for this part are ready: the sliding sprinkler, January and July, and why some places get two rainy seasons while others get one.

## Subtopic: Two Buckets and a Weather Map

Why is Durban green and Port Nolloth desert, when they sit at nearly the same latitude? Put your hand over two buckets — one filled with warm bath water, one with cold tap water. The warm bucket loads the air above it with steam; the cold one gives almost nothing. Now stretch those buckets into ocean currents. Up the east coast flows the warm Mozambique-Agulhas water: air crossing it loads up with moisture, turns light and eager to rise, and delivers rain to KwaZulu-Natal. Up the west coast creeps the cold Benguela: air crossing it is chilled, heavy and nearly dry — it can manage fog on the beach at Port Nolloth, but not rain. Same sun, different bucket, opposite coasts.

The interior answers to the Kalahari High, and it behaves like a lid on a hinge. In winter the lid clamps down below the level of the escarpment, sealing the plateau off from moist ocean air — that is your bone-dry, ice-blue Highveld winter. In summer the baking ground weakens the high, the lid lifts above the escarpment, Indian Ocean air pours in over the edge, and the afternoon thunderstorms return to Gauteng. The whole seasonal story of the Highveld is a lid going up and down.

Last skill: the weather map is just this lesson drawn in symbols. H means lid — sun, no rain. L means chimney — cloud and rain. Lines packed close together mean wind. A line of little triangles is a cold front charging at Cape Town, and the triangles point the way it is moving. At each town a tiny circle tells you the cloud, the number beside it the temperature, and a tail with feathers flies FROM the wind's direction. Read H, L, triangles and feathers, and tomorrow's weather is yours before the forecaster says a word. And when a news bulletin mentions El Niño, translate it: warm Pacific water, our summer rains usually shrink; La Niña, cool Pacific water, our summers usually flood.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — warm bucket against cold bucket, the lid on its hinge, and the chart read symbol by symbol. Get these right and every synoptic map in the exam becomes a story you already know.
