**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one comes up every single year: the ITCZ is a LOW-pressure belt sitting on the hottest part of the earth, and we are told lows bring rain. But the Sahara is also boiling hot and bone dry. Why does heat make rain at the equator and desert at 20 degrees north?

**Tutor:** Because heat at the surface is only half of the machine — the other half is what the air above is doing, and that is decided by the circulation, not by the thermometer. At the equator, surface heating and large-scale CONVERGENCE work together: the trade winds of both hemispheres arrive and pile into the ITCZ trough, and converging air has no exit except upward. Rising air cools, condenses its moisture and rains. Over the Sahara the opposite machinery operates: it lies under the subsiding limb of the Hadley circulation, where air that rose at the equator comes back down. That descending air is compressed and warmed, its relative humidity collapses, and it builds an inversion that caps the surface completely. So the Saharan surface can reach 45 degrees and it makes no difference — every thermal that tries to rise hits the warm lid above and stops. The examinable sentence is this: rainfall follows VERTICAL MOTION, not surface temperature. Convergence and uplift give rain; subsidence and divergence forbid it, no matter how hot the sand gets.

**Bianca:** Next one caused an argument in our study group: if the Kalahari High is strongest in winter, and highs mean sinking dry air, why is it the SUMMER interior that gets thunderstorms? Surely the weak season of a high still beats no high at all?

**Tutor:** The key is that the high never disappears in summer — what changes is the HEIGHT of its inversion relative to the escarpment, and that small vertical shift throws the whole switch. In winter the Kalahari High is strong and its subsidence inversion lies below the level of the escarpment, roughly below the height of the plateau edge itself. Moist air from the Indian Ocean simply cannot climb over the rim; it is trapped against the coast, and the plateau above sits in dry, endlessly clear air. In summer, intense heating of the plateau surface weakens the anticyclone and lifts that inversion well above the escarpment. Now the doorway is open: moist tropical air streams in over the eastern rim, the baking surface drives convection beneath a much higher ceiling, and by mid-afternoon the cumulonimbus towers are standing over Gauteng. So do not write that the high vanishes in summer — write that it weakens and its inversion RISES above the escarpment, admitting moist air, while in winter it strengthens and the inversion SINKS below the escarpment, excluding it. That precise wording, with the escarpment as the reference level, is what the memorandum rewards.

**Bianca:** Third one: Port Nolloth and Durban sit at almost the same latitude, and the exam loves asking why one is desert and one is subtropical. We always mention the currents but still lose marks. What is the full chain the markers want?

**Tutor:** The full chain has four links, and most answers stop after one. Link one: name the currents correctly — the cold Benguela Current flowing along the west coast, fed by upwelling of deep cold water, and the warm Mozambique-Agulhas system flowing down the east coast from the tropics. Link two: state what each does to the air ABOVE it. Air resting on the cold Benguela is chilled from its base, and cold air both holds little moisture and becomes stable — heavy air below, lighter air above, no tendency to rise. Air resting on the warm Agulhas water is heated from its base and loaded with evaporated moisture, making it buoyant and unstable — primed to rise. Link three: convert stability into weather. The west coast's stable air can manage only fog, when it drifts over the cold water and its vapour condenses at the surface; it cannot build vertical cloud, so the coast is arid and cool — that is the Namib's southern tail. The east coast's unstable moist air rises readily, especially where the land lifts it, and delivers the rain that keeps KwaZulu-Natal green and humid. Link four: close with the comparison — same latitude, opposite currents, opposite climates. Four links, four marks, and the answer reads like a mechanism instead of a fact.

**Bianca:** Last one: on the synoptic chart everyone mixes up the numbers on the station model — temperature, dew point, pressure. Is there a foolproof order for reading a station so we stop guessing in the exam?

**Tutor:** There is, and it works because the model is laid out the same way at every station on every chart. Read it like a clock, starting at the upper left. Upper left: AIR TEMPERATURE in degrees Celsius — the big obvious number. Directly below it, lower left: DEW-POINT temperature, always less than or equal to the air temperature; the closer the two numbers, the moister the air, and when they nearly touch, expect fog or rain. Centre: the CIRCLE, whose shading gives cloud cover — open for clear, half-shaded for partly cloudy, fully black for overcast. From the circle, the WIND SHAFT: it flies FROM the direction the wind is coming from, so a shaft extending to the north-west of the station means a north-westerly wind; then count the feathers, each full feather ten knots, each half feather five. Practise the sequence aloud on any chart: temperature, dew point, cloud, wind direction, wind speed. Five reads, always in that order. Then lift your eyes to the chart around the station and add the context — which pressure cell it sits under and whether a line of cold-front triangles is approaching from the south-west — and you can write the station's weather forecast as a full sentence, which is exactly the form the interpretation questions ask for.
