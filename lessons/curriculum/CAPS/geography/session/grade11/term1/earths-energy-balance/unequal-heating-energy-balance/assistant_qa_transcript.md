**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it caused a real argument: if the earth's tilt is 23,5 degrees and it never changes, how can the tilt cause anything to change during the year? Surely a constant thing produces a constant result?

**Tutor:** That objection is sharper than it looks, and answering it properly is where the marks are. The tilt is indeed constant — the axis leans 23,5 degrees from the vertical and keeps pointing in the same direction in space all year long. What changes is not the lean but the earth's POSITION relative to the sun as it revolves. Hold the lean fixed and walk once around a lamp, and there is necessarily one point in the circuit where the southern end of the axis leans toward the lamp, and a point exactly opposite where it leans away. At the first, sunlight arrives on the southern hemisphere from high overhead and stays up for long hours — our December. At the second, it arrives at a low slant for short hours — our June. So the tilt supplies the geometry and the revolution supplies the change; neither one alone produces seasons. If the axis stayed upright, revolution would change nothing. If the axis leaned but rolled around so it always faced the sun the same way, revolution would also change nothing. It is precisely the combination of a fixed lean and a moving position that makes the overhead sun migrate between the two tropics, and that migration is the season.

**Bianca:** Next: our group could not tell convection and advection apart in an exam. They both involve moving air. What actually separates them?

**Tutor:** One word: direction. Convection is VERTICAL transfer, advection is HORIZONTAL transfer, and that distinction is the whole answer. Convection begins at a heated surface: the air in contact with it warms, expands, becomes less dense than its surroundings and rises, while cooler denser air sinks in to replace it. That is why a Highveld summer morning can be clear and the afternoon can carry an enormous thunderhead — the whole column has been overturned by rising warm air. Advection moves heat sideways, and the air need not be rising at all. The sea breeze that pushes cool marine air into Durban is advection. The berg wind that carries hot interior air down to a coastal town is advection. The Agulhas Current sliding warm tropical water down the east coast is oceanic advection. The exam test is simple: ask what the heat is doing, up or across. If a question says "warm air rises over the heated interior", write convection. If it says "warm air is carried inland from the ocean" or "cold air moves in from the south-west behind a front", write advection. Getting the direction word right is often worth the whole mark, and mixing them up is the commonest error in this section.

**Bianca:** This one worried a lot of us: how do we know there is a surplus at the equator and a deficit at the poles? We can't measure the whole planet ourselves, so is this just something we have to trust?

**Tutor:** You do not have to trust it — you can reason your way to it, and the examiner rewards exactly that reasoning. Start with what each latitude receives and what each loses. Receiving depends on the angle of incidence, path length and albedo, all of which favour low latitudes overwhelmingly. Losing depends on temperature: every surface radiates longwave energy outward, and while cold polar surfaces radiate less than hot tropical ones, the difference in LOSS between equator and pole is far smaller than the difference in GAIN, because the long polar winter night keeps radiating heat away with no sunlight arriving at all. Put the two curves side by side and they cross at roughly 40 degrees in each hemisphere. Equatorward of that crossing, gain beats loss — surplus. Poleward of it, loss beats gain — deficit. Then comes the clinching observation you can make from ordinary records: the tropics are not becoming infinitely hot and the poles are not becoming infinitely cold, decade after decade. An uncorrected surplus and deficit would produce exactly that runaway, and it does not happen. Therefore the surplus energy must be leaving the tropics and arriving at the poles — carried by winds, by depressions, by water vapour holding latent heat, and by ocean currents. The argument is logical, not merely observational, which is why it makes such a good essay paragraph.

**Bianca:** Last one: the greenhouse effect keeps coming up, and half the class thinks it is a bad thing while the other half thinks it is what keeps us alive. Which is it?

**Tutor:** Both, and the distinction the syllabus wants is between the NATURAL greenhouse effect and the ENHANCED greenhouse effect. Start with the mechanism, because it explains both. Solar radiation arrives as shortwave, and the atmosphere is largely transparent to it, so it passes through and heats the surface. The surface, being much cooler than the sun, radiates back as longwave — and to longwave the atmosphere is not transparent at all. Water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide absorb it and re-radiate part of it downward. That trapping is the natural greenhouse effect, and it lifts the planet's average surface temperature from roughly minus 18 degrees Celsius to about plus 15. Without it, the earth would be a frozen rock and no life as we know it would exist. So the effect itself is not the problem — its intensification is. Adding greenhouse gases faster than natural processes remove them thickens the blanket, impedes the outgoing longwave further, and forces the surface to warm until outgoing energy once again balances incoming. That is the enhanced greenhouse effect. When you answer, use the balance language: the planet must always settle at whatever temperature makes energy out equal energy in, so anything that obstructs the outgoing side raises that settling temperature. Framed that way, the greenhouse question stops being a slogan and becomes physics you can defend.
