# Part 1 — Expert

Grade 10 established that the atmosphere is heated from below, by the earth's surface. This session establishes why that heating is UNEQUAL, and what the atmosphere does about it: the geometry that hands the tropics more energy per square metre than the poles, the tilt and revolution that swing that advantage north and south through the year, the processes that move heat once it arrives, and the surplus-and-deficit budget that turns the planet into a heat engine.

## Subtopic: Insolation and the Latitude Effect

INSOLATION — incoming solar radiation — reaches the top of the atmosphere at a near-constant 1 367 watts per square metre, measured at right angles to the beam. That figure is identical above the Kalahari and above Antarctica. What differs is the ANGLE at which the beam meets a curved earth.

Near the equator the midday sun stands close to overhead and the rays strike almost perpendicularly, so a bundle of radiation of a given cross-section lands on a small patch of ground and energy per square metre is high. Move poleward and the curved surface leans away: the identical bundle is smeared across a much larger, oblique patch. Same energy, more ground, less heating. Name it as the memorandum names it — the ANGLE OF INCIDENCE.

Angle carries a second penalty: PATH LENGTH. A vertical ray crosses the thinnest depth of atmosphere; an oblique polar ray slants through several times that depth, and every extra kilometre scatters and absorbs more of it. High latitudes are punished twice — spread thin, then filtered hard.

Third, ALBEDO — the percentage of insolation reflected straight back without heating anything. Fresh snow and ice reflect roughly 80 to 90 per cent; open ocean reflects under 10 per cent. Cold surfaces reject most of the little they receive while tropical oceans absorb nearly everything, so albedo widens the gap geometry has already opened — a positive feedback, and examiners like that phrasing.

Assemble the standard budget: of the insolation arriving at the top of the atmosphere, about 30 per cent is reflected to space, about 20 per cent is absorbed within the atmosphere, and roughly half is absorbed at the surface. The surface then heats the air touching it — which is why the troposphere COOLS with height, at an average lapse rate near 6,5 degrees Celsius per kilometre, and why Mafadi's summit is bitter while Ladysmith below it swelters.

Work through this section's questions now: angle of incidence, path length and albedo, and how each deepens the difference between equatorial and polar heating.

## Subtopic: The Axis, the Revolution and the Seasons

The earth's axis is tilted 23,5 degrees from the vertical — equivalently, 66,5 degrees from the plane of its orbit — and holds that lean pointing in one fixed direction all year. That constancy is PARALLELISM, and without it the tilt would produce no seasons at all.

Combine parallelism with REVOLUTION — one orbit every 365 and a quarter days — and the overhead sun migrates between 23,5 degrees north and 23,5 degrees south. About 22 December it reaches the Tropic of Capricorn: our summer solstice, longest day, sun highest. About 21 June it reaches the Tropic of Cancer: our winter solstice, shortest day, sun lowest. About 21 March and 23 September it crosses the equator: the equinoxes, day and night equal at every latitude.

Quantify it, because calculation marks live here. Midday solar altitude equals 90 degrees minus the angular distance between the place and the overhead sun. Cape Town lies at 34 degrees south. On 22 December the declination is 23,5 degrees south, so the angular distance is 34 minus 23,5, which is 10,5 degrees, and the altitude is 79,5 degrees — very nearly overhead. On 21 June the declination is 23,5 degrees north, so the angular distance is 34 plus 23,5, which is 57,5 degrees, and the altitude is 32,5 degrees. A swing of 47 degrees, and with it a fall from roughly fourteen and a half hours of daylight to under ten: a higher sun concentrating the energy, and a longer day delivering it for more hours.

One national detail worth carrying: the Tropic of Capricorn crosses Limpopo, so the far north lies inside the tropics and sees the sun pass overhead twice a year, while everywhere further south never does.

Now the trap. The orbit is slightly elliptical, and the earth is CLOSEST to the sun in early January. If distance drove seasons, January would be summer everywhere — and it is deep winter in Europe. Distance is not the cause; the tilt is.

Attend to this section's questions before moving on: tilt and parallelism, the four solstice and equinox positions, and the altitude calculation worked for a given latitude and date.

## Subtopic: How Energy Moves Through the Atmosphere

Energy that has arrived must be redistributed, and the syllabus names the mechanisms precisely.

RADIATION transfers energy as electromagnetic waves and needs no medium. Incoming solar radiation is SHORTWAVE because the sun is extremely hot; outgoing terrestrial radiation is LONGWAVE because the earth is comparatively cool. Greenhouse gases — water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane — pass the shortwave in but absorb the longwave going out and re-radiate part of it downward. That is the greenhouse effect, and it lifts the planet's mean surface temperature from roughly minus 18 degrees Celsius to about plus 15.

CONDUCTION transfers heat molecule to molecule by direct contact. Air conducts poorly, so it matters only in the lowest few centimetres, where scorching Kalahari sand heats the air resting on it. Never offer conduction to explain anything higher up.

CONVECTION transfers heat VERTICALLY within a fluid. Air warmed at the surface expands, becomes less dense and rises; cooler denser air sinks to replace it. On a Highveld summer afternoon convection lifts moist air until it condenses into towering cumulonimbus and delivers the thunderstorm Gauteng sets its clock by.

ADVECTION is the HORIZONTAL counterpart — heat carried sideways by moving air or water. The afternoon sea breeze pushing cool marine air into Durban is advection; so is a berg wind carrying hot interior air to the coast, and the warm Agulhas Current dragging tropical heat southward.

LATENT HEAT is the quiet giant. Evaporating one kilogram of water absorbs roughly 2,5 megajoules with no rise in temperature; the energy rides invisibly inside the vapour and is released wherever that vapour condenses — often a thousand kilometres inland and several kilometres up. More energy travels poleward this way than by any other atmospheric route.

The questions for this section are ready: match each process to its example, and justify why conduction is confined to the lowest layer while latent heat reaches across oceans.

## Subtopic: Surplus, Deficit and the Global Heat Engine

Now the balance sheet. At each latitude compare the shortwave energy ABSORBED with the longwave energy LOST to space. Between roughly 40 degrees north and 40 degrees south, absorption exceeds loss: a permanent SURPLUS. Poleward of about 40 degrees, loss exceeds absorption: a permanent DEFICIT. South Africa straddles that boundary — the Limpopo lowveld sits well inside surplus territory while the seas south of Cape Agulhas lean toward deficit.

Here is the decisive observation. Uncorrected, the tropics would heat without limit and the poles cool without limit. Records show nothing of the sort: the equator-to-pole difference stays broadly stable from decade to decade. Therefore energy MUST be transported poleward, continuously and on an enormous scale — the engine behind this entire term.

Two carriers share the work. The ATMOSPHERE does roughly three-quarters: the tri-cellular circulation with its rising tropical and sinking subtropical air, the mid-latitude westerlies and their depressions, the jet streams aloft, and latent heat riding inside water vapour. The OCEANS do the remaining quarter — the warm Agulhas Current sweeping tropical water down the east coast until it turns back on itself near 36 degrees south, and the cold Benguela Current drawing polar water north along the west coast. Two coasts of one country, two limbs of the same global correction.

Finally, the planetary balance. Averaged over the whole earth and over years, total energy in equals total energy out. Alter either side and temperature must shift until balance returns: raise greenhouse gas concentrations and outgoing longwave is impeded, so the surface warms. The budget is not a diagram to memorise but an accounting identity the planet must satisfy.

Turn to the closing questions of this part: locate the surplus and deficit zones by latitude, justify why poleward transfer must exist, and split that transfer between its atmospheric and oceanic carriers.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same energy story with a torch, a soccer ball and a braai — same facts, same answers, nothing left out.

## Subtopic: A Torch Against a Wall

Shine a torch straight at a wall in a dark room and you get a small, tight, blazing circle. Now tilt it so the beam hits at a slant. Same torch, same battery, same light — but it spreads into a big, stretched, dim oval. Nothing was lost; it was shared over more wall.

That is the whole equator-and-poles story. The sun shines equally on all of us. Near the equator the beam hits almost straight on and lands in a small hot circle; at the poles the earth's curve turns the wall sideways and the same beam smears into a weak oval. Hot circle, dim oval — that is why Musina roasts and Antarctica does not.

A second thing works against the poles. Think of crossing a crowded taxi rank: straight across is short and quick, but cut diagonally and you push past far more people and arrive worn out. Slanting sunlight crosses much more air, and air scatters and soaks up bits of it the whole way. Polar sunlight arrives spread thin AND already robbed.

Then colour. A black bakkie parked in the sun becomes an oven while a white one stays bearable — the white one bounces the light away. Ice and snow are the whitest surfaces on earth; the dark ocean is the black bakkie and soaks up nearly everything. Cold places stay cold partly because they are white. Geographers call that bouncing-back albedo.

Nice work — the questions for this piece are with you now: the torch angle, the crowded crossing, and the two bakkies.

## Subtopic: The Ball That Never Straightens Up

Put a soccer ball on a stick and lean it over by about 23 and a half degrees. Walk that leaning ball in a circle around a lamp, with one rule: never straighten it and never turn the lean around. That stubbornness is the reason we have seasons.

Walk to where the bottom of the ball leans TOWARD the lamp. Light now falls straight down on the southern part — our December: sun high, long days, swimming at Umhlanga. Walk to the far side of the room and the bottom leans AWAY. Same ball, same lamp, but light arrives at a slant: June, sun low, short days, frost on the Highveld.

Numbers make it real for Cape Town. In December the midday sun climbs to about 79 and a half degrees — almost overhead, so your lunchtime shadow is a stubby thing at your feet. In June it reaches only about 32 and a half degrees and the same shadow stretches long across the pavement: a 47-degree drop, plus about four and a half hours less daylight. Less strength, less time — winter.

Now kill the myth. People say winter comes because we are further from the sun. We are actually CLOSEST in early January — the middle of our summer, and of Europe's snow. If distance ran the seasons, everyone would be hot together. It is the lean, not the distance. Two halfway stops finish the picture: around 21 March and 23 September the lamp shines square on the ball's middle, and everyone gets twelve hours of day and twelve of night.

Your questions for this section are ready now — the lean that never changes, December against June, and why the distance answer loses marks.

## Subtopic: Kettle, Braai and Taxi — Moving Heat

Stand at a braai and you can feel all the ways heat travels, without touching anything.

Hold your hands toward the coals and your palms warm instantly across the gap. Nothing touches you, no wind blows — warmth crosses the space in a straight line. That is RADIATION, exactly how the sun reaches us and how the earth loses heat at night.

Now grab the metal grid handle left lying on the fire. That heat crawled along the metal to your fingers: CONDUCTION, heat passing by touch. Air is terrible at it, which is why only the very bottom layer of air, sitting on hot Kalahari sand, gets heated this way.

Watch the shimmer going straight UP off the coals. Hot air is lighter, so it climbs and cooler air slides underneath. That is CONVECTION — blown up to Highveld size, the reason a clear December morning becomes a grey thunderhead and a four o'clock downpour.

The wind picks up and carries that warm smoky air sideways across the yard into the neighbour's washing. Heat moving SIDEWAYS is ADVECTION — like cool sea air pushing into Durban in the afternoon, or warm Agulhas water sliding down the east coast.

Last one, and it is sneaky. When you sweat and a breeze passes you feel cold — because turning that water into vapour STEALS heat from your skin and carries it off invisibly. That hidden passenger is LATENT HEAT. The ocean sweats too, on a giant scale, handing the heat back in the sky wherever the vapour becomes cloud.

Finish with the big picture, and think of bread. The tropics are a spaza that gets far more bread delivered than it can eat — a surplus. The poles are a spaza that never gets enough — a deficit. Yet neither drowns nor starves, year after year, which can only mean something carries loaves from the busy shop to the empty one all day, every day. The taxis doing most of that work are the winds, about three loaves in four; the trucks doing the rest are ocean currents, and South Africa has one of each — warm water heading south past Durban, cold water heading north past Port Nolloth.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — braai heat matched to its proper name, and the surplus, the deficit and the delivery system explained in your own words. Get these and the engine behind the whole term is yours.
