**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: mountains are closer to the sun than the beaches are, yet the top of Sani Pass can be freezing while Durban swelters. If the sun does the heating, shouldn't higher places be warmer?

**Tutor:** That puzzle is the perfect test of whether the heating story has really landed, because the answer overturns the everyday assumption. The sun does not heat the air directly to any great degree — the atmosphere is largely transparent to incoming shortwave radiation, which passes through and is absorbed by the GROUND. The heated ground then warms the atmosphere from below, by radiating longwave energy upward, by conduction into the thin layer touching it, and by convection lifting that warmed air. So the atmosphere is a pot heated from the bottom, not a room heated from the ceiling. The consequence is that temperature falls as you climb away from the heat source, at an average of about 6,5 degrees Celsius per kilometre. The top of Sani Pass stands nearly three kilometres above Durban, so on an ordinary day it sits roughly eighteen to nineteen degrees colder — enough to turn a sweltering coastal afternoon into a freezing mountain one. The few hundred metres you gain toward the sun are nothing against a distance of one hundred and fifty million kilometres, but they are everything in terms of how far you have moved away from the true radiator, which is the earth's surface itself.

***

**Bianca:** Next one: the overhead sun only ever migrates between 23 and a half degrees north and 23 and a half degrees south. Why does it turn around exactly there — why can it never stand overhead in Johannesburg?

**Tutor:** The limits are set by the tilt itself, and seeing why is a lovely piece of geometry. The overhead sun is simply the one latitude where the incoming rays meet the surface at exactly ninety degrees, and that latitude is fixed by how far the axis leans the sunlit hemisphere toward the sun. The lean is 23,5 degrees and, by parallelism, it never grows or shrinks — the earth only changes its position around the orbit. At the December position the southern hemisphere is angled sunward by the full 23,5 degrees, so the perpendicular point stands at 23,5 degrees south, the Tropic of Capricorn, and it can go no further because there is no more lean available. Six months later the geometry is mirrored at the Tropic of Cancer. Every latitude between the tropics watches the perpendicular point pass overhead twice a year on its way from one turning point to the other. Johannesburg, at 26 degrees south, lies just outside the turning circle — a mere two and a half degrees too far poleward — so its midday sun climbs to 87,5 degrees in December, agonisingly close to the zenith, but never reaches it. That is also why the tropics are drawn on every world map at exactly those latitudes: they are not decoration, they are the turning points of the overhead sun, and their position is nothing more or less than the tilt written onto the globe.

**Bianca:** Third one: the atmosphere lets the sun's radiation in, but then traps the earth's radiation on the way out. How can the same air be a door in one direction and a wall in the other?

**Tutor:** Because the two streams of radiation are not the same kind of radiation, and the atmosphere is choosy about wavelength. The temperature of a body decides the wavelength it radiates at: the sun, at thousands of degrees, radiates mostly SHORTWAVE energy, while the earth's surface, at a mild average of about fifteen degrees, radiates LONGWAVE energy. Greenhouse gases — water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane — happen to be nearly transparent to shortwave but strongly absorbing at longwave. So the incoming sunlight sails through the open door, is absorbed by the ground, and is converted: the same energy leaves the surface again, but now at long wavelengths, and to those wavelengths the atmosphere is a wall. The gases absorb the outgoing stream and re-radiate part of it back downward, which is why the surface settles about thirty-three degrees warmer than it would on an airless planet — minus eighteen lifted to plus fifteen. It is not one-way glass by magic; it is the same energy changing its wavelength in transit, and the air treating each wavelength differently. That conversion step, shortwave absorbed then longwave re-emitted, is the sentence to write whenever the greenhouse effect must be explained rather than merely named.

**Bianca:** Last one: if the winds carry three-quarters of the heat and the oceans only a quarter, why do the two currents on our coasts get so much attention? A quarter sounds like the small partner.

**Tutor:** A quarter of a planetary heat engine is an immense quantity of energy, and the oceans' share punches far above its fraction for two reasons. The first is water's heat capacity: a cubic metre of seawater stores vastly more heat than a cubic metre of air, so a slow, deep current is a freight train of energy where the wind is a fleet of vans — the Agulhas Current alone moves warm water on a scale that reshapes the climate of the whole east coast. The second reason is persistence: winds shift daily, but the currents flow along the same tracks year after year, so their heating and cooling is stamped permanently onto the coasts they wash. South Africa is the world's clearest showroom for this. East London and Lambert's Bay sit on the same landmass, yet the east coast is green, humid and warm because the Agulhas delivers tropical water southward, while the west coast is dry and cool because the Benguela hauls cold sub-Antarctic water northward and chills the air above it. Same country, same sun — the quarter carried by the sea decides which coast gets the sugar cane and which gets the semi-desert. So respect the fraction, but never mistake it for unimportance: in the places where a current runs, the current IS the climate.
