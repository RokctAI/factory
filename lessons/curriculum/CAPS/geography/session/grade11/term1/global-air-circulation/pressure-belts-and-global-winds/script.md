# Part 1 — Expert

Unequal heating creates an energy surplus in the tropics and a deficit toward the poles. The atmosphere's answer is circulation, built here in order: the chain from temperature to pressure to wind; the belts of high and low pressure that ring the planet and slide with the sun; the three-cell model and the forces that bend every wind inside it; and the air masses and local winds that deliver the model to a South African doorstep.

## Subtopic: Temperature, Pressure and Wind — the Chain

Atmospheric pressure is the weight of the column of air above a point, measured in hectopascals with a barometer. Mean sea-level pressure is 1 013 hectopascals.

The chain begins with temperature. Heat air and it expands, becomes less dense and RISES. With the column lifting away, fewer molecules press on the surface — so surface pressure FALLS and a LOW-pressure cell forms. Cool air contracts, becomes denser and SINKS, piling molecules onto the surface, so pressure RISES and a HIGH-pressure cell forms. Warm equals low, cold equals high, at the surface.

Wind is the horizontal movement of air from high pressure toward low. On a chart the pressure field appears as ISOBARS — lines joining places of equal pressure — read exactly like contours: tightly packed isobars mean a steep PRESSURE GRADIENT and strong wind; widely spaced isobars mean light wind.

The weather consequences follow from the vertical motion, not the horizontal. In a low, air RISES, expands and cools adiabatically; its water vapour condenses; cloud forms and rain follows. Lows are unsettled and wet. In a high, air SUBSIDES, is compressed and warms adiabatically; relative humidity falls and cloud evaporates. Highs are clear, dry and settled. That pairing answers more examination questions than any other single fact in the topic.

Finally the naming convention, because direction marks are lost carelessly. A wind is named for the direction it comes FROM: air arriving out of the south-east is a south-easter. The compass rose carries 16 cardinal points, each 22,5 degrees apart, since 360 divided by 16 is 22,5. North-east is a bearing of 045 degrees, south-east 135, south-west 225, north-west 315. Cape Town's summer south-easter therefore blows from 135 degrees.

Take this section's questions now: the temperature-pressure-wind chain in the correct order, isobar spacing read for wind strength, and wind direction named and converted to a bearing.

## Subtopic: The World's Pressure Belts

Because heating varies systematically with latitude, pressure does too — in belts that circle the globe.

At the equator, intense heating lifts air continuously: the EQUATORIAL LOW-pressure belt, also called the Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone. Surface winds converge into it and rise, so horizontal winds are feeble — the doldrums — while heavy convectional rain falls almost daily.

Near 30 degrees north and south the air that rose at the equator has travelled poleward aloft, cooled and grown dense, and now subsides: the SUBTROPICAL HIGH-pressure belts. Subsiding air is warming and drying, so this latitude carries the world's great deserts — the Sahara, the Kalahari and Namib, the Australian interior. Three semi-permanent anticyclones dominate our region: the South Atlantic High off the west coast, the South Indian High off the east coast, and the continental or Kalahari High over the interior, at its strongest in winter.

Near 60 degrees lie the SUB-POLAR LOW-pressure belts, where warm westerlies meet cold polar air along the POLAR FRONT and rise — the birthplace of the travelling mid-latitude cyclones whose cold fronts sweep across the Cape. At the poles, bitterly cold dense air sinks to form the POLAR HIGHS.

Now the detail that carries the most marks. The belts migrate with the overhead sun, shifting roughly five degrees of latitude — north in our winter, south in our summer. In June the belts move north, dragging the westerlies and their cold fronts over the south-western Cape, which is why Cape Town's rain falls in winter, while the continental high sits over the interior giving clear, dry, cold Highveld winters. In December they move south, the westerlies retreat over the ocean, and moist tropical air reaches deep into Limpopo and Gauteng. One migration, two rainfall regimes, one country.

This section's questions are with you now: name each belt with its latitude and its air motion, and use the seasonal migration to account for winter rainfall in the south-west and summer rainfall over the interior.

## Subtopic: The Tri-cellular Circulation and the Forces That Bend It

Between equator and pole the circulation organises itself into three vertical loops per hemisphere — picture each as a revolving door standing on its edge.

The HADLEY CELL, from the equator to 30 degrees, is strongest because heat drives it directly. Air rises at the equatorial low, releases latent heat, streams poleward some ten to fifteen kilometres up, cools, subsides at 30 degrees to build the subtropical highs, and returns along the surface as the TRADE winds.

The FERREL CELL, from 30 to 60 degrees, is indirect — turned by its two neighbours rather than by heating. Surface air leaves the subtropical high moving poleward as the WESTERLIES, is lifted at the polar front near 60 degrees, and flows back aloft toward 30 degrees.

The POLAR CELL has dense cold air sinking at the pole, spreading equatorward as the POLAR EASTERLIES and rising again at the polar front. High in the troposphere, at the cell boundaries, run the JET STREAMS — narrow ribbons of west-to-east wind frequently exceeding 200 kilometres per hour.

Now the forces. The PRESSURE GRADIENT FORCE drives air from high to low at right angles to the isobars, strengthening as isobars crowd together. The CORIOLIS FORCE, a consequence of the earth's rotation, deflects that moving air — to the LEFT in the Southern Hemisphere, to the right in the Northern. It is zero at the equator, greatest at the poles, and changes direction only, never speed. Deflection is why our trades blow from the south-east and our westerlies arrive from the north-west.

Above about a kilometre friction disappears and the two forces balance exactly, giving GEOSTROPHIC FLOW: wind blowing PARALLEL to straight isobars instead of across them. Nearer the surface friction slows the air, weakening Coriolis, so wind crosses the isobars into the low — ten to twenty degrees over smooth sea, twenty-five to forty-five over rough land. The practical summary is Buys Ballot's Law for our hemisphere: back to the wind, low pressure on your RIGHT. It follows that Southern Hemisphere lows circulate clockwise and inward, highs anticlockwise and outward.

Work through the questions for this section: the three cells with their surface winds, Coriolis deflection stated for the correct hemisphere, and geostrophic flow distinguished from surface flow.

## Subtopic: Air Masses, Berg Winds and Monsoons

An AIR MASS is a large body of air with fairly uniform temperature and humidity, taking its character from the SOURCE REGION where it stagnated and modifying as it travels. Maritime tropical air over the warm Indian Ocean is warm and humid, delivering moisture to the east coast and the summer-rainfall interior; continental tropical air over the Kalahari is hot and very dry, driving our heatwaves; maritime polar air off the Southern Ocean is cold and moist and arrives behind every Cape cold front.

Local winds arise from local pressure differences. SEA and LAND BREEZES come from unequal heating of land and water: by day the land heats faster, pressure over it falls and a cool sea breeze blows onshore — Durban's afternoon relief; by night the reverse gives an offshore land breeze. VALLEY and MOUNTAIN WINDS work the same way vertically: by day heated slopes send air up-valley as an anabatic wind, and by night cold dense air drains down-slope as a katabatic wind, pooling on the valley floor and creating the frost hollows and temperature inversions familiar on Free State farms.

The BERG WIND is South Africa's Föhn-type wind and the calculation examiners like. Air is drawn from the interior plateau, crosses the escarpment and descends toward the coast, compressed and warming at the DRY ADIABATIC LAPSE RATE of about 1 degree Celsius per 100 metres. Work it: air leaving the plateau edge at 1 800 metres at 12 degrees Celsius descends to sea level. Warming equals 1 800 divided by 100, multiplied by 1 degree — 18 degrees. Arrival temperature is 12 plus 18, which is 30 degrees Celsius. Because warming raises the air's capacity to hold moisture while adding none, relative humidity collapses: berg winds are hot, dry and dusty, raise extreme fire danger, and typically precede a cold front by a day. The Alpine Föhn and the Chinook are the same mechanism, with a first act added — moist air forced up the windward slope, cooling at the saturated rate of about 0,5 degrees per 100 metres and raining there, then descending the lee slope at 1 degree per 100 metres and arriving warmer than it began.

MONSOONS scale the sea-breeze principle to a continent and a season. In summer the Asian landmass heats rapidly, a vast thermal low develops, and moist air is drawn off the Indian Ocean as the SOUTH-WEST monsoon, delivering torrential rain to India. In winter the land cools, an enormous high builds over Siberia, and dry air flows outward as the NORTH-EAST monsoon.

The final questions of this part are in front of you: air masses matched to their source regions, the berg-wind warming calculated in full with units, and the monsoon explained as a seasonal reversal driven by land-sea heating contrasts.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same circulation from a kitchen, a merry-go-round and a hair dryer — same winds, same answers.

## Subtopic: Hot Air Rises — the Whole Machine in a Kitchen

Boil a kettle and watch the steam. It goes up, every time. Warm air is lighter than the air around it, so it climbs — and when it climbs it stops pressing down on the floor, so pressure down there drops. That is a LOW.

Now open the freezer and feel the cold air spill out at your ankles. Cold air is heavy, sinks, and piles up pressing hard on the floor. That is a HIGH.

Wind is what happens next, and it works like a full taxi and an empty one. Nobody stands in the packed taxi if there is space next door — people move from crowded to uncrowded, and air does exactly the same, flowing from the crowded high to the empty low. The closer the two taxis are parked, the faster people scramble across. On a weather map the isobars show that crowding: lines squeezed together mean a big pressure difference over a short distance, so a strong wind.

Here is the part that decides the weather, and it is about UP and DOWN, not sideways. In a low, air is rising. Rising air cools, and cool air cannot hold its moisture, so it lets go of it — clouds, then rain. Think of your bathroom mirror fogging up. In a high, air is sinking, getting squashed and warming, which dries it out. Think of a hair dryer. So: low means wet and unsettled, high means clear and dry. Learn those two lines and half the topic is done.

One last habit: winds are named for where they come FROM. Air arriving from the south-east is a south-easter. The compass is cut into 16 points, each 22,5 degrees apart, because 360 divided by 16 is 22,5 — so north-east is 045 degrees and south-east is 135.

Well done — the questions for this section are with you now: rising and sinking air, isobar crowding, and naming a wind properly.

## Subtopic: Three Loops Around the Planet

Picture the space between the equator and the South Pole as three revolving doors stacked side by side, each one turning air around a loop.

Door one sits over the tropics. At the equator the sun cooks the ground all year, air shoots up, dumps its rain and drifts away high overhead toward us. By about 30 degrees south — the latitude of the Kalahari and the Namib — it has cooled and grown heavy, so it sinks. Sinking air is drying air, which is exactly why the world's biggest deserts sit in a ring at 30 degrees. Then it crawls back along the ground as the trade winds. That is the strongest door, because the sun turns it directly.

Door two covers the middle latitudes, roughly 30 to 60 degrees, and it is the lazy one — it does not turn itself, it gets pushed by the doors on either side. Its surface wind is the westerlies, the belt that hurls cold fronts at Cape Town.

Door three sits over the pole, where freezing heavy air sinks and creeps outward as the polar easterlies until it collides with the westerlies at about 60 degrees and the two shove each other upward. At the joins between the doors run the jet streams — thin fast rivers of wind that regularly beat 200 kilometres an hour.

Now the trick that explains our whole country. Those belts are not nailed down: they slide north and south chasing the sun, by about five degrees. In winter everything slides north, so the westerlies and their cold fronts reach the Western Cape — that is why Cape Town's rain comes in June, and why the Highveld sits under a dry, freezing high. In summer everything slides south, the fronts retreat out to sea, and wet tropical air pushes down into Limpopo and Gauteng.

Great progress — your questions for this part are ready: the three loops, the desert ring at 30 degrees, and the sliding belts that give South Africa two different rainy seasons.

## Subtopic: Why Wind Never Walks Straight

Stand at the edge of a spinning roundabout in a park and roll a ball straight across to a friend on the far side. It never arrives straight. It looks like something bent it sideways — but nothing touched it; the ground itself turned underneath it. That is the CORIOLIS force, and the earth is the roundabout.

The only rule to memorise: in the Southern Hemisphere moving air bends LEFT; in the Northern, right. It is strongest at the poles, zero at the equator, and it changes direction without changing speed. That bending is why air heading toward the equator arrives as a south-easter instead of a straight southerly.

There is a beautiful shortcut for finding pressure without a chart. Stand with your back to the wind, here in the south, and low pressure is on your RIGHT. That is Buys Ballot's Law, and from it you get the two rotations for free: down here, lows spin clockwise, highs spin anticlockwise.

Now the wind that comes for the coast. A berg wind starts as ordinary air on the high interior, gets pulled over the escarpment and tumbles down toward the sea — and here is the thing about falling air: it gets squashed by the heavier air below, and squashing heats it, at about 1 degree Celsius for every 100 metres it drops. Take air leaving the plateau edge at 1 800 metres on a mild 12-degree morning. Drop it to sea level: 1 800 divided by 100 is 18, so it warms 18 degrees and arrives at 12 plus 18, which is 30 degrees Celsius. Hot air that gained no water is bone-dry air, which is why a berg wind day is scorching, dusty and dangerous for fires — and why it so often turns up the day before a cold front.

Finally, a monsoon is just an enormous sea breeze that changes with the season instead of the afternoon. In summer India's land heats far faster than the ocean, pressure over the land drops, and wet air rushes in from the sea. In winter the land turns cold, pressure climbs, and dry air pours back out to sea. Durban's afternoon breeze, blown up to a continent.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — the roundabout and which way our winds bend, the back-to-the-wind rule, and the berg-wind warming worked out in degrees. Nail these and the atmosphere's whole delivery system is yours.
