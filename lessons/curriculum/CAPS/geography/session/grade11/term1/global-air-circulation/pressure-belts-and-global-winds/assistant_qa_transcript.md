**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: we were taught that warm air rises and makes low pressure. But the subtropical highs at 30 degrees sit over the hottest deserts on earth. How can boiling hot Sahara and Kalahari air produce HIGH pressure?

**Tutor:** Excellent objection, and it separates a surface-level answer from a real one. The rule "warm rises, cold sinks" describes air heated at the surface relative to the air immediately around it. The subtropical high is not made at the surface at all — it is made from above. Follow the air that rose at the equator: it climbs, sheds its moisture as tropical rain, and then streams poleward some ten to fifteen kilometres up, radiating heat to space the whole way. By roughly 30 degrees of latitude it has cooled, grown dense, and there is nowhere left to go but down. That descending column is what presses on the desert surface and registers as high pressure on a barometer. The surface air beneath may be blisteringly hot, but the weight of the whole subsiding column above it decides the pressure reading. And the subsidence is exactly why the deserts are there: sinking air is compressed and warmed, its relative humidity collapses, cloud cannot survive, and rain almost never falls. So the correct sequence in an answer is upper-level convergence and subsidence first, surface high second, aridity third — not "it is hot, therefore high".

**Bianca:** Next one caused a fight in our study group. Half of us say winds blow from high to low pressure. The other half say winds blow parallel to the isobars. Which is it, because they cannot both be right?

**Tutor:** They are both right, at different heights, and the question is really about how much friction there is. Start with the pressure gradient force alone: it does push air straight from high to low, at right angles to the isobars. That is the starting impulse. But the moment the air moves, the Coriolis force acts on it, deflecting it left in our hemisphere. High above the ground — above roughly a kilometre, where nothing rubs against the surface — those two forces come into exact balance, and the outcome is remarkable: the wind ends up blowing PARALLEL to straight isobars, neither closing on the low nor escaping it. That is geostrophic flow, and it is why upper-air charts show wind following the isobars like a road. Near the surface it is different, because friction with land, sea and vegetation drags on the air and slows it. Slower air feels less Coriolis deflection, so the balance tips back toward the pressure gradient and the wind crosses the isobars at an angle, spiralling in toward the low — around ten to twenty degrees over smooth sea and twenty-five to forty-five degrees over rough land. So write it this way in an exam: the pressure gradient force initiates the motion, Coriolis deflects it, friction decides how completely the two balance, and therefore surface winds cross isobars while upper winds run parallel to them.

**Bianca:** This one worried us for a whole test: how do we actually remember which way things spin in the Southern Hemisphere? Every textbook picture we practise on seems to be drawn for the north.

**Tutor:** Do not memorise pictures — derive them from one sentence, and you will never be caught out. The sentence is Buys Ballot's Law for our hemisphere: stand with your back to the wind and low pressure lies on your right. Now reason it through once, slowly, and you will keep it for good. Put a low-pressure cell in front of you and take a parcel of air sitting to the north of it. For that parcel, the low is to its south. For the low to end up on the parcel's right, the parcel must be travelling eastward — face east and your right hand points south, toward the low. Now take a parcel sitting to the east of the low: for the low, which lies to its west, to be on its right, the parcel must be travelling south. So air north of the low goes east, air east of the low goes south, air south of it goes west, air west of it goes north. Trace that around and you have drawn a clockwise circulation. Highs are the mirror image: anticlockwise, and spiralling outward rather than inward. One law, thirty seconds of reasoning, both rotations recovered from scratch. That beats memorising, because under pressure memory flips and reasoning does not.

**Bianca:** Last one: berg winds and Föhn winds keep being called the same thing, but the berg wind seems to come off a flat plateau while the Föhn comes over a mountain range. Are they really the same process?

**Tutor:** Same physics, slightly different setting, and the exam wants you to see the shared mechanism. The engine in both is compression on descent. Air that sinks is squeezed by the denser air beneath it, and squeezing raises its temperature at the dry adiabatic lapse rate of about 1 degree Celsius per 100 metres — with no heat added from outside at all. That alone makes descending air hot, and because it gains no water on the way down while its capacity to hold water rises steeply, its relative humidity collapses and it arrives desiccating. The classic Alpine Föhn adds a first act: moist air is forced up the windward slope, cools at the saturated rate of about 0,5 degrees per 100 metres, rains out its moisture on that side, and then descends the lee side at the faster dry rate — so it arrives at the bottom warmer than it started, having lost its water on the far slope. The South African berg wind usually skips that first act, because the air begins on the interior plateau already dry, and simply descends the escarpment. Run the numbers and the drama is obvious: air leaving the plateau edge at 1 800 metres on a mild 12-degree morning warms by 1 800 divided by 100, which is 18 degrees, and reaches the coast at 30 degrees Celsius. Hot, dry, gusty air over dry vegetation is the reason berg-wind days carry extreme fire warnings, and the reason a coastal town can record its highest temperature of the year in the middle of winter.
