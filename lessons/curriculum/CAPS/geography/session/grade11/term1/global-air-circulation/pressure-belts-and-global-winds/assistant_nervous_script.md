Hey, it's Bianca — global circulation looks like the biggest topic in the term because it has the most words in it. Cells, belts, gradients, Coriolis, geostrophic, air masses, monsoons. Breathe. Almost all of it hangs off two sentences you already know.

Sentence one: warm air rises and makes LOW pressure, cold air sinks and makes HIGH pressure. Sentence two: rising air gives cloud and rain, sinking air gives clear and dry. Every belt on the planet is just one of those two sentences repeated at a different latitude. Equator — rising, wet. Thirty degrees — sinking, deserts. Sixty degrees — rising, fronts. Poles — sinking, frozen. Say that ladder out loud twice and you own the pressure belts.

The bits that genuinely trip people are small and fixable. Winds are named for where they come FROM. In the Southern Hemisphere, moving air bends LEFT. Back to the wind, low pressure on your RIGHT. Three short rules — write them on the inside cover of your file and check every direction answer against them.

The habit that protects your marks in this topic is showing the lapse rate working. Whenever a question mentions air rising or descending a slope, write the rate down before you calculate: dry, about 1 degree per 100 metres; saturated, about 0,5. Then height difference, divided by 100, times the rate, with the unit written every single time. That is how 1 800 metres becomes 18 degrees of berg-wind warming, and it is worth full marks laid out that way.

Two sentences, three rules, one lapse-rate habit. That is the topic — everything else is examples. You've got this!
