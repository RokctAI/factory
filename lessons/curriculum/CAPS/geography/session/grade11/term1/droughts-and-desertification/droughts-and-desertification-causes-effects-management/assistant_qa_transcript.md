**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one costs marks every year: the Karoo gets very little rain, so is the Karoo permanently in drought? Half of us said yes in the practice test and got nothing for it.

**Tutor:** And the memorandum was right to give nothing, because that answer misses the single most important word in the definition: NORMAL. Drought is not low rainfall — drought is rainfall well below the average that a particular place normally receives and has organised its life around. The Karoo's farms, towns, vegetation and stocking rates are all built on an expectation of perhaps 200 to 400 millimetres a year; when that arrives, the system works, and no drought exists, however dry it looks to a visitor from Durban. Drought strikes the Karoo when even that modest salary fails — when two or three seasons deliver half of the expected total and the veld and boreholes built for 300 millimetres receive 150. Flip it around to prove the point: if Durban, built on roughly 1 000 millimetres a year, received the Karoo's normal 300, that would be a devastating drought for Durban, even though 300 millimetres is a perfectly healthy year in Sutherland. So always anchor your definition to the local average, and you will also see why drought can happen anywhere on earth — including rainforests — and why a desert is not a drought but a climate.

***

**Bianca:** Next: drought and desertification keep blurring together in our essays. If drought is natural and desertification is human, why do they always arrive in the same paragraph, and how do we keep them apart for marks?

**Tutor:** Keep them apart with one sentence each, then join them with a mechanism. Drought is a temporary CLIMATIC event: a period of below-normal rainfall that ends when the rains return. Desertification is a lasting change in the LAND: degradation of soil and vegetation in dry regions, caused mostly by human pressure — overgrazing, over-cultivation, deforestation, bad irrigation — until the land behaves like desert even in a normal rainfall year. Now the join, which is where the thinking marks live: drought and desertification amplify each other. During a drought, vegetation is already stressed, so the same number of cattle strips the veld far faster, and desperate people cut more trees and plough more marginal land to survive — so drought ACCELERATES desertification. In the other direction, degraded land makes every drought BITE harder: bare, compacted soil sheds rain as runoff instead of storing it, so even a modest dry season empties the soil-moisture account immediately. A healthy veld can ride out a poor season; a degraded one collapses. Structure an essay exactly that way — define each, then show the two-way amplification — and you have separated them and connected them, which is precisely what the question wants.

**Bianca:** Third one, on the case studies: for management strategies, is it enough to write general things like save water and plant trees, or do the examiners want the named programmes?

**Tutor:** General measures earn the basic marks; named, placed, specific examples earn the top ones, and this topic hands you three that are easy to carry. First, CAPE TOWN'S DAY ZERO response, 2017 to 2018: demand cut from around 1 200 million litres a day to near 500 through restrictions, steep block tariffs, pipe-pressure reduction and relentless public communication — use it whenever a question asks how cities manage water scarcity, and quote at least one number, because numbers signal real knowledge. Second, WORKING FOR WATER: a South African government programme that employs teams to clear alien invasive plants such as wattles and eucalypts from catchments; the aliens drink far more water than indigenous vegetation, so clearing them measurably increases river flow while creating rural jobs — a strategy that heals land, water and livelihoods in one move, which examiners love as an evaluation point. Third, the GREAT GREEN WALL in the Sahel: describe it accurately as a mosaic of community-led restoration projects — farmer-managed tree regeneration, grazing management, water harvesting — stretching across the continent, not as a literal single wall of trees, because that correction itself shows understanding. One local city case, one national land programme, one continental initiative: with those three placed and detailed, every management question is covered.

**Bianca:** Last one: the four types of drought feel like pure memorisation. Meteorological, agricultural, hydrological, socio-economic — is there a logic that stops us scrambling the order under pressure?

**Tutor:** There is, and it is the logic of water physically working its way down through the system — follow the water and the order derives itself. Rain falls from the ATMOSPHERE first, so the first shortage is atmospheric: meteorological drought, rainfall below normal, and nothing more. If the shortfall continues, the next place the water fails is the SOIL, the first stop below the atmosphere: soil moisture drops below what plants need, crops wilt and grazing thins — agricultural drought. Persist further and the shortage reaches the STORAGE system beneath and beyond the soil: rivers fed by that soil water shrink, dams fed by those rivers drop, and the groundwater table that everything ultimately rests on sinks — hydrological drought. And only after the physical system has failed at every level does the shortage arrive at PEOPLE'S LIVES as the final destination: supply below demand, food prices rising, jobs lost, families migrating — socio-economic drought. Atmosphere, soil, storage, society: each stage is simply the water failing one level further down. Recite it as a journey rather than a list, and you can reconstruct the order from scratch in the exam even if the labels momentarily desert you.
