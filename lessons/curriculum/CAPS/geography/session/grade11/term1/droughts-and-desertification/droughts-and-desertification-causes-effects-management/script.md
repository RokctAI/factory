# Part 1 — Expert

The circulation lessons explained why rain sometimes fails to arrive; this session examines what happens when it fails for years, and what people do to the land while waiting. Four pieces: drought defined properly and traced to its causes; desertification as the man-made disease that drought exposes; the effects of both on people and environments from the Sahel to the Karoo; and the management strategies — early warning, water discipline and land restoration — that turn a natural hazard into a survivable one.

## Subtopic: Drought — Definitions and Causes

A DROUGHT is an extended period of rainfall well below the average that a place normally receives and has organised its life around. That last clause matters: 250 millimetres a year is normal for Upington and would be catastrophic for Durban. Drought is always measured against the local average, which is why the DBE marks definitions that say below NORMAL rainfall, not simply low rainfall.

Geographers grade drought in four deepening stages. METEOROLOGICAL drought is the rainfall shortfall itself — the season the rains underperform. If it persists, AGRICULTURAL drought follows: soil moisture falls below what crops and grazing need, and yields fail. Next comes HYDROLOGICAL drought, when the shortage reaches the water system — river flow shrinks, dam levels drop, boreholes and groundwater tables sink. Last and worst is SOCIO-ECONOMIC drought, when water supply can no longer meet human demand and the shortage sets prices, jobs and migration in motion. One failed season is meteorological; three failed seasons is all four.

The causes begin with the circulation already mastered. Southern Africa lies on the desert side of the subtropical high-pressure belt, so BELOW-normal years arrive whenever subsidence strengthens or the rain-bearing systems stall: an ITCZ that stops short in the north, a Kalahari High that refuses to weaken in summer. EL NIÑO years tilt the odds further — warm eastern Pacific water reorganises the global circulation and southern African summers dry out, as 2015-16 proved. Add CLIMATE CHANGE, which is raising temperatures, increasing evaporation and making rainfall more variable, and the natural deck is stacked. Human causes then deepen every natural drought: growing populations and thirsty crops raise demand, leaking infrastructure wastes supply, and degraded catchments shed water instead of storing it.

Take this section's questions now: drought defined against the local average, the four types placed in their order of deepening, and natural causes separated from human ones.

## Subtopic: Desertification — the Land Degradation Disease

DESERTIFICATION is the degradation of land in arid and semi-arid regions until it becomes desert-like — soil stripped of nutrients and vegetation, unable to hold water or support life. It is not a desert advancing like an army; it is productive land dying in patches, usually where people press hardest on it. Drought exposes desertification, but people cause most of it.

The causes form a chain worth reciting. OVERGRAZING: too many livestock strip the grass cover faster than it regrows, and trampling compacts the soil so rain runs off instead of sinking in. OVER-CULTIVATION: fields cropped season after season without rest or fertiliser exhaust the soil's nutrients and its structure crumbles to dust. DEFORESTATION: trees and shrubs cut for firewood and building leave the soil naked to wind and rain. POOR IRRIGATION in hot climates waterlogs the soil and, as the water evaporates, leaves salt behind — SALINISATION — until nothing grows. Behind all four stands POPULATION PRESSURE: more people needing more food, fuel and grazing from the same fragile land, often because poverty leaves no alternative. Climate change then multiplies everything by heating and drying the margins.

Once the vegetation is gone the physics is merciless. Bare soil is baked hard, so rain — when it comes — runs off in flash floods, carving gullies called DONGAS and carrying the topsoil away. Wind lifts the loosened dust into storms. With the topsoil gone, less vegetation can return, which exposes more soil, which erodes faster: a positive feedback loop spiralling downward. The world's warning region is the SAHEL, the semi-arid belt along the Sahara's southern edge, where overgrazing, firewood cutting and repeated drought have degraded huge areas. South Africa carries its own version: the DBE estimates that most of the country is vulnerable, with the Karoo, the Kalahari margins and parts of the Eastern Cape's former homelands — where crowded settlement forced overgrazing — showing the classic dongas and dust.

This section's questions are ready: desertification defined as land degradation, the human causal chain recited, and the runaway feedback loop explained.

## Subtopic: Effects on People and the Environment

Begin with the environment. Drought and desertification strip VEGETATION, and every loss cascades: grazing disappears, habitats collapse and biodiversity falls as animals die or migrate. SOILS lose structure and blow or wash away — and soil that took a thousand years to form is not replaced on any human timescale. WATER systems fail from the top down: rivers become sand beds, wetlands dry, dams drop toward dead storage and boreholes chase a sinking water table. Even the air changes, as dust storms carry the lost land across whole provinces.

The human effects follow the water down. FOOD SECURITY goes first: crops fail, livestock die — South Africa's 2015-16 drought killed tens of thousands of cattle and cut the maize harvest so deeply that the country had to import maize — and food prices climb, hitting the poorest households hardest. LIVELIHOODS go next: farm workers lose seasonal work, farm debt climbs, and rural families send members to the cities — DROUGHT MIGRATION that swells informal settlements. HEALTH deteriorates as clean water runs short and malnutrition spreads. And scarcity breeds CONFLICT, from disputes over boreholes and grazing to tension between provinces and countries sharing rivers — the herder-farmer conflicts of the Sahel carry that warning at continental scale.

South Africa's recent history supplies the case study every examiner expects: Cape Town's DAY ZERO crisis. After three failed winter rainfall seasons from 2015 to 2017, the city's dams fell toward 13 percent, and in early 2018 officials published the date — Day Zero — when household taps would be turned off and residents would queue at collection points for 25 litres each. The city cut demand by more than half, from around 1 200 million litres a day toward 500, through restrictions, steep tariffs, pressure management and a relentless public campaign. The rains of 2018 returned, Day Zero never arrived, and the crisis became the world's most studied lesson in urban drought response.

## Subtopic: Management — Predicting, Saving and Restoring

Management strategies group into three families: see it coming, use less, and heal the land.

Seeing it coming is EARLY WARNING. Weather services monitor rainfall, El Niño indicators and satellite images of vegetation condition; a GIS overlays these on maps of drought-prone districts so that fodder, water tankers and relief funds move BEFORE the disaster peaks. Farmers respond to warnings by reducing herd sizes early — selling animals while they still have value — and by planting drought-resistant crops and indigenous livestock breeds suited to dry country.

Using less is WATER DEMAND MANAGEMENT. Households harvest rainwater, reuse greywater and obey restrictions; steep block tariffs make heavy users pay more. Agriculture, which consumes the majority of South Africa's water, switches from wasteful flood irrigation to DRIP IRRIGATION that delivers water to the roots, irrigates at night to cut evaporation, and mulches the soil to keep moisture in. Municipalities fix leaking pipes — a significant slice of urban supply is lost to leaks — and diversify sources with groundwater, recycling and, on the coast, desalination.

Healing the land reverses the desertification chain link by link. ROTATIONAL GRAZING rests camps so grass recovers; stocking rates are matched to what the veld can carry. Fields rest under ROTATION and cover crops; contour ploughing and terracing slow runoff on slopes; gully check dams trap soil in the dongas. Trees return as windbreaks and woodlots, taking the firewood pressure off the veld. South Africa's WORKING FOR WATER programme pays teams to clear thirsty alien invasive plants — wattles, pines and eucalypts that drain rivers — restoring water while creating jobs, and LandCare projects rehabilitate eroded communal land. At continental scale, the GREAT GREEN WALL initiative is restoring a mosaic of trees, grasslands and farmland across the Sahel from Senegal to Djibouti — less a wall of trees than thousands of community land-restoration projects under one banner.

The final questions of this part are in front of you: the three families of strategy, specific farming and household measures under each, and Working for Water and the Great Green Wall as named case studies.

## Subtopic: The Rainfall Bank Account

Think of a place's water as a bank account. Rain is the salary paid in; evaporation, rivers, farms and taps are the spending. A drought is not simply a small salary — Upington lives comfortably on a salary that would bankrupt Durban. A drought is a salary that suddenly drops far below what THIS account was built on. That is why the definition must say below NORMAL rainfall for that place.

The account fails in four stages, and the exam loves the order. First the pay cut itself: rain below normal — meteorological drought. Then the pantry empties: soil moisture too low for crops and grazing — agricultural drought. Then the savings run out: rivers shrink, dam levels slide, boreholes drop — hydrological drought. Finally the debt collectors arrive: supply cannot meet demand, prices climb, jobs vanish, families move — socio-economic drought. Pay cut, pantry, savings, debt. Four stages, in that order, every time.

What cuts the salary? Mostly the machinery from the previous lessons: our country sits on the sinking-air side of the circulation, so any year the rain belt stalls short or the Kalahari High refuses to lift, the salary shrinks. An El Niño year loads the dice toward drought; climate change turns the whole payroll erratic. And people deepen every shortfall by spending harder — more taps, more thirsty crops, leaking pipes — exactly when the salary drops.

Well done — this section's questions are with you: the bank account definition, the four stages in order, and the dice-loaders behind a failed season.

## Subtopic: How to Make a Desert

Here is the recipe, and people cook it by accident all over the world. Take dry, fragile land. Add too many goats and cattle — the grass is bitten and trampled faster than it can regrow. Plough the same field every year until the soil is grey dust. Chop the trees for firewood. If there is irrigation, splash water around in the heat until the ground goes white with salt. Congratulations: the land is now bald.

Bald land is where the physics takes over. Think of soil as a head of hair protecting a scalp. While the hair is thick, rain lands softly, soaks in and feeds the roots. Shave it off and the scalp bakes hard; now rain bounces and races away in muddy torrents, ripping open those gullies you see from the roadside — dongas — and carrying the topsoil to the sea. Wind lifts the rest as dust. Less soil means less grass regrows, which bares more ground, which erodes faster. The process feeds itself downhill, like a rolling snowball. That self-feeding spiral is desertification — not the Sahara marching, but the land dying patch by patch where it is pressed hardest.

You do not need to travel to the Sahel to see it, though the Sahel is the world's textbook case. Parts of the Karoo, the Kalahari's edges and the crowded old homeland areas of the Eastern Cape show the same shaved hills and gullies, because history packed too many people and animals onto too little land.

## Subtopic: Day Zero and the Bucket Brigade

When drought hits a city, it looks like Cape Town in early 2018. Three winters of rain had failed. The dams sank toward 13 percent, and the city published an actual date — Day Zero — when the taps would be closed and every resident would queue for 25 litres a day at collection points. Twenty-five litres is two buckets: one short shower uses more. The city then did the thing everyone said was impossible: it halved its water use. Two-minute showers, greywater in the garden, pool covers, brutal tariffs on heavy users, pressure turned down in the pipes, a dashboard showing every suburb's usage. Day Zero kept retreating until the 2018 rains washed it away. Remember both halves of that story: the danger was real, and behaviour actually beat it.

Fighting the bigger battle takes three moves, and they map onto any exam answer. Move one, SEE IT COMING: satellites watch the veld turn brown, a GIS maps which districts are sliding into trouble, and warnings let farmers sell stock early and plant drought-hardy crops before the disaster, not after. Move two, SPEND LESS: drip irrigation that waters roots instead of air, night-time watering, mulch on the soil, fixed leaks, harvested rainwater — agriculture drinks most of our water, so a small saving there beats a big saving in the bathroom. Move three, HEAL THE LAND: rest the veld in rotation so the grass gets its hair back, match herds to what the land can carry, plug the dongas with check dams, plant windbreaks, and clear the alien trees that drink our rivers — that is Working for Water, which turns river restoration into thousands of jobs. Across the Sahel the same idea runs continent-sized as the Great Green Wall: not one wall of trees, but thousands of villages re-greening their own land from Senegal to Djibouti.
