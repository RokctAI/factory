# Part 1 — Expert

Term 4 opens the resources theme with the resource everything else stands on: soil. This session covers how slowly soil forms and how fast it is lost; the causes of soil erosion — human, animal and physical, past and present; the evidence and effects across South Africa; the management strategies that prevent and control it; and the GIS toolkit the syllabus attaches to this topic.

## Subtopic: Soil: The Living Skin That Feeds the Country

SOIL is not crushed rock; it is a living mixture — mineral particles weathered from parent rock, organic matter called HUMUS, water, air and billions of organisms — arranged in layers called HORIZONS: the dark, humus-rich TOPSOIL at the surface, the poorer SUBSOIL beneath, then weathered rock grading down to solid parent material. Nearly all fertility lives in that thin topsoil: its humus stores nutrients and moisture and binds particles into stable crumbs.

The number that drives the whole topic is the formation rate. Under African conditions it can take several hundred years to build a few centimetres of topsoil — weathering, plant growth, decay and organism activity working patiently. A single storm on bare ground can strip more than that in an afternoon. Soil is therefore effectively a NON-RENEWABLE resource on any human timescale, and every argument in this session flows from that asymmetry.

South Africa's endowment makes the stakes national. Only about 12 per cent of our land is arable — suitable for growing crops — and barely 3 per cent is truly high-potential. The rest is too dry, too steep or too thin. A country that feeds sixty million people from so small a fertile fraction cannot afford to watch it wash away — yet South Africa loses an estimated several hundred million tonnes of topsoil per year, much of it down rivers toward the sea.

Hold one process picture: vegetation is the soil's bodyguard. Leaves break the force of falling rain, roots bind the crumbs, and plant litter feeds the humus that holds it all together. Nearly every cause of erosion, whatever its name, works by removing that bodyguard.

The questions for this section are ready: the horizons, the formation-versus-loss asymmetry, and the arable percentages.

## Subtopic: Causes of Soil Erosion: Human, Animal and Physical

The syllabus sorts causes into three families and asks for past and present.

PHYSICAL causes are the erosive agents themselves. WATER erodes in an escalating sequence: raindrop SPLASH dislodges particles on bare ground; SHEET erosion carries a thin, even film of topsoil downslope; RILL erosion cuts small channels; and unchecked rills deepen into GULLIES — the dongas that scar so much of South Africa. WIND erosion lifts dry, loose soil from bare fields, as the ploughed-up American plains showed in the 1930s Dust Bowl. Steep slopes, intense thunderstorms — the Highveld pattern — drought cycles and thin natural cover make South Africa physically erosion-prone before any person acts. But note the examiner's nuance: physical agents mostly finish what land use starts; bare ground is the precondition, and bare ground is usually made.

HUMAN causes make the ground bare. OVERCULTIVATION exhausts humus by planting the same fields season after season without rest or fertiliser. Ploughing straight up and down slopes turns every furrow into a drain that concentrates runoff. DEFORESTATION for timber, firewood and clearing removes the bodyguard wholesale — and in rural South Africa, household firewood dependence keeps the pressure on. Mining strips vegetation and leaves waste dumps to wash and blow. Construction and informal settlement growth expose raw ground on urban edges. And the deep past matters: apartheid's homeland system concentrated too many people and animals on too little land, forcing the overuse whose scars — the Eastern Cape's dongas — remain today. Present-day pressures continue: poverty, insecure tenure and population growth still push marginal land too hard.

ANIMAL causes centre on OVERGRAZING: too many animals stripping grass faster than it regrows, exposing the surface. TRAMPLING compacts the soil along paths so water cannot soak in and runs off instead — cattle tracks to water points often become the starter channels for gullies.

## Subtopic: Evidence and Effects Across South Africa

The evidence is written on the landscape. DONGAS — steep-sided gullies, some deep enough to swallow a bakkie — cut through parts of the Eastern Cape, KwaZulu-Natal and Limpopo, especially in the former homeland districts where overuse was engineered. Rivers run brown after storms: that colour IS topsoil in transit. Dams silt up — reservoirs across the country have lost significant storage capacity to sediment, with the Welbedacht Dam on the Caledon River the textbook case, its capacity mostly gone within decades of construction. Add dust plumes off dry fields, exposed plant roots, and fences standing on pedestals of soil the wind has stripped from around them.

Now the effects, and the syllabus wants them on PEOPLE and on the ENVIRONMENT.

On people: falling yields as the fertile layer thins, so farming incomes shrink and food prices climb; rural livelihoods collapse where grazing and cropland degrade, pushing migration to towns; siltation shortens the life of the dams that store water for cities and irrigation — a water-security cost every user pays; and fertiliser must substitute, expensively, for the free fertility that washed away.

On the environment: rivers and wetlands choke with sediment, smothering aquatic life; the land's capacity to absorb and slowly release water declines, so floods sharpen and dry-season flows weaken; degraded veld carries less biodiversity; and at the extreme, erosion tips into DESERTIFICATION — the Grade 11 Term 1 concept returning — where productive land becomes effectively barren.

The summary sentence worth memorising: erosion is a slow-motion disaster — no single storm makes headlines, but the cumulative loss undermines food security, water security and rural livelihoods simultaneously.

Attend to this section's questions: three pieces of evidence with places, and paired effects on people and environment.

## Subtopic: Management, and the GIS Toolkit for Soil

Management strategies split into PREVENTION — keeping soil covered and anchored — and CONTROL, repairing damage already done.

Prevention on croplands: CONTOUR PLOUGHING, running furrows along the slope's contours so each ridge dams runoff instead of channelling it; STRIP CROPPING, alternating bands of cover crops with row crops; CROP ROTATION and fallow periods to rebuild humus; MULCHING and keeping stubble on fields over the dry season so ground is never bare; and windbreak rows of trees to slow erosive wind. On grazing land: match stock numbers to the veld's CARRYING CAPACITY, rest camps in rotation so grass recovers, and manage the routes to water so trampling never concentrates.

Control where gullies already run: small check dams and GABIONS — wire baskets packed with rock — across the channel to slow water and trap sediment; re-vegetating raw surfaces with grasses and hardy shrubs to re-anchor them; and fencing recovering land off from grazing. National programmes carry these to scale: LandCare, Working for Water clearing invasive aliens that displace binding groundcover, and Working on Fire protecting the vegetation shield.

The syllabus closes this topic with GIS, because managing erosion needs its maps. SPATIALLY REFERENCED DATA is information tied to coordinates on the earth's surface — a donga's position, a field's boundary, a rainfall reading with its station location. Remote sensing supplies much of it, and two RESOLUTIONS describe any sensor: SPATIAL resolution, the ground size of one pixel — a 10-metre pixel sees a field, not a furrow — and SPECTRAL resolution, which wavelength bands the sensor records; healthy vegetation reflects infrared strongly, so infrared bands expose bare, stressed ground long before the eye would. In the GIS itself, features are stored as POINT data — a borehole, a gully head; LINE data — a river, a contour, a fence; and AREA (polygon) data — a field, a catchment, an erosion-risk zone. Overlay slope, rainfall, soil and vegetation layers, and the GIS maps where erosion risk concentrates — telling LandCare where to build the gabions first.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same session in three pictures anyone can hold: a slow bank account, a footpath that becomes a canyon, and stitches in torn cloth. Nothing left out.

## Subtopic: The Bank Account That Fills by Teaspoons

Think of topsoil as a bank account that fills at one teaspoon a year. Weathering rock, rotting leaves, earthworms working the dark layer — a few centimetres of fertile soil takes centuries to save up. Now picture one thunderstorm on a bare, ploughed slope emptying decades of that account in an afternoon, the savings flowing brown down the river. Deposits by teaspoon, withdrawals by bucket — that is the entire soil crisis in one line, and it is why geographers treat soil as non-renewable: nothing you lose today comes back in your lifetime.

Now see the stakes for South Africa. Picture the country as ten plates of food: only about one of those plates stands on land good enough to grow crops, and only a sliver of that is top-quality. Sixty million people eat off that one plate. Meanwhile the country loses hundreds of millions of tonnes of topsoil a year — the plate itself, washing toward the sea.

Last piece: the bodyguard. Every blade of grass is security for the account — leaves soften the rain's punch, roots grip the crumbs, dead leaves feed the glue that holds soil together. Remember this and every cause of erosion becomes one story: something fired the bodyguard.

Nice work — the questions for this piece are with you now: teaspoons and buckets, the one plate in ten, and the bodyguard.

## Subtopic: How a Footpath Becomes a Canyon

Erosion has a plot, and you can watch it happen in any overgrazed camp.

Act one: too many cattle for the grass — overgrazing — and a patch goes bare. Act two: rain hits the bare patch like thousands of tiny hammers; each drop splashes soil loose. Act three: the water can no longer soak into the trampled, compacted ground, so it flows — first as a thin sheet quietly stealing the whole surface a millimetre at a time. Act four: the sheet gathers into little finger-channels — rills — easy to step over, easy to ignore. Act five: the rills join, deepen with every storm, and one year the farmer cannot drive across the camp anymore because a DONGA has opened — a raw canyon with cliff sides, swallowing the field that fed a family. And the path the cattle walked to the dam every day? Compacted hard as a stoep, it was the first drain — many dongas begin life as a footpath.

Wind runs a parallel plot on dry, bare fields — lifting the fine, fertile grains first and leaving the coarse sand behind, the story America's Dust Bowl told the world in the 1930s.

One more act, because history is a character in South Africa's version: apartheid crowded people and cattle onto the smallest, poorest land in the homelands. Too many hooves on too few hectares was built into the map — which is why the deepest dongas run through the Eastern Cape's former homeland districts today. Past cause, present scar.

Your questions for this section are ready now: the five acts in order, the footpath clue, and history's role in the plot.

## Subtopic: Stitching the Land Back Together

Torn cloth does not mend itself; someone stitches it. Land is the same, and the stitches have names.

To stop new tears, keep the cloth covered. Plough ALONG the slope, not down it — contour ploughing — so every furrow becomes a tiny dam instead of a drain. Never leave fields naked between seasons: mulch, stubble and cover crops are the soil's winter blanket. Rotate crops so the humus glue rebuilds. On grazing land, run only as many animals as the grass can feed — the veld's carrying capacity — and move the herd camp to camp so every pasture gets its rest.

Where a donga already gapes, slow the water down. Wire baskets packed with rocks — gabions — go across the channel like speed bumps: each storm's water stumbles, drops its load of soil, and slowly the canyon floor rises and grass can take hold again. Plant the raw banks, fence the healing land, and give it years. National teams are on this job — LandCare building the stitches, Working for Water pulling out the thirsty alien trees that elbow out the binding grass.

And how do the menders know where to stitch first? They look from space. Satellites tie every reading to map coordinates — spatially referenced data. Each image has a pixel size — spatial resolution, how small a thing it can see — and a set of light bands — spectral resolution; sick or missing vegetation glows differently in infrared, so bare ground betrays itself from orbit. Inside the computer map, a gully head is stored as a point, a river as a line, a risk zone as an area — and stacking the slope, rain and soil layers shows exactly which valley needs the gabions before the next storm.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — the stitches for fields and herds, the speed bumps in the donga, and the view from space matched to its terms. Get these and you hold both the wound and the cure.
