**Bianca:** Questions that come up a lot on this topic! First: erosion happens on its own in nature — rivers carved the Fish River Canyon long before people. So how can the exam blame humans for what water and wind were always going to do?

**Tutor:** The distinction the exam wants is between GEOLOGICAL erosion and ACCELERATED erosion, and the difference is rate. Natural, geological erosion runs at roughly the pace of soil formation — the landscape loses a little, builds a little, and the two stay near balance over centuries, which is why thick soils could accumulate at all. What land misuse does is snap that balance: strip the vegetation, compact the surface, and the SAME rain and wind suddenly remove soil tens or hundreds of times faster than it forms. That multiplied rate is accelerated erosion, and it is the human signature. Here is the courtroom way to see it: water and wind are the weapon, but bare ground is the opportunity, and land use supplied the opportunity. The proof is comparative — fence off a patch of overgrazed hillside, let grass return, and erosion on that patch collapses back toward the natural rate while the unfenced slope keeps gullying. Same rain on both. So when a question asks you to discuss physical causes, give them their due — steep slopes, intense Highveld thunderstorms, drought cycles — but land the point that physical agents mostly finish what land use starts. The canyon took millions of years; the donga behind the kraal took thirty. The speed is the confession.

**Bianca:** Next: why do dongas show up so much more in the former homelands? Some learners in class felt the textbook was unfairly blaming those communities' farming.

**Tutor:** The class is right to bristle, because the honest answer blames the system, not the farmers — and saying so precisely is worth marks. Rewind the history. The homeland policy assigned the majority of the population to a small fraction of the country's land — and largely the driest, steepest, thinnest-soiled fraction at that. People were not free to move to better land or to town; livestock were often a family's only bank account, so herds could not simply be cut. The result was arithmetic, not ignorance: too many people and too many hooves on too few hectares, with no fallow possible because every field had to feed someone every year. Overgrazing and overcultivation were engineered by the map itself. Add insecure land tenure — nobody invests in contour banks and gabions on land they may not hold next decade — and the soil paid the price for decades. That is why the syllabus insists on past AND present causes: the deepest scars are inherited from a political geography, while present-day pressures — poverty, firewood dependence, population growth — keep working on land already wounded. The exam-safe sentence: the erosion of the former homelands is the physical fingerprint of apartheid's land allocation, and any explanation that mentions only farming practice without the land question has missed the cause behind the cause.

**Bianca:** This one is practical: if a farmer can only afford ONE anti-erosion measure, which one gives the most protection, and how do we argue that in a question?

**Tutor:** The strongest single answer is: keep the ground covered — because cover attacks the erosion sequence at its first link. Walk the logic. Every water-erosion story starts with raindrop splash on BARE soil; if mulch, stubble or growing plants intercept the drops, the sequence never starts — no splash, no sheetwash, no rills, no donga. Cover also feeds humus, and humus binds crumbs that resist entrainment, so the measure strengthens the soil while shielding it. That is why the cheap, unglamorous options — leaving stubble after harvest, mulching, cover crops, resting overgrazed camps until grass returns — outperform expensive engineering per rand spent: they re-hire the bodyguard rather than replacing him with walls. Structural measures earn their place at specific points: contour ploughing where cropland slopes, and gabions once a gully already runs, because vegetation alone cannot heal a channel that concentrates a whole slope's runoff. So the argument structure for the exam: state the priority — prevention beats cure and cover is prevention; justify it by pointing at the sequence — cover breaks the chain at splash, the first link; then concede the exception — existing gullies need check dams and gabions to slow the water before plants can re-anchor. Priority, mechanism, exception: that is a full-marks paragraph, and it works for the farmer's budget too.

**Bianca:** Last one: the GIS terms feel bolted onto this topic — resolutions, points, lines, areas. How do we keep them straight, and why are they in the soil section at all?

**Tutor:** They are here because fighting erosion is a WHERE problem, and GIS is geography's machine for where. Fix the terms with one mission: a provincial team must decide where to build gabions first. Step one, they need data tied to places — every rainfall reading, gully position and field boundary carries coordinates. That is spatially referenced data, the phrase that simply means information pinned to the map. Step two, they order satellite imagery, and two questions decide its usefulness. How small a thing can one pixel see? That is SPATIAL resolution — a 10-metre pixel distinguishes fields and dongas; a 1-kilometre pixel sees only regions. Which kinds of light does the sensor record? That is SPECTRAL resolution — and the star band is infrared, because healthy vegetation bounces infrared back strongly, so bare and stressed ground stands out in infrared long before an ordinary photo shows trouble. Memory hook: spatial is size, spectral is colours — beyond the colours your eye has. Step three, they store what they find: each gully HEAD is a dot on the map — point data; each stream and contour is a drawn route — line data; each catchment or risk zone is a shaded shape — area or polygon data. Dot, route, shape. Step four is the payoff: overlay the slope layer, the rainfall layer, the soil layer and the bare-ground layer, and the places where all four scream at once are where the gabions go first. Run that mission in your head in the exam and every GIS term lands in its slot with a purpose attached.
