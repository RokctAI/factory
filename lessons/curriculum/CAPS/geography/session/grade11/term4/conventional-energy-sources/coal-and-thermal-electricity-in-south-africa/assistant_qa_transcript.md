**Bianca:** Questions that come up a lot on this one! First: if pumped storage schemes like Ingula use MORE electricity than they make, why on earth did Eskom build them? It sounds like a machine for losing money.

**Tutor:** It would be — if all electricity were worth the same at all hours, and the whole trick is that it is not. Demand breathes: at three in the morning the country sleeps, factories idle, and the coal fleet, which cannot easily be switched off and on, generates power almost nobody wants — cheap, nearly surplus energy. At six in the evening every stove, geyser and heater switches on at once, and the grid strains at its costliest, most fragile hour. Pumped storage arbitrages those two moments. At night it buys the cheap surplus to pump water uphill into the top reservoir — banking energy. At the evening peak it releases the water down through the turbines and sells that energy back precisely when the grid is desperate. Yes, about a quarter to a fifth of the energy is lost in the round trip — no store is perfect — but the alternative to losing 20 per cent is far worse: building entire extra power stations that run only two hours a day, or letting the peak collapse into load-shedding. There is a second service, too: a pumped storage station can go from standstill to full power in minutes, while a coal boiler takes many hours — so Ingula and Drakensberg are also the grid's emergency reserve, the fire extinguisher behind glass. So the exam formulation: pumped storage is not a source but a battery and a shock absorber — it converts cheap off-peak energy into precious peak energy and buys the grid time in a crisis, and that service is worth more than the energy it consumes.

**Bianca:** Next: the lesson says only about a third of the coal's energy becomes electricity. Where do the other two-thirds actually GO, and why can't engineers just fix that?

**Tutor:** Follow the energy and you will see the leak is built into physics, not carelessness. Start in the furnace: some heat escapes up the stack with the hot flue gases, and some leaves in the ash — small losses, a few per cent each. The giant loss happens at the cold end of the plant. To keep the turbine spinning, the spent steam on its far side must be condensed back into water — that is what the cooling towers do — and condensing steam means deliberately throwing its remaining heat away into the air. That rejected heat is the bulk of the missing two-thirds. Why not keep it? Because a steam cycle only extracts work from the DIFFERENCE between hot and cold: the turbine is a waterfall for heat, and a waterfall needs a bottom. Thermodynamics sets a hard ceiling on the fraction of heat that can become work between any two temperatures, and a practical coal plant's roughly 35 to 40 per cent sits not far under that ceiling. Engineers do push: superheating steam past 500 degrees and modern supercritical designs — Medupi and Kusile's boilers — squeeze out several extra percentage points, and some countries pipe the waste heat into city heating, which South African cities were never built for. But no engineering repeals the ceiling. The exam value of this: when you write that coal stations are about one-third efficient, you can now add WHY — the cooling towers are not a flaw in the design; they are the price the steam cycle pays to run at all.

**Bianca:** This one starts arguments: with all the smoke and the acid water, why doesn't South Africa just shut the coal stations down now? What is the strongest honest answer for an essay?

**Tutor:** The strongest honest answer holds four facts in one hand, and refuses to drop any of them. Fact one: dependence — roughly 80 per cent of our electricity is coal, and no replacement fleet of that size exists yet; switch coal off tonight and the country goes dark, with hospitals, water pumps and the whole economy behind it. Fact two: livelihoods — on the order of ninety thousand direct mining jobs, plus power station workers, rail workers and entire municipalities like Emalahleni whose rates, shops and schools stand on coal wages; an overnight shutdown is a regional depression by decree. Fact three: the costs are equally real — the Highveld's lungs, the acid mine drainage, the carbon bill arriving as border taxes — so doing nothing is also not free; it just sends the invoice to different people, mostly poor ones living near the stacks. Fact four: transitions can be sequenced — old stations retiring on schedule, renewables building at speed, workers retrained and towns diversified — which is exactly what the phrase JUST TRANSITION means, and international finance packages exist to fund it. So structure the essay as a rejection of both extremes: not 'shut it all now', which confuses the destination with the first step, and not 'keep it forever', which pretends the bills drawer is empty. The defensible line: run the countdown deliberately — retire, replace, retrain — fast enough to catch the climate and trade deadlines, slow enough not to break the grid or abandon coal towns. Examiners reward that sequenced answer far above either slogan.

**Bianca:** Last one: for the nuclear case study, learners keep asking the same thing — if Koeberg is clean and reliable, why did South Africa cancel the plan to build more reactors?

**Tutor:** Because the case study's own two columns collided with a budget, and walking through that collision is the best answer you can write. The pull column is real: Koeberg has supplied most of the Western Cape's baseload since 1984 with no operating carbon, and a fuel so concentrated that a fingertip pellet equals a tonne of coal. If those were the only facts, more reactors would be obvious. Now the push column, which decided the matter. Nuclear plants are the most expensive machines humanity builds: the proposed fleet carried a price tag in the region of a trillion rand, at a time when the fiscus was already strained — the deal collapsed substantially over affordability, and the courts also ruled the procurement process unlawful, which is its own lesson about governance. Build times compound the cost: a decade or more per station, meaning the money is spent long before the first kilowatt arrives — while wind and solar farms deliver power within two years of signature and their prices kept falling throughout the debate. Add the unsolved thousand-year waste question at Vaalputs, and rare-but-enormous accident risk pricing itself into insurance and public trust. Notice the intellectual honesty the syllabus is teaching: the same technology can be genuinely low-carbon AND genuinely unaffordable at a given moment — advantages and disadvantages are not for decoration; they are weighed, and the weighing has a result. Koeberg itself, meanwhile, gets life-extension work rather than siblings: keeping the reactor you have is far cheaper than building the one you don't. That is the full case study in four sentences, and it ends, like the whole session, at the same place — no single source wins; the mix does.
