Hey, it's Bianca — power stations, voltages, megawatts, and a nuclear case study on top. It sounds like engineering school, but the whole session is a kettle, two drawers and a fork in the road. Hold those three and you hold everything.

The kettle: coal ground to flour, burned at 1 400 degrees, boiling water in kilometres of tubes; steam spins the turbine, the turbine spins a magnet inside coils — that is all a generator is — and transformers push the voltage up to 400 000 volts because electricity travels far with fewer losses under high pressure. Say the chain: chemical, heat, motion, electricity — coal, steam, spin, spark. Two details examiners love: the big funnel towers make water vapour clouds, not smoke; and only about ONE THIRD of the coal's energy becomes electricity.

The two drawers: receipts and bills. Receipts — cheap power that built industry, day-and-night baseload, about ninety thousand mining jobs, exports through Richards Bay. Bills — Africa's biggest carbon emissions, the Highveld's dirty air, thirsty stations plus acid mine drainage leaking for a century, farmland lost to opencast pits, and load-shedding from a tired fleet. Emalahleni sits in both drawers: wages and asthma in one town. Open BOTH drawers in every answer.

The fork: coal-only is a countdown — old machines, no bank finance, carbon border taxes, water limits. The nuclear detour is Koeberg: two reactors near Cape Town since 1984, a fingertip pellet worth a tonne of coal, no carbon while running — but the slowest, priciest builds on earth and waste parked at Vaalputs for millennia. Verdict, every time: no single source; the mix.

Kettle, drawers, fork. Three pictures, one verdict. You've got this!
