# Part 1 — Expert

Every development goal of Term 3 runs on electricity, and South Africa makes most of its electricity one way: burning coal. This session maps the country's conventional energy sources — thermal and hydro; walks through how a coal-fired power station actually works; weighs coal's advantages and disadvantages, from cheap baseload to acid mine drainage and a polluted Highveld; and asks whether conventional sources can carry South Africa's long-term needs, with nuclear energy as the case study.

## Subtopic: South Africa's Energy Mix: Thermal and Hydro

South Africa is a COAL COUNTRY by geological gift: vast, shallow, cheaply mined coal seams concentrated in Mpumalanga, with more in Limpopo's Waterberg and the Free State. On maps of energy production, the pattern leaps out: a tight cluster of giant THERMAL power stations — Kendal, Matimba, Majuba, Lethabo, and the newest giants Medupi and Kusile — sitting directly on the coalfields, because it is far cheaper to send electricity down a wire than to rail coal across a country. Roughly 80 per cent of our electricity is coal-generated; Eskom's grid then carries it nationwide over high-voltage transmission lines, with the greatest flows toward Gauteng's cities and industry.

The graphs tell the same story a second way: a mix pie overwhelmingly coal-black, with slivers for nuclear — Koeberg near Cape Town, the continent's only nuclear power station — hydro, pumped storage and the growing renewables wedge examined next session.

HYDRO-ELECTRICITY, the other conventional source, uses falling water to spin turbines. South Africa's contribution is small for a blunt physical reason: we are a dry country with variable rivers — the water constraint that shaped every topic since Grade 10. True hydro stations at the Gariep and Vanderkloof dams on the Orange River help meet peaks; imports arrive from Cahora Bassa on the Zambezi in Mozambique, a reminder that Africa's great hydro potential lies north of us. PUMPED STORAGE schemes — Drakensberg, Palmiet, and Ingula on the escarpment — are a clever twist worth explaining precisely: two reservoirs, one high, one low. At night, when demand is low, spare grid power pumps water uphill; at the evening peak the water is released back down through the turbines. They consume more energy than they produce — they are not a source but a giant battery, storing cheap off-peak energy to sell the grid its own power back at peak.

The questions for this section are ready: why stations sit on coalfields, the mix percentages, and how pumped storage differs from true hydro.

## Subtopic: Inside a Coal-Fired Power Station

The exam asks for principles and processes, so walk the energy chain: chemical energy to heat to kinetic energy to electrical energy.

Coal arrives — often on a conveyor straight from a neighbouring mine — and is ground in mills to a powder fine as flour, because powder burns fast and completely. The PULVERISED coal is blown into a furnace shaped like a building-sized firebox, where it burns at around 1 400 degrees Celsius. The furnace walls are lined with kilometres of tubing filled with purified water: the BOILER. Water becomes superheated steam at enormous pressure and temperature — in modern stations, beyond 500 degrees Celsius.

That steam jets against the blades of a TURBINE, spinning the shaft at 3 000 revolutions per minute — kinetic energy. The shaft turns the GENERATOR, where a great electromagnet rotating inside coils of conductor induces electricity — Faraday's principle at national scale. TRANSFORMERS then step the voltage up to as much as 400 kilovolts for transmission, because high voltage cuts losses over distance; substations near users step it back down.

Two support systems complete the picture. COOLING: spent steam must be condensed back to water to repeat the cycle, done in the vast hyperbolic cooling towers whose white plumes are water vapour, not smoke — though dry-cooled stations like Kendal and Matimba, designed for water-scarce sites, use air instead, a South African speciality. ASH AND FLUE GAS: burning leaves ash — dumped and ideally partly recycled into cement — while flue gases exit the smokestacks carrying carbon dioxide, sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides, the environmental bill of the next subtopic.

Efficiency check for evaluation questions: only around a third of the coal's chemical energy leaves as electricity; most of the rest escapes as waste heat. Two-thirds of every coal train, burned to warm the sky.

Work through this section's questions now: the energy chain in order, the role of pulverising, and why transmission uses high voltage.

## Subtopic: Weighing Coal: Advantages and Disadvantages

The exam wants both columns argued, so build them properly.

Advantages. ABUNDANCE: South Africa's reserves are measured in decades at current use — energy security no import can match. COST: shallow seams beside power stations made our electricity historically among the world's cheapest, the foundation on which mines, smelters and industry were built. RELIABILITY: coal delivers BASELOAD — steady power day and night, windless or still — which is what keeps hospitals, smelters and traffic lights alive. JOBS: coal mining employs on the order of ninety thousand workers directly, with entire Mpumalanga towns — Emalahleni, literally the place of coal, Middelburg, Secunda — built on the industry. Plus exports: coal earns foreign exchange through Richards Bay, one of the world's great coal terminals.

Disadvantages, and they are heavy. CLIMATE: coal is the most carbon-intensive fuel, and it makes South Africa the largest carbon dioxide emitter in Africa and among the top per-capita emitters anywhere — a diplomatic and trade liability as carbon border taxes emerge. AIR: the Mpumalanga Highveld's cluster of stations makes its airshed one of the most sulphur dioxide- and nitrogen oxide-polluted on earth; respiratory disease in towns like Emalahleni is the human invoice. WATER: wet-cooled stations drink enormous volumes in a dry country, and ACID MINE DRAINAGE — water turned acidic and metal-laden in old workings — seeps from abandoned mines toward rivers, a bill that outlives the mine by a century. LAND: opencast mining strips farmland in the province that grows much of our maize, and rehabilitation obligations are too often dodged through abandoned, ownerless mines. HEALTH AND SAFETY: mining remains dangerous work. And RELIABILITY IN PRACTICE: an ageing, under-maintained fleet gave the country load-shedding — proof that even abundant coal fails without functioning machines.

The balanced sentence: coal built South Africa's industrial economy and still keeps its lights on, but the bills — carbon, air, water, land, health — are all overdue at once.

Attend to this section's questions: two advantages and two disadvantages with specifics, and the Emalahleni evidence on both sides.

## Subtopic: Can Conventional Sources Carry the Future? Nuclear as Case Study

The syllabus question: can South Africa meet its long-term energy needs conventionally? Weigh it.

The case for coal continuing: reserves remain for decades; the stations, mines, rail lines and skills exist; Medupi and Kusile — among the largest coal stations on earth — are designed to run to mid-century; and coal still anchors employment in Mpumalanga. The case against: the fleet outside the new giants is old and increasingly unreliable — load-shedding was the audit; new coal finance has dried up as banks refuse the climate risk; water constraints tighten; carbon border taxes threaten coal-made exports; and our Paris Agreement commitments require emissions to fall. Conventional-only is therefore less a plan than a countdown.

Enter the nuclear case study: KOEBERG, near Cape Town, two reactors, about 1 900 megawatts, supplying most of the Western Cape's baseload since 1984 — sited there deliberately, far from the coalfields, to anchor the grid's southern end. The principle: URANIUM FISSION — splitting heavy atoms releases heat, which raises steam, and from the turbine onward the station is a thermal plant like any other. The fuel is fantastically concentrated: one uranium pellet the size of a fingertip holds the energy of about a tonne of coal.

Nuclear's advantages: no carbon dioxide in operation; true baseload; tiny fuel volumes, so no trains of fuel and no ash mountains; and South Africa holds uranium reserves. Its disadvantages: the highest construction costs and longest build times in the energy world — the abandoned plan for new reactors collapsed over exactly this; radioactive WASTE that must be isolated for thousands of years, currently stored at Koeberg and at the Vaalputs facility in the Northern Cape; accident risk — vanishingly rare but Chernobyl- and Fukushima-scale when it comes; and Koeberg's own life-extension works show ageing applies to reactors too.

The defensible conclusion: conventional sources alone cannot carry South Africa's long-term needs — coal's constraints are structural, nuclear is low-carbon but slow and costly to build — so the realistic path is a MIX, which is exactly where the next session picks up.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same session with a kettle, a bill drawer and a fork in the road. Nothing left out.

## Subtopic: The Kettle the Size of a Suburb

Strip away the chimneys and pylons, and a coal power station is your kettle, scaled up until it powers a nation.

At home: element heats water, steam rattles the lid. In Mpumalanga: coal ground to flour-fine powder burns at 1 400 degrees, and the kettle is kilometres of water-filled tubes lining the furnace walls. The steam does not rattle a lid — it blasts a turbine's blades into a 3 000-revolutions-per-minute spin, and that spinning shaft turns a giant magnet inside coils of wire, which is all a generator is: motion becoming electricity. Then transformers pump the voltage sky-high — up to 400 000 volts — because electricity travels long wires with less loss at high pressure, and Gauteng is far away.

Three homely details finish the picture. The huge funnel-shaped towers? They are for cooling the used steam back into water — and the white clouds above them are water vapour, not smoke; the actual smoke leaves by the tall thin stacks. The stations huddle ON the coalfields because wire is cheaper than rail: send the electricity travelling, not the coal. And the sobering one: of every three coal trucks burned, only about one truck's worth becomes electricity — the rest is heat lost to the sky. Remember the chain for the exam: chemical to heat to motion to electricity — coal, steam, spin, spark.

Nice work — the questions for this piece are with you now: the kettle chain, the towers, and the one-truck-in-three.

## Subtopic: The Drawer of Unpaid Bills

Coal deserves honest bookkeeping: one drawer of receipts for what it gave us, one drawer of bills still unpaid.

The receipts. Cheap power from shallow coal built the mines, smelters and factories — the industrial South Africa story. Steady BASELOAD power, day and night, wind or no wind, keeps hospitals and traffic lights alive. Around ninety thousand families eat from coal mining wages, and whole towns — Emalahleni, the place of coal — stand on it. Coal exports through Richards Bay bring in foreign money. Real receipts; respect them in any essay.

Now open the bills drawer. The climate bill: coal makes us Africa's biggest carbon emitter, and the world is starting to tax that at the border. The air bill: the Highveld's cluster of stations gives its towns some of the dirtiest air on earth — children's lungs in Emalahleni are part of the price of the lights. The water bill: thirsty wet-cooled stations in a dry country, plus the nastiest line item — acid mine drainage, old flooded mines slowly leaking acid, metal-heavy water toward rivers, a bill that keeps arriving a century after the mine closed. The land bill: opencast pits chewing the same Mpumalanga farmland that grows our maize. And the reliability bill: an old, tired fleet that gave the whole country load-shedding.

Notice Emalahleni appears in both drawers — pay slips and asthma pumps in the same town. That is the honest picture: coal built the house AND is quietly burning some of it down. Both drawers, always, in any answer.

Your questions for this section are ready now: three receipts, three bills, and the town in both drawers.

## Subtopic: The Fork in the Road

So can South Africa just keep driving down the coal road? Look at the road signs.

Sign one: the coal in the ground lasts decades more — the tank is not empty. Sign two: the trucks are old — outside the two new giants, Medupi and Kusile, the fleet is worn, and load-shedding was its breakdown report. Sign three: the banks have stopped lending for new coal — climate risk has made it money nobody will finance. Sign four: the world is starting to charge carbon taxes at its borders, which threatens everything we make with coal power. Add the water shortage and our own climate promises, and coal-only is not a road — it is a countdown.

The marked detour is nuclear, and the case study is Koeberg, near Cape Town: two reactors keeping most of the Western Cape's lights on since 1984. Its trick is concentration — splitting uranium atoms releases so much heat that a fuel pellet the size of your fingertip equals about a tonne of coal; from the steam onward it is just the kettle again. The pull: no carbon while running, true day-and-night baseload, no ash mountains, and South Africa even mines uranium. The push: nuclear stations are the most expensive, slowest builds in the energy world — our own plan for new reactors collapsed over the price tag; the waste stays dangerous for thousands of years, parked at Koeberg and at Vaalputs in the Northern Cape; and disasters, though vanishingly rare, are enormous when they come.

So the exam-safe verdict at the fork: neither coal alone nor any single source can carry the country's future — coal is counting down, nuclear is clean but slow and dear — so the answer is a MIX, and the sun-and-wind lane of that mix is the next session's whole story.
