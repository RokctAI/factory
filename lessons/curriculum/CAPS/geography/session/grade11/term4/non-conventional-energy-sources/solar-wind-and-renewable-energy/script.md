# Part 1 — Expert

The previous session ended at a fork: coal counting down, nuclear slow and costly. This session drives the other lane — NON-CONVENTIONAL energy: what qualifies, why South Africa is unusually well endowed; solar energy with South African and world examples; wind energy the same way; and the future of non-conventional energy in this country, weighed for its effects on economy and environment.

## Subtopic: What Counts as Non-conventional, and Why South Africa Is Blessed

NON-CONVENTIONAL energy sources are the alternatives to the established fossil-and-nuclear order — solar, wind, and their smaller siblings: biomass and biogas, geothermal, wave and tidal power, and small-scale hydro. Most are RENEWABLE — their flows replenish naturally — which fossil fuels are not; note the terms are cousins, not twins, since a source can be non-conventional without being large-scale renewable, like biogas from a feedlot digester.

Two properties define the family. First, the FUEL IS FREE — sunshine and wind arrive without mining, railing or importing; the cost sits almost entirely in building the harvesting machine. Second, the flows are INTERMITTENT — the sun sets, the wind drops — so unlike coal's steady baseload, non-conventional supply must be paired with storage, backup or a smart mix. Hold both properties; every advantage and disadvantage in this session grows from them.

Now the endowment, because South Africa's is genuinely exceptional. Most of the interior receives more than 2 500 hours of sunshine per year, with daily solar radiation among the highest reliably measured anywhere — the Northern Cape is, by physical measure, one of the world's premium solar territories. The coasts — Eastern Cape, Western Cape, and the west coast north of Cape Town — carry strong, usable winds off two oceans. Compare Germany: cloudier than our worst province, yet a world solar leader. Whatever has slowed South Africa's build, it was never the resource.

The delivery mechanism to name: the RENEWABLE ENERGY INDEPENDENT POWER PRODUCER PROCUREMENT PROGRAMME — REIPPPP — under which private developers bid competitively to build wind and solar farms and sell the power to the grid under long contracts. Since 2011 it has drawn hundreds of billions of rand in private investment and built over a hundred plants, and its auctions drove prices down until new wind and solar became cheaper per kilowatt-hour than new coal — the quiet economic reversal behind this whole topic.

The questions for this section are ready: define the family, the two properties, the endowment evidence, and REIPPPP's role.

## Subtopic: Solar Energy: South African and World Examples

Solar arrives in two working technologies, and the exam expects the distinction.

PHOTOVOLTAIC — PV — panels convert sunlight DIRECTLY into electricity in semiconductor cells: no moving parts, no water, no noise; they work anywhere the sun shines, from a calculator to a farm of a million panels; output follows daylight instantly, peaking at noon and dying at dusk. CONCENTRATED SOLAR POWER — CSP — is the other route: fields of mirrors — heliostats — track the sun and focus its heat onto a receiver, where a fluid, often molten salt, is heated to hundreds of degrees; the heat raises steam and, from the turbine onward, the station is the familiar thermal kettle. CSP's decisive gift is STORAGE: hot salt stays hot for hours in insulated tanks, so a CSP plant keeps generating into the night — solar power after sunset, answering intermittency directly.

South African examples, concentrated where the sun is: the Northern Cape. Around Upington and De Aar stand PV farms among the country's largest; the CSP flagships cluster nearby — Khi Solar One's tower outside Upington, the KaXu and Xina parabolic-trough plants at Pofadder, and REDSTONE, the tower plant near Postmasburg with twelve hours of molten-salt storage — a solar station built to serve the evening peak. At the household scale, rooftop PV boomed during load-shedding, and SOLAR WATER HEATERS — simple panels that heat water directly — remain the cheapest solar of all, cutting a geyser's share of a household bill without any electronics.

World examples for scale: China dominates both panel manufacturing and installed capacity, with desert solar farms visible from orbit; Germany proved policy can beat cloud; Morocco's Noor complex at Ouarzazate is Africa's CSP giant, powering cities after dark from the Sahara's edge.

Work through this section's questions now: PV against CSP, the storage difference, and one South African and one world example of each.

## Subtopic: Wind Energy: South African and World Examples

A modern wind turbine is a simple sentence written very large: moving air turns aerodynamic blades, the blades turn a shaft, the shaft turns a generator — kinetic energy to electricity, no heat, no steam, no water. The machines are enormous for a physical reason: wind power rises with the SWEPT AREA of the blades and with the CUBE of wind speed — double the wind, eight times the power — so towers reach past a hundred metres into faster, steadier air, and blades stretch longer than a rugby field. Siting is everything: coastal ridges, open plateaus, gaps that funnel wind.

South Africa's wind country is the coastal belt. The EASTERN CAPE leads: the Jeffreys Bay wind farm, among the country's largest, its turbines lining the N2 near the surf town; the Cookhouse farm inland of Gqeberha; and clusters around the Coega industrial zone. The WESTERN CAPE follows — farms at Caledon and Darling among others — and the NORTHERN CAPE's west coast belt is filling. Under REIPPPP, wind has become one of the cheapest sources of new power in the country, and each farm brings construction jobs, land rentals to farmers — turbines and sheep share the same veld comfortably — and mandated community revenue shares over twenty-year contracts.

World examples: DENMARK, the pioneer, generates roughly half its electricity from wind and exports its turbine industry — proof a small country can lead a global technology; CHINA hosts the largest installed fleet; the NORTH SEA nations build offshore farms in shallow seas where winds blow harder and steadier than on land — offshore being the frontier South Africa has yet to enter.

Wind's own two-column check: clean, fuel-free, quick to build, cheap at auction — against intermittency, visual and noise objections from neighbours, bird and bat collisions demanding careful siting, and the need for backup when the air stills.

Attend to this section's questions: the cube law, two South African farms with provinces, Denmark's share, and wind's costs.

## Subtopic: The Future: Economy, Environment and the Honest Obstacles

The syllabus asks for the FUTURE of non-conventional energy in South Africa and its effects on ECONOMY and ENVIRONMENT — an evaluation, so weigh it.

Economic effects, positive column: new wind and solar are now the CHEAPEST new power available here, so every renewable megawatt restrains future tariffs; REIPPPP has drawn hundreds of billions of rand in investment; construction and maintenance jobs spread to rural provinces that industrial South Africa passed by — the Northern Cape hosts billions in plant it could never have attracted otherwise; community trusts channel revenue into local development; and green industry — panel assembly, tower manufacture — offers a manufacturing ladder if localisation holds. Negative column: coal employs on the order of ninety thousand miners concentrated in Mpumalanga towns with few alternatives, and renewables employ fewer people per plant once built, mostly elsewhere — the transition redistributes work across provinces and skills, which is why the phrase JUST TRANSITION exists: retraining, pensions and new industry for coal country, funded partly by international climate finance packages.

Environmental effects: overwhelmingly positive against the coal baseline — no carbon dioxide, no sulphur dioxide or Highveld smog, and almost no water use, a decisive virtue in a dry country. But honest accounting lists the costs: large land footprints, though farming continues under turbines; bird and bat mortality without careful siting; manufacturing and eventual disposal of panels, blades and batteries carry their own material and mining demands.

The obstacles that will decide the pace: the GRID — the best sun and wind lie in the Northern and Eastern Cape, far from Gauteng's demand, and transmission lines are full; building thousands of kilometres of new line is now the binding constraint. INTERMITTENCY — managed by the mix: wind blows at night when solar sleeps, CSP and batteries store, pumped storage shifts, and gas or hydro can peak. POLICY CERTAINTY — investment flows when auction rounds run on schedule and stalls when they stall.

The defensible conclusion: South Africa's non-conventional future is not a question of resource — ours is world-class — but of delivery: grid, storage, policy and a just transition for coal country. The sun and wind will take a growing share; the argument is only about speed.

Turn to the closing questions of this part: both economic columns, the environmental balance, and the three obstacles.

## Subtopic: Fuel That Falls From the Sky

Imagine a filling station where the petrol is free — forever. You pay to build the pumps, and then the fuel just arrives, every day, at no charge. That is the entire economic logic of non-conventional energy: sunshine and wind cost nothing; only the catching equipment costs money. Coal must be mined, railed and burned every single day it works; a solar panel is paid for once and then harvests free fuel for twenty-five years.

There is a catch, and it is honest to name it immediately: the free fuel keeps its own hours. The sun clocks out at dusk; the wind takes unannounced breaks. Coal's great virtue was burning steadily through the night — so the free-fuel station needs a storage tank or a partner. Hold that trade-off: free but moody, against costly but steady. The whole modern energy argument is those five words.

Now the almost unfair part: South Africa's sky pours out more free fuel than nearly anywhere on earth. The Northern Cape gets over 2 500 hours of sunshine a year — world-premium sun — while two oceans drive reliable wind onto our coasts. Germany, under its grey blanket of cloud, built a world-leading solar industry with HALF our sunshine. We have the fuel; the race is about pumps.

And the pumps are being built: under the REIPPPP programme, private companies bid to build wind and solar farms, over a hundred plants stand already — and the auctions pushed prices so low that new sun and wind now undercut new coal per unit. The free fuel became the cheap fuel too.

Nice work — the questions for this piece are with you now: the free filling station, the moody hours, and the endowment.

## Subtopic: Sun Traps and Wind Catchers

Three machines harvest the free fuel, and each has a one-line secret.

The PV panel is a sunlight-to-electricity converter with NO moving parts — no steam, no spin, silent as a windowpane. Sun in, current out, strongest at noon, asleep by dusk. That is what boomed on South African rooftops through load-shedding, and what carpets the mega-farms around Upington and De Aar.

The CSP plant is a field of mirrors playing the magnifying-glass trick you know from childhood — thousands of mirrors steering sunlight onto one tower until the target glows. The heat cooks molten salt to hundreds of degrees, the salt boils water, and from the steam onward it is the old familiar kettle. But here is CSP's superpower: hot salt in a giant thermos stays hot for HOURS — so Redstone near Postmasburg, with twelve hours of storage, serves supper-time electricity from lunchtime sun. Solar after sunset: intermittency answered directly.

The wind turbine is a pinwheel the height of a skyscraper, and its secret is the cube law: power grows with the CUBE of wind speed — double the wind, EIGHT times the power. That is why the towers climb past a hundred metres into faster air and why the blades outstretch a rugby field, and why they stand where they stand: Jeffreys Bay's turbines beside the N2, Cookhouse on the Eastern Cape plateau, Caledon in the Western Cape — coastal wind country. Sheep graze happily underneath; farmers collect rent; the community holds a revenue share.

World name-drops for the essay: China builds and hosts the most of everything solar and wind; Denmark runs about half its country on wind; Morocco's Noor complex lights Saharan-edge cities after dark with mirrors.

## Subtopic: The Relay Race and the Baton

Think of South Africa's energy future as a relay race. Coal has run a long, strong, dirty leg — and the runner is tired: old stations, no new finance, the world taxing carbon at its borders. Sun and wind are the fresh runners warmed up on the infield — cheaper per unit now than new coal, clean, and fed by the best sky in the world. The race's hard part is the BATON PASS, and three things can fumble it.

Fumble one: the extension cord. The best sun and wind live in the Northern and Eastern Cape; the demand lives in Gauteng — and the transmission lines between them are full. Thousands of kilometres of new line are now the real bottleneck; power with no wire to travel is power wasted.

Fumble two: the night shift. The new runners keep odd hours, so the grid needs storage — CSP's hot salt, batteries, pumped storage pushing water uphill — plus the lucky fact that coastal wind often blows at night while solar sleeps. A well-designed MIX covers the clock; no single source has to.

Fumble three: the coal towns. Around ninety thousand mining jobs and whole municipalities — Emalahleni, Secunda — stand on the old runner's leg. Pass the baton carelessly and those towns fall; pass it justly — retraining, pensions, new factories, international transition money — and the race gains a province instead of losing one. That is what JUST TRANSITION means, and examiners reward the phrase with its meaning attached.

The finish-line verdict, honestly weighed: environment — sun and wind win by a distance, no carbon, no smog, almost no water, minus real but manageable costs in land, birds and panel disposal. Economy — cheaper power, huge investment, rural jobs, against redistribution pain in coal country. The future is not in doubt about direction, only about speed — and the speed depends on wires, storage and justice.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — the three fumbles, the mix that covers the clock, and the verdict with both columns. Get these and the whole energy debate rounds off in your favour.
