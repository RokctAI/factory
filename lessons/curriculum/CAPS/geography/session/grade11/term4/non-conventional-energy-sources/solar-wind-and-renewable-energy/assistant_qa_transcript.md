**Bianca:** Questions that come up a lot on this topic! First one, and it feels like the obvious one: if the Northern Cape has world-premium sun and wind and solar are now the cheapest new power, why isn't the whole country already running on renewables? What is actually stopping it?

**Tutor:** The blunt answer is wires, and it surprises everyone the first time. A power plant is useless unless its electricity can travel to the people who want it, and South Africa's transmission grid was built for the old geography of energy: thick, strong lines radiating out of Mpumalanga's coalfields, because that is where the power came from for a century. The new geography is the opposite corner of the country — the Northern Cape's sun, the Eastern and Western Cape's wind — and the lines out of those provinces are thin and, by now, essentially full. Recent bidding rounds saw excellent, cheap projects turned away not because the power was unwanted but because there was no capacity left on the wires to carry it. Fixing this means building thousands of kilometres of new high-voltage transmission line and dozens of substations — years of steel, servitudes and construction — which is why the lesson calls the grid the binding constraint. Add two supporting reasons: policy rhythm, because private investment surges when auction rounds run on schedule and stalls when they drift; and the night problem, because until storage scales up, every solar megawatt needs a partner for the dark hours. But if the exam asks for the single biggest obstacle, name the grid — the fuel is world-class and now the cheapest available; what South Africa is short of is the extension cord.

**Bianca:** Next: my uncle says renewables can never run a country because the sun goes out at night. The lesson says intermittency is manageable. Who is right, and how do we argue it properly?

**Tutor:** Your uncle has correctly named the problem and wrongly declared it unsolvable, and the proper argument walks through the toolbox. Start by conceding the truth: PV output dies at dusk, wind takes unannounced breaks, and no amount of enthusiasm changes that — a grid of nothing but solar panels would indeed fail at supper time. Now the tools, each real and running today. Tool one: geographic and technological spread. Coastal wind often blows at night while solar sleeps, and wind in the Eastern Cape does not still at the same moment as wind in the Western Cape — a portfolio across space and type is far steadier than any single farm. Tool two: storage that already works at scale. CSP plants like Redstone hold twelve hours of heat in molten salt, serving the evening peak from afternoon sun; pumped storage schemes like Ingula bank cheap midday energy by pushing water uphill; and grid batteries are being added every round. Tool three: dispatchable partners — hydro, gas peakers, and for now the existing coal fleet — filling the residual gaps while they shrink. Tool four: demand management, shifting flexible loads like water heating into sunny hours. Then close with the living proof: Denmark runs about half a country on wind and its lights stay on, because it planned the mix. The exam-grade sentence: intermittency is a genuine property of each renewable SOURCE, but not of a well-designed SYSTEM — the system is engineered around it, and the engineering already exists.

**Bianca:** This one is close to home: my cousin works at a Mpumalanga mine, and his town is scared of this whole topic. When we write about the transition, how do we handle the coal jobs question honestly?

**Tutor:** Handle it by refusing to wave it away, because the fear is legitimate and the syllabus's own phrase — just transition — exists precisely because of it. Set out the facts on both sides. Coal directly employs on the order of ninety thousand mineworkers, plus power station staff, rail crews and the entire ecosystems of towns like Emalahleni and Secunda — municipal rates, shops, schools, taxi routes, all standing on coal wages. Renewables will not replace those jobs one-for-one in the same places: once built, a solar farm needs few permanent staff, and the construction booms happen mostly in the Northern, Eastern and Western Cape — different provinces, different skills. Pretending otherwise loses both marks and trust. Now the other side: the coal decline is coming REGARDLESS of renewables — the fleet is ageing toward retirement, banks refuse new coal finance, and carbon border taxes threaten coal-made exports — so the real choice is not 'transition or no transition' but managed or unmanaged. A just transition is the managed version, and it has concrete content you should list: retraining programmes for mine and plant workers; early pensions where retraining is unrealistic; deliberately locating new industry — battery plants, panel assembly, turbine towers, mine rehabilitation work itself — in coal country; community ownership stakes in new plants; and the international climate finance packages pledged specifically to fund all of this in South Africa. The essay verdict: the energy transition passes the environmental test easily; whether it passes the social test depends on whether the just transition is funded and delivered where your cousin lives — and writing that sentence shows the examiner you understand both the geography and the people in it.

**Bianca:** Last one: for the exam, how do we keep PV and CSP straight under pressure? Half the class swapped them in the last test.

**Tutor:** Use their names literally and attach one image to each, and the confusion dies. PHOTOVOLTAIC: photo means light, voltaic means electricity — light becomes electricity DIRECTLY, inside a semiconductor sandwich, with no heat step, no steam, no moving parts. Image: a silent black windowpane on a roof. Its behaviour follows from the directness — output tracks the light instantly, peaks at noon, vanishes at dusk, and it scales from a calculator strip to the mega-farms around Upington and De Aar. CONCENTRATED SOLAR POWER: the name says concentrate — thousands of mirrors steer sunlight onto one receiver, like the childhood magnifying-glass trick grown enormous. Image: a glowing tower in a field of mirrors. Nothing electrical happens at the mirrors; they only gather HEAT, cooking molten salt to hundreds of degrees, and from there it is the ordinary thermal kettle — salt boils water, steam spins turbine, turbine spins generator. And that heat step is CSP's whole advantage: heat is cheap to store, so the salt sits in an insulated tank and keeps making steam after sunset — Redstone's twelve hours meaning supper-time power from lunchtime sun — which PV cannot do without separate batteries. Quick self-test the night before: which one has moving parts? CSP — tracking mirrors, turbine, generator. Which one works on your roof? PV. Which one keeps going after dark? CSP, thanks to the thermos. Direct light-to-power windowpane against mirror-heat kettle with a thermos — anchor those two pictures and no exam pressure will swap them again.
