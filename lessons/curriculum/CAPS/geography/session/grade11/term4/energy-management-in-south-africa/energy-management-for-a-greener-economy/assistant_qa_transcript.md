**Bianca:** Questions that come up a lot on this final topic! First: the lesson keeps saying the cheapest kilowatt-hour is the one never used. But if everyone uses less electricity, doesn't Eskom earn less and get into even deeper trouble?

**Tutor:** You have spotted a real tension — utilities do earn by selling — but walk the system and the saving still wins, for three reasons. First, timing: the grid's crisis has never been total annual demand; it is the PEAK — those few evening hours when everything switches on together and the system must either meet the spike or shed load. Peak demand forces the most expensive responses: burning diesel in open-cycle gas turbines at many times coal's cost, or load-shedding, whose cost lands on the whole economy in spoiled stock, stopped factories and lost shifts. Efficiency and load-shifting flatten exactly that spike, so they save the utility its most expensive hours even while trimming its sales. Second, capital: every megawatt of avoided demand is a megawatt of power station, transmission line and substation that never needs financing — and in a country whose builds run late and over budget, avoided construction is the most reliable project there is. Third, the alternative arithmetic: when supply falls short, the utility does not merely lose the sale — it pays penalties in diesel, maintenance strain and political crisis. There is also a fairness point the exam likes: reduced waste lowers bills for households who spend painful shares of income on energy, which serves the development goals of Term 3 directly. So the honest formulation: efficiency does trim revenue per customer, but it rescues the utility from its costliest hours and its heaviest capital burden — which is why Eskom itself has run demand-management programmes begging customers to use less at peak. A business that pays you to buy less of its product is telling you where its true costs live.

**Bianca:** Next: why does the syllabus split responsibilities between government, business and individuals? Surely energy is government's job — they run Eskom, they make the laws.

**Tutor:** The split exists because each of the three holds levers the other two physically cannot reach, and the topic's design is to make you name them. Test it by elimination. Could government do it alone? It can write building regulations, tax carbon, run the IRP and the auctions — but it does not operate the factories that consume most of the country's electricity, and it cannot stand in sixty million kitchens at six in the evening deciding what switches on. The state sets the field; it does not play every position. Could business do it alone? Firms can audit, insulate and cover their roofs with panels — but without government's grid, auctions and rules, their power has nowhere to wheel and their competitors can undercut them by polluting freely; and business has no reach into household behaviour either. Could individuals do it alone? Households drive the evening peak and their choices sum to national numbers — but no household can build a transmission line, restructure Eskom or impose a carbon tax. Now notice the deeper pattern: the three levels LOCK TOGETHER. The government's appliance label only works when the individual reads it; the business's rooftop solar only pays because government reformed licensing; the individual's geyser timer only matters because millions of others tick alongside it under tariffs government designed. That interlocking is why the syllabus phrase is responsibilities of government, businesses AND individuals — an essay that covers one level is a third of an answer. Structure yours as three paragraphs, one lever set each, and close with the interlock sentence: the greener economy is not anyone's job; it is everyone's overlapping job, and the overlaps are where it either happens or fails.

**Bianca:** This one is practical: a question asks us to advise a family on cutting their electricity bill, biggest savings first. What is the correct order, and why does order matter?

**Tutor:** Order matters because marks reward proportion — advising someone to unplug their phone charger before touching the geyser is arithmetic malpractice. Run the audit as the money runs. Target one, always: WATER HEATING. The geyser is the hungriest machine in the house, often around 40 per cent of the bill, so it gets the full ladder — thermostat down to 60 degrees, a geyser blanket on the tank and pipes, a timer so it heats only before use, and, if the family can invest, a solar water heater or heat pump, after which the sun does the house's heaviest job free. Target two: SPACE HEATING AND COOKING in season — seal draughts and insulate before switching anything on, wear the jersey, heat the person not the room; in the kitchen, lids on pots, pressure cooker for the long simmers, microwave for the small jobs, full loads only in the washing machine. Target three: LIGHTING — swap every remaining old bulb for an LED, same light for about a tenth of the power; it is not the biggest saving but it is the easiest, cheapest and most certain, which is why it is everyone's first move in practice. Target four: the SMALL DRAINS — standby lights glowing all night, the second fridge running half-empty in the garage; individually tiny, collectively a steady leak. And add the family's petrol to the answer — lift clubs and walking short trips are energy management with a fuel gauge. Then the mark-winning extra: SHIFT as well as save. Moving the geyser and washing off the evening peak helps the national grid even before total use falls. Close your advice with the ranking sentence itself: start where the energy is — geyser first, red-eyed standby lights last — because that single sentence shows the examiner you understood proportion, not just a list.

**Bianca:** Last one: what actually IS a greener economy? Some of us wrote 'an economy with more trees' in the practice test and got nothing.

**Tutor:** The trees answer fails because it describes scenery, and the term describes a RELATIONSHIP — between an economy's growth and its environmental cost. Here is the definition that scores: a greener economy is one that keeps growing — producing goods, services and jobs — while its energy use, resource consumption and emissions SHRINK. The technical word is decoupling: prosperity rising while pollution falls, the two curves separating on the graph. Make it concrete with the counter-example first: South Africa's twentieth-century economy was the opposite — every rand of growth dragged coal smoke behind it, because mining, smelting and heavy industry ran on the world's cheapest, dirtiest electricity. Growth and emissions were welded together. Greening the economy means cutting that weld, and this whole session listed the cutting tools: efficiency, so each product needs less energy — the invisible power station; cleaner supply, so each kilowatt-hour carries less carbon — the sun and wind of the previous session; rules and prices that make waste expensive — carbon tax, building standards, labels; and new green industries — panel assembly, turbine towers, mine rehabilitation — so the transition creates the jobs it disturbs, which is where the just transition and sustainable lifestyles plug in. Note what the definition does NOT say: it does not say smaller economy, poorer people or no industry — that mistake is called degrowth in other debates, and it is not what CAPS asks. The examiner's version in one line: a greener economy grows MORE while burning LESS — and if you attach one number, offer the export angle: with carbon border taxes arriving, greener is no longer only cleaner; it is how South African products stay sellable abroad. Definition, decoupling, tools, trade — four sentences, full marks.
