# Part 1 — Expert

The energy topic closes where the syllabus points it: MANAGEMENT. Sources were the last two sessions' question; this one asks how South Africa manages energy toward a GREENER ECONOMY and SUSTAINABLE LIFESTYLES — and it assigns the responsibilities by name: government, businesses and individuals. The logic throughout: the cheapest, cleanest kilowatt-hour is the one never used.

## Subtopic: Why Energy Must Be Managed

Begin with the case for management, because it carries the marks that explain everything after it.

South Africa is an energy-INTENSIVE economy: mining, smelting and heavy industry consume electricity at rates high for our income level, and roughly 80 per cent of that electricity is coal-made. Three pressures make managing this unavoidable. SUPPLY: the load-shedding years proved demand can outrun a strained system — and the fastest relief was never a new station, which takes a decade, but lower and smarter demand, which takes a season. CLIMATE AND TRADE: as one of the world's most carbon-intensive economies per unit of output, South Africa faces both its Paris Agreement commitments and the arrival of CARBON BORDER TAXES, under which exports made with dirty electricity pay penalties at rich-country borders — meaning our coal-heavy grid is becoming a tax on every exported ton of steel and fruit. EQUITY: millions of households spend painful shares of income on energy, and clinics and schools fail without reliable power, so wasted energy is development lost.

Now the central concept pair. A GREENER ECONOMY is one that grows while its resource use and emissions shrink — decoupling prosperity from pollution; energy is its main lever because electricity touches every sector. SUSTAINABLE LIFESTYLES are the household-scale mirror: patterns of consumption that could be generalised to everyone without exhausting the planet. Between economy and lifestyle sits the topic's master idea: ENERGY EFFICIENCY — getting the same service, light, heat, motion, from less energy. Efficiency is called the invisible power station: saving one power station's worth of demand is cheaper, faster and cleaner than building one, and it emits nothing at all.

The questions for this section are ready: the three pressures, the greener economy definition, and the invisible power station.

## Subtopic: Government's Responsibilities

Government holds the levers no one else can pull: law, tax, planning and its own vast operations.

PLANNING: the INTEGRATED RESOURCE PLAN — the IRP — is the official electricity roadmap, setting how much coal retires and how much solar, wind, storage, gas and nuclear gets built by which year; investors, Eskom and municipalities all steer by it, which is why keeping it updated and honest is itself an energy policy. Alongside it, government runs the REIPPPP auctions that procure renewable plants, and drives the restructuring of Eskom — separating transmission into an independent grid company so that many producers can compete fairly on one set of wires.

PRICING AND TAX: the CARBON TAX, first of its kind in Africa, makes emitters pay per ton of carbon dioxide, nudging industry toward cleaner processes; levies fund efficiency programmes; and tariff design can reward off-peak use — pricing the truth about what energy costs when.

RULES AND STANDARDS: building regulations require new buildings to meet energy performance standards — insulation, efficient lighting, solar water heating; appliance labelling lets buyers compare the running cost of fridges and geysers; minimum efficiency standards ban the worst appliances outright. These quiet rules save more energy than most headline projects.

DIRECT DELIVERY: government subsidised the rollout of millions of SOLAR WATER HEATERS and efficient lighting to poor households — cutting bills and peak demand together; free basic electricity supports the poorest; and the JUST ENERGY TRANSITION PARTNERSHIP channels international finance — pledged in the tens of billions of dollars — into grid building, coal-town retraining and green industry.

And EXAMPLE: the state is the country's biggest landlord and fleet owner; retrofitting public buildings, efficient streetlights and clean procurement move markets by themselves.

## Subtopic: Business's Responsibilities

Business consumes most of South Africa's energy, so its choices weigh heaviest — and increasingly, they pay.

EFFICIENCY AS PROFIT: an ENERGY AUDIT walks a firm's operations metering where the joules actually go — compressed-air leaks, worn motors, heat escaping uninsulated pipes — and ranks the fixes by payback. Efficient motors with variable-speed drives, LED lighting in warehouses and offices, heat recovered from furnaces and re-used, insulation everywhere: the typical audit finds double-digit percentage savings, meaning firms were paying for energy that did nothing. Since load-shedding priced the cost of POWER FAILURE into every business plan, efficiency plus backup became survival planning, not virtue.

SELF-GENERATION: the rooftop revolution — shopping centres, factories, farms and mines covering roofs and land with PV; licensing reforms opened the door to large private plants, and mining houses now build wind and solar farms measured in hundreds of megawatts. Every panel eases the grid and cuts the firm's carbon count — and WHEELING lets a company generate power at a sunny site and consume it, via the grid, at another.

SUPPLY-CHAIN PRESSURE: exporters must now report their products' CARBON FOOTPRINT — the emissions embedded in making them — because European carbon border rules tax it; banks and investors screen for climate risk; and green certification sells. Cleaner energy has become a condition of market access, which converts sustainability from ethics into strategy.

AND HONESTY: the counterweight duty is avoiding GREENWASHING — marketing an unchanged, polluting operation as eco-friendly. Real management is measured: baseline, target, audit, report. Firms that merely rebrand undermine both the climate and the customers' trust the term greener economy depends on.

Attend to this section's questions: the audit logic, self-generation and wheeling, and the carbon footprint as market access.

## Subtopic: Individuals and Sustainable Lifestyles

Individuals command the smallest units of energy but the largest number of them — and households drive the evening peak that has always been the grid's breaking point.

Start where the money is: WATER HEATING. The electric geyser is the single hungriest machine in a typical home, often around 40 per cent of the bill. The efficiency ladder: turn the thermostat to 60 degrees, insulate the tank and pipes with a geyser blanket, put the geyser on a timer so it heats only before use — and, when possible, replace the element's work with a SOLAR WATER HEATER or heat pump, after which the sun does the heaviest job in the house. LIGHTING is the cheap victory: an LED gives the same light as the old incandescent bulb for about a tenth of the electricity and lasts years longer. COOKING AND HEATING: lids on pots, pressure cookers, microwaves over ovens for small portions; in winter, insulate and seal before heating, wear the jersey, warm the person not the whole room. APPLIANCES: read the energy label before buying; run full loads; kill standby power, the small red-eyed drain that never sleeps. TRANSPORT: lift clubs, public transport, walking the short trips — fuel is energy too, and the biggest single saving many households can make.

Then the citizen layer above the consumer layer: shifting use off the evening peak helps the whole grid, not just the bill; rooftop PV and solar geysers turn homes into micro power stations; and supporting — with votes, objections and community voice — the plans, plants and lines the transition needs is also energy management, because infrastructure gets built where communities allow it.

The honest closing note: individual action alone cannot decarbonise a coal grid — but the evening peak is literally the sum of household choices, and the load-shedding years proved the sum can move. Sustainable lifestyle is the name for making the movable sum smaller.

Turn to the closing questions of this part: the geyser ladder, two cheap victories, and the citizen layer.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole session in three pictures: a leaking bucket, a toolbox with three spanners, and a stadium wave. Nothing left out.

## Subtopic: The Leaking Bucket

South Africa's energy problem is usually drawn as an empty tap: not enough power stations. Redraw it as a LEAKING BUCKET: plenty pours in at the top while streams leak out of holes on every side — draughty buildings, ancient bulbs, uninsulated geysers, worn-out motors, lights burning in empty offices.

Now the money question: what is cheaper — building another dam to fill the bucket faster, or plugging the holes? A new power station costs hundreds of billions of rand and takes a decade. Plugging holes — insulation, LEDs, timers, efficient motors — costs a fraction, works within months, and never emits anything. That is why efficiency is called the INVISIBLE POWER STATION: the station you never have to build because the demand it would have served no longer exists. The kilowatt-hour you never use is the cheapest and cleanest one there is — that sentence is the entire topic in one line.

And the pressure to plug the holes is no longer only local. The world has started checking how much coal smoke is baked into products at the border — carbon taxes on imports — so every leak in the bucket quietly raises the price of South African steel and fruit in foreign shops. A greener economy simply means an economy that grows while the leaks shrink: more prosperity per litre poured.

Nice work — the questions for this piece are with you now: the bucket, the invisible power station, and the border check.

## Subtopic: The Toolbox With Three Spanners

Who plugs the holes? The syllabus hands out three spanners with names on them.

The GOVERNMENT spanner is the big one: only government can write the rules of the game. It draws the national roadmap — the IRP, deciding what gets built and retired by when; it runs the auctions that buy wind and solar farms; it taxes carbon so pollution finally has a price tag; it writes building rules so new houses are born efficient and appliance labels so buyers can see a fridge's appetite before paying; and it has handed out millions of solar water heaters to poor households. Biggest of all right now: rebuilding the grid and steering the JUST transition money so coal towns land softly.

The BUSINESS spanner turns the biggest bolts, because firms use most of the country's energy. The smart ones start with an energy AUDIT — following the electricity through the factory to find where it is wasted: hissing compressed-air leaks, tired motors, naked hot pipes. Fixes pay for themselves, which makes efficiency the rare virtue that turns a profit. Then the roofs: malls, factories and mines have covered themselves in solar panels, and mining houses now build whole wind farms. And the export rule of the new decade: foreign buyers now ask how much smoke is inside your product — a dirty grid has become a trade problem, so cleaning up is strategy, not charity. One warning label on this spanner: no GREENWASHING — painting the logo green while changing nothing fools nobody for long.

The INDIVIDUAL spanner is the smallest but there are sixty million of them, and they turn the same few bolts in every home — the next picture's whole story.

## Subtopic: The Stadium Wave

Can one person's geyser timer matter against a coal grid? Alone, no — and honesty scores marks. But the evening peak, the moment the national grid groans hardest, is nothing but millions of private choices landing at the same time: geysers, stoves, heaters, kettles, six o'clock, every day. A stadium wave is just individuals lifting their arms — and it can circle a stadium. Household choices are the wave.

So run the plays that matter most, in order of size. Play one, the geyser — the hungriest machine in the house, around 40 per cent of the bill: thermostat to 60, wrap it in a blanket, put it on a timer, and if the family can, let the SUN take over the job with a solar water heater. Play two, light: swap every old bulb for an LED — same light, a tenth of the power, lasts years. Play three, kitchen: lids on pots, the microwave for small jobs, full loads only in the washer. Play four, winter: seal the gaps, wear the jersey, heat the person, not the ceiling. Play five, standby: the little red eyes watching TV all night drink power for nothing — switch them off at the wall. Play six, wheels: share lifts, take the taxi or bus, walk the short trip — petrol is energy with a fuel gauge.

Then the plays beyond the meter: shift what you can to off-peak so the whole wave flattens; a rooftop panel turns a home into a tiny power station; and speaking up for the lines, plants and transition plans your community needs is energy management with a citizen's voice.

The close of the whole topic: government writes the rules, business turns the big bolts, and sixty million households are the wave. A greener economy is all three moving; a sustainable lifestyle is your seat in the stadium doing its part. The kilowatt-hour never used stays the champion — cheapest, cleanest, and available to absolutely everyone tonight.
