**Bianca:** First question from my side, because it trips people in essays: the carbon tax and the carbon border tax keep getting mixed up. Are they the same thing wearing two hats, or genuinely different?

**Tutor:** Genuinely different, and separating them cleanly is worth real marks. The carbon tax is domestic: South Africa's own law, the first of its kind in Africa, charging South African emitters a fee for every tonne of carbon dioxide they release. Its purpose is to change behaviour at home — make the polluting process more expensive than the cleaner one, so the finance director starts agreeing with the environmental officer. The carbon border tax is foreign: a rule written in other capitals, mainly in Europe, that inspects imports at the border and asks how much carbon was emitted in making them. If a South African steel beam was forged with coal-fired electricity, the importer pays a levy that a beam made with hydro or wind power escapes. Notice who holds the pen in each case: our government writes the first, other governments write the second — which means we can adjust the first but can only respond to the second. And notice the trap they create together: if South Africa keeps its grid dirty, its exporters pay twice — once to the local tax and again at the foreign border — while cleaning the grid discharges both bills at once. In an evaluation answer, use them as your supply-and-trade pressure pair: the domestic tax pushes from inside, the border tax pulls from outside, and both point the economy in the same green direction.

**Bianca:** Next: the lesson calls efficiency the invisible power station, and it sounds almost too clever. Can saving electricity really substitute for building a power station, or is that just a nice slogan?

**Tutor:** Test the slogan with arithmetic and it stops being a slogan. Suppose the country's homes hold roughly six million electric geysers, and a geyser blanket, a timer and a sixty-degree thermostat cut each one's draw during the evening peak by a few hundred watts. Multiply a few hundred watts by six million homes and you are removing something in the order of one to two thousand megawatts from the peak — which is the output of an entire large power station, delivered not over a decade of construction but over a season of installations. That is not hypothetical: the mass rollout of solar water heaters and efficient bulbs was pursued precisely because it was the cheapest new capacity available. Now compare the two power stations side by side. The visible one costs hundreds of billions of rand, drinks water, burns fuel forever, and arrives ten years after the decision. The invisible one costs a fraction, needs no water, burns nothing, emits nothing and starts working the week the geyser timer is fitted. There is one honest limitation to include: efficiency can only trim demand that exists — it cannot electrify a clinic that has no connection, and it cannot be harvested twice; once the holes are plugged, the next saving is harder to find. So the mature formulation is this: efficiency is the first power station a country should build, because it is the cheapest — but it is not the last one, and the grid still needs real generation behind it.

**Bianca:** Here is the sceptical one my friends actually ask: why should a business spend money on being green when its competitors just carry on polluting? Does the greener economy not punish the honest firm?

**Tutor:** It used to look that way, and the syllabus answer is that three forces have reversed the punishment. Force one is the meter: an energy audit is not charity, it is a treasure hunt. The compressed-air leak hissing in the corner of a factory can cost more per year than a worker's salary; a variable-speed drive on a big motor pays for itself in months. The firm that audits first pockets savings its polluting competitor keeps paying, so being wasteful is the expensive option even before anyone mentions the climate. Force two is the border: the polluting competitor's products now carry their smoke with them into the export market, where carbon border rules tax it and buyers' procurement policies screen it. Two identical fruit juices on a European shelf are no longer identical if one was pressed with coal power — the clean one clears the border cheaper. Force three is the bank: lenders and insurers now price climate risk, so the dirty firm borrows at worse rates, insures at worse premiums, and finds certain investors simply closed to it. Add load-shedding, which made the firm with its own solar roof the only one still trading through a power cut, and the honest firm is not the sucker in this story — it is the survivor. The one real unfairness to concede: these forces press hardest on exporters and big borrowers, and a small local polluter can hide longer. That is exactly why government's rules — the tax, the standards, the labels — exist: to make sure hiding eventually costs more than complying.

**Bianca:** Last one, and it is the one I would write wrong without this session: if a question asks me to assess the individual's role, how do I avoid the two bad answers — pretending one household saves the planet, or shrugging that nothing I do matters?

**Tutor:** By making the evening peak your star witness, because it destroys both bad answers at once. Against the shrug, present the arithmetic: the peak that used to trigger load-shedding is not caused by industry, which runs steadily all day — it is caused by households, six million geysers and stoves and heaters switching on in the same hour. That spike is literally the sum of individual choices, and a sum changes when its terms change. When millions of homes fitted timers, swapped bulbs and shifted washing to mid-morning during the crisis years, the national evening peak measurably flattened — individuals, in aggregate, did what no single power station could do as quickly. Against the fantasy, present the ceiling: no quantity of geyser blankets converts a coal station into a wind farm. The grid's carbon intensity is set by government's roadmap and business's investments, so the individual acting alone decarbonises nothing — the honest sentence the lesson insists on. Then resolve the two with the structure the syllabus gives you: three actors, three scales. Government writes rules one household never could; business moves megawatts one household never could; and households move the peak — and the politics — that neither of the other two can reach, because infrastructure is built where communities allow it and governments follow votes. So the full-credit answer ranks the household plays by size, geyser first; states plainly that individual action is necessary but not sufficient; and closes with the wave: one raised arm is nothing, sixty million raised arms circle the stadium. Assessed, weighed and honest — that is the shape that earns everything the question offers.
