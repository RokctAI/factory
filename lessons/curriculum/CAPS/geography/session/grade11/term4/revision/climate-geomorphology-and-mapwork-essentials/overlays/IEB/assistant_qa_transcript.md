**Bianca:** Revision question one, because it is the connection people miss: the lesson says one loop explains both the rainforest and the desert. Can you make that link so tight that I could never confuse cause and effect again?

**Tutor:** Follow one parcel of air all the way around and the link welds itself shut. Start over the equatorial forest at midday: the overhead sun pours energy in, the surface heats, and our parcel expands and rises like a hot-air balloon with no envelope. Rising air cools, and cool air cannot hold its moisture — so it condenses into those towering equatorial thunderheads and falls as the daily rain that keeps the forest a forest. But notice what the parcel is like after the storm has wrung it out: it is now high, cold and dry, sliding poleward along the top of the troposphere. Around 30 degrees of latitude it has cooled enough to sink — and sinking reverses everything. Descending air compresses and warms, and warming air moves further away from saturation with every metre it drops. It arrives at the surface as a warm, bone-dry lid that dissolves clouds rather than building them. Park that lid over a continent permanently and you have manufactured a desert: the Sahara and the Kalahari's fringe sit at exactly the latitude where the equator's exhaust descends. So the causal sentence runs in one direction only: the rain at the equator is the rising half of the loop, and the deserts at 30 degrees are the sinking half of the same loop — the forest and the desert are two ends of a single conveyor belt. If a question shows you a world map of deserts hugging 30 degrees, the answer is never coincidence; it is the Hadley cell's dry exhaust, named and explained.

**Bianca:** Next, the distinction that costs the most marks: drought versus desertification. Learners can recite the definitions but still muddle them inside an essay. What test can I apply mid-sentence to stay right?

**Tutor:** Use the two-question test: how long, and who did it? Question one, duration and reversibility. Drought is an event — a season or a run of seasons in which rain falls significantly below what the region expects — and when the rains return, a droughted landscape can recover, because the soil and its seed bank were bruised but not destroyed. Desertification is a condition: the land itself has been degraded — soil compacted, stripped or salted, vegetation lost past the point of self-repair — so that even a good rain year no longer restores it; the water runs off the sealed surface instead of soaking in. That is why the lesson insists a wet region can suffer drought, but only degraded dryland undergoes desertification. Question two, agency. Drought's driver sits in the climate system — most famously El Niño tipping our summer rains toward failure — and no farmer caused it. Desertification's drivers are overwhelmingly human: overgrazing, overcultivation, fuelwood stripping, salting irrigation. Now watch how the two interact, because that interaction is where the top marks hide: misuse loads the gun and drought pulls the trigger. In a drought year, land that was well managed browns and bounces back; land that was overgrazed and bared loses its soil to the first hard rain after the dry, and steps permanently down toward desert. So mid-sentence, ask: am I describing a temporary rainfall event with a climatic driver, or a lasting land condition with a human driver? If your sentence says the drought caused the desertification, correct it — the honest verb is exposed. The drought exposed what misuse had prepared. Write that and the distinction holds under any pressure.

**Bianca:** Now the geomorphology half. The lesson files every landform under flat, tilted or massive. If a photograph or a diagram appears with no labels at all, what is the fastest reliable route to naming the landform?

**Tutor:** Run a three-step identification drill, always in the same order: skyline, symmetry, then stage. Step one, the skyline — it answers the biggest question first: how does the rock lie? A stepped or tabletop skyline, flat summits with cliff edges, says horizontal strata: you are in the layer-cake family, and a caprock is protecting something soft. A skyline of long ridges says tilted strata. A skyline of smooth curves, bald rounded summits, no steps and no ridges, says massive rock — granite, and you are in dome-and-tor country. That single glance eliminates two thirds of all possible answers. Step two, symmetry — it refines within the family. In ridge country, compare the two flanks: one long and gentle, one short and steep means a cuesta; slopes approaching each other in steepness, a homoclinal ridge; both flanks steep and matched with a knife-edge crest, a hogsback — remember the rule, the steeper the tilt, the more equal the slopes. In dome country, symmetry is the giveaway itself: exfoliation keeps every profile rounded, and a stack of rounded blocks balancing on each other is a tor. Step three, stage — only for the tabletop family: measure the summit against the height. A broad upland still capped is plateau; a flat top wider than the hill is high is a mesa; a top narrower than the hill is high is a butte; and no flat top at all, just a soft cone, means the cap is gone — a conical hill, the sequence's last chapter. Then close every identification with the process sentence, because a name alone earns little: differential erosion for the cake family, differential erosion across tilted beds for the ridges, exfoliation and deep rotting of joints for the granite. Skyline, symmetry, stage, process — four beats, and no unlabelled diagram can ambush you.

**Bianca:** Last one, for the mapwork finish: under time pressure, gradient and intervisibility questions get rushed and dropped. Give me a fail-safe routine for each — slow enough to be right, fast enough for the clock.

**Tutor:** Two small algorithms — rehearse them until they run without thought. Gradient first, four moves. Move one: read the two spot heights, or take the contour values at the two points, and subtract for the vertical interval — say the points sit at one thousand two hundred and one thousand metres: the rise is two hundred metres. Move two: measure the straight-line distance on the map with your ruler and convert with the scale — on a one in fifty thousand sheet, every centimetre is five hundred metres, so a measurement of eight centimetres is four thousand metres. Move three: divide — gradient is vertical over horizontal, two hundred over four thousand. Move four: reduce until the top is one — divide both by two hundred and state it as one in twenty. Two checks catch nearly every error: both numbers must be in the SAME unit, metres, before dividing — mixing metres with kilometres is the classic slip — and the answer must pass the sense test: one in twenty is a gentle farm road, one in five is seriously steep; if your maths says one in nought point five, a unit died somewhere. Intervisibility next, three moves. Move one: pencil the straight line between the two stations. Move two: read the contour values along that line in order, and ask the only question that matters — do the heights rise and then fall between the stations? Ground higher than the line of sight in the middle is a wall: not intervisible. Move three: when no summit intervenes, check the shape of the slope itself — read the contour spacing: if the lines bunch near the top of the slope and open downhill, the slope is convex, it bulges like a shoulder, and the bulge can block the view even though no point on it is higher than either station; concave slopes, bunched at the bottom, leave the sight line clear. Say the slope-shape reason in your answer, not just yes or no — the reason is where the marks sit. Rise over run reduced to one in something; line, heights, shape. Rehearse both routines this week and the mapwork minutes become the calmest of the whole session.
