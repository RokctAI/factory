**Bianca:** The confusions that surface every time this year gets revised. First: learners can recite the three cells, but the question about why deserts sit at 30 degrees still goes wrong. Where is the disconnect?

**Tutor:** The disconnect is that learners memorise the cells as shapes instead of following one parcel of air and asking what it can and cannot do at each stage. Follow it: over the equator the parcel is heated, rises, cools as it climbs — and cooling air must shed moisture, so it rains hard, which is the equatorial jungle belt. By the time the parcel drifts poleward aloft and arrives above 30 degrees, it has already spent its water. Now it sinks — and sinking air WARMS by compression, and warming air moves away from saturation, so not only does it carry no moisture, it actively swallows cloud. Permanent subsidence means permanent clear skies: the Sahara, the Australian deserts, the Kalahari's margin, all filed neatly along that latitude. The exam version of the disconnect is writing deserts are at 30 degrees because it is hot there — wrong mechanism, and the marker knows it, because the equator is hotter and soaked. The creditable sentence names subsidence: air that rose and rained at the equator subsides at 30 degrees, warming and drying, suppressing cloud and rain. Rise means rain, sink means drought — carry that four-word pair into every circulation question, including the Kalahari High in winter, which is the same physics parked over our own plateau.

***

**Bianca:** Next: drought and desertification keep swapping places in answers. What is the cleanest way to keep them apart under pressure?

**Tutor:** Test them against time and against blame. Time first: drought is an EVENT — a season or a run of years when rainfall falls significantly below what that region expects — and it ends; the rains return. Desertification is a CONDITION — long-term degradation of the land itself, and when the rains return to desertified land, the land can no longer answer: the soil is compacted, stripped or salted, so the water runs off rather than in. Then blame: drought's causes are overwhelmingly natural — El Niño's shifted rainfall, the ordinary variability of a dry continent — while desertification's causes are overwhelmingly human: overgrazing, overcultivation, deforestation for fuelwood, careless irrigation. The two interlock, and that interlock is what the best questions probe: drought does not CAUSE desertification, it EXPOSES it. A well-managed veld survives a drought and recovers; an overgrazed veld hit by the same drought crosses a threshold and does not come back. So in an evaluation answer, sequence it: human misuse loads the gun through the good years, drought pulls the trigger, and management — carrying capacity respected, grazing rotated, trees replanted — is about unloading the gun before the next dry cycle, which on this continent is never far away.

**Bianca:** Third: the ridge family. Cuesta, homoclinal ridge, hogsback — answers name the wrong one constantly. Is there a foolproof identifier?

**Tutor:** One number identifies them: the angle of dip, and if the question shows or describes the strata, read the angle before anything else. Gently dipping strata, up to roughly 25 degrees, make a CUESTA — and its signature is maximum asymmetry: a long, gentle dip slope you could drive a bakkie up, against a short, steep scarp. Moderate dips make the HOMOCLINAL RIDGE — asymmetry shrinking, both slopes now clearly inclined. Near-vertical strata make the HOGSBACK — asymmetry gone, both faces steep and matched, the ridge crest narrow. The memory line from the lesson does the work: the steeper the tilt, the more equal the slopes. Two refinements collect the remaining marks. First, always name BOTH slopes with their reasons — the dip slope follows the top surface of the resistant layer; the scarp slope cuts across the exposed, broken edges of all the layers, which is why it is steep and why springs and quarries favour it. Second, if the question offers a map instead of a cross-section, the identifier translates directly into contour spacing: strong one-sided asymmetry in the contours is a cuesta, near-symmetry along a narrow ridge line is a hogsback. Angle first, asymmetry second, and the family sorts itself.

**Bianca:** Last one: mass movement questions often describe a scenario — a road cutting, a wet week, a collapsed slope — and ask what happened and why. What is the strongest answering routine?

**Tutor:** Classify, then prosecute. Classification needs two axes the syllabus gives you: speed and water. Ask how fast, and how wet. Imperceptible and fairly dry: soil creep — cite the leaning fence posts and curved tree trunks if the scenario mentions them. Wet and flowing like thick liquid: earthflow, or mudflow when it is fast and channelled after a cloudburst. Sudden, along a surface: a slide — and look for the shape of the surface, because a straight plane means landslide while a curved, rotating drop with a crescent scar at the head means slump. Free-falling from a cliff: rockfall. Naming the movement is usually half the marks; the prosecution is the other half. Ask who primed the slope and who triggered it. Priming: steepness, deep weathered material, stripped vegetation, and — in the scenario you quoted — the road cutting, which removed the slope's foot support, the commonest human contribution. Trigger: nearly always water, the wet week that added weight and lubricated the plane, occasionally vibration. Then, because South African papers reward it, land the human geography: the people below the slope are so often those with least choice of ground — the Durban floods carried informal homes off saturated slopes — so prevention is not only gabions, drains, retaining walls and replanting, but honest decisions about where people are allowed to be settled at all. Classify, prosecute, protect: that sequence answers any slope the paper can collapse.
