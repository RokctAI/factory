# Part 1 — Expert

This revision session assembles the physical geography of the Grade 11 year: the atmosphere of Term 1 — the energy balance, global circulation, Africa's climate, and the droughts and desertification that follow when the rain fails — and the geomorphology of Term 2 — landscapes built from horizontal, inclined and massive rock, the slope as the unit of landscape, and mass movement as the slope in motion — closing with the mapwork that binds it all, because every landform in this term is also a pattern of contour lines waiting on the 1:50 000 sheet. The descriptions are fully verbal: every diagram the paper could show is reconstructed here in words.

## Subtopic: The Energy Engine and Global Circulation

Everything begins with unequal heating. The earth is a sphere, so the tropics receive the sun's rays nearly vertical — concentrated onto a small area, passing through little atmosphere — while the poles receive them at a low angle, smeared over a wide area through a long slice of air. The result is an ENERGY SURPLUS between the tropics and roughly 40 degrees of latitude, and an ENERGY DEFICIT poleward of it. The tilt of the earth's axis at 23 and a half degrees, carried around the annual revolution, swings the overhead sun between the Tropic of Cancer in June and the Tropic of Capricorn in December, dragging the whole heating pattern north and south through the year. Without a response, the tropics would roast and the poles would deepen into ice; the response is CIRCULATION — the atmosphere and oceans hauling surplus energy poleward.

The atmosphere's answer is the chain from temperature to pressure to wind. Heated air expands, rises and leaves LOW pressure at the surface; cooled air sinks and piles up HIGH pressure; and air flows from high to low as WIND. Apply that chain around the globe and the planet rings itself with pressure belts: the EQUATORIAL LOW where the overhead sun drives air upward; the SUBTROPICAL HIGHS near 30 degrees where that risen air, having cooled aloft, subsides; the SUBPOLAR LOWS near 60 degrees; and the POLAR HIGHS of dense frozen air. The belts are not fixed — they migrate with the overhead sun, a few degrees north in the southern winter, south in the southern summer, and that migration writes climate: it is why the Mediterranean and the south-western Cape trade rain seasons with the tropics.

Between the belts run the cells and the winds. The HADLEY CELL: air rises at the equator, flows poleward aloft, subsides at 30 degrees and returns along the surface as the TRADE WINDS. The FERREL CELL between 30 and 60 degrees drives the WESTERLIES — the mid-latitude winds that steer this country's cold fronts. The POLAR CELL completes the set, sending polar easterlies outward from the ice. And no wind flows straight, because the rotating earth deflects every moving parcel — the CORIOLIS FORCE, bending winds LEFT in the southern hemisphere and right in the northern, which is why the south-east trades arrive on Durban's beachfront and the westerlies roar over the Cape from the south-west. State the model in that order — heating, pressure, cells, deflection — and any circulation question is answerable.

Take this section's questions now: the geometry of surplus and deficit, the pressure belts and their migration, the three cells, and Coriolis deflection southern-style.

## Subtopic: Africa's Climate, Droughts and Desertification

Point the machine at one continent. Africa sits astride the equator, so it carries the equatorial low and BOTH subtropical high belts at once, and one master switch decides its rain: CONVERGENCE against SUBSIDENCE. Where air converges and rises, it rains; where it subsides, it will not. The rising branch is the INTER-TROPICAL CONVERGENCE ZONE — the ITCZ — the thundery seam where the trade winds of the two hemispheres meet under the overhead sun. The ITCZ migrates with the sun, reaching southern Africa in the southern summer, and its arrival IS the wet season: one belt of rain sweeping north and south over the continent like a slow windscreen wiper, giving the equator two rainy seasons and the edges of the tropics one. The subsiding branches are the subtropical highs, and southern Africa lives under three: the SOUTH ATLANTIC HIGH off the west coast, the SOUTH INDIAN HIGH off the east, and — seasonally, over the interior plateau — the KALAHARI HIGH, whose winter subsidence locks the Highveld into months of dry, cloudless, frosty-morning weather. The east coast still gets moisture because the South Indian High feeds warm, wet air onto the land past the warm Agulhas current, while the west coast is doubly dried: subsiding air above, and the cold BENGUELA current below chilling the air so it carries fog but almost no rain — the Namib's recipe.

When the rains fail, the vocabulary must be precise. DROUGHT is not aridity: a desert is permanently dry, while drought is rainfall significantly BELOW THE EXPECTED for a region — a wet place can suffer drought. The syllabus grades it: METEOROLOGICAL drought, the rainfall shortfall itself; HYDROLOGICAL drought, when rivers, dams and groundwater run down; AGRICULTURAL drought, when soil moisture fails the crops; and SOCIO-ECONOMIC drought, when the shortfall reaches livelihoods and food prices. Southern Africa's droughts ride recognisable drivers — above all EL NIÑO, the periodic warming of the eastern Pacific that typically shifts our summer rains toward failure, with LA NIÑA the wetter mirror. Effects cascade: failed harvests, dying herds, empty dams, rural incomes collapsing, migration to towns.

DESERTIFICATION is a different thing, and the distinction is examinable: drought is a temporary shortfall; desertification is the long-term DEGRADATION of land in dry areas until it behaves like desert — and its causes are mostly human. Overgrazing strips the veld beyond recovery; overcultivation mines the soil's fertility; deforestation for fuelwood bares the ground; and poor irrigation waterlogs and salts it. Drought then exposes what misuse prepared: the bared, compacted soil cannot absorb the rare rain, erosion carries it off, and the productive edge of the drylands — the Sahel along the Sahara's southern rim, parts of the Northern Cape and Karoo at home — retreats. Management mirrors the causes: match stock numbers to the veld's carrying capacity, rotate grazing, plant drought-hardy trees as the Sahel's Great Green Wall attempts at continental scale, contour-plough, and support the rural poor whose survival choices drive the misuse.

Pause for this section's questions: convergence against subsidence, the wandering ITCZ, the three highs around southern Africa, the drought ladder, and drought against desertification.

## Subtopic: Reading Rock into Landscape — Horizontal, Inclined and Massive

Term 2's landscapes divide by one question: how does the rock lie? Where sedimentary strata lie FLAT — the Karoo's vast layered basin — erosion carves the tabletop family. A hard, resistant cap rock, often dolerite that intruded as horizontal sills, protects softer shales beneath. Rivers cut down, slopes retreat sideways, and the remnants stand in sequence: the PLATEAU, wide upland still capped; the MESA, a broad flat-topped hill whose top is wider than its height; the BUTTE, the shrunken next stage, taller than its narrow top is wide; and finally the CONICAL HILL, the cap rock entirely stripped, the profile softened to a cone. Every Karoo roadside teaches the sequence, and the exam wants both the description and the process: differential erosion — soft rock retreating faster than its hard protector — with the caprock's edge held vertical as a small cliff while debris aprons the slope below.

Tilt the same strata and the landforms change family. Inclined strata weather into asymmetrical ridges, each with two unequal slopes: the DIP SLOPE, gentle, following the tilt of the resistant layer, and the SCARP SLOPE, steep, cutting across the layers' broken edges. The angle of dip names the ridge: gentle dip, up to about 25 degrees, makes a CUESTA — long gentle back, short steep face; moderate dip, a HOMOCLINAL RIDGE with the two slopes approaching each other in steepness; and near-vertical strata make a HOGSBACK, both slopes steep and symmetrical, the ridge narrow as a roof ridge. South Africa's showpieces: the cuestas ringing the Cape's folded belt, and hogsbacks along tightly folded ranges. These landscapes matter economically — dip slopes catch and channel groundwater, scarps supply building stone, the ridges direct roads through the gaps called poorts.

Where the rock is MASSIVE — igneous, structureless, without layers — the shapes turn round. A BATHOLITH is a vast dome of granite that crystallised deep underground inside the crust; erosion strips the cover and exposes it as sweeping bare domes. Granite sheds its skin: released from the pressure of the rock once above it, the dome expands minutely and peels in curved shells — EXFOLIATION, or onion-skin weathering — keeping the profile rounded. Paarl Mountain's triple domes are the national example. Where jointing divides the granite into blocks, water rots the block corners underground, and when erosion removes the rotted grit the survivors stand stacked as TORS — the balancing castle-rock piles of the Magaliesberg foothills and the Matopos across the border. Three rock arrangements, three landform families: flat strata make steps and tables, tilted strata make asymmetric ridges, massive granite makes domes and tors — that one sentence organises the whole term.

This section's questions are ready: the mesa-to-conical sequence with its process, the three tilted ridges by dip angle, and the granite family from batholith to tor.

## Subtopic: Slopes, Mass Movement and the Map

The slope is the unit all those landscapes share, and the syllabus dissects it into four ELEMENTS, recited from the top down: the CREST, the convex upper surface, rounded by weathering; the CLIFF or free face, the steep wall of exposed bedrock, source of all falling debris; the TALUS or scree slope, the straight apron of broken rock resting below the cliff at the steepest angle loose material can hold; and the PEDIMENT, the long, gently concave foot slope running out to the plains. Not every slope carries all four — young mountain slopes do, old smoothed landscapes may keep only crest and pediment — and slopes evolve: in arid South Africa the classic story is PARALLEL RETREAT, the cliff eating backward at a constant angle while the pediment widens at its feet, which is exactly how a mesa shrinks to a butte and a butte to a conical hill.

Slopes also move, and MASS MOVEMENT is the downslope transfer of rock, soil and debris under GRAVITY — no river, wind or ice required. Classify by speed and water content, slow to fast. SOIL CREEP: imperceptibly slow, dry, revealed only by leaning fence posts, tilted poles and curved tree trunks. EARTHFLOW and MUDFLOW: wet movements, soil soaked to a fluid that flows — mudflows the fastest and deadliest, racing down channels after cloudbursts. The SLIDES: a LANDSLIDE moves rock and debris suddenly along a plane; a SLUMP slides along a curved surface, rotating backward as it drops, leaving a crescent scar at the top. ROCKFALL: the fastest and simplest, blocks dropping free from a cliff to feed the talus. The priming factors are the topic's analytical core: steepness, water — the great trigger, adding weight and lubricating the plane — removal of slope-foot support by rivers or road cuts, stripped vegetation whose roots once bound the soil, and the shaking of blasting or quakes. Human costs are South African and current: informal homes on steep, saturated slopes carried down in the Durban floods, rockfall netting bolted over Chapman's Peak Drive after fatal falls closed it.

Now put the term on the map, because every one of these forms has a contour signature. A mesa reads as a tight ring of crowded contours — the cliff — around a nearly empty flat top, with a widening skirt of evenly spaced lines — the pediment; a butte is the same signature drawn small. A cuesta reads as contours packed close along the scarp side and spread wide down the dip side — asymmetry visible at a glance. A granite dome reads as smooth, concentric, evenly rounded contours. The slope elements read off spacing directly: crowding at the free face, even moderate spacing down the talus, progressive widening across the pediment; a convex crest bunches its contours downhill, a concave pediment bunches them uphill. Add the standard calculations — gradient as vertical interval over horizontal equivalent stated as 1 in so many, cross-sections drawn by voice from contour crossings, intervisibility judged by whether a convex slope humps between two points — and the physical year closes where the paper will open: on the map sheet.

The last expert questions are with you now: the four elements in order, the mass movements graded by speed and water, and the contour signatures of mesa, cuesta and dome.

## Subtopic: Three Conveyor Belts and a Merry-Go-Round

The whole circulation topic is a delivery problem: the equator ordered too much heat and the poles ordered too little, so the atmosphere runs delivery routes. Route one is simple: hot equatorial air rises like the air over a braai, drifts poleward high up, cools, and sinks back down near 30 degrees — a loop, the first conveyor belt. But notice what that sinking does: sinking air is dry air pressing down — no clouds, no rain — and 30 degrees is exactly where the world's great deserts sit. The Sahara, the Kalahari fringe, the Namib: not accidents, but the exhaust of the equator's rising rain. One loop explains both the jungle and the desert.

Two more belts complete the set — the middle one driving our westerlies, the polar one spinning cold air off the ice — but the exam's favourite trick is the bend. Nothing on a spinning planet travels straight. Try to roll a ball across a moving merry-go-round: the ball runs true, but to you, turning with the platform, its path curves. Winds do the same over the turning earth — curving LEFT south of the equator, right north of it. That is Coriolis, and it is why the trade winds arrive in South Africa from the SOUTH-EAST and the storm winds of the Cape winter from the SOUTH-WEST: honest north-south deliveries, bent sideways by the spin.

Slide the whole machine with the seasons and southern Africa's year appears. In summer the sun stands overhead in the south, and the great rain seam — the ITCZ, where the two hemispheres' trades collide and boil upward — dips far enough south to soak the subcontinent: thunderstorm season. In winter the seam retreats north, and the sinking air of the Kalahari High settles over the plateau like a lid: dry, still, frosty mornings, that white-gold Highveld winter light. Meanwhile the westerlies and their fronts, also shifted north, finally reach the Cape — which is why Cape Town's rain arrives exactly when Johannesburg's stops. One migrating machine, two opposite rain seasons, one country.

Quick check — this section's questions are coming to you now: the braai loop and the deserts at 30 degrees, the merry-go-round bend, and the migrating seam that splits our rain.

## Subtopic: The Layer Cake, the Tilted Books and the Rising Loaf

All of Term 2 is baking. Start with a layer cake: soft sponge layers under one hard chocolate top. Leave it out in the rain — the exposed sponge washes away easily, but wherever the chocolate cap survives, the sponge beneath stays protected. The rain nibbles inward from the edges, the cap's rim breaking off bit by bit, and the cake shrinks — from a wide table, to a small table, to a stump narrower than it is tall, until the day the last chocolate goes and the unprotected sponge slumps into a soft cone. Wide table, small table, tall stump, cone: plateau, mesa, butte, conical hill — the Karoo skyline is a rained-on cake, and the hard chocolate is the dolerite cap.

Now tilt the bake. Lean a row of hardcover books at a gentle angle and look at one book's edge: a long gentle slope up its cover, a short steep drop off its spine. That is a cuesta — gentle DIP slope along the tilted hard layer, steep SCARP slope across the broken edges. Tilt the books steeper and the two slopes grow more alike — a homoclinal ridge. Stand them nearly upright and both sides drop steep and matched — a hogsback, narrow as a roof ridge. One memory hook carries all three: the steeper the tilt, the more equal the slopes.

Last, the loaf with no layers at all. Granite is baked underground as one massive batch — a batholith — and when erosion finally uncovers it, it behaves like fresh bread out of a tin: released from pressure, its surface expands and flakes off in curved crusts. That peeling is exfoliation — onion-skin weathering — and it keeps granite hills smooth and domed, never stepped, because there are no layers to make steps. Paarl's great grey domes are the loaf at full size. And where cracks divide the granite into blocks underground, water rots the corners off each block like sugar cubes dunked at their edges; wash away the rot and the surviving rounded cores sit stacked on each other — tors, the balancing boulder piles that look man-made and are pure chemistry. Cake, books, loaf: if you can say which bake a landscape is, you can name its landforms and the process that made them.

This section's questions are up now: the shrinking cake sequence, the three book-tilts, and the peeling loaf with its sugar-cube tors.

## Subtopic: One Walk Down the Hillside — Slopes, Slides and the Exam

Take one slow walk down a Karoo koppie and the last topics walk with you. You start on the CREST — rounded underfoot, curved like a shoulder, because weathering has been smoothing this top for ages. Three steps more and the ground vanishes: the CLIFF, bare vertical rock — this is where every loose block on the hillside was born. Below it your boots hit loose angular rubble lying at the steepest angle it can bear without sliding — the TALUS, and you can feel its honesty: kick a stone and it clatters down but stops; the whole slope stands exactly at its angle of rest. The rubble thins, the ground eases, and you finish on the long soft run-out to the plain — the PEDIMENT. Crest, cliff, talus, pediment: four elements, one walk, and the marks come from naming each and saying what it is made of and why it slopes as it does.

Now watch the hillside move. Most days it moves too slowly to see: soil creep, betrayed only by the fence posts leaning downhill like tired soldiers and the tree trunks curving to stay upright. Add water and it moves faster: soak the soil until it is porridge and it flows — an earthflow, or after a cloudburst, a racing mudflow. Undercut it — a river gnawing the foot, a road blasted through the base — and whole slabs let go at once: a landslide along a straight plane, or a slump rotating backward along a curved one, leaving that crescent bite-mark at the top of the scar. And from the cliff itself, the simplest move of all: rockfall, blocks dropping free to feed the talus. The exam's underlying question is always the same: what primed the slope — steepness, water, lost vegetation, a cut-away foot — and the strongest answers name the trigger, because the trigger is nearly always WATER or US.

Finish the walk with the map in hand, because the paper will. Your koppie appears as rings of contour: crowded rings where you stood at the cliff, even spacing down the talus, lines relaxing wider and wider across the pediment. A cuesta announces itself by asymmetry — lines packed on the scarp side, lazy on the dip side. A granite dome draws smooth round rings like ripples in a pond. And the calculations are the same routines rehearsed since Grade 10: gradient as height difference against distance, said as 1 in something; the cross-section spoken crossing by crossing; intervisibility answered by asking whether a convex bulge humps the sight line between two points. Walk the hill, read its rings, run the routines — and the physical paper holds nothing you have not already stood on.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the four-element walk, the movements from creep to rockfall with their triggers, and the koppie read from its rings.
