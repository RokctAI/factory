**Bianca:** First revision question, because it decides entire essays: when a question asks me to assess a country's level of development, how do I stop myself from just writing GDP and moving on? Give me the full indicator drill.

**Tutor:** Run the drill as three deliberate corrections, in order, each one fixing the last measure's blind spot — and say the corrections out loud in your answer, because the reasoning is where the marks are. Start where everyone starts: GDP, the value of everything produced inside the country in a year, or GNI, the income of its people wherever earned. State it, then correct it: a total favours big countries, so divide by population — per capita — and now you can compare a giant with a small state fairly. Correct again: a per-person average is silent about distribution — a country where one clique banks everything and millions hold nothing can post the same average as a genuinely comfortable society. So bring in the Gini coefficient, zero for perfect equality rising toward one for total concentration, and anchor it locally: South Africa's Gini stands among the highest on earth, which is why average income here tells you less than almost anywhere else. Correct a third time: money measures still count only money, so cross to the social indicators — life expectancy, literacy, access to clean water, and the sharpest single instrument of all, infant mortality, because keeping babies alive requires clinics, nutrition, sanitation and educated mothers all functioning at once; nothing fakes it. Then close with the synthesis: the HDI, folding health, knowledge and living standards into one score between zero and one, whose real lesson is comparative — two countries with identical incomes can diverge widely, because policy decides whether money becomes classrooms or stays in accounts. That is the drill: total, per person, spread, lives, synthesis. Five sentences, each correcting the last, and no assessment question can catch you writing one number and hoping.

**Bianca:** Next, from the trade section: the lesson says the shop is rigged. But could a sceptic not answer that countries like South Korea got rich inside the very same shop? How do I handle that in an argument?

**Tutor:** Handle it by agreeing with the sceptic's fact and then reading it more carefully than they did, because the East Asian story does not disprove the rigged shop — it reveals the escape route. Establish the rigging first: a country confined to selling primary products faces prices that lurch with every commodity cycle and drift downward over decades against the price of manufactures, so its terms of trade slide — the same trainload of ore buys fewer machines each year. That is a structural tilt, not an excuse. Now read Korea properly: it did not prosper by selling raw materials harder — it changed what it sold. Textiles first, then steel, then ships, electronics and vehicles: each step captured more value added, each step trained the workforce for the next — the value-chain climb, which is precisely what the word beneficiation prescribes for South Africa: the jewellery rather than the raw gold, the stainless steel rather than the chrome ore. Then add the honest qualifications that make the answer rigorous rather than romantic. The climb was not luck: it took deliberate state strategy, ferocious investment in education, and access to open export markets at the right moment. And the ladder is harder to climb today — automation thins the cheap-labour rungs that Korea used, and established exporters crowd the shelves. So the balanced conclusion: the shop is genuinely rigged against those who stay in the raw-materials aisle, AND the rigging can be beaten by those who change aisles — which converts the model from a complaint into a policy: invest in skills, process at home, trade up. An argument that holds both halves at once is exactly the kind that collects everything on offer.

**Bianca:** The energy section now. If an essay asks whether South Africa should stop building coal and go renewable, what is the trap in that question, and what does the complete answer look like?

**Tutor:** The trap is the word should, which invites you to pick a side and cheer — when the question is actually testing whether you can weigh four things simultaneously: cost, reliability, environment and people. Build the answer in four movements. Movement one, cost: through the competitive bidding rounds, new wind and solar in South Africa now price below new coal per unit — so on pure economics the argument is largely settled, and you should say so plainly with the auction evidence. Movement two, reliability — the honest complication: the sun sets and the wind lulls, and a grid cannot lull. Intermittency does not defeat renewables, but it does bill them: molten-salt storage that carries Northern Cape sunshine into the evening, batteries, pumped-storage schemes like Ingula working as water batteries, something steady underneath — Koeberg's low-carbon ember — and above all a stronger transmission network to move Karoo power to Gauteng. A renewable answer that omits the storage-and-grid bill is a slogan, not an analysis. Movement three, environment: coal's charge sheet — heavy per-person carbon emissions, the Highveld airshed, gigalitres of water in a dry country, acid mine drainage seeping from old workings — set against renewables' vastly lighter footprint, honestly noting their own land and materials costs. Movement four, people, the movement most answers forget: whole municipalities on the coalfields live on coal wages, and an energy plan that strands them trades a climate problem for a social one. Name the just transition and give it content — retraining, new industry sited in the coal towns, community stakes in the replacement plants. Then conclude with a direction rather than a slogan: the destination is renewable-led because sun, wind and price all point there; the speed is set by storage, grid and justice. Four movements, one verdict, nothing dodged — that is the complete answer.

**Bianca:** Last one, and make it the send-off: the lesson keeps saying essays are won by walking the circle of the year. Walk it for me once, slowly, so I can hear exactly what connecting topics sounds like on paper.

**Tutor:** Listen for the joins — I will mark each one as we pass it. Begin with development, because that is where every human geography argument begins: a country trying to turn growth into lives, clinics and classrooms. Development runs on energy — there is the first join — because factories, hospitals and schools are all, at bottom, electricity users, and in South Africa that electricity is still overwhelmingly coal. Coal reaches back into the physical half of the year — second join — because burning it spends scarce water and loads the atmosphere, and a warming atmosphere is not an abstraction here: it loads the dice for the El Niño droughts and the harder, more violent storms you revised in the climate topic. Those storms land on the land — third join — and where the veld has been overgrazed bare or the slopes ploughed carelessly, the rain does not feed the soil, it takes it: splash to sheet to rill to donga, the erosion ladder running to its bitter last rung. And the lost soil circles all the way back — final join — because a country that loses its topsoil loses harvests, rural livelihoods and food security, which is to say it loses exactly the development the first sentence was pursuing. There is the circle, closed. Now notice what sustainability turns out to be: not a slogan bolted onto the end, but the habit of checking every one of those joins before acting — power the growth from the sun and wind we hold in world-class measure, pin the soil down with cover and contour, sell the jewellery instead of the ore, accept aid that builds bakeries rather than delivering bread. In your final exams, one paragraph that walks those joins — naming each mechanism as it passes — outscores three pages of separated topics, because it shows the one thing geography actually teaches: that nothing on this map happens alone. Walk the circle once a day this week, out loud, and you will walk into that room carrying the whole year in one hand.
