**Bianca:** The questions this material always raises. First: if GDP per capita is so misleading, why does every exam still ask about it? What is the examinable skill?

**Tutor:** The skill is not calculating it — it is interrogating it, and the paper is testing whether you can hold a number and its limitations in the same answer. GDP per capita earns its place because it is comparable, available for every country, and broadly tracks the capacity to fund development. The interrogation has three standard moves, and a full answer makes all three. Move one: it is an average, so it conceals distribution — quote South Africa as the standing proof, a middle-income country by per capita measures carrying one of the highest Gini coefficients ever recorded, so the average describes almost nobody's actual life. Move two: it counts production, not wellbeing — a year of rebuilding after a disaster raises GDP while lives are worse, and unpaid work, subsistence farming and the informal sector barely register, which understates real activity across much of Africa. Move three: offer the repair, not just the complaint — pair it with the HDI, which converts income into what it becomes, health and schooling; countries with near-identical incomes sit far apart on HDI because policy differs. Number, flaws, repair: that three-step turns a definition question into a top-band answer, and it works for every indicator in the topic.

**Bianca:** Next: learners write that trade is unfair and lose the marks anyway. What does a precise version of that answer look like?

**Tutor:** Precision means naming the mechanism, not the feeling. Start with the pattern: the periphery exports primary products and imports manufactures — a pattern laid down in the colonial era and still visible in South Africa's minerals-out, machines-in accounts. Then the mechanism with its proper name: the TERMS OF TRADE, the ratio of export prices to import prices. Primary prices are volatile — a drought or a glut swings them — and over time they drift downward relative to manufactured prices, so the same export tonnage buys fewer imports each year: the treadmill that runs backwards. Then the amplifiers: protectionism in wealthy markets, especially agricultural subsidies that let their farmers undersell African producers; and dependence on one or two commodities, which turns a single price collapse into a national crisis. Then the escape, because evaluation questions want a way out: beneficiation — process the ore, capture the value added — diversification of exports, regional markets through the African Continental Free Trade Area, and the East Asian precedent of export-led industrialisation, entering at cheap manufactures and climbing the skill ladder. Pattern, mechanism, amplifiers, escape: four sentences, each carrying a term the marker can tick, where the trade is unfair carried none.

**Bianca:** Third: the energy question that decides essays — should South Africa abandon coal? What structure keeps that answer balanced instead of one-sided?

**Tutor:** Weigh it on three scales, and only then judge. Scale one, economics: coal is cheap, local and paid-for infrastructure, employing whole towns — but the renewable bidding rounds have made new wind and solar cheaper than new coal, so the economic argument has actually switched sides for NEW capacity, which is the sharpest fact in the whole topic. Scale two, reliability: the grid needs its steady floor, and coal currently provides the baseload — but the answer to intermittency is not permanent coal; it is storage and flexibility: pumped-storage schemes like Ingula banking energy as raised water, molten-salt solar plants generating into the night, batteries, a stronger grid, and Koeberg's steady low-carbon contribution. Scale three, people and environment: on one side carbon emissions among the world's highest per person, Highveld air, scarce water and acid mine drainage; on the other, the coalfield communities whose livelihoods are real and whose abandonment would be its own injustice — which is exactly what the phrase JUST TRANSITION exists to answer: retraining, new industries sited in the old regions, and a stake in the replacement. So the balanced verdict writes itself: not abandonment overnight, but a managed decline of coal as plants retire, renewables and storage built at speed where the endowment is world-class, and the transition engineered so its costs do not land on the poorest. Three scales, then the verdict — never the verdict first.

**Bianca:** Last one: sustainability appears in the soil topic, the energy topic and the development topic. If one essay question asks for all of it, how should the answer be organised?

**Tutor:** Organise it around the one definition and the one metaphor, then walk the circle. Open by quoting the definition exactly — meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own — because that sentence is the marking anchor. Translate it once: live off the interest of natural systems, never the capital. Then apply it in three arenas, one paragraph each. Soil: capital that forms a few centimetres in centuries and vanishes in storms; sustainable practice is cover, contour ploughing, rotation, stocking to carrying capacity, gabions in the dongas — spending interest, guarding capital. Energy: coal is inherited capital being burned once, sun and wind are pure interest; sustainability means shifting the economy onto the interest while managing the just transition for those the capital employed. Development: growth that hollows out health, education or the resource base is capital spent and booked as income; genuine development compounds — educated people, held soil, clean energy each feeding the next. Close with the circle, because synthesis earns the top band: development needs energy, coal energy shifts the climate, a shifted climate hardens the droughts and storms, the storms strip the soil, and stripped soil starves development — so sustainability is not a topic but the test applied at every join. Definition, metaphor, three arenas, the circle: that is a full-mark scaffold, and it holds no matter how the question is phrased.
