If a year of circulation cells and rock families feels like too much to hold, slow down — the whole session runs on two machines and one walk, and you already own all three.

Machine one, upstairs: unequal heating. Vertical rays at the equator, slanted at the poles — surplus and deficit. Air rises at the equator, sinks at 30 degrees, and that sinking parks the deserts. Three cells, winds bent LEFT down here — trades from the south-east, westerlies from the south-west. Slide the machine with the sun: summer brings the ITCZ and thunderstorms to the interior; winter brings the Kalahari High's dry lid, and sends the fronts to the Cape instead. Drought is rain below what is expected — four types, meteorological to socio-economic, El Niño the usual suspect. Desertification is different: long-term land damage, mostly human — overgrazing, overcultivation — that drought exposes.

Machine two, downstairs: how does the rock lie? Flat layers under a hard cap: plateau, mesa, butte, conical hill — the shrinking cake. Tilted layers: cuesta, homoclinal ridge, hogsback — the steeper the tilt, the more equal the slopes. No layers at all: granite domes peeling by exfoliation, tors stacked where joints rotted underground.

Then the walk: crest, cliff, talus, pediment — four elements, top to bottom. The hillside moves by creep, flow, slide, slump and rockfall, and the trigger is nearly always water or people. On the map: crowded rings for cliffs, asymmetry for cuestas, smooth circles for domes, gradient said as 1 in something.

Two machines, one walk, a map to read them on. Say the sequence once out loud and go in steady. You've got this.
