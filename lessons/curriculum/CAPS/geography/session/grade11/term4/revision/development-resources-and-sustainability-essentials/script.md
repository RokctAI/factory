# Part 1 — Expert

This revision session assembles the human geography of the Grade 11 year: the development topic of Term 3 — what development means, how it is measured, the models that explain where it happens, and the roles of trade and aid — and the resources topic of Term 4 — the conventional and non-conventional energy that powers South Africa, the management of energy toward a greener economy, and the soil on which everything ultimately stands. The through-line is SUSTAINABILITY: meeting the needs of the present without robbing the future, a definition the examiner expects word for word and this session applies to money, power stations and topsoil alike.

## Subtopic: What Development Means and How It Is Measured

DEVELOPMENT is improvement in the quality of people's lives — not merely growth in an economy's size. That distinction rules the topic: ECONOMIC GROWTH is more output, measurable in money; development asks whether the growth reaches health, education, choice and dignity. The vocabulary must be current DBE usage: DEVELOPED and DEVELOPING countries; the NORTH-SOUTH DIVIDE, the old line drawn on the world map with the wealthy industrial North — which includes Australia and New Zealand despite the geography — above the poorer South; and the reminder that these are categories with blurred edges, straddled by fast-industrialising countries such as Brazil, India and China.

Measurement is where marks are won and lost. The economic indicators: GROSS DOMESTIC PRODUCT, the value of all goods and services produced INSIDE a country in a year; GROSS NATIONAL INCOME, the income of a country's people and firms wherever earned; and both are usually taken PER CAPITA — divided by population — to compare countries. Their flaw is the flaw of every average: per capita income says nothing about who actually receives it. The GINI COEFFICIENT measures exactly that gap — inequality on a scale from zero, perfect equality, toward one, total concentration — and South Africa's, among the highest measured in the world, is the number behind every photograph of a suburb beside a shack settlement. The social indicators go where money measures cannot: LIFE EXPECTANCY, INFANT MORTALITY — the most sensitive single test of living conditions — LITERACY RATE, and access to safe water and sanitation. The HUMAN DEVELOPMENT INDEX combines three dimensions — a long and healthy life, knowledge, and a decent standard of living — into one score between zero and one, and its lesson is comparative: countries with equal income can have very different HDI, because policy decides whether money becomes clinics and classrooms.

Development is also uneven INSIDE countries, and the frame for that is CORE AND PERIPHERY: the core — Gauteng, the industrial heartland — accumulates investment, skills and services; the periphery — the former homeland regions above all — supplies labour and raw materials and receives remittances. The model explains the flows you revised in migration, and it scales: the world has its global core and periphery, a country has its own, and even a city does. Alongside it sits ROSTOW'S MODEL — five stages from traditional society through preconditions, take-off and the drive to maturity, to high mass consumption — useful as a vocabulary of stages, criticised fairly in every good answer: it assumes every country walks the same staircase the West walked, ignores colonial history and the disadvantages of late starters, and treats development as guaranteed. Quote the criticism; it is usually worth as much as the model.

Take this section's questions now: growth against development, the indicator toolkit with its flaws, Gini and HDI, and core-periphery against Rostow.

## Subtopic: Trade, Aid and the Rules of the Game

Countries develop inside a trading system, and the system has rules that favour the incumbents. The founding pattern is colonial and persists: the periphery exports PRIMARY products — ores, crops, crude — and imports MANUFACTURED goods. The trap is in the prices. Primary prices swing wildly and drift downward against the price of manufactures, so the TERMS OF TRADE — the ratio of a country's export prices to its import prices — move against raw exporters: each year the same tonnage of ore buys fewer machines. The escape is BENEFICIATION and industrialisation — processing your own materials, capturing the value added — which is why every development plan, South Africa's included, preaches moving up the value chain: exporting jewellery rather than raw gold, components rather than chrome ore.

The vocabulary of trade must be exact. The BALANCE OF TRADE is exports minus imports in goods; a surplus earns, a deficit drains. FREE TRADE removes barriers; PROTECTIONISM raises them — tariffs, quotas, subsidies — and the developing world's standing complaint is that rich countries preach free trade while subsidising their own farmers, undercutting African producers in their home markets. GLOBALISATION is the deepening integration of world markets, transport and communication: it has lifted hundreds of millions through export-led growth — the East Asian route: start with cheap manufactured exports, climb the skill ladder — but it also concentrates power in multinational corporations and exposes small economies to shocks decided elsewhere. South Africa trades inside it through BRICS, through the African Continental Free Trade Area's promise of a continental market, and through the old dependency it is still trying to shed: minerals out, machines in.

Then AID, the topic examiners load with judgement questions. Define the types: HUMANITARIAN or emergency aid, the immediate response to disaster; DEVELOPMENT AID, longer-term support for projects and capacity; TECHNICAL assistance, the transfer of skills; MULTILATERAL aid routed through bodies like the World Bank against BILATERAL aid given state to state; and TIED or CONDITIONAL aid, given with strings — spend it on the donor's goods, adopt the donor's policies. The debate must be argued both ways. For: aid saves lives in emergencies, builds clinics, schools and dams the budget could not, transfers skills, and at its best — vaccination campaigns, the fight against HIV — transforms whole societies. Against: aid can create DEPENDENCY, undermining local production when free food beats the local farmer's price; it leaks to corruption; tied aid serves the donor first; and debt-financed aid became the debt trap that crushed budgets across Africa in the 1980s and 1990s. The balanced conclusion the marker wants: aid works when it builds capacity and answers local priorities — and the deeper answer to development is fair trade and investment, not charity.

Pause for this section's questions: the primary-product trap and terms of trade, protectionism against free trade, globalisation weighed, and the aid debate argued both ways.

## Subtopic: Powering the Country — Conventional and Non-Conventional Energy

Energy is where the development promises meet physics. South Africa's electricity is overwhelmingly CONVENTIONAL and overwhelmingly COAL: over four-fifths of generation burns coal, mined cheaply and abundantly on the Mpumalanga Highveld coalfields, where the giant thermal stations stand on the coal seams and the transmission grid radiates outward. The process must be narratable: coal is pulverised and burned, the heat boils water to superheated steam, steam spins the turbine, the turbine spins the generator, and cooling towers exhale the waste heat as the white plumes on the Highveld horizon. Coal's case: it is cheap, local, proven, employs tens of thousands, and delivers reliable BASELOAD — the constant floor of supply a grid needs. Coal's charge sheet: carbon dioxide that makes South Africa one of the world's heavier per-capita emitters, sulphur dioxide and particulates over the Highveld's airshed, water consumed in a dry country, landscapes scarred by mining, and ACID MINE DRAINAGE — water turned acidic in old workings, leaking toward rivers on the Witwatersrand. Add the other conventionals: NUCLEAR at Koeberg near Cape Town, low-carbon and steady, but costly and slow to build with a waste problem measured in centuries; and HYDRO-ELECTRICITY, clean and instant, but starved in South Africa by the water topic's oldest fact — too few strong rivers — so our hydro is small, part-imported from Cahora Bassa in Mozambique, with PUMPED-STORAGE schemes like Drakensberg and Ingula acting not as sources but as giant batteries, pumping water uphill in the quiet hours to release through turbines at peak.

NON-CONVENTIONAL energy is the other lane, and South Africa is unusually well endowed. SOLAR: most of the interior receives among the world's best sunshine — the Northern Cape's clear dry skies host photovoltaic farms and concentrated solar plants around towns like Upington, the mirror fields that store heat in molten salt and generate after dark. WIND: the coastal belts of the Eastern and Western Cape drive turbine farms from Jeffreys Bay to the West Coast. The mechanism that built them is examinable: the RENEWABLE ENERGY INDEPENDENT POWER PRODUCER PROGRAMME, competitive bidding that pulled private investment into wind and solar and drove their prices below new coal. The honest weighing: renewables are clean, fuel-free, quick to build and increasingly the cheapest electricity on offer; but the sun sets and the wind lulls — INTERMITTENCY — so they need storage, flexible backup and a stronger grid, and the coalfield communities whose jobs are tied to the old system need a JUST TRANSITION, the phrase policy uses for moving to clean energy without discarding the workers of the fossil era.

This section's questions are ready: the coal chain from seam to socket with its costs, nuclear and hydro's trade-offs, pumped storage as battery, and the solar-wind case with intermittency and the just transition.

## Subtopic: Soil, Sustainability and the Greener Economy

The year's last resource is the first one: SOIL. It forms at a pace measured in centuries — a few centimetres of topsoil in several hundred years under African conditions — and is lost at a pace measured in single storms, which makes it effectively NON-RENEWABLE on any human timescale. Only about 12 per cent of South Africa is arable and barely 3 per cent high-potential, yet the country loses soil at several hundred million tonnes a year. The erosion sequence must be narratable: land is stripped by overgrazing, overcultivation or clearing; raindrops splash bare soil loose; SHEET EROSION shaves the surface thinly and invisibly; runoff gathers into RILLS, small channels a plough can still cross; rills deepen into GULLIES — the DONGAS that scar the Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal — and the land is finished for farming. The remedies mirror the causes: keep ground covered with crop residue and cover crops, plough along the CONTOURS so each furrow becomes a small dam, terrace steep land, rotate crops and rest the veld, fence stock to the veld's carrying capacity, and heal existing dongas with GABIONS — wire baskets of rock that slow each flood until it drops its load and the channel silts back up.

SUSTAINABILITY is the concept that binds soil to energy to development: meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own — living off the interest of natural systems, never the capital. Applied to energy it becomes the GREENER ECONOMY, and the syllabus assigns the responsibilities by name. GOVERNMENT: set the policy path away from coal, run the renewable procurement rounds, enforce air and water standards, carbon-tax the polluters, and manage the just transition for coal regions. BUSINESS: generate and buy clean power, audit and cut energy use, design efficient buildings and processes, and answer for their emissions honestly rather than in advertising. INDIVIDUALS: the cumulative megawatts of small choices — efficient lighting and appliances, solar water heaters, insulation and ceilings in housing, public transport where it exists, and the discipline of switching off — remembering that the cheapest, cleanest kilowatt-hour is the one never used.

Close the year by connecting the circle, because the exam's essay questions live in the connections. Development needs energy; energy from coal spends water, air and climate; climate change loads the dice for the droughts of Term 1 and the floods that carry away the topsoil of Term 4; lost soil undermines the farming that feeds development. The way out of the circle is the sustainability toolkit applied at every join: renewable power where the sun is best on earth, soil held on the land by contour and cover, trade that captures value instead of exporting it raw, and aid and investment that build capacity rather than dependency. A candidate who can walk that circle — resource to economy to atmosphere and back — is not reciting topics; they are doing geography.

The last expert questions are with you now: the erosion ladder from splash to donga with its remedies, sustainability defined verbatim, the three responsibility lists, and the circle that joins the year.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same year again — with a school report, a rigged shop, a braai fire and a bank account.

## Subtopic: The Scoreboard and the Rigged Shop

Judging a country by its GDP is like judging a school by its total marks: add up everything and the biggest school always wins, even if half its learners are failing. So the first fix is per capita — divide by the number of learners — and the second fix is to look past the average at the SPREAD, because an average of 60 per cent can hide a class of distinctions sitting next to a class of zeros. That spread has a name and a number: the Gini coefficient, and South Africa's is among the highest anywhere — the statistical shadow of the suburb built beside the shack settlement. The third fix is to stop counting only money: the Human Development Index reads three lines of the report card at once — how long people live, how much schooling they get, what they can afford — and scores the country between zero and one. Two countries with the same income can score very differently, because the score measures what the money was turned INTO. That is the whole measurement section in one sentence: count per person, check the spread, and follow the money into lives.

Now why some countries stay poor: picture a shop where the rules were written before you arrived. You may only sell raw things — ore, beans, crude — and you must buy back finished things — phones, machines, medicine. Raw prices wobble and slide; finished prices march steadily up. So every year your trolley of ore buys slightly less than last year — that sliding ratio is the terms of trade, and it is the trap built into the old colonial pattern: wealth flows to whoever does the MAKING. The escape is to start making: process your own gold into jewellery, your own chrome into steel — beneficiation, climbing the value chain — the route East Asia took from cheap factories to rich economies. And the map of who makes and who supplies repeats at home: Gauteng is the country's own core, making and banking, while the old homeland regions supply the labour and receive the remittances — one shop, rigged the same way, at two scales.

Aid, in this picture, is the shop's charity counter — and it must be argued both ways in one breath. When the flood hits, emergency aid is simply right. But permanent free bread puts the local baker out of business: that is dependency. Aid with strings — spend it on the donor's trucks — serves the giver first. So the marker's favourite conclusion: aid that builds bakeries beats aid that delivers bread, and fairer shop rules would beat both.

Quick check — this section's questions are coming to you now: the school report fixes, the rigged shop and its escape, and bread against bakeries.

## Subtopic: The Braai Fire and the Two Free Fuels

South Africa's electricity is one enormous braai fire that has burned since your grandparents' time. The fuel is coal, dug on the Mpumalanga Highveld where the power stations squat directly on the seams; the fire boils water, the steam spins turbines, and the smoke — carbon dioxide, sulphur — drifts over the Highveld while the cooling towers breathe their white plumes. The fire's virtues are real: cheap fuel, local jobs, and above all RELIABILITY — a braai fire burns steadily all night, and a grid needs that steady floor of heat, the baseload. But the bill grows: one of the world's heavier carbon footprints per person, dirty air over the coalfields, scarce water spent by the gigalitre, and old mines quietly leaking acid water toward the rivers.

Now the two free fuels. The sun over the Northern Cape is among the best on the planet — Upington's skies host mirror farms that focus heat into molten salt and keep generating after sunset — and the Cape coasts run steady wind through white turbine forests from Jeffreys Bay northward. Their fuel costs nothing, they build fast, and through the bidding programme that invited private builders to compete, their electricity became cheaper than new coal. So why not switch tomorrow? Because the sun clocks off and the wind takes days off — intermittency — and a grid cannot take days off. The braai answer: you keep SOMETHING steady under the grill while the free fuels do the daytime work, you build batteries — including the water kind, pumped-storage schemes like Ingula that push water uphill at midnight and let it fall through turbines at supper time, a dam used as a battery — and you strengthen the wires that carry Karoo sun to Gauteng stoves. Nuclear at Koeberg already gives one steady low-carbon ember; hydro stays small because our rivers are small, and some is piped in from Mozambique.

The last piece is the people of the fire. Whole towns on the coalfields cook, school and live off coal wages, and a switch that abandons them would trade one injustice for another. Policy's phrase for doing it right is the JUST TRANSITION: retrain the workers, build the new industries where the old ones stand, and let the coal communities own a share of the sun and wind that replace the seams. An exam answer that weighs kilowatts but forgets the miners is half an answer; the full answer powers the country AND carries its people across.

This section's questions are up now: the fire's virtues and bills, the two free fuels and their day off, the dam used as a battery, and the transition done justly.

## Subtopic: Living Off the Interest — Soil and the Sustainable Year

End with the bank account under everything: topsoil. Nature deposits into it at about a teaspoon a year — centuries to build the few dark centimetres that feed the country — and careless farming withdraws by the bucket: one thunderstorm on a bare, overgrazed slope can take decades of deposits in an afternoon. Watch the theft in stages: the veld is grazed bare; raindrops hammer the naked ground and splash it loose; a thin sheet of runoff shaves the surface so quietly the farmer never sees it leave; the sheet gathers into finger channels; the channels join and dig, storm after storm, until a donga yawns where a field was — cliff-walled, spreading, finished. And the fix list works the stages backward: keep the ground covered so the raindrops land on mulch, plough along the contour so every furrow is a tiny dam, terrace the steep land, rest and rotate the veld, match the herd to what the grass can carry — and where a donga already gapes, pack it with gabions, the wire baskets of rock that trip each flood into dropping its stolen soil until the wound silts closed.

Now name the principle the whole year has been circling: SUSTAINABILITY — meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own. Say it like a bank rule: live off the interest, never the capital. Soil is capital; the harvest is interest — erosion is spending the capital. A river's flow is interest; draining the wetland is burning the capital. Coal is pure capital, a one-time inheritance being burned; sun and wind are pure interest, arriving fresh forever. Even development obeys the rule: an economy that grows by hollowing out its people's health and schooling is spending capital and calling it income.

The final move is the one that wins essays: walk the circle of the year and show the joins. Development needs energy — Term 3 needs Term 4. Coal energy spends water and warms the climate — Term 4 reaches back into Term 1, loading the dice for El Niño droughts and harder storms. Those storms strip the bare soil — Term 1 falls on Term 4's dongas. And the lost soil starves the farming that development was meant to feed — the circle closes where it began. Sustainability is simply the habit of checking every link of that circle before acting: greener power under the best sun on earth, soil held down by cover and contour, trade that sells the jewellery and not just the gold, aid that builds the bakery. Hold the circle, quote the definition, assign the responsibilities — government, business, you — and the human geography paper has nothing left to surprise you with.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the teaspoon and the bucket, capital against interest across soil, water and coal, and the circle of the year walked join by join.
