Hey, it's Bianca — the aid topic feels like a minefield of opinions, and nobody wants to guess wrong about whether aid is good or bad. Relief: the exam never asks you to pick a side. It asks you to weigh, and the weighing kit is small.

First, the three types, straight from the syllabus. TECHNICAL aid is the fishing lesson — skills that keep working after the money is spent. CONDITIONAL aid is the gift with strings — either buy-from-my-shop (procurement tying) or run-your-house-my-way (policy conditionality, and structural adjustment is your named case). HUMANITARIAN aid is the fish — fast relief when the flood hits. Fish, strings, lesson: three types locked.

Then two case studies per side, with mechanisms. Working: smallpox eradicated, and PEPFAR helping South Africa's ARV programme lift life expectancy off the floor. Failing: structural adjustment cutting African clinics in the eighties and nineties, and food dumped outside emergencies until local farmers stopped planting. Notice each case comes with a WHY — that mechanism is where the marks sit.

Finally, the verdict sentence that closes any aid essay: aid is scaffolding — essential while the building goes up, wrong if it never comes down. Aid works when it targets a finishable job, follows the recipient's own plan, and is designed to become unnecessary.

One bonus fact examiners love: South Africa sits on both sides of the table — receiving health funding while assisting other African countries through the African Renaissance Fund. Co-operation, not charity.

Three types, four cases, one scaffold. That is the entire topic in one pocket. You've got this!
