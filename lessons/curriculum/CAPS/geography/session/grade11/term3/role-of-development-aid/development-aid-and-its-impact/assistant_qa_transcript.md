**Bianca:** Questions that come up a lot before tests on this one! First: trillions in aid have flowed to Africa over sixty years, and the lesson still calls parts of the continent underdeveloped. Doesn't that prove aid simply doesn't work?

**Tutor:** That argument sounds devastating until you disaggregate it, and disaggregating it is exactly the skill the essay wants. 'Aid' is not one experiment with one result — it is thousands of different interventions with wildly different designs and track records, and lumping them produces nonsense either way. Split the ledger. Aid aimed at specific, measurable, finishable problems has an extraordinary record: smallpox is eradicated — gone from the earth — polio nearly so, malaria deaths cut steeply by nets and treatment, and South Africa's own life expectancy recovered from the low fifties partly on PEPFAR-financed antiretrovirals. Nobody looking at those numbers can say aid does not work. Aid used as a permanent budget crutch, or as loans that hardened into debt, or as food dumped on functioning markets, has an equally clear record of failure — dependency, beached farmers, ministries answering to donors. Same word, opposite outcomes, and the difference lies in design: a finishable target, the recipient's own plan, and an exit built in. There is also a scale point worth one more sentence: total aid, spread over sixty years and a billion people, is far smaller per person than the word 'trillions' suggests, and much of it cycled back to donors through tied procurement. So the examiner's answer is neither defence nor prosecution: aid is a tool with a sharp edge and a blunt edge, and the evidence tells you which edge is which.

***

**Bianca:** Next: what is actually wrong with tied aid? If Germany gives us money and asks us to buy German trucks, we still end up with trucks, don't we?

**Tutor:** You end up with trucks — the question is how many, at what price, and what you lost along the way. Work the arithmetic first. Studies of tied aid consistently find it inflates costs substantially — the donor's firms face no competition for the contract, so the same grant buys fewer trucks than an open tender would. A hundred million of tied money might deliver what seventy or eighty million of untied money delivers; the difference is a quiet refund to the donor's own economy, which is why critics say tied aid is partly a subsidy to donor exporters wearing charity's jacket. Now the losses beyond price. The recipient's own truck assemblers, mechanics and suppliers get no contract, no experience, no growth — the aid builds the donor's industrial capacity instead of the recipient's, which inverts the entire point of development assistance. Maintenance chains you to the donor too: spare parts, specialised tools and training all come from one distant source, so when the aid programme ends the trucks age into scrap that local workshops were never equipped to service. There is a fit problem as well — equipment designed for European conditions is not always what rural African roads need. None of this means refusing such offers is always right; a country may reasonably take discounted trucks over no trucks. It means the exam answer must name the mechanism: tying recycles the money home, inflates the cost, and starves local capacity — three specific harms, not a vague complaint about strings.

**Bianca:** This one comes straight from the case studies: how exactly does free food HURT a hungry country? Half the class refuses to believe feeding people can be bad.

**Tutor:** The class's instinct is right in the emergency and wrong outside it, and the distinction carries the marks. During a genuine crisis — the flood, the drought year, the war — people have no money and markets have collapsed; free food saves lives and harms nobody's livelihood, because there was no functioning market to harm. The damage begins when free or heavily subsidised food keeps arriving AFTER markets recover, or flows into regions that were never in crisis. Follow one maize farmer. She plants, harvests, and takes her bags to the local market — where sacks of donated grain are being distributed at no charge a street away. Her price collapses; she cannot cover seed and fertiliser; next season she plants less, or nothing. Multiply her by a district and the region's own food production shrinks — so next year the region needs MORE food aid, and the year after more still. The aid manufactured the shortage it was sent to relieve. That is why modern practice, where markets still function, increasingly sends cash or vouchers instead of shiploads of grain: hungry families buy from local farmers, so the same money feeds people AND keeps the ploughs running. The exam formulation: humanitarian food aid is judged by timing and exit — magnificent inside the emergency, corrosive when it outstays it, and the KwaZulu-Natal floods against long-term dumping cases give you one example for each side.

**Bianca:** Last one: for the essay, is South Africa an aid recipient or a donor? And which case studies are safest to use?

**Tutor:** South Africa is both at once, and stating that dual position crisply is an easy analysis mark. On the receiving side: unlike much of the continent, aid is a small share of our national budget — we fund most of our own state — but it is concentrated and visible in health, where PEPFAR and Global Fund financing supported the antiretroviral programme, and in newer green-economy finance, where international partnerships fund the shift away from coal. On the giving side: South Africa extends assistance within the continent through the African Renaissance Fund, contributes peacekeepers and disaster relief, and hosts trilateral co-operation where a traditional donor and South Africa jointly fund work in a third African country. That dual role is itself exam material — it shows the donor-recipient boxes dissolving into co-operation, the syllabus's preferred frame. For safe case studies, build a balanced set of four. Positive: the ARV story with the life-expectancy recovery, and Gift of the Givers' rapid flood relief in KwaZulu-Natal — one long-term, one emergency. Negative: structural adjustment gutting African health budgets in the eighties and nineties, and food aid outside emergencies undercutting local farmers — one conditionality case, one distortion case. Two and two, each with a mechanism attached, then close with the scaffolding verdict: aid must be built to come down. That structure answers any aid essay the paper can ask.
