# Part 1 — Expert

Why is development so hard to achieve? This session confronts the tangle of factors that hold countries back — and insists, as CAPS does, that they are COMPLEX and INTERRELATED, never a single villain. It then narrows to South Africa's particular challenges, examines community-based development in rural and urban settings, and closes with the effect development itself has on the environment.

## Subtopic: The Tangle of Factors That Hold Development Back

CAPS lists the factors, and the list is best learned as a chain where each link pulls the others.

ACCESS TO RESOURCES: some countries hold fertile soil, minerals and water; others are landlocked, drought-prone or resource-poor. But resources are not destiny — the Democratic Republic of Congo is mineral-rich and poor, Singapore resource-bare and rich; what matters is whether resources are converted into broad development or captured by a few, a failure called the resource curse.

ENERGY: no factories, cold chains, irrigation pumps or evening study without reliable power. Hundreds of millions of Africans remain off-grid, and even connected economies stall when supply fails — South Africa's load-shedding cost the economy billions of rand and uncounted jobs.

HISTORY: colonialism drew borders that split nations and bundled rivals, built railways from mine to port rather than town to town, and educated tiny elites; apartheid then engineered underdevelopment deliberately. Modern maps of poverty still trace these old lines.

TRADE IMBALANCES: the previous session's mechanism — raw exports, manufactured imports, deteriorating terms of trade — drains the capital development needs.

POPULATION GROWTH: rapid growth means each year's gains are divided among more people; schools and clinics chase a moving target.

EDUCATION AND TRAINING: the master key — without skills, investment finds no workers and technology finds no users. Every development success story, from South Korea onward, invested in education first.

NATURAL-RESOURCE LIMITS and ENVIRONMENTAL DEGRADATION: overworked soils, felled forests and emptied fisheries subtract from tomorrow's production, so poverty forces short-term choices that deepen poverty.

The examiner's word is INTERRELATED: drought cuts harvests, so children leave school to work, so skills fall, so investment stays away, so poverty deepens, so land is overworked, so the next drought bites harder. Any answer treating one factor in isolation misses the design of the question.

The questions for this section are ready: list the factors, illustrate the interconnections, and use the resource curse correctly.

## Subtopic: South Africa's Particular Challenges

South Africa carries the general burdens plus a set stamped by its own history.

The APARTHEID SPATIAL LEGACY: forced removals and homeland policy deliberately placed millions far from jobs, on the worst land, with the thinnest services. That geography outlives the laws that drew it — workers still commute hours and spend large shares of income travelling from townships to work, and the former homelands remain the poorest districts in the country.

INEQUALITY AND UNEMPLOYMENT: a Gini coefficient near 0,63 and unemployment above 30 per cent — above 40 per cent among the youth — form the core challenge. An economy that grows without absorbing labour builds pressure instead of releasing it.

EDUCATION AND SKILLS: the pipeline leaks — strong enrolment, weak outcomes — so vacancies for engineers and artisans coexist with mass joblessness. That mismatch, not a lack of ambition, is the bottleneck.

SERVICE DELIVERY: millions gained housing, electricity and water after 1994 — a genuine achievement — yet backlogs, failing municipal maintenance and protests over broken taps and dark streets show the machinery straining.

HEALTH: the HIV/Aids epidemic cut life expectancy sharply before the world's largest antiretroviral programme turned the curve; tuberculosis and the quiet growth of lifestyle diseases still load the system.

ENERGY INSECURITY: load-shedding is a development issue, not merely an inconvenience — it closes the spaza's fridge, the clinic's cold chain and the factory's shift, and it deters the investment that would create jobs.

Hold the balance CAPS expects: enormous post-1994 gains in access to services and social grants that keep millions from destitution, set against stubborn unemployment, inequality and institutional strain. Both halves are true; essays that see only one half score half.

Work through this section's questions now: the spatial legacy, the numbers for inequality and unemployment, and the balanced assessment.

## Subtopic: Community-Based Development: Rural and Urban Approaches

If top-down mega-projects often miss the poor, COMMUNITY-BASED DEVELOPMENT works from the other end: the community identifies its needs, supplies its labour and knowledge, and owns the result. The principles: PARTICIPATION — people help plan, not just receive; local knowledge respected; appropriate technology; skills transferred so the project outlives its funders; and ownership, because people maintain what is theirs.

Rural approaches, with cases worth citing. The national WORKING FOR WATER programme pays local teams to clear thirsty alien vegetation — wattle, pine, gum — from catchments: water returns to streams, fire risk falls, and tens of thousands earn wages and training. Community food gardens and stokvel-style savings groups build food security and capital where banks do not reach. Farmer cooperatives pool small harvests to reach markets no single grower could — wool growers' associations in the Eastern Cape lifted village clip prices by grading and selling collectively.

Urban approaches. The Violence Prevention through Urban Upgrading initiative in Khayelitsha let residents co-design safe walkways, lighting and public buildings along the routes they actually walk — crime fell where design listened. Informal settlement upgrading IN PLACE — providing tenure, water, electricity and paths while people keep the homes and networks they built — has largely replaced demolition-and-relocation, because relocation was breaking the job connections and social webs that keep poor households afloat.

The critical note for evaluation marks: community projects are small relative to national need, can be captured by local elites, and depend on patient funding. They complement state-scale delivery; they cannot replace it. The strongest answer names a case, its principle, its gains, and this limit.

Attend to this section's questions: the principles, one rural and one urban case with detail, and the honest limitation.

## Subtopic: Development and the Environment

Development changes the environment, and the syllabus asks you to face the trade-off honestly.

The damage list is concrete in South Africa. Mining: acid mine drainage from abandoned Witwatersrand shafts seeps acidic, metal-laden water toward rivers; coal fields scar Mpumalanga's high-potential farmland. Energy: coal-fired power stations make the Mpumalanga Highveld one of the world's most polluted airsheds, loading lungs and crops alike. Agriculture: overgrazing and poor cultivation drive soil erosion and silt dams; irrigation salinises soils. Urbanisation: settlements spread over wetlands and aquifer recharge zones; sewage works overflow into rivers like the Vaal. Industry: effluent, waste dumps and greenhouse gases — South Africa ranks among the world's top per-capita carbon emitters because of coal.

Two truths must be held together. First: environmental damage is anti-development in the medium run — eroded soil grows less food, polluted water sickens workers, degraded land loses tourism — so destruction is not a price of development but a loan against it, repaid with interest by the poor, who live closest to the damage and can least afford to move. Second: poverty itself degrades environments — households without electricity strip woodland for fuel; hungry herders overstock fragile range. Environment and development are therefore not opponents but partners in the same solution: SUSTAINABLE DEVELOPMENT, meeting present needs without compromising future generations, delivered through tools you can name — environmental impact assessments required before major projects, rehabilitation obligations on mines, protected areas earning tourism income, and the renewable energy build examined next term.

Turn to the closing questions of this part: two damage cases with places attached, the loan-against-development argument, and the poverty-degradation circle.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same session in three ordinary pictures: a knot, a stoep meeting, and a borrowed roof. Nothing left out.

## Subtopic: The Knot in the Earphones

Why is poverty so hard to fix? Because its causes come pre-tangled, like earphones from a pocket. Pull one wire and the knot tightens somewhere else.

Watch the knot form around one family. The drought arrives — that is the resource wire. The harvest fails, so the eldest leaves school to herd goats — the education wire pulls tight. Without school, no matric; without matric, no job in town; the family sells cheap raw wool and buys expensive shop goods — the trade wire. The village has no electricity, so no fridge, no welding business, no studying after dark — the energy wire. And behind it all, the family farms this dry corner because a law once moved their grandparents off better land — the history wire, still knotted through everything.

Now the exam trick: never explain one wire alone. The marks live in the tangling — drought pulls education, education pulls jobs, jobs pull land care, land care pulls the next drought deeper. Write the chain, use the word interrelated, and the question is yours.

One more wire with a strange name: the resource curse. Finding treasure under your floor should make you rich — but if only a few hands grab it, the treasure funds their fights and everyone else stays poor. Congo has the treasure; Singapore has none and prospers. It is never the rocks; it is what a country does with them.

Nice work — the questions for this piece are with you now: the wires, the tangling, and the curse.

## Subtopic: The Meeting on the Stoep

Two ways to fix a broken neighbourhood. Way one: officials far away design a project, trucks arrive, something big gets built, the officials leave. Sometimes it works; often the pump rusts because nobody local can fix it or feels it is theirs. Way two starts with chairs on a stoep: residents saying what they actually need, planning it, building it with their own hands, and owning it afterwards. That is community-based development, and its golden rule is simple — people maintain what is THEIRS.

Real stoep-projects to name in the exam. Working for Water hires local teams to rip out alien trees — the wattle and gum drinking the streams dry. The water comes back, fires calm down, and the workers walk away with wages and training. Eastern Cape wool farmers pooled their small harvests, graded them properly, and suddenly buyers paid real prices no lone farmer ever saw. In Khayelitsha, residents co-designed lit walkways and public buildings along the exact paths they walk at night — and crime dropped, because the people who walk the path know where the fear lives. And in informal settlements, the smart approach now is to upgrade IN PLACE — bring the taps, tenure and electricity to where people already built their lives — instead of trucking families far away from their jobs and networks.

Keep one honest sentence for the evaluation mark: stoep projects are powerful but small — they cannot build a national grid or a highway. Community hands AND state muscle, both, each doing what it does best.

Your questions for this section are ready now: the golden rule, two named projects, and the honest limit.

## Subtopic: Borrowing From Tomorrow's Roof

Imagine a family that burns its roof beams to stay warm this winter. Warm tonight — soaked next summer. That is what development becomes when it eats its own environment: not progress, but a loan from tomorrow, repaid with interest.

South Africa has real examples on every side. Old gold mines under Johannesburg slowly fill with water that turns acidic and creeps toward rivers — acid mine drainage, a bill still arriving decades after the profits left. Mpumalanga's coal power stations keep the lights on AND make its air some of the dirtiest on earth, over the same soil that grows our maize. Overgrazed slopes wash into dams as silt, so the land loses soil and the city loses water storage in one motion. And the cruel twist: the bill lands on the poor first — it is the shack settlement, not the golf estate, that sits beside the mine dump and drinks from the brown river.

But flip it, because the exam wants both directions: poverty also strips the environment. No electricity means chopping the last woodland for firewood; no income means one cow too many on tired land. Poverty damages environment damages poverty — the knot again.

So the answer is not to stop building; it is to build without burning the beams. The tools have names: impact studies before big projects, mines forced to rehabilitate what they dig, parks that turn wild land into tourism wages, and the shift from coal toward sun and wind. One sentence ties the whole term together: meet today's needs without stealing from your grandchildren's — development that plans to still be standing when they arrive.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — the roof-beam loan, the bill that lands on the poor, and the tools that build differently. Get these and the whole development story holds together.
