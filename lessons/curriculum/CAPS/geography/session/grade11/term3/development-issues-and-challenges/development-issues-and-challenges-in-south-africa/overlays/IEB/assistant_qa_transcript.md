**Bianca:** This one splits the class every time: is South Africa poor because of history, or is that just an excuse now? How should we handle it?

**Tutor:** Handle it the way geographers handle everything — as a mechanism, not a mood. The claim is not that history explains everything; it is that history set the board on which today's game is played. Apartheid planning physically placed millions of people far from jobs, on the least productive land, with the thinnest services — and geography does not repeal itself when a law does. A worker in a former homeland district today faces longer commutes, weaker schools and fewer employers than a worker in a metro, for reasons decided decades before their birth. That is a measurable, mappable mechanism: compare poverty maps with the old homeland boundaries and the lines still coincide. But the same discipline cuts the other way. History cannot explain a municipal pump that failed last year for lack of maintenance, or a skills pipeline that leaks today. So the strong answer holds both: name the inherited mechanism precisely — spatial legacy, mine-to-port infrastructure, elite-only schooling — and then name the present-day failures that are choices, not inheritances. Weak answers pick a side and wave at it. Strong answers show the chain: where the past still binds, where the present is failing on its own, and how the two interlock. That is exactly what the word interrelated is asking you to do.

**Bianca:** Why do community projects like the rooibos co-ops matter if they're so tiny? A few farmers in the Cederberg won't change national statistics.

**Tutor:** Correct — and the mark scheme agrees with you, which is why the limitation sentence exists. But smallness is not the same as unimportance, for three reasons. First, community projects solve the last-mile problem no national programme can: a ministry can fund water infrastructure, but only the people of one valley know which spring froze last winter and which path the children actually walk. Participation converts that local knowledge into design, which is why Warwick Junction worked — the planners drew what the traders already knew. Second, they change the arithmetic of maintenance. A pump installed by outsiders is an object; a pump a community built is an asset with owners, and owners repair things. That is the difference between development that decays and development that compounds. Third, they build capability — wages matter, but the skills and organisation stay behind, so the Wupperthal farmers who learned processing and grading can negotiate forever after. The mature evaluation is therefore a partnership argument: the state must do what only scale can do — grids, hospitals, highways — while communities do what only closeness can do. Say both, name your cases, state the elite-capture and funding risks honestly, and you have the complete answer rather than half of one.

**Bianca:** The environment section confuses me. If development causes damage and poverty also causes damage, aren't we trapped either way?

**Tutor:** It looks like a trap only until you see that both arrows point at the same solution. Walk the two directions slowly. Development damages environment when the gains are taken as a loan: the coalfield powers the grid but acidifies the river, the irrigation scheme feeds the city but salts the soil. Poverty damages environment out of necessity: a household without electricity fells the last woodland, a herder without income overstocks tired veld. Now notice what the two cases share — in both, people are forced or tempted to spend tomorrow's natural capital to survive or profit today. So the answer to both is the same discipline: development that pays its way as it goes instead of borrowing from the future. That is all sustainable development means. And it is not a slogan; it is a toolbox with named instruments. Impact assessments force the loan to be declared before a project starts. Rehabilitation duties force the mine to repay its own damage rather than leaving the bill. Protected areas flip the ledger so that intact land earns income through tourism. Renewable energy removes the choice between electricity and clean air entirely. When a question gives you a scenario, identify which arrow is operating — profit-driven damage or poverty-driven damage — then prescribe the tool that breaks that specific loop. Diagnosis first, then the matching instrument.

**Bianca:** For the essay on South Africa's challenges, how do we avoid just writing a long sad list? Lists feel like they score badly.

**Tutor:** They do, because a list has no argument — and this essay is begging for one. Give it a spine in three moves. Move one: choose unemployment and inequality as your centre of gravity, because almost every other challenge either feeds it or flows from it, and say so explicitly. The spatial legacy feeds it by stranding workers far from jobs; the leaking skills pipeline feeds it by leaving vacancies unfillable; energy insecurity feeds it by scaring off the investment that would absorb labour. Suddenly your paragraphs are connected by arrows instead of commas. Move two: give every claim a number or a place. Above thirty per cent unemployed, past forty for youth; Gini near 0,63; the former homelands still the poorest districts; the clinic fridge that defrosts during a blackout. Specifics are the difference between knowledge and vibes. Move three: end with the balance, stated as a tension rather than a shrug — millions connected to services and protected by grants since 1994, AND machinery under real strain, both true simultaneously, which is precisely why the problem is hard. An essay shaped that way — one engine, evidence attached, honest balance at the end — reads as an argument about a country, not a list of complaints, and it collects the marks a list leaves on the table.
