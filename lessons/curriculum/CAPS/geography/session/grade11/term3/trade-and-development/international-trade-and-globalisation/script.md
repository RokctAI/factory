# Part 1 — Expert

Development does not happen in sealed containers: countries buy, sell, borrow and compete, and the rules of that exchange decide who accumulates and who stays supplying. This session covers international trade and world markets, the terms of trade that quietly rig the game, the types of trading relationships, globalisation, and the export-led strategy that some countries rode from poverty to riches — examined critically, as the syllabus demands.

## Subtopic: International Trade and World Markets

INTERNATIONAL TRADE is the exchange of goods and services across borders: EXPORTS are what a country sells abroad, IMPORTS what it buys in, and the difference is the BALANCE OF TRADE — a surplus when exports exceed imports, a deficit when they do not.

The decisive distinction for development is WHAT gets traded. PRIMARY PRODUCTS are raw materials — minerals, crops, timber, unprocessed fish. MANUFACTURED GOODS are processed products with skill and technology folded in: machinery, vehicles, electronics, medicines. Most value is added in the processing, so a country exporting raw ore and importing finished machinery is exporting cheap and importing expensive — the classic peripheral trade profile.

COMMODITIES — standardised raw materials such as coal, platinum, maize, crude oil and coffee — trade on WORLD MARKETS, where global supply and demand set a single ruling price that no small producer controls. Commodity prices also swing violently: a drought in Brazil doubles coffee prices; a slowdown in China halves iron ore. A country dependent on one or two commodities therefore lives on a rollercoaster it does not drive — Zambia rises and falls with copper, Nigeria with oil.

South Africa's profile makes the point concrete. Our major exports: platinum-group metals, gold, coal, iron ore, manganese, plus fruit, wine and vehicles. Our major imports: machinery, electronics, refined petroleum, pharmaceuticals. The vehicle exports — built in Gqeberha, East London, Durban and Pretoria — are the encouraging exception: manufactured, value-added exports, exactly the category development strategy tries to grow.

## Subtopic: Terms of Trade and Trading Relationships

TERMS OF TRADE measure what a country's exports can BUY: the ratio of average export prices to average import prices. If your export prices rise faster than your import prices, your terms of trade improve — the same volume of exports buys more imports. If import prices outrun export prices, your terms DETERIORATE: you must ship ever more tonnes of ore to afford the same machine.

Here is the structural problem the examiner wants named: over the long run, primary product prices have tended to fall relative to manufactured goods prices. Raw materials face competition and substitution, while manufactured goods carry technology, branding and patents that protect their prices. So countries locked into primary exports suffer slowly deteriorating terms of trade — running up an escalator that is moving down. This is the mechanism behind the phrase TRADE IMBALANCE between the developing and developed world, and it is why simply exporting more raw material rarely closes the gap.

Trading relationships come in recognisable types. FREE TRADE removes tariffs and quotas so goods move unhindered — efficient in theory, but it pits unequal players against each other. PROTECTIONISM shields home industries with tariffs, quotas and subsidies; rich-country farm subsidies are the sore point, letting European and American produce undercut African farmers in their own markets. TRADE BLOCS liberalise trade among members: the European Union, and in our region SADC and the African Continental Free Trade Area, which aims to knit Africa's small national markets into one continental one. TRADE AGREEMENTS such as AGOA give South African vehicles and fruit preferential access to the United States market. And FAIR TRADE schemes pay certified producers — of coffee, cocoa, wine — a guaranteed floor price plus a social premium, trading a little efficiency for a lot of stability.

## Subtopic: Globalisation and Its Impact on Development

GLOBALISATION is the growing interconnection of the world's economies, cultures and populations, driven by cheaper transport — above all the shipping container — instant communication, and the lowering of trade barriers. Its signature institution is the TRANSNATIONAL CORPORATION or TNC: a company operating across many countries, headquarters in one, factories in others, markets everywhere — Toyota, Coca-Cola, Anglo American, MTN.

The impacts on development cut both ways, and the exam wants both columns.

The gains. Globalisation lets countries sell to the whole world, not just neighbours; it carries investment, factories and technology into developing economies; it created hundreds of millions of manufacturing jobs, lifting East Asia in particular out of poverty at historic speed; and it gives consumers cheaper goods. South African examples: vehicle plants integrated into global supply chains, fruit and wine on European shelves, MTN operating across Africa and the Middle East.

The costs. The benefits concentrate — the map of globalisation looks like the core-periphery model, with investment flowing mainly to a few favoured locations. TNCs hold bargaining power over small governments: they can demand tax breaks and lax regulation, repatriate profits to headquarters, and relocate the moment cheaper labour appears elsewhere — the RACE TO THE BOTTOM. Local infant industries can be crushed by imports before they mature; South Africa's clothing and textile industry shed tens of thousands of jobs to cheaper Asian imports. And interconnection transmits shocks: a financial crisis born on Wall Street in 2008 cut South African exports and jobs within months.

The balanced verdict: globalisation is a powerful engine that pulls unevenly — those with skills, infrastructure and bargaining power ride it; those without can be run over by it.

Attend to this section's questions: the drivers, the TNC, and two impacts in each column.

## Subtopic: Export-led Development, Critically Examined

EXPORT-LED DEVELOPMENT is the strategy of growing by selling manufactured goods to the world market, rather than only substituting imports at home. Its showcase is the ASIAN TIGERS — South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore and Hong Kong — followed at scale by China.

The recipe as South Korea ran it: start with cheap-labour manufactures like textiles; earn foreign exchange; reinvest deliberately in education and infrastructure; climb the value ladder step by step — textiles to steel to ships to cars to semiconductors. In 1960 South Korea's income per person resembled Ghana's; within two generations it joined the developed world. The state did not stand back: it protected infant industries, directed bank credit, and disciplined firms by demanding export success in return.

Now the critical examination the syllabus explicitly orders. First, the strategy needs open, growing world markets — it worked while rich countries absorbed the exports, and if every country tries simultaneously, the markets flood. Second, timing and conditions mattered: the Tigers grew under access and geopolitical support that later entrants struggle to replicate. Third, the early rungs are brutal — low wages, long hours and suppressed unions carried the first decades, raising the question of who pays for take-off. Fourth, it is vulnerable to external shocks: export economies caught the 2008 crisis first and hardest. Fifth, environmental costs were deferred — the smog over industrial East Asia is part of the bill.

And South Africa? We pursue export-led elements — vehicles under successive motor industry programmes, fruit and wine, AGOA access — but face obstacles the Tigers did not: distance from major markets, electricity insecurity, skills shortages and rigid input costs. The honest conclusion: export-led growth is a proven route, not a guaranteed one; it demands state capacity, education and reliable infrastructure — and those are developmental achievements in themselves.

Turn to the closing questions of this part: the recipe, the Tiger evidence, and three critical limits.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same trade story at street level — a spaza, a swap, and a ladder. Same facts, nothing left out.

## Subtopic: The Spaza and the Supermarket

Imagine a street with one spaza shop and one giant supermarket. The spaza sells what it can gather cheaply — loose fruit, firewood. The supermarket sells processed goods — bread, airtime, medicine — where the profit lives in the processing. Now notice the trap: the spaza owner sells cheap raw things and BUYS expensive processed things, every day, forever. Money trickles one way.

That street is the world economy. Countries selling raw materials — ore, unroasted coffee beans, unprocessed fruit — are the spaza; countries selling machines, medicines and electronics are the supermarket. The coffee farmer earns a few rand per kilogram of beans; the same kilogram, roasted, branded and served in Sandton cups, earns hundreds. The difference — the value added — stays where the processing happens, not where the bean grew.

South Africa plays both parts. We sell platinum, coal and iron ore like a spaza — but our car factories in Gqeberha and Durban sell finished vehicles to the world like a supermarket. Growing that second basket is the whole game of trade policy: stop selling only the beans, start selling the coffee.

## Subtopic: Swapping Bags of Oranges for Phones

Terms of trade sounds technical; it is really a swap rate. Say one bag of oranges swaps for one phone. Next year, phone prices climb and orange prices sag — now it takes THREE bags for the same phone. You are working harder, growing more, shipping more, and affording less. Your terms of trade have deteriorated, and nothing you did caused it.

That is the quiet unfairness built into the world market. Over the decades, raw products — the oranges, the ore, the beans — have tended to lose value against manufactured goods, because anyone can grow oranges but only a few firms make phones, and the phone-makers hold the patents and the brands. So the raw-material countries run up an escalator that is moving down.

What can be done? Countries sign TEAM RULES. Trade blocs like SADC and the African Continental Free Trade Area drop the fees between neighbours so Africa can trade with Africa instead of only shipping raw goods north. Deals like AGOA let South African cars and oranges into the American market at a discount. Fair trade labels promise the farmer a guaranteed price so the swap rate cannot collapse under her. And the rich world's own trick — subsidising its farmers so their produce undercuts ours — is the rule most worth arguing about in any essay.

Your questions for this section are ready now: the swap rate, why it drifts against raw sellers, and the team rules that push back.

## Subtopic: The Ladder the Tigers Climbed

Sixty years ago South Korea was as poor as most of Africa. Today it builds the phone in your pocket. The route was a ladder, climbed one rung at a time — and it is the most argued-about ladder in economics.

Rung one: sew T-shirts. Cheap labour, simple factories, sell to the world, bank every dollar. Rung two: spend those dollars on schools and steel mills. Rung three: ships. Rung four: cars. Rung five: computer chips. At every rung, the country sold OUT to the world market — export-led — and poured the earnings into the next rung. China ran the same ladder at ten times the size.

But before anyone calls it a recipe, read the warnings on the label. The ladder needs someone at the top buying — it worked because rich markets stayed open, and they will not absorb everyone climbing at once. The bottom rungs are brutal: the first generation works long hours for low pay so the next can study engineering. A shock at the top — like 2008 — shakes everyone clinging below. And the smoke: East Asia deferred its environmental bill, and it came due as smog.

South Africa is on the ladder — our vehicle exports prove it — but climbing with load-shedding, distant markets and a skills gap is climbing with a heavy backpack. So the exam-safe verdict: export-led growth is a real ladder, proven by the Tigers, but not an escalator — it lifts only those who can grip it with education, infrastructure and steady government.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — the rungs in order, the warnings on the label, and South Africa's backpack. Get these and the trade story is yours to argue either way.
