**Bianca:** Questions that come up a lot on this topic! First: if trading raw materials is such a losing game, why doesn't South Africa just refine and manufacture everything itself before exporting? It seems so obvious.

**Tutor:** The strategy has a name — BENEFICIATION, adding value to raw materials before export — and it is official South African policy precisely because the logic is obvious. The hard part is everything the logic hides. Processing is capital-hungry: a platinum refinery or a steel mill costs billions of rand and years to build, and it must then compete against established refiners in China, Europe and elsewhere who already own the technology, the scale and the customer relationships. Processing is also energy-hungry, and this is where South Africa's advantage collapsed: smelters were built here in the era of the world's cheapest electricity, and load-shedding plus steep tariff increases turned that advantage into a liability — some ferrochrome smelters have literally shut while our raw chrome ships to China for smelting there. Add skills shortages, distance from major markets, and the tariff structure of rich countries, which often lets raw materials in cheaply but taxes processed goods more heavily — a deliberate ladder-blocking trick called tariff escalation. So the exam answer is balanced: beneficiation is the right direction, and the vehicle industry proves South Africa CAN export sophisticated manufactures, but declaring it is easy and doing it requires cheap reliable energy, skills, capital and market access. Name both the goal and the obstacles and you have the full marks.

**Bianca:** Next: globalisation is supposed to bring investment and jobs, but the lesson also says TNCs can leave whenever they want. So is a TNC arriving in your country good news or bad news?

**Tutor:** It is a bargain, and like any bargain the answer depends on the terms you strike. Take the arrival first. A TNC brings capital the country did not have, technology and management skills that spill over into local firms, jobs directly in the plant and indirectly through suppliers, and access to its global distribution network — the reason a bakkie built in Rosslyn can be sold in Europe. Those are real, measurable gains, and countries compete hard for them. Now the other side of the ledger. Profits flow back to headquarters, not into local reinvestment, unless conditions keep them here. The bargaining power is asymmetric: a company whose annual revenue exceeds the host country's GDP can extract tax holidays, cheap land and soft regulation, and the threat of relocation disciplines governments permanently — that is the race to the bottom. Jobs can be low-skill assembly while research and design stay at headquarters, so the ladder's top rungs never arrive. The verdict examiners reward: a TNC is good news for development when the host state is strong enough to negotiate — insisting on local suppliers, training, and taxes actually paid — and risky news when the state is weak. South Africa's motor industry shows the good case: decades of negotiated programmes tied TNC presence to local content and exports. The point is never TNC good or TNC bad; it is who holds the pen when the deal is written.

**Bianca:** This one caused an argument: my cousin says free trade obviously helps everyone, because both sides choose to trade. But then why does the lesson say free trade pits unequal players against each other?

**Tutor:** Both statements are true at different levels, and separating the levels is the skill. Your cousin's logic is sound for any single exchange: nobody trades voluntarily unless both sides gain something, so trade is not theft. The catch is in the phrase 'unequal players' — the gains from trade can be real on both sides and still wildly unequal, and free trade can also destroy capacity that never gets rebuilt. Run the infant industry case, because it is the classic. A new clothing factory in a developing country starts inefficient — every factory does — and needs years to learn, build scale and train workers. Under instant free trade it faces imports from giant, decades-old Asian factories from day one, and it dies in its cradle; South Africa's own textile industry shed tens of thousands of jobs exactly this way. The rich countries preaching free trade today, remember, all protected their infant industries when they were industrialising — Britain, America, Japan, all of them. Then add the subsidy hypocrisy: European and American governments pay their farmers billions, so subsidised produce lands in African markets cheaper than local farmers can grow it — free trade for our goods, protected trade for theirs. So the balanced answer: trade genuinely enriches both partners on average, but WHO within each country gains, and whether a country gets stuck on the raw-materials rung, depends on the rules — which is why blocs, agreements and fair trade schemes exist to rewrite them.

**Bianca:** Last one: for the essay, if export-led development worked for South Korea, what is the single biggest reason we cannot just copy their plan exactly?

**Tutor:** If you must choose one, choose this: the world that rewarded South Korea's climb no longer exists in the same form — and pair it immediately with the domestic point that take-off is built at home first. Unpack both. South Korea began climbing in the 1960s into rich markets that were growing fast, absorbing imports hungrily, and offering the Tigers favourable access; there was also far less competition on the bottom rungs — a handful of countries sewing T-shirts, not dozens. Today a new climber enters a crowded staircase with China occupying nearly every rung at once, world trade growing slowly, and rich countries turning protective again. That is the external half. The domestic half: South Korea's climb rested on foundations laid before and during it — near-universal education, land reform, disciplined state direction of banks, and electricity and ports that simply worked. Export-led growth did not replace those developmental achievements; it was powered by them. South Africa's specific gaps — energy insecurity, a skills shortage, distance from markets — are precisely foundations, not details. So the essay conclusion writes itself: the Tigers prove the ladder is real, but copying the plan means copying the preconditions, and the preconditions are the hard part. A strategy, not a spell — that is the tone the memorandum rewards.
