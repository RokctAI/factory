**Bianca:** The class chat is full of this one: if a country gets rich enough, can't it simply buy development? Why isn't growth enough on its own?

**Tutor:** Because money only becomes development at the moment it changes ordinary lives, and nothing guarantees that moment happens. Growth means the total pile of goods and services got bigger — it says nothing about where the pile went. History gives clean test cases. Some oil-exporting states have posted enormous incomes per person while life expectancy and schooling lagged for decades, because the revenue circulated at the top instead of building clinics and classrooms. Meanwhile countries with modest incomes have delivered long lives and near-universal literacy by spending deliberately on health and education. So wealth creates the possibility of development, never the fact of it. That is why the discipline insists on the sentence: growth is necessary but not sufficient. Necessary, because taps, vaccines and teachers all cost money — a shrinking economy struggles to fund any of them. Not sufficient, because the conversion step can fail: wealth can leak offshore, pool in few hands, or be spent on prestige projects that touch nobody's daily life. When you meet this in an essay, argue the conversion step. Show the pile growing, then ask whether the second street in town ever got its tar, its taps and its nurse. If it did not, you are looking at growth without development, and you can say so with confidence.

**Bianca:** BMW builds cars at Rosslyn and MTN earns money in Nigeria. Which country's numbers does each one land in? I keep flipping them.

**Tutor:** Use one rule and it never flips again: GDP follows the soil, GNP follows the passport. GDP — domestic — asks only WHERE the production physically happened. Rosslyn is South African soil, so every car built there sits in South Africa's GDP, and Germany's ownership is irrelevant to that question. GNP — national — asks only WHOSE company or citizen did the producing, and does not care where on the planet they stood. BMW carries a German passport, so the same Rosslyn production also sits in Germany's GNP. Now run MTN through the identical rule. The network activity happens on Nigerian soil, so it belongs to Nigeria's GDP. The company carries a South African passport, so it belongs to South Africa's GNP. Notice the elegance: one factory, one rule, two ledgers, and the two examples are mirror images of each other — a foreign firm producing here, and our firm producing abroad. When a question mentions any cross-border company, write the two words in the margin first: soil, passport. Decide the soil answer, then the passport answer, and the marks are safe. And remember that for most countries the two totals sit close together, which is why questions test the definitions rather than the arithmetic.

**Bianca:** Why is infant mortality called the most sensitive indicator? Doesn't life expectancy already cover the health side of things?

**Tutor:** They overlap, but they listen at different frequencies. Life expectancy is a lifetime average — it accumulates everything that happens to people across seventy or more years, which makes it powerful but slow. A clinic built this year shifts life expectancy gradually, over decades. Infant mortality is measured over a single year of life, so it responds almost immediately when conditions change, in either direction. And think about what a baby's survival actually requires: the mother reached antenatal care, the birth was attended, the vaccines arrived unbroken through the cold-chain, the water was clean, and the household could afford food. That is health care, infrastructure, logistics and income all being tested at once, on the most vulnerable person in the country. An adult can survive a weak system through their own resilience; an infant cannot. So when the rate falls year after year — as South Africa's has, down to the mid-twenties per thousand — you are watching the whole delivery system improve in real time, not just one hospital or one budget line. That is why geographers reach for it first when they want the truth about a place. If you can only quote one number to judge development, quote that one, and explain exactly why it carries so much information.

**Bianca:** The essay asking whether South Africa is a developed country scares people. How do we structure the answer so it scores well?

**Tutor:** Structure it as a court case, not a cheer or a complaint. Open by naming your measuring instruments — you will judge with GDP per capita, the Gini coefficient, the HDI and life expectancy, because impressions do not count as evidence. Then present the case for development: an upper-middle-income economy around six thousand US dollars per person, a diversified industrial base, deep financial markets, an HDI of about 0,71. Next, with equal seriousness, the case against: unemployment above thirty per cent, a Gini near 0,63 that ranks among the highest anywhere, life expectancy still in the mid-sixties. Now the crucial move — do not average the two lists into a shrug. Interpret them: the numbers describe one country containing two realities, Umhlanga and Inanda inside a single metro. Then conclude with a precise label: South Africa is a developing country, specifically a newly industrialised one, holding pockets of MEDC prosperity and pockets of LEDC deprivation. That sentence works because every clause in it is carried by an indicator you already quoted. The pattern to memorise is instruments, evidence for, evidence against, interpretation, verdict. Five moves, each one earning its own marks — and the verdict lands with authority because the numbers walked in first.
