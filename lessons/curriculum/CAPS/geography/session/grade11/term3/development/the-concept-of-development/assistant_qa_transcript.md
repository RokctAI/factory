**Bianca:** Questions that come up a lot with this topic! First one: if South Africa has the JSE, highways, malls and a motor industry, why do we still get called a developing country? Half the class thinks that label is just unfair.

**Tutor:** The label is not an insult — it is a summary of evidence, and the evidence cuts both ways. Yes, South Africa has Africa's most sophisticated financial sector, a diversified industrial base and world-class infrastructure in its metros. If those were the whole country, MEDC would be the right call. But classification looks at the whole population's condition, not the best districts. Unemployment stands above 30 per cent, the Gini coefficient of about 0,63 marks us among the most unequal societies on earth, life expectancy sits in the mid-sixties, and millions of households still lack reliable water and sanitation. The HDI of roughly 0,71 places us in the medium-to-high band, well short of the 0,9-plus scores of Norway or Switzerland. So the honest classification is a newly industrialised, upper-middle-income DEVELOPING country — one containing pockets that look like Germany and pockets that look like the poorest parts of the continent. And here is the exam skill: never argue the label with pride or grievance; argue it with indicators. Quote the GDP per capita, the Gini, the HDI and life expectancy, weigh both sides, and land on a conclusion. That balanced, evidence-led paragraph is exactly what the evaluation marks are printed for.

***

**Bianca:** Next: GDP and GNP sound identical, and half of us swap them in tests. Is there a foolproof way to keep them straight?

**Tutor:** Anchor each word to its middle letter. The D in GDP stands for DOMESTIC, which means inside the house — so GDP counts everything produced inside the country's borders, no matter who owns the factory. The N in GNP stands for NATIONAL, which follows the nationality of the owner — so GNP counts everything produced by a country's citizens and companies, wherever on the planet they operate. Then run the one test case that settles every exam question: the Toyota plant in Durban. It stands on South African soil, so its production lands in South Africa's GDP. It is owned by a Japanese company, so the same production lands in Japan's GNP. One factory, two different ledgers, depending on whether you are asking WHERE the work happened or WHOSE company did it. If a question mentions foreign-owned firms operating locally, or local firms operating abroad, that is your cue that the D-versus-N distinction is being tested. And remember the practical point: for most countries the two figures run close together, which is why the exam tests the definition rather than the arithmetic.

***

**Bianca:** This one caused an argument: if GDP per capita already divides by population to make things fair, why do we need the Gini coefficient at all? Isn't that double counting?

**Tutor:** No — the two numbers answer completely different questions, and the exam loves learners who can say which is which. Dividing by population fixes ONE distortion: it stops big countries looking rich merely because they are big. What it cannot fix is distribution, because a division produces an average, and an average is silent about spread. Run the arithmetic: ten people share 100 rand. If each holds 10 rand, the average is 10. If one person holds 91 rand and nine people hold one rand each, the average is still 10. Identical per capita figure, utterly different societies. The Gini coefficient exists to measure exactly that difference: 0 for the first society, approaching 1 for the second. South Africa is the textbook case — a per capita income that looks comfortably middle-income, sitting beside a Gini of about 0,63 that reveals how few hands hold the wealth. So the pairing is not double counting; it is two instruments reading two dials. Per capita reads the SIZE of the economy per person; Gini reads the SHARING of it. Any answer about South African development that quotes one without the other is telling half the story, and the memorandum knows it.

**Bianca:** Last one: why does the HDI use life expectancy and years of schooling specifically? They feel randomly chosen when there are hundreds of indicators out there.

**Tutor:** They are chosen because each is a summary indicator — a single number that silently contains dozens of others. Life expectancy is the final output of an entire health system. You cannot achieve a long-lived population without clean water, adequate nutrition, immunisation, functioning clinics, safe childbirth and control of disease; every one of those failures subtracts years. So one number stands in for the whole health chapter. Years of schooling works the same way for opportunity: it reflects whether schools exist, whether children can afford to attend, whether they must work instead, and whether girls are educated equally. Income per person then covers the material dimension. Three numbers, three dimensions of a decent life — long, educated, and provided for — which is philosopher-speak made measurable. The design choice to AVERAGE them is the clever part: a petro-state cannot buy a top HDI while its people die young, and a poor country that keeps its people healthy and schooled, like Cuba or Sri Lanka historically, scores far above its income rank. When the exam asks why HDI is a better measure than GDP alone, that is the answer: GDP measures what a country HAS; the HDI measures what its people's lives are actually LIKE.
