# Part 1 — Expert

Term 3 turns from physical geography to human geography's largest question: why do some places offer their people long, healthy, educated lives while others do not, and what can be done about it? This session builds the toolkit — what development actually means, the terminology the examiner expects, the indicators that measure it, and the honest way to compare countries without sorting the world into two crude boxes.

## Subtopic: What Development Means — and What It Does Not

DEVELOPMENT, in the CAPS definition, is the process of improving people's quality of life — their wealth, yes, but also their health, education, safety, freedom and dignity. Hold onto the two-sided phrasing, because the classic exam error is to equate development with money alone.

Distinguish it from ECONOMIC GROWTH. Growth means the economy produces more this year than last — a bigger total of goods and services. Growth is measurable and useful, but it says nothing about who benefits. A country can grow while most of its people stay poor, if the new wealth pools in few hands. Development requires that growth translates into better lives: clean water reaching homes, clinics stocked, children finishing school. The memorandum phrase is that economic growth is NECESSARY for development but not SUFFICIENT.

Two more paired terms. STANDARD OF LIVING is the material side — income, possessions, services you can afford. QUALITY OF LIFE is the whole picture — standard of living plus health, education, security, environment and political freedom. A miner earning well but breathing coal dust in a dangerous shaft has a reasonable standard of living and a poor quality of life; the exam rewards learners who can pull those apart.

Then the country labels. MEDCs are more economically developed countries — Germany, Japan, Australia. LEDCs are less economically developed countries — Chad, Malawi, Afghanistan. Between them sit the NICs, newly industrialised countries, whose manufacturing has surged within living memory — South Korea, Brazil, Mexico, and in most classifications South Africa. The older shorthand of First and Third World is dated; use MEDC, LEDC and NIC, and remember that the DBE also accepts developed and developing.

The questions for this section are ready: define development against growth, separate standard of living from quality of life, and place the labels correctly.

## Subtopic: Measuring Development in Money: GDP, GNP and the Gini Coefficient

If development must be compared, it must be measured, and the money measures come first.

GDP — gross domestic product — is the total value of goods and services produced WITHIN a country's borders in a year, no matter who owns the factories. GNP — gross national product — is the total produced by a country's CITIZENS and companies, wherever in the world they operate. A Toyota plant in Durban counts inside South Africa's GDP but inside Japan's GNP. For most countries the two run close; the distinction is a definition mark the paper loves.

Raw totals mislead, because big populations produce big totals. Divide by population to get GDP PER CAPITA — output per person — which lets a small rich country outrank a large poor one, exactly as it should. South Africa's GDP per capita of roughly 6 000 US dollars sits far above Malawi's few hundred and far below Norway's eighty thousand-plus: the profile of a middle-income country.

But per capita figures are averages, and averages hide distribution. Enter the GINI COEFFICIENT, which measures income INEQUALITY on a scale from 0 — everyone earns the same — to 1, where one person earns everything. Most European countries sit near 0,3. South Africa's Gini coefficient of about 0,63 is among the highest on earth, which is why our respectable average income coexists with deep poverty. One sentence carries the exam point: per capita income tells you the size of the cake per person; the Gini coefficient tells you how the cake is actually sliced.

Work through this section's questions now: GDP against GNP, the per capita correction, and what a Gini of 0,63 says about South Africa that GDP per capita cannot.

## Subtopic: Beyond Money: HDI and the Social Indicators

Money measures miss what money is FOR, so geographers lean on social indicators.

LIFE EXPECTANCY is the average number of years a newborn can expect to live. It compresses an entire health system into one number — nutrition, clean water, clinics, disease burden. Japan exceeds 84 years; several LEDCs sit in the fifties; South Africa, climbing again after the antiretroviral rollout, stands in the mid-sixties.

INFANT MORTALITY RATE counts deaths of children under one year per 1 000 live births. It is the most sensitive indicator of all, because keeping babies alive demands everything working at once — maternal health care, immunisation, sanitation, income. Iceland records about 2; the worst-served countries exceed 60; South Africa has fallen to the mid-twenties. Falling infant mortality is often the first signature of real development.

Literacy and years of schooling measure whether people can claim opportunity; access to safe water and electricity measure whether the state delivers.

The HDI — HUMAN DEVELOPMENT INDEX — deliberately combines three dimensions: health, measured by life expectancy; education, measured by schooling years; and income, measured by GNI per capita. Each is scaled and the result lands between 0 and 1. Norway and Switzerland exceed 0,95; Niger and Chad sit below 0,45; South Africa scores about 0,71 — high on income and schooling, dragged down by health. That is the HDI's whole virtue: a country cannot buy a good score with money alone, so it exposes exactly the gap between wealth and wellbeing that GDP hides.

Attend to this section's questions: what infant mortality is uniquely sensitive to, the three components of the HDI, and why South Africa's HDI trails its income rank.

## Subtopic: A Continuum, Not Two Boxes

The final skill is judgement about the categories themselves.

The BRANDT LINE, drawn in 1980, split the world into a rich North and a poor South, sweeping under Europe and the United States, then dipping to include Australia and New Zealand. It remains a useful first sketch, and the paper still names it. But treat it as history as much as geography: it froze a snapshot, and the snapshot has blurred. South Korea and Singapore, south of the line, now outrank many European economies; parts of eastern Europe, north of it, struggle.

The truthful picture is a CONTINUUM — countries strung along a line from lowest to highest development with every position filled, not clustered in two camps. And single countries contain the continuum internally: Sandton's glass towers and Diepsloot's shacks share a city; Gauteng's economy dwarfs the Eastern Cape's former homeland districts. Development differences run WITHIN countries as sharply as between them, which is why the Gini coefficient matters so much here.

Placing South Africa is a favourite essay task, so assemble the evidence. Upper-middle income by GDP per capita; industrialised, with banks, motor plants and the JSE; HDI around 0,71. Yet unemployment above 30 per cent, a Gini near 0,63, and life expectancy in the sixties. The defensible answer: South Africa is a developing country — an NIC — with pockets of MEDC prosperity and pockets of LEDC deprivation, and any answer that picks one box without the evidence loses the evaluation marks.

Turn to the closing questions of this part: the Brandt line and its limits, the continuum idea, and the balanced placement of South Africa.

## Subtopic: Two Streets in One Town

Picture two streets ten minutes apart. On the first, houses have geysers and fibre, the clinic is stocked, the school has a computer lab, and the taps never run dry. On the second, water comes from a shared standpipe, the clinic runs out of medicine by Thursday, and matric passes are rare. The first street is what development LOOKS like; the second is what its absence looks like. Development is the work of moving the second street toward the first — and notice that it is not only about money. It is medicine, taps, teachers, safety.

That is also the gap between two terms examiners love. STANDARD OF LIVING is what your income buys — the fridge, the phone, the roof. QUALITY OF LIFE is the whole deal — health, schooling, safety, clean air, having a say. A man with a good salary living beside a burning mine dump has the first without the second.

And growth versus development? If the town's factory doubles its output but every extra rand leaves town in the owner's bakkie, the town GREW and did not DEVELOP. Growth is the engine revving; development is the wheels actually turning.

Nice work — the questions for this piece are with you now: the two streets, the two terms, and the revving engine.

## Subtopic: The Scoreboard Problem

Comparing countries is like judging soccer teams, and the trap is trusting one number on the scoreboard.

Total goals scored — that is GDP, the whole economy's output. But a club with fifty teams will out-score a club with five, so divide by the number of teams: goals per team, GDP per capita, output per person. Better. One more wrinkle: WHERE the goals are scored. Goals scored inside your own stadium, whoever scores them, is GDP — production inside the borders. Goals scored by your own registered players, wherever they play in the world, is GNP. The Toyota plant in Durban scores in South Africa's stadium for Japan's club.

Now the cruellest trick averages play. Suppose one team wins 10-nil every week and the other forty-nine lose. The club's AVERAGE looks healthy while nearly everyone loses. That is South Africa's story: a decent average income, and a Gini coefficient of about 0,63 — one of the most unequal score-sheets on the planet. The Gini runs from 0, everyone equal, to 1, one person owns the lot. So carry both numbers: the average tells you the size of the cake per person, the Gini tells you how it is sliced — and in South Africa the slicing is the story.

Your questions for this section are ready now: goals in whose stadium, the per-team division, and the average that hides the losers.

## Subtopic: The Report Card With Three Subjects

Money is one subject on the report card. The HDI insists on marking three.

Subject one, health — measured by life expectancy, how long a newborn can expect to live. Subject two, education — years of schooling. Subject three, income — earnings per person. Average the three and you get a mark between 0 and 1. Norway sits above 0,95, straight distinctions. Niger sits below 0,45. South Africa scores about 0,71 — strong in the income subject, decent in schooling, pulled down hard by health. You cannot cram one subject and top the class; that is deliberate, and it is exactly why the HDI catches countries that are rich but unwell.

The sharpest single test on the whole card is the smallest: INFANT MORTALITY, deaths of babies under one per 1 000 born. Keeping a baby alive needs everything working at the same time — the mother's clinic visits, the vaccines, clean water, food on the table. If that number falls, the whole system is improving; South Africa's has fallen to the mid-twenties, and that trend line is genuine development.

Last, throw away the two-box map. The old Brandt line drew rich North, poor South — but South Korea has long since jumped the line, and our own country holds Sandton and Diepsloot within one metro. The world is a LADDER with every rung occupied, and South Africa stands mid-ladder: a developing country with one foot on a high rung and one on a low one. Say it with the evidence and the marks are yours.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — the three subjects, the baby test, and where on the ladder South Africa honestly stands. Get these and every topic this term has its foundation.
