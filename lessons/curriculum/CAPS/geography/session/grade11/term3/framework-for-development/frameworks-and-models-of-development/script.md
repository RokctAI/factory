# Part 1 — Expert

Measuring development was the last session's work; this one supplies the FRAMEWORKS — the aspects development must cover, and the two great models geographers use to explain WHERE and HOW it happens: the core-periphery model, which explains the map, and Rostow's stages, which claim to explain the timeline. Both models earn marks twice — once for describing them, and once for criticising them intelligently.

## Subtopic: The Five Aspects of Development

CAPS names five aspects, and an essay that touches all five reads like a full answer.

ECONOMIC development is the engine room: rising production, jobs, income and investment. Without it nothing else is funded — but the previous session showed it is not sufficient on its own.

SOCIAL development converts money into lives: education, health care, housing, safe water, and the position of women, since no country develops while half its population is excluded from school and work.

SUSTAINABLE development is the famous Brundtland definition, quoted verbatim in memoranda: development that meets the needs of the present WITHOUT compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. A country that logs every forest and drains every aquifer to boost this decade's GDP is not developing; it is spending its children's inheritance.

APPROPRIATE-SCALE development matches the solution to the community. A nuclear power station serves a national grid, but a remote Karoo village may be better served by solar panels and a borehole pump it can maintain itself. Big is not automatically better; the technology must fit local skills, budgets and needs — which is why the term appropriate technology travels with it.

SPATIAL development asks WHERE: development is never spread evenly, and planning must address the gap between booming cores and neglected peripheries — the exact problem the next subtopic models.

The questions for this section are ready: name the five aspects, quote Brundtland precisely, and match appropriate technology to its setting.

## Subtopic: The Core-Periphery Model

The CORE-PERIPHERY model says development organises space into a powerful centre and a dependent edge, and the pattern repeats at every scale.

The CORE is the region where economic activity, investment, skills and political power concentrate: factories, head offices, universities, the best infrastructure. The PERIPHERY supplies the core with raw materials and labour, and receives comparatively little in return. Between them many versions place a SEMI-PERIPHERY, regions climbing from one status toward the other.

The model's engine is CUMULATIVE CAUSATION: success attracts success. A factory opens in the core; suppliers, banks and workers follow; taxes fund infrastructure; better infrastructure attracts the next factory. Meanwhile the periphery experiences the BACKWASH EFFECT — its young, skilled and ambitious people, and its capital, drain toward the core, leaving the periphery older, poorer and less able to catch up. Flowing the other way, sometimes, are SPREAD EFFECTS: investment, remittances and demand trickling from core to periphery. Development planning is largely the attempt to strengthen spread and weaken backwash.

Apply it at three scales, because that is the exam's favourite move. Globally: the industrial North as core, much of Africa, Asia and Latin America as periphery supplying minerals and crops. Nationally: Gauteng — producing roughly a third of South Africa's GDP on the smallest provincial footprint — as core, with the Eastern Cape and Limpopo's former homeland districts as periphery, exporting workers to the mines and cities. Locally: a city centre and its office towers as core, the townships and informal settlements on the urban edge as periphery, connected by long, expensive daily commutes.

Work through this section's questions now: define core, periphery and backwash, and run the model at global, national and local scale.

## Subtopic: Rostow's Stages of Economic Growth

Walt Rostow's 1960 model claims every country climbs one staircase of five steps.

Stage one, TRADITIONAL SOCIETY: subsistence farming dominates, technology is limited, surplus is small. Stage two, PRECONDITIONS FOR TAKE-OFF: commercial farming spreads, infrastructure — railways, ports, banks — is built, often financed from outside, and an entrepreneurial class emerges. Stage three, TAKE-OFF: the decisive burst, where manufacturing industries grow rapidly, investment climbs past roughly 10 per cent of national income, and growth becomes self-sustaining. Stage four, DRIVE TO MATURITY: industry diversifies beyond the take-off leaders, technology spreads across the whole economy, cities swell. Stage five, AGE OF HIGH MASS CONSUMPTION: incomes are high enough that most households buy consumer durables, and services dominate employment.

Rostow drew the staircase from the history of Britain and the United States, and that is both its power and its weakness. Its power: it captures a real pattern — industrialisation genuinely did transform Britain, then Germany, then Japan, then South Korea, and the take-off idea usefully names the moment growth becomes self-feeding.

Its weaknesses, which the exam expects you to argue. It assumes every country follows the Western path, ignoring different cultures, resources and choices. It ignores colonialism — Britain's take-off was fuelled partly by colonies whose own development was deliberately suppressed, so the ladder some countries climbed was pulled up behind them. It ignores the core-periphery trap: peripheral countries locked into exporting raw materials may never accumulate the capital take-off requires. And it treats high mass consumption as the summit, with no stage for sustainability — a five-step race to the shopping mall, written before anyone asked whether the planet could host eight billion mass consumers.

Attend to this section's questions: order the five stages, identify the take-off criteria, and rehearse two solid criticisms.

## Subtopic: Reading Real Places With Both Models

Frameworks earn their keep only when they explain real ground, so run both models over South Africa.

Rostow first. South Africa's preconditions arrived with the mineral revolution — railways and ports built after 1870 to serve diamonds and gold. Manufacturing take-off came roughly between the 1930s and 1960s behind tariff protection. Today the economy shows drive-to-maturity features — diversified industry, dominant services — while mass consumption describes the insured suburbs but not the periphery. One country refuses to sit on one step, which is itself a criticism of the model: apartheid deliberately held most of the population off the staircase, and no stage model has a stage for that.

Now core-periphery, which explains what Rostow cannot: the SPATIAL pattern. Apartheid was core-periphery engineering by law — the homelands were designed as labour reservoirs, peripheries whose backwash of migrant workers fed the mining core. That is why the model fits South Africa so disturbingly well, and why post-1994 policy — Spatial Development Initiatives, industrial development zones at Coega and Richards Bay, corridor projects like Maputo — is explicitly an attempt to build new cores and pump spread effects outward.

The synthesis for essays: Rostow describes a TIMELINE and assumes all regions ride it together; core-periphery describes a MAP and insists they do not. Use Rostow to discuss a country's history, core-periphery to discuss its geography, and criticise both — one for ignoring space and history's injustices, the other for offering no automatic escape route.

Turn to the closing questions of this part: South Africa placed on Rostow's staircase, apartheid read through core-periphery, and the two models compared.

## Subtopic: Five Boxes Every Plan Must Tick

Think of development as a house being built, with five inspectors who each check one thing.

The first inspector checks the WALLET: is the economy producing more — jobs, factories, income? No money, no house. The second checks the PEOPLE: is the money becoming clinics, classrooms, taps and safety? A pile of bricks is not a home. The third inspector checks TOMORROW: are we building with materials we can replace, or burning the roof beams to warm today's room? That is sustainability — meeting today's needs without robbing your grandchildren of theirs, and that exact sentence, from the Brundtland report, is worth memorising word for word.

The fourth inspector checks the FIT: is the solution the right SIZE for the place? A remote village given a giant high-tech water plant it cannot repair has been given a monument, not development. Solar panels and a borehole the community can fix themselves — that is appropriate scale, appropriate technology. The fifth inspector checks the MAP: is the building happening everywhere, or only in the rich suburb while the rest of town waits? Development that piles up in one corner is the oldest problem in the book — and it gets its own model, next.

Nice work — the questions for this piece are with you now: five inspectors, five aspects, one Brundtland sentence.

## Subtopic: The Magnet and the Empty Edges

Drop iron filings on a table with a magnet in the middle and watch them slide inward. That is the core-periphery model in one image.

The magnet is the CORE — the city or region where the factories, banks, universities and government offices cluster. The edges are the PERIPHERY — the places that send the core their minerals, their crops and, most painfully, their people. Every January, buses leave the Eastern Cape villages for Johannesburg carrying the young and the skilled. Geographers call that drain the BACKWASH — the periphery's best flowing coreward. And the magnet grows stronger with everything it attracts: more workers bring more shops, more taxes, better roads, which attract the next factory. Success feeding success — cumulative causation.

Sometimes the current runs the other way: a worker's remittances build a house back home, a company opens a branch plant in a small town. Those trickles are SPREAD EFFECTS, and government planning is basically plumbing — trying to widen the spread pipes and narrow the backwash drain. Coega's industrial zone near Gqeberha is exactly that: an attempt to bolt a new magnet onto the periphery.

The trick that earns marks: the pattern repeats at every zoom level. Zoom out: rich North, supplying South. Zoom to South Africa: Gauteng magnet, homeland-era peripheries. Zoom to one city: office-tower core, township edge, and the long taxi ride between them. One model, three scales, nine marks.

Your questions for this section are ready now: the magnet, the backwash, the spread, and the three zoom levels.

## Subtopic: The Aeroplane on the Runway

Rostow's model says every economy is an aeroplane, and history is one long flight.

The plane starts parked: TRADITIONAL SOCIETY, everyone farming to eat, nothing spare. Then the ground crew arrives: PRECONDITIONS — railways laid, ports built, banks opened, commercial farms selling surplus. Then the famous moment, TAKE-OFF: factories multiply, investment passes about 10 per cent of income, and the plane's speed starts generating its own lift — growth feeding growth. Climb-out is the DRIVE TO MATURITY, industry spreading into every sector; cruising altitude is HIGH MASS CONSUMPTION, where ordinary families buy cars and fridges and most people work in services.

It is a tidy story, and for Britain, Japan and South Korea the flight really happened. But now the criticisms, because the exam pays for them. First, the model assumes every plane uses the same runway — the Western one — as if there were one route to a good life. Second, it forgets that some planes were fuelled by siphoning other planes' tanks: colonial powers took off partly on the resources and suppressed markets of their colonies. Third, some planes are held at the gate by the magnet problem — peripheries stuck exporting raw materials never accumulate the fuel take-off needs. Fourth, the destination is a shopping mall: the model crowns mass consumption as the goal and never asks what eight billion mass consumers would do to the planet.

South Africa? Parts of the economy are cruising; millions of passengers were deliberately kept off the flight for decades. A one-staircase, one-aeroplane model has no stage for apartheid — say that in an essay and you are doing real geography.

Here come the last questions of the lesson — the five flight phases, the take-off moment, and the four criticisms that turn description into evaluation. Get these and both great models are in your pocket.
