**Bianca:** Questions that come up a lot on this one! First: the core-periphery model says the core keeps getting stronger — cumulative causation, success attracting success. But then how does ANY periphery ever develop? South Korea did it. Gauteng itself was once empty veld. Doesn't the model contradict reality?

**Tutor:** Sharp question, and the answer earns evaluation marks because it shows the model's limits without throwing it away. The model describes a TENDENCY, not a prison sentence. Left alone, backwash usually beats spread and gaps widen — that part of the model matches the evidence painfully well. But three forces can break the cycle. First, deliberate intervention: governments can bolt a new magnet onto the periphery, which is exactly what South Africa attempts with the Coega industrial development zone and the Maputo corridor, and what South Korea did with state-directed investment in shipbuilding and electronics. Second, the core can price itself out: when land, wages and congestion in the core grow expensive enough, firms relocate outward, and yesterday's periphery — think of factories moving from Johannesburg toward smaller centres — catches the overflow. Third, new resources or technologies can create cores from nothing, which is Gauteng's own story: gold turned empty highveld into Africa's economic engine within a generation. So the exam-ready formulation is this: the model explains why inequality PERSISTS by default, and the exceptions are precisely the cases where spread effects were deliberately engineered or the economics of the core shifted. Use South Korea and Coega as your evidence and you have both the model and its critique in one paragraph.

***

**Bianca:** Next: Rostow says take-off needs investment above 10 per cent of national income. Where does a poor country find that money in the first place, if it is poor precisely because it has nothing spare?

**Tutor:** You have just articulated the vicious circle of poverty, and the fact that you can see it is why examiners love this criticism of Rostow. A subsistence economy consumes nearly everything it produces, so savings are tiny, so investment is tiny, so productivity stays low, so the economy stays subsistence — the circle closes. Historically, countries broke it in a handful of ways, each with a cost. Britain squeezed surplus from agricultural revolution and from its colonies — other people's circles were broken open to fund Britain's take-off. Japan and later South Korea forced savings up through the state: taxes and controlled banks channelled household money into industry for decades before consumers saw the benefit. Some countries borrowed the capital abroad, which works when the investment pays off and becomes a debt trap when it does not — several African and Latin American economies spent the 1980s discovering that. Others invited foreign direct investment, letting multinational companies supply the capital in exchange for profits flowing out. Notice what this does to Rostow's neat staircase: the second step is not a step, it is a wall, and every route over the wall — colonial extraction, forced saving, debt, foreign ownership — carries consequences the model never mentions. That is a first-class essay paragraph: name the circle, list the escape routes, attach the costs.

**Bianca:** This one confused the whole study group: is South Africa the core or the periphery? Within Africa we look like the core, but globally we export minerals like a periphery. Which one goes in the answer?

**Tutor:** Both — and saying both, with scales attached, is the difference between a pass answer and a distinction answer. The core-periphery model is scale-dependent, so a single place can hold different positions at different zoom levels simultaneously. Zoom to the world economy: South Africa exports platinum, coal, iron ore and fruit, and imports machinery, electronics and refined products — a classically peripheral, raw-material-selling relationship with the global core, softened by some sophisticated exports like vehicles from the Eastern Cape plants. Many geographers therefore file us in the SEMI-PERIPHERY: industrialised enough to escape the pure periphery, not powerful enough to join the core. Zoom to the continent: South Africa is unmistakably core — the deepest financial markets, the busiest ports, and a magnet whose backwash draws workers from Zimbabwe, Mozambique, Lesotho and Malawi exactly as Gauteng draws them from the Eastern Cape. Zoom inside the country: Gauteng core, former homeland periphery. So the technique for any question like this is to refuse the either-or and answer in three sentences, one per scale. Semi-periphery globally, core continentally, and internally a country that contains the entire model within its own borders.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: in an essay, are we supposed to AGREE with Rostow or with core-periphery? What if the question asks which model is better?

**Tutor:** Neither model is 'the right one' — they answer different questions, and the strongest essays make them work together before judging them. Rostow is a model of TIME: it narrates how one economy transforms through stages, and it genuinely fits the histories of Britain, Japan and South Korea. Its blind spot is space and power — it assumes every country climbs alone, on an empty staircase, when in fact climbers interfered with each other: colonialism drained some economies to fuel others, and trade relationships still lock some countries into selling cheap raw materials. Core-periphery is a model of SPACE: it explains why development concentrates and why gaps persist, and it fits South Africa's engineered homeland geography with uncomfortable precision. Its blind spot is change over time — read literally, it offers no escape route, yet escapes demonstrably happen. So structure the essay in three moves. One: what each model claims, accurately described — stages and take-off for Rostow; core, periphery, backwash and spread for the other. Two: what each explains well, with a real example each. Three: what each misses, using the other model as the critic — Rostow ignores the map, core-periphery ignores the staircase. Conclude that a geographer needs both eyes open: one on history, one on the map. Examiners reward that balanced verdict far above cheerleading for either side.
