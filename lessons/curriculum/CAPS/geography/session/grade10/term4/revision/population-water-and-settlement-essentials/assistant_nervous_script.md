If human geography feels like an ocean of definitions before the paper, take a slow breath — this whole session is three pictures and one chain, and every mark hangs off one of them.

Picture one: the party. Distribution is WHERE the guests are; density is people divided by the size of the room. Gauteng is the kitchen — jobs, from gold onward. The Northern Cape is the stoep — dry, so empty. And density is an average that lies, so always ask how people are really spread inside it.

Picture two: the photograph. A pyramid is a country lined up by age. Three glances: base for births, sides for survival, top for old age. Triangle means young and growing with heavy dependency; column means old and rich with too few workers. The transition model is just the journey between them — deaths fall first and the population explodes, then births follow and it settles. South Africa: base narrowing toward two children, the HIV notch in the young adults, life expectancy recovering.

Picture three: the bucket. Half the world's rainfall, unevenly poured, a third leaking from the pipes. The responses: dams and the Lesotho transfer, the free basic 6 000 litres, drip irrigation where two-thirds of the water goes, and every household fixing its leaks. Day Zero is the warning story.

And the chain that earns the top marks: people moved for work, settled on the land left over, and the leftover land is the floodplain — short lag, high peak, and the April 2022 floods as the proof. Migration, settlement and water are one story told three ways.

Three pictures, one chain. Say them once in your own words before you walk in, and walk in steady. You've got this.
