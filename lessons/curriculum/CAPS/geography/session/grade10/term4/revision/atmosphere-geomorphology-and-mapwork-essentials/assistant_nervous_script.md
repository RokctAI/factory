If a whole year of physical geography in one revision session sounds like too much, breathe — it folds down to three pictures and two calculations, and you already know all of them.

Picture one: the stove. The sun heats the ground, the ground heats the air — heated from below. From that one line: why summits are cold, why afternoon storms build, why rising air is the only recipe for rain. Then the three lifts — hot ground for convectional, mountains for orographic with its rain shadow, cold fronts for the Cape's winter rain. Blanket and sunscreen stay separate: greenhouse gases trap outgoing heat, ozone blocks incoming ultraviolet.

Picture two: the eggshell. Plates jostle on churning mantle rock. Apart makes ridges and rift valleys, together makes subduction volcanoes and fold mountains, past each other makes earthquakes. Bend rock and you get anticline up, syncline down — the Cape Fold Mountains. Snap it and you get faults, horst up, graben down. Two rulers for quakes: magnitude measures the energy, one number; Mercalli measures the damage, place by place.

Picture three: the bathtub. Contours are rings of equal height, 20 metres apart. Tight rings steep, wide rings gentle, V points upstream. And the two calculations are routines, not maths: gradient is height difference against distance, said as 1 in something — 200 metres over 4 kilometres is 1 in 20. The station model is a badge: cloud in eighths, wind tail with knots in feathers, temperature top left, dew point below it — close together means rain is near.

Three pictures, two routines, a handful of South African postcards — Highveld storms, the Drakensberg's dry side, the Cape's toothed winter fronts. Fold it that small and walk in calm. You've got this.
