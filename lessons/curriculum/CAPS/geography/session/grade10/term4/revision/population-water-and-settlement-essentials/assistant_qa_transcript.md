**Thandi:** The recurring confusions on this material, starting with the calculation everyone thinks they know: natural increase. Why do answers keep coming out ten times too big or too small?

**Tutor:** Because two different units share the same numbers, and the conversion between them is exactly a factor of ten. Birth and death rates arrive per THOUSAND: 30 births per thousand, 10 deaths per thousand. Subtract and you have natural increase of 20 per thousand — but examiners usually want it as a PERCENTAGE, which is per HUNDRED, so divide by ten: 2 per cent. Learners who skip that step write 20 per cent and describe a country doubling every few years, which should have failed the sense check: no country on earth grows at 20 per cent naturally; the fastest growers in history managed around 3 to 4. So build the habit: calculate per thousand, convert to per cent, then test the answer against reality. And hold the definition's boundary too — NATURAL increase deliberately excludes migration. A question about total population change is inviting you to add net migration on top, and naming that distinction is often a mark on its own: South Africa's population grows by births over deaths AND by net inward migration from the region, and the two are counted separately.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the pyramid questions. When a diagram is described and the answer must identify the country type, what separates a full-mark answer from a half-mark one?

**Tutor:** Evidence chained to consequence — every observation must drag its cause and its implication with it. The half-mark answer says wide base, so developing country and stops. The full answer runs the three glances with reasons: the base is wide, therefore the birth rate is high, common where families depend on child labour and infant mortality makes parents insure with numbers; the sides narrow quickly, therefore many die young — low life expectancy, weak healthcare; the top is nearly empty, so few reach old age. Then it lands the implication examiners reward most: dependency. A triangle means each working adult supports many children — money goes to schools and clinics rather than investment; a column means each worker supports many pensioners — the developed world's quieter crisis. And for South Africa specifically, expect a pyramid with a deliberate anomaly and name it: the notch in the young-adult bars from the Aids years, the recovery after antiretroviral treatment scaled up, the base narrowing as fertility fell toward two. An anomaly named and explained is usually the discriminating mark of the whole question — the examiner put it there to be noticed.

***

**Thandi:** Third: push and pull factors sound simple, yet answers on rural-urban migration keep losing marks. What goes missing?

**Tutor:** Two things: pairing, and consequences at BOTH ends. Push and pull are mirror halves and gain force when matched — rural unemployment paired with the city's jobs, failing rural schools paired with urban education, drought-struck subsistence farming paired with a wage. An answer that lists five pushes and no pulls has explained why people leave but not why they choose the city. Then the marks most answers abandon: effects on both places. The sending region — say the rural Eastern Cape — loses its young and economically active, so fields go untended, villages age, and skills drain away; but it gains remittances, money posted home that often becomes the region's lifeblood. The receiving city gains workers, energy and consumers, but its housing cannot keep pace, so arrivals build informally on leftover land, and services strain. Close the loop with the honest geography: leftover land in South African cities means floodplains and steep slopes, which is where the water topic collides with this one. A migration answer that follows the migrant from the dying village to the flood-prone settlement, counting costs and gains at both ends, touches every mark the scheme holds.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: the water topic feels like a list of schemes and percentages. Is there a structure that holds it together under exam pressure?

**Tutor:** Yes — run everything through supply, demand and delivery, in that order. Supply is what nature and engineering provide: roughly 464 millimetres of average rain against the world's 860, falling mostly in the east, feeding seasonal rivers; so engineering stretches supply — storage dams like Gariep and the Vaal, the Lesotho Highlands transfer moving water between basins to Gauteng, boreholes mining groundwater in the west. Demand is who uses it: agriculture near two-thirds through irrigation, then industry and mining, then households — which tells you instantly where savings bite hardest: drip irrigation and lined canals on farms outweigh shorter showers in town, though both matter. Delivery is the justice layer: the constitutional right, the free basic 6 000 litres per poor household per month, and the obstacles — distance in rural areas, backlogs in growing settlements, and the third of municipal water lost to leaking pipes before it reaches any tap. Any question the paper asks — describe the problem, evaluate a scheme, suggest strategies — is answerable inside those three headings, and Cape Town's Day Zero countdown of 2018 serves as the case study that touches all three: supply collapsed in drought, demand was forced down by half, and delivery politics decided who queued where. Structure first, numbers second, and the topic stops being a list.
