# Part 1 — Expert

This revision session gathers the physical geography of the whole Grade 10 year into one sitting: the atmosphere of Term 1 — its composition, structure, heating and moisture; the restless crust of Term 2 — plate tectonics, folding, faulting, earthquakes and volcanoes; and the mapwork that runs through both papers — contours, gradient and the synoptic chart, all described fully in words so every map and diagram can be reconstructed from sound alone. Treat it as the final walk through the examinable core: every definition given here is one the examiner expects verbatim, and every South African example is one worth quoting.

## Subtopic: The Atmosphere — Composition, Structure and Heating

Begin with what the air actually is. The ATMOSPHERE is a mixture of gases held to the earth by gravity: about 78 per cent NITROGEN, about 21 per cent OXYGEN, a little less than 1 per cent argon, and then the small but mighty traces — CARBON DIOXIDE at about 0.04 per cent, WATER VAPOUR varying from almost nothing over the Kalahari to around 4 per cent over a humid coast, and OZONE. The traces matter out of all proportion to their quantity: carbon dioxide and water vapour absorb the earth's outgoing heat, and ozone absorbs incoming ultraviolet radiation.

The atmosphere is arranged in four layers, and the examiner wants each layer with its temperature behaviour. The TROPOSPHERE is the bottom layer, holding nearly all weather, water vapour and dust; temperature FALLS with height in it, at the normal lapse rate of about 6.5 degrees Celsius per kilometre, up to the boundary called the TROPOPAUSE. Above it the STRATOSPHERE, where temperature RISES with height because the OZONE LAYER sits there, absorbing ultraviolet radiation and warming the air around it — this is the layer that shields skin, crops and plankton, and the reason the world banned CFCs under the Montreal Protocol. Then the MESOSPHERE, the coldest layer, where temperature falls again and meteors burn up; and finally the THERMOSPHERE, where thin air soaks up intense solar energy and temperature climbs steeply.

Heating comes next, and one sentence rules the topic: the atmosphere is heated FROM BELOW, by the earth's surface, not directly by the sun. Incoming solar radiation — INSOLATION — is shortwave; on its journey down, some is reflected by clouds and the surface, some scattered, some absorbed, so only about half reaches the ground. The warmed surface then radiates LONGWAVE, terrestrial radiation upward, and it is this longwave energy that greenhouse gases absorb — which is why temperature falls as you climb away from the heater. Four processes move the heat: RADIATION, energy travelling as waves; CONDUCTION, heat passing by contact from ground to the air touching it; CONVECTION, warmed air rising in vertical currents; and ADVECTION, heat carried sideways by wind. The GREENHOUSE EFFECT is natural and necessary — without it the earth would average around minus 18 degrees — but the ENHANCED greenhouse effect, from added carbon dioxide and methane, is the warming problem. Finally, the factors that set one place's temperature apart from another's: latitude, altitude — the reason Johannesburg at 1 700 metres is cooler than Durban at the same latitude band — distance from the sea, ocean currents — the warm Agulhas against the cold Benguela — and aspect.

Take this section's questions now: the gases and their percentages, the four layers with their temperature behaviour, heating from below, and the four transfer processes.

## Subtopic: Moisture, Clouds and the Three Rainfall Types

Rain is a sequence, and revision means owning every step of it. Air holds invisible WATER VAPOUR, delivered by EVAPORATION from oceans, dams and soil, and by TRANSPIRATION from plants. HUMIDITY measures that vapour: absolute humidity is the actual mass of vapour in a cubic metre of air, while RELATIVE HUMIDITY — the exam's favourite — is the amount of vapour in the air expressed as a percentage of the maximum that air at that temperature could hold. Warm air can hold more vapour than cold air, so cooling air raises its relative humidity without adding any water. Cool air far enough and it reaches DEW POINT — the temperature at which relative humidity hits 100 per cent and the air is saturated. Cool it further and CONDENSATION begins: vapour turns to droplets on microscopic particles called condensation nuclei — dust, salt, smoke. Condensation at ground level makes dew, frost, fog and mist; condensation aloft makes CLOUD.

Clouds come in three families, named by shape and height. CIRRUS: high, thin, wispy ice-crystal streaks — fair weather now, often change coming. CUMULUS: heaped, cauliflower towers built by convection — and when a cumulus grows into the deep, anvil-topped CUMULONIMBUS, expect thunder, hail and heavy rain. STRATUS: flat grey layers covering the sky, bringing drizzle and gloom. For rain to fall, droplets must merge until heavy enough to drop — but the deeper logic is this: ALL significant rain requires air to RISE, because rising air cools, and cooling is what wrings the water out. There are exactly three ways air is forced to rise, and they are the three types of rainfall.

CONVECTIONAL RAIN: the ground heats strongly, air above it rises in convection currents, cools, condenses, and builds afternoon thunderstorms — the Highveld summer storm, complete with lightning and hail, is South Africa's textbook case. OROGRAPHIC or RELIEF RAIN: moist air is forced up the side of a mountain barrier; it cools and rains on the WINDWARD slope, then descends the far side warming and drying, leaving a RAIN SHADOW on the LEEWARD slope — the wet seaward face of the Drakensberg against the dry interior behind it, or the green windward slopes of the Cape ranges against the arid Little Karoo. FRONTAL or CYCLONIC RAIN: two air masses meet and the warm one is forced to rise over the cold one along a FRONT — this is the winter rain of the Western Cape, delivered by cold fronts swinging up from the Southern Ocean. One province in this country gets its rain mainly in winter from fronts; most of the rest gets it in summer from convection — that contrast alone has carried many an exam answer.

Pause here for this section's questions: relative humidity and dew point, the cloud families, and the three rainfall types matched to their South African homes.

## Subtopic: The Restless Crust — Plates, Folds, Faults and Quakes

Term 2 in one architecture. The earth is layered: a thin, cool CRUST — OCEANIC crust, thinner, denser, basaltic; CONTINENTAL crust, thicker, lighter, granitic; below it the MANTLE, hot rock that flows slowly, with the crust and uppermost mantle forming rigid LITHOSPHERE plates riding on the softer ASTHENOSPHERE; and at the centre the CORE, a liquid outer core of iron and nickel around a solid inner core. The evidence that continents move: the jigsaw fit of Africa and South America, matching fossils such as Mesosaurus and Glossopteris on both sides of the Atlantic, matching rock sequences and coalfields, and glacial scars in places now warm — assembled by Alfred Wegener into CONTINENTAL DRIFT, and later explained by an engine he lacked: CONVECTION CURRENTS in the mantle, hot rock rising, spreading, cooling and sinking, dragging the plates.

The plates meet in three ways, and each boundary manufactures its own catalogue. DIVERGENT boundaries: plates pull apart, magma rises to fill the gap, making mid-ocean ridges like the Mid-Atlantic Ridge and, on land, rift valleys. CONVERGENT boundaries: plates collide — ocean meets continent and the denser ocean plate SUBDUCTS beneath, melting to feed volcanoes and raising ranges like the Andes; ocean meets ocean and subduction builds island arcs like Japan; continent meets continent and, with nothing able to sink, the crust crumples into fold mountains like the Himalayas. TRANSFORM boundaries: plates slide past each other, building nothing but storing the strain that snaps as earthquakes — the San Andreas Fault. Where the force BENDS rock instead of breaking it, layers buckle into FOLDS: the upfold is the ANTICLINE, the downfold the SYNCLINE, and compressed folds stack into FOLD MOUNTAINS — South Africa's own Cape Fold Mountains, folded around 300 million years ago, long before the Andes existed. Where rock SNAPS instead, the crack is a FAULT: tension pulls blocks apart in a NORMAL fault, compression drives one block over another in a REVERSE fault. Drop a block between two parallel faults and you have a GRABEN or rift valley — the East African Rift Valley, slowly tearing the continent — and the raised block left standing is a HORST or block mountain.

Earthquakes and volcanoes complete the set. An EARTHQUAKE is the sudden release of stored strain energy; the point of release underground is the FOCUS, the point on the surface directly above it the EPICENTRE, where shaking is usually strongest. Two scales, never to be confused: MAGNITUDE scales such as Richter measure the ENERGY released, instrumentally, one value per quake; the MERCALLI scale measures INTENSITY — the observed effects and damage — and varies from place to place. The death toll depends less on magnitude than on preparedness: building codes, drills and rescue capacity explain why equal quakes kill unequally. A VOLCANO is an opening through which magma, gas and fragments reach the surface: runny, low-silica lava builds broad, gentle SHIELD volcanoes; sticky, gas-rich lava alternates with ash to build steep, explosive COMPOSITE volcanoes, the dangerous kind at subduction zones. South Africa has no active volcano, but the Pilanesberg is the eroded root of an ancient one — and the benefits of volcanic regions, fertile soils and geothermal energy, are why millions still live on their slopes.

The questions for this section are ready: crust to core, the drift evidence, the three boundaries with their landforms, anticline against syncline, horst against graben, and the two earthquake scales.

## Subtopic: Mapwork Essentials — Scale, Contours, Gradient and the Synoptic Chart

Mapwork is the one certainty on the paper, so rehearse the toolkit until it is reflex. The standard sheet is the 1:50 000 TOPOGRAPHIC MAP — one unit on the map is 50 000 of the same unit on the ground, so 2 centimetres represents 1 kilometre. Scale comes in three dresses: the word scale, the ratio scale, and the line scale you measure against. Position is fixed by degrees, minutes and seconds of latitude and longitude, south and east for this country. Direction runs from TRUE NORTH, but a compass points to MAGNETIC NORTH; the angle between them is the MAGNETIC DECLINATION, given on the map sheet and changing slightly each year, and a bearing question expects you to take the true bearing and adjust it by the declination, calculated for the current year, to state the magnetic bearing.

Now the language of relief. A CONTOUR LINE joins points of equal height above sea level; on the 1:50 000 sheet the CONTOUR INTERVAL is 20 metres. The rules: contours never cross, never split, and close spacing means STEEP land while wide spacing means gentle land. From those rules, whole landscapes rise off the flat paper: circles inside circles climbing to a hilltop; a mountain with a flat summit ringed by tightly stacked contours reading as the Karoo's tabletop hills; contours forming a V that points UPSTREAM wherever they cross a river valley; the same V-shapes pointing downhill along a spur; an even ladder of contours on a uniform slope, contours bunched at the top of a slope reading as convex, bunched at the bottom reading as concave; and a saddle of low ground between two summits reading as a neck or poort between them.

Two calculations recur every year. GRADIENT is vertical rise over horizontal distance, both in the same units: if two points differ by 200 metres in height and lie 4 000 metres apart, gradient is 200 over 4 000, which reduces to 1 in 20 — meaning you climb 1 metre for every 20 travelled. Always state it as 1 in something, and interpret it: 1 in 8 is steep, 1 in 200 is nearly flat. The CROSS-SECTION turns a line on the map into a side-on profile: read off each contour crossing along the line, plot height against distance, and describe the shape — where it climbs, where it flattens, whether a dam wall or a proposed road would sit sensibly on it. And the SYNOPTIC CHART is mapwork's weather twin: a STATION MODEL packs one place's weather into a circle — shading for cloud cover in eighths, a wind shaft flying WITH the wind toward the station, feathers counting speed in knots, temperature upper left, dew point lower left. Around the stations run ISOBARS joining equal pressure, labelled in hectopascals around highs and lows; and the toothed line of a COLD FRONT tells its before-and-after story: ahead of it, warm, northerly wind and falling pressure; behind it, a sharp temperature drop, wind swinging to the south-west, rising pressure, and the showers that water the Cape's winter wheat.

The last expert questions are with you now: scale and declination, contour patterns named from their description, the gradient calculation, and the station model read element by element.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole year again — this time with a stove, a wet towel, a cracked eggshell and a bathtub.

## Subtopic: The Pot on the Stove — Air, Heat and Rain Made Simple

Picture the earth as a pot of water on a stove — except the flame is upside down. The sun does not heat the air on the way down; its energy mostly passes through and heats the GROUND, and the ground then heats the air sitting on it. That is the single sentence that unlocks Term 1: the atmosphere is heated from below. It explains why mountaintops are cold although they are nearer the sun — they are further from the heater. It explains why afternoon is hotter than noon — the ground needs time to warm and pass its heat upward. And it explains convection: air touching the warm ground expands, gets lighter, and rises like the wobble above a braai fire.

Now hang a wet towel over the stove. Warm air is a thirsty sponge — it soaks up invisible water vapour, and the warmer it is, the more it can hold. Cool that air down and the sponge shrinks: at some temperature the air is holding all it possibly can — that temperature is the DEW POINT — and any further cooling squeezes water out as droplets. On cold grass at dawn, that squeeze is dew; in the air above you, it is cloud. So the recipe for rain is simply: get moist air to RISE, because rising air always cools. And there are only three lifts in the building. Lift one, the hot-ground lift: the Highveld bakes all morning, air rockets up after lunch, and by four o'clock a purple thunderstorm is dropping hail on Soweto — convectional rain. Lift two, the mountain lift: sea air slides up the Drakensberg's face and rains on the climb, then rolls down the far side dry — orographic rain, and the dry far side is the rain shadow. Lift three, the wedge lift: a cold front from the Southern Ocean drives under the Cape's warm air like a spade under soil, forcing it up — frontal rain, Cape Town's soggy winter. Three lifts, three rainfall types, three South African postcards.

One more stove fact wins marks: the pot has a lid. Carbon dioxide and water vapour act like a blanket over the pot, catching the heat the ground sends back up. A natural blanket is why earth is livable at all; the problem is that burning coal keeps knitting the blanket thicker. Keep blanket and shield separate in your head: the OZONE layer, high in the stratosphere, is a different thing entirely — sunscreen, not blanket — stopping ultraviolet on the way IN, while greenhouse gases stop heat on the way OUT.

Quick check — this section's questions are coming to you now: the upside-down flame, the three lifts, and blanket against sunscreen.

## Subtopic: The Cracked Eggshell — Plates, Folds and Faults Made Simple

Hard-boil an egg, tap the shell all over, and hold it gently: a thin, cracked shell over a soft inside, every piece able to jostle its neighbours. That is the earth — the shell pieces are PLATES, and the jostling is driven from below, where slowly churning hot rock in the mantle rises, spreads sideways, and drags the shell pieces along like foam on stirred soup.

Pieces of shell can do three things to each other, and each move builds its own scenery. They can pull APART — molten rock rises into the gap, and you get the long volcanic seams of the mid-ocean ridges, or on land a widening trench like the East African Rift Valley, where the continent is slowly unzipping. They can push TOGETHER — and then the heavier piece dives underneath, melts, and feeds a line of volcanoes, or, when two continents meet and neither can dive, the land simply crumples upward into the Himalayas. Or they can grind PAST each other — no mountains, no volcanoes, just strain quietly stored and then let go all at once as an earthquake.

Now shrink the drama to a towel and a slab. Push a towel along a table from both ends and it rises into ridges and valleys: rock does the same when squeezed slowly, and the ridges are anticlines, the dips synclines — the Cape Fold Mountains are a towel that was pushed about 300 million years ago and never smoothed out. But push or pull rock too fast for bending and it SNAPS: that break is a fault. Pull two breaks apart and the block between them slides down — a rift valley; leave a block standing high between two dropped neighbours — a block mountain. And when snapped rock finally slips, the jolt races outward as an earthquake: the underground snapping point is the focus, the ground-level spot directly above it — where the news cameras go — is the epicentre. Remember the two rulers: one ruler measures the size of the snap itself, the energy — that is magnitude, one number per quake; the other measures what people felt and what fell down — that is Mercalli intensity, and it changes from suburb to suburb. Same quake, two different questions.

This section's questions are up now: the eggshell, the three moves, the towel against the snap, and the two rulers.

## Subtopic: The Bathtub Rings — Mapwork Rehearsed Out Loud

Contour lines terrify learners until they see the bathtub. Let dirty water drain slowly from a tub with a toy mountain standing in it: every time the water level drops 20 metres — in map terms — it leaves a ring on the mountain. Each ring is a contour: a line joining every point at exactly the same height. Rings tightly packed on the side of the toy mean a steep face, because the slope climbs many rings in a short distance; rings far apart mean a gentle rise. A ring closing on itself with smaller rings inside it is a summit. Rings making an arrow wherever a stream crosses them — that arrow of the V points UPSTREAM, into the high ground, one of the most reliable marks on the whole paper.

Now rehearse the two calculations as spoken routines. Gradient: find the height difference between the two points — subtract the lower contour from the higher; find the distance between them — measure on the map and convert with the scale, on a 1:50 000 sheet every 2 centimetres being 1 kilometre; then divide the distance by the height difference and say it as 1 in the answer. Two hundred metres of climb over 4 kilometres: 4 000 divided by 200 is 20, so the gradient is 1 in 20 — climb 1 metre for every 20 metres walked, a slope a loaded bakkie takes without complaint. Cross-section: walk the ruler line with your voice — starts at 1 060 metres by the river, climbs steadily through 1 100, ramps steeply to the 1 240 crest where the contours crowd, then descends gently to the far plain. Say the shape and you have drawn it.

Finish with the weather map, read like a text message from the sky. Each town wears a little badge — the station model. The circle's shading says how much cloud, in eighths. The tail on the circle is a wind arrow flying INTO the station — tail from the north-west means a north-westerly wind — and the feathers on the tail count the speed in knots. Top left of the badge, the temperature; bottom left, the dew point — and when those two numbers sit close together, the air is nearly saturated and rain is close. Then find the toothed line marching toward the Cape: the cold front. Ahead of it the badge reads warm and the pressure is falling; as it passes, wind swings to the south-west, the temperature drops, pressure climbs, and the rain arrives. Read one badge, track one front, and the synoptic question is banked.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now: bathtub rings and the upstream V, the gradient routine spoken in full, and the station badge decoded.
