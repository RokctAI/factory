**Thandi:** Questions that come up every time this material is revised. First: if the atmosphere is heated from below, why is the hottest part of the day around two or three in the afternoon and not at noon, when the sun is highest?

**Tutor:** Because the air's heat arrives second-hand, and second-hand delivery takes time. At noon the sun's input peaks, but the ground is still absorbing faster than it loses — it keeps warming past noon, the way a pot keeps heating after the flame is at maximum. Only once the surface has banked that energy does it pass the heat upward, by conduction to the touching air, then convection lifting it. So air temperature lags the sun by two to three hours, peaking mid-afternoon. The same lag logic answers two other favourites. The seasonal version: the southern hemisphere's hottest month is January, not December when the solstice falls, because land and ocean keep absorbing past the solstice. And the daily minimum: coldest just before sunrise, not at midnight, because the ground has been radiating heat away all night and the account is emptiest at the end of the night. One mechanism — surfaces store and release slowly — three exam answers. If a question ever pairs a graph of insolation with a graph of temperature, your first sentence should name the lag and your second should explain it with heating from below.

**Thandi:** Next: learners mix up which rainfall type belongs where in South Africa. Is there a reliable way to pin the map down?

**Tutor:** Pin it to season and lifter. Ask two questions of any place: when does its rain come, and what forces the air up there? The interior plateau — the Highveld, most of the summer-rainfall region — gets summer rain because that is when the ground bakes hard enough to drive convection: the lifter is heat, the type is convectional, the signature is the afternoon thunderstorm with hail. The south-western Cape gets winter rain because that is when the westerlies and their cold fronts migrate far enough north to reach it: the lifter is the front wedging under warm air, the type is frontal, the signature is days of steady soaking rain, not an afternoon outburst. And wherever moist air meets a mountain wall regardless of season — the KwaZulu-Natal side of the Drakensberg, the seaward slopes of the Cape ranges — the lifter is relief, the type is orographic, and the signature is the sharp wet-against-dry contrast across the crest: green windward, rain-shadow dry leeward, the Little Karoo being the standard example. Season plus lifter identifies the type every time, and stating both is usually exactly where the marks sit.

**Thandi:** Third: the boundary types. Divergent, convergent, transform — answers keep listing the wrong landforms under the wrong boundary. What is the fix?

**Tutor:** Track the crust like an accountant: is crust being made, destroyed, or neither? Divergent boundaries MAKE crust — plates part, magma rises into the gap and hardens — so their landforms are constructions of new rock: mid-ocean ridges under the sea, volcanic rift valleys on land. Convergent boundaries DESTROY crust — one plate dives and melts — so their landforms are the products of destruction: the deep trench where the plate bends down, the volcano line fed by its melting, and the crumpled ranges. The one convergent case with no destruction is continent against continent — neither can sink, so nothing melts, and all the energy goes into folding: the Himalayas, no volcanoes on top. Transform boundaries neither make nor destroy — plates only slide — so they build no mountains and no volcanoes; their entire output is stored strain released as earthquakes. So run the audit before listing: made, destroyed, or neither, then the landforms follow logically instead of from memory. And keep South Africa's position clear: we sit in the middle of the African plate, far from active boundaries, which is why our earthquakes are few and our fold mountains — the Cape Fold ranges — are ancient scars from a collision long finished, not active growth.

**Thandi:** Last one: in the mapwork question, learners lose marks on gradient even when they know the formula. Where does it actually go wrong?

**Tutor:** In three places, all avoidable. First, the units: gradient demands both measurements in the SAME unit, and the paper deliberately gives height in metres while your ruler reads centimetres of map. Convert the distance first — on 1:50 000, multiply centimetres by 500 to get metres — and only then divide. Most lost marks die at that step. Second, the vertical interval: it is the difference between the two point heights, not the height of either point, and if a point sits between contours you take the contour values the question implies, subtracting lower from higher. Third, the presentation: divide the horizontal distance by the vertical interval and write the answer as a ratio of the form 1 in so many — 4 000 divided by 200 gives 1 in 20 — and then, because interpretation carries its own mark, say what it means: a metre of climb for every 20 travelled, gentle enough for a road. The examiner's mark scheme typically splits the marks across conversion, calculation and expression, so showing the working is not decoration — it is where the marks live. Rehearse the routine out loud until it runs without thinking: difference, convert, divide, one in something, interpret.
