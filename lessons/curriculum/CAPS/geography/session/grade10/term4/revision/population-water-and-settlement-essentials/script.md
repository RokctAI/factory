# Part 1 — Expert

This revision session gathers the human geography of the Grade 10 year: the population topic of Term 3 — where people are, how their numbers grow, how their structure is read from a pyramid, and why they move — together with the water topic of Term 4 — flooding and the management of water in one of the driest countries on earth — and the settlement thread that runs through both: where South Africans live, why they live there, and what happens when the water and the people meet in the wrong place. Every definition is stated in the DBE's preferred wording, and every case is South African wherever the syllabus allows.

## Subtopic: Where People Are — Distribution, Density and Why

Two technical terms carry this whole section, and they are not interchangeable. POPULATION DISTRIBUTION describes WHERE people are — the pattern of their spread over an area, described in words like clustered, dispersed, dense along the coast, empty in the west. POPULATION DENSITY measures HOW MANY — the number of people per unit of area, calculated as total population divided by total area, expressed as people per square kilometre. The calculation must be automatic: a district of 2 million people on 40 000 square kilometres has a density of 50 people per square kilometre. And its trap must be named: density is an AVERAGE, and averages flatten reality — Egypt's national density looks moderate, yet nearly everyone lives crammed along the Nile with almost nobody in the desert around it.

The world's pattern is profoundly uneven: roughly nine of every ten people live north of the equator, and the great clusters — East Asia, South Asia, Europe, the north-east of North America — sit on fertile lowlands, river valleys and coasts. The empty areas repel for reasons you must be able to list: too dry, like the Sahara and the Kalahari; too cold, like Siberia and the polar lands; too high and rugged, like the Himalayas and Andes plateaus; too densely forested and disease-prone, like parts of the Amazon and Congo basins.

The factors explaining any pattern divide into PHYSICAL and HUMAN. Physical: water supply above all, then climate, soil fertility, relief and natural resources. Human: employment, transport routes, trade, history and politics. South Africa demonstrates both sets in one map. The east is wetter than the west — the rainfall gradient runs from over 1 000 millimetres on the eastern escarpment to under 100 in the Namib fringe — so people thicken eastward. But the country's densest concentration sits on the dry Highveld, far from any coast: GAUTENG, the smallest province carrying roughly a quarter of the national population, exists because of GOLD — the Witwatersrand discoveries of 1886 pulled labour, railways and industry onto the plateau, and the human factor overrode the physical ones. Meanwhile the NORTHERN CAPE, the largest province, carries around 2 people per square kilometre because aridity offers agriculture almost nothing. Add the coastal cities — Cape Town, Durban, Gqeberha — clustered on harbours and trade, and the apartheid legacy that concentrated millions in former homeland areas on marginal land, and the South African distribution map is fully explained.

Take this section's questions now: distribution against density, the density calculation and its average trap, and the physical and human factors behind South Africa's pattern.

## Subtopic: Growth, Structure and the Population Pyramid

Growth first. The vital rates are counted per thousand people per year: the BIRTH RATE, the number of live births per 1 000; the DEATH RATE, deaths per 1 000. Subtract one from the other and you have NATURAL INCREASE, usually expressed as a percentage — a birth rate of 30 per thousand against a death rate of 10 gives natural increase of 20 per thousand, which is 2 per cent per year. Natural increase deliberately ignores migration; total population change adds it back in. Supporting indicators complete the toolkit: the FERTILITY RATE, the average number of children born per woman, with about 2.1 being replacement level; the INFANT MORTALITY RATE, deaths of babies under one year per 1 000 live births — the single most sensitive indicator of a country's living conditions; and LIFE EXPECTANCY, the average years a newborn can expect to live.

The DEMOGRAPHIC TRANSITION MODEL organises how these rates change as countries develop, in stages you must narrate. Stage one: birth and death rates both high, population barely growing — the pre-industrial world. Stage two: the death rate FALLS as clean water, vaccines and food arrive, but the birth rate stays high — population explodes; much of the poorest world entered this stage in the twentieth century. Stage three: the birth rate follows downward as children survive, women gain education and contraception spreads — growth slows. Stage four: both rates low, population stable — most developed countries. Stage five, now visible in Japan and much of Europe: births below deaths, population shrinking and ageing. OVERPOPULATION, the concept examiners phrase carefully, is not a number of people but a RELATIONSHIP: more people than the resources of an area can support at an acceptable standard of living — a sparsely peopled desert can be overpopulated while a dense, productive city is not.

The POPULATION PYRAMID draws a country's age and sex structure: horizontal bars, males left, females right, ages stacked from newborns at the base to the aged at the apex. A WIDE BASE and narrow top — the classic triangle — means high birth rate, high infant mortality, low life expectancy: a young, fast-growing, developing population, with a heavy YOUTH DEPENDENCY load on its working adults. A NARROW BASE with straight sides and a broad top means low births, long lives: a developed, ageing population with rising AGED DEPENDENCY. The DEPENDENCY RATIO compares the non-working ages — under 15 and over 64 — with the working ages between. South Africa's own pyramid is transitional: a base that has narrowed as fertility fell toward roughly 2.3, but still youthful — and it carries a visible scar: the HIV and Aids pandemic cut into the young-adult bars and lowered life expectancy sharply in the 2000s before treatment programmes, the largest in the world, turned life expectancy upward again. Reading any pyramid, comment on three things: the base, the sides, the top — births, survival, old age.

## Subtopic: People on the Move — Migration, Urbanisation and Settlement

MIGRATION is a permanent or semi-permanent change of home, and its vocabulary must be exact. Direction: an EMIGRANT leaves a country, an IMMIGRANT enters one — same person, two labels. Scale: INTERNATIONAL migration crosses borders; INTERNAL migration moves within them, and its dominant modern form is RURAL-URBAN migration. Duration and choice supply more pairs: permanent against temporary — including seasonal harvest workers and daily COMMUTERS — and VOLUNTARY migration, chosen for work or family, against FORCED migration, where REFUGEES flee war or persecution, joining the internally displaced who never cross a border.

The machinery of explanation is PUSH AND PULL. Push factors expel from the origin: unemployment, drought, poor services, landlessness, conflict. Pull factors attract to the destination: jobs, schools, hospitals, city lights and the reports of those who went before. South Africa runs on two case studies. Internally, rural-urban migration from the former homeland areas — the Eastern Cape and Limpopo above all — toward Gauteng and the coastal metros: the young and economically active leave, so the sending regions age and lose their most energetic people while remittances flow back; the receiving cities gain workers and vitality but strain under housing shortages. Internationally, South Africa is the region's magnet: migrants from Zimbabwe, Mozambique, Lesotho and beyond, following a century-old path first cut by mine labour migration — with the tensions over jobs and services that flare as xenophobia being part of the honest answer.

Where the movers arrive is the settlement story. URBANISATION is the rising PERCENTAGE of a population living in towns and cities — a proportion, not a headcount — and South Africa is past two-thirds urban and climbing. Cities cannot absorb arrivals as fast as they come: the housing backlog answers with INFORMAL SETTLEMENTS, self-built homes on unclaimed land, usually the land nobody else wants — steep slopes, floodplains, land beside industry. Services lag: water points instead of taps, pit latrines, unpaved streets that turn to mud. The response is not demolition but upgrading — water, sanitation, electricity and secure tenure brought to where people already are — because the settlements exist for the soundest of reasons: they are where the jobs can be reached. Hold this section's ending in mind for the next one, because where the poorest settle — the floodplain, the wetland edge — is precisely where the water topic turns dangerous.

This section's questions are ready: the migration vocabulary in pairs, push against pull with the South African flows, and urbanisation with the informal settlement reality.

## Subtopic: Water in a Dry Country — Floods and Water Management

South Africa is among the thirty driest countries on earth. Average rainfall is about 464 millimetres a year against a world average near 860; the rain falls unevenly — generous on the eastern seaboard, meagre in the west — and unreliably, in a land of high evaporation and recurring drought. Two-thirds of the country receives less than 500 millimetres, the conventional minimum for rain-fed cropping. The consequences organise the whole topic: our rivers are few, seasonal and heavily engineered — the Orange, the Vaal, the Tugela — our storage lives in dams like Gariep and the Vaal Dam; inter-basin TRANSFER SCHEMES move water from wet catchments to thirsty ones, above all the Lesotho Highlands Water Project piping mountain water to Gauteng; and GROUNDWATER carries much of the arid west through boreholes into underground aquifers.

Yet this dry country floods, and revision must hold both truths. Floods need the physical triggers: intense convectional downpours, days of rain from a CUT-OFF LOW, tropical cyclone remnants in the north-east — falling on catchments primed by steep slopes, baked or saturated soil, thin vegetation. And floods become disasters through human choices: urban surfaces sealed under tar and roofs so runoff races into drains, wetlands — the natural sponges — drained and built over, and settlement pressed onto floodplains, with informal settlements on the flood-prone land the market left behind. The FLOOD HYDROGRAPH reads the result: on its axes of time against discharge, an urbanised or degraded catchment draws a short LAG TIME and a high, narrow PEAK — the flashy signature — while a vegetated catchment draws the long lag and low broad peak of absorption. Laingsburg 1981 and the KwaZulu-Natal floods of April 2022 stand as the two case studies: the racing flash flood and the days-long regional flood, both driven by cut-off lows, both deadliest where people stood on the river's own ground.

Management closes the year. The constitution promises sufficient water, and policy delivers a FREE BASIC WATER allowance — 6 000 litres per household per month — yet delivery strains against rural distance, urban backlogs, ageing pipes and non-payment. Nearly two-thirds of the country's water goes to AGRICULTURE through irrigation, so efficiency there — drip irrigation, lining canals, scheduling — buys more than any household saving; but households and industry must still do their part: fixing leaks that lose a third of municipal water, water-wise gardens, rainwater tanks, recycling in industry, and tariffs that price waste while protecting the poor. SUSTAINABLE water use means drawing no faster than the resource renews, protecting wetlands and catchments as working infrastructure, and planning for a climate that will make the dry country drier. Cape Town's near-miss with Day Zero in 2018 — the city within weeks of shutting its taps before savings and rain rescued it — is the standing warning that management is not optional.

The last expert questions are with you now: the dryness numbers and the engineering answers, flood causes physical and human with the hydrograph signatures, and the management toolkit from free basic water to sustainability.

## Subtopic: The Party in the Kitchen

Every house party ends the same way: thirty people in the kitchen, three in the lounge, nobody on the cold stoep. Nobody planned that — people simply drift to where the food, the drinks and the friends are. That is population distribution: the PATTERN of where people end up. Density is the counting that follows: people per room — thirty in the kitchen makes it crowded because the kitchen is small; the same thirty in a hall would feel empty. So density is always people DIVIDED BY space, and a big number means crowded only relative to the size of the room.

Now stretch the party to a country. South Africans crowd into their kitchens: Gauteng, tiny on the map, holds around a quarter of everyone — because that is where the food is, and the food is JOBS, cooked up by a gold rush that started in 1886 and never really stopped pulling people. The coastal cities are the lounge with the good music: harbours, trade, work. And the Northern Cape is the cold stoep: enormous, beautiful, and nearly empty — about 2 people per square kilometre — because almost no rain means almost no farming means almost no work. The whole map obeys party logic: people go where life is offered, and avoid where it is refused — too dry, too steep, too remote.

One trap before moving on: density is an average, and averages lie smoothly. Say the party's average is five people per room and you miss the whole story of the kitchen crush. Egypt looks moderately full on paper; in truth it is a near-empty desert with one overflowing corridor along the Nile. When an exam asks you to comment on a density figure, the smart move is always the same: ask how the people are actually spread inside that average.

## Subtopic: The Stadium Photograph

A population pyramid is just a photograph of a whole country lined up by age — babies at the bottom of the stands, elders at the top, men on one side, women on the other. Once you see the photograph, reading it is three glances. Glance one, the bottom row: is it packed? A wide base means babies are arriving fast — a high birth rate. Glance two, the slope of the sides: do the rows shrink quickly as you look up? Fast shrinking means people are dying young — hard lives, high infant mortality. Glance three, the top: is anyone up there? A broad top means people live long enough to fill the pensioner seats.

Two classic photographs to memorise. The triangle — huge bottom row, sides narrowing fast, empty top — is a young, poor, fast-growing country: many children, few grandparents, and the workers in the middle carrying a heavy load of dependants. The column — slim bottom, straight sides, wide top — is a rich, slow country: few babies, long lives, and a new problem, too few workers holding up too many pensioners. Countries move from triangle toward column as they develop, and that journey has a name and stages: first death rates fall — the explosion, because babies keep coming while funerals grow rare — then birth rates follow as children survive and women gain schooling and choices, and the photograph slowly straightens.

South Africa's photograph is in between: still youthful at the base, but narrowing — families now average near two children — and marked by history: the HIV years carved a visible notch into the young-adult rows and dragged life expectancy down through the 2000s, until the treatment programme, the world's largest, turned the numbers back up. And remember what the photograph cannot show alone: overpopulation is not how many people stand in the stadium, but whether the stadium's kitchens can feed them — a relationship between people and resources, never a raw count.

## Subtopic: The Bucket with a Hole — South Africa's Water Story

Picture the country's water as one bucket that must serve sixty million people — and start with the honest facts about the bucket: it is small, it fills slowly and unevenly, and it leaks. Small: South Africa gets roughly half the world's average rainfall, most of it on one side of the country. Fills unevenly: some years the summer rains arrive generous, some years barely at all. And leaks: about a third of the water piped through our towns escapes through broken pipes and dripping taps before anyone uses it. Everything in the water topic is a response to that bucket.

Response one: engineering. Build storage — the big dams; move water from full buckets to empty ones — the Lesotho Highlands scheme sending mountain water down to Gauteng's taps; and drill down to the water hiding underground, the boreholes that keep the dry west alive. Response two: fairness. The constitution says everyone must have water, so every poor household is entitled to a free basic amount each month — 6 000 litres — though getting it to a remote village or a growing settlement tests the promise daily. Response three: behaviour. Two-thirds of the bucket goes to farming, so a farmer switching to drip irrigation saves more than a thousand households showering faster — but the households matter too: fix the leaks, harvest the roof's rain, price waste properly. Cape Town proved the stakes in 2018, counting down to Day Zero before saving its way back from the brink.

And then, in this dry country, the bucket sometimes overflows — and where the flood goes is the settlement story finishing itself. The city arrivals who could not find housing built on the land nobody wanted, and the land nobody wanted is so often the floodplain — flat, empty, and empty for a reason: the river built it and returns to claim it. Seal the city under tar so the rain cannot soak in, drain the wetland sponges, and the storm arrives at those doorsteps faster and higher — the short lag and high peak the hydrograph draws for a sealed catchment. April 2022 in KwaZulu-Natal made the chain plain: days of rain on saturated ground, rivers up across a metro, and the deaths concentrated in informal homes on slopes and riverbanks. Revision should hold the whole chain in one sentence: people moved for work, settled where the land was left over, and the leftover land was the water's own — which is why population, settlement and water are one story, not three topics.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the small leaking bucket and its three responses, and the chain that links migration, settlement and the flood.
