**Thandi:** Revision questions from the class. First: density calculations keep going wrong even when the division is easy. Where does it actually break down?

**Tutor:** In the setup and in the sense-check, almost never in the arithmetic. Setup first: the formula is total population divided by total area, in that order — people over space — and the units of the answer are people per square kilometre, so if a question supplies area in hectares or mixes a city's population with a province's area, convert and match before touching the division. Run our example: 3 million people over 25 000 square kilometres; 3 million divided by 25 000 is 120, so 120 people per square kilometre. Now the sense-check, which is where the thinking marks live: ask whether the figure is dense or sparse FOR THAT KIND of place — 120 is moderately dense for a metropolitan region, would be absurdly high for the Karoo, and would be laughably low for inner Johannesburg. And then add the sentence that lifts the answer: because density is an average, state how the people are probably arranged inside it — clustered along the transport spines, thin at the margins. Divide correctly, check the scale of the answer, and comment on the arrangement: three steps, full marks.

**Thandi:** Next: the demographic transition model feels like five arbitrary stages to memorise. Is there a way to hold it as one idea instead?

**Tutor:** Hold it as one sentence: deaths fall first, births follow later, and every stage is just a snapshot of that time gap. Why do deaths fall first? Because dying is reduced by things done TO a population — piped clean water, vaccination campaigns, food security — which arrive quickly once a country can afford or receive them. Why do births lag? Because having children is decided WITHIN households, and that decision changes only as generations of parents watch their children survive, as girls stay in school, and as contraception becomes normal — slow, cultural change. Now the stages narrate themselves: stage one, neither has fallen — high births, high deaths, no growth. Stage two, deaths have fallen but births have not — the gap yawns open and population explodes. Stage three, births are falling to close the gap — growth slows. Stage four, both are low — stability. Stage five, births have fallen below deaths — shrinking, ageing societies like Japan. One process, five snapshots. And link it to the pyramid in the same breath: the wedge is a photograph of stage two, the block a photograph of stage four — which is why reading a pyramid and placing a country on the model are the same skill wearing two uniforms.

**Thandi:** Third: questions about informal settlements can feel uncomfortable to answer. What does a strong, fair answer actually look like?

**Tutor:** It looks like three refusals, each backed by geography. First, refuse to treat the settlement as a mistake: explain it as a rational response — people migrated toward work because push factors at home were real and pull factors in the city were real, the formal housing supply could not match the arrival rate, and self-built housing near employment beats formal housing three taxi rides away; that is why upgrading in place, with water, sanitation, electricity and secure tenure, has replaced demolition as the defensible policy. Second, refuse to ignore the danger: name honestly why the available land was available — the floodplain, the steep slope, the strip beside the factory — and connect it to the flood section: the residents face the shortest lag times and the highest peaks with the fewest resources to recover, which is how the Jukskei's December 2022 victims came to be where the water rose. Third, refuse to strip out the people: dependency on shared standpipes, the school hours lost to fetching and queuing, the dignity cost of pit toilets — these belong in the answer as facts, not asides. Rational origins, honest risks, human costs, and upgrading as the response: that structure is both fair and full-credit.

**Thandi:** Last one: the lesson keeps insisting population, settlement and water are one story. If an exam asks them separately, is the linking wasted?

**Tutor:** Never — the links are where the top marks hide, because linked answers demonstrate synthesis rather than recall, and synthesis is what the highest-order questions are built to reward. Suppose the question is only about flooding: an answer that explains the short lag and high peak, then adds that the people in the flood's path are there because rural-urban migration outran housing supply, has just imported the population topic to explain the death toll — that is analysis no flood-only answer reaches. Suppose the question is only about migration: noting that arrivals settle on floodplains and wetland margins converts a description of movement into an assessment of consequence. Suppose it is only about water management: pointing out that free basic delivery is hardest exactly where informal settlement grows fastest explains WHY the policy strains, not just that it does. The chain is short enough to deploy anywhere — work pulled people, housing lagged, leftover land was the river's, and management must now serve the people standing on it. Learn that chain once, and whichever of the three topics the question wears, you arrive with the other two as reinforcements.
