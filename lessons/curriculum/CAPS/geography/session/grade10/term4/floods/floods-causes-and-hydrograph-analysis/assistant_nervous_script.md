Hey, it's Thandi — if the hydrograph looks like the scariest graph of the year, hold on — this whole topic is one kitchen experiment, one wave with five labels, and three questions. That is genuinely all of it.

The experiment: pour water on a sponge, then on a sheet of corrugated iron. The sponge drinks it and leaks a slow trickle for ages — that is a healthy, vegetated catchment. The iron sheds everything instantly — that is rock, baked ground, saturated soil, and above all the paved city. Every cause of flooding, physical or human, is just something that turns sponge into iron: thunderstorm intensity, steep slopes, a full sponge from last week's rain, cut trees, drained wetlands, tar and rooftops.

The wave with five labels, in order: base flow, the river's everyday groundwater-fed murmur. Rising limb — steep, because the fast surface water arrives first. Peak discharge — the maximum, the danger moment. Lag time — and define it exactly: the gap between the HEAVIEST rain and the HIGHEST flow. Falling limb — gentler than the rise, because the slow soil water arrives last. Five labels, one storm's biography.

The three questions interpret any hydrograph they can throw at you: how high the peak, how soon the lag, how steep the climb. High, soon, steep — flashy and dangerous, the city or the bare mountain. Low, late, gentle — the sponge. The urban-against-rural comparison is just those three questions answered twice.

Case studies, one line each: Laingsburg 1981 — cut-off low, 400 millimetres in two days on bare Karoo rock, no lag, town on the floodplain, 104 lives: the flash flood. eThekwini April 2022 — days of rain on saturated ground, rivers up for days, over 400 lives, mostly in informal settlements on dangerous land: the regional flood.

Sponge or iron, five labels, three questions, two towns. Fold it that small and walk in calm. You've got this.
