# Part 1 — Expert

A flood is the most common natural disaster in South Africa and the one most likely to appear in a learner's own life — a river over its banks, a settlement under brown water, a road washed away. This session explains what turns rain into flood, separating the physical causes from the human ones, then describes the characteristics of floods, and finally teaches the geographer's instrument for reading a river's response to a storm: the flood hydrograph, described here entirely in words, including the urban-against-rural comparison that examiners favour.

## Subtopic: What Turns Rain into a Flood — Physical Causes

A FLOOD occurs when water overflows land that is normally dry — most often when a river receives more water than its channel can carry and spills across its floodplain. Rain is the trigger, but rain alone is not the explanation: the same storm can pass harmlessly over one catchment and devastate the next. The difference lies in the catchment's physical character.

Start with the rainfall itself. INTENSITY matters more than total: fifty millimetres falling in one hour overwhelms the ground's ability to absorb it, while the same fifty spread over a week soaks in quietly. South Africa's summer-rainfall interior is thunderstorm country, built for exactly the short, violent downpours that floods love. DURATION matters for the big regional floods: days of sustained rain from a CUT-OFF LOW — a pocket of cold air that breaks away from the upper westerly winds and stalls, spinning slowly and wringing moisture over the same region for days. Cut-off lows have caused several of South Africa's worst floods, including Laingsburg in 1981 and the KwaZulu-Natal disaster of April 2022. Tropical cyclones from the warm Indian Ocean add a third source in the north-east, drenching Limpopo, Mpumalanga and Mozambique, as cyclones Eline and Idai did.

Then the ground the rain meets. ANTECEDENT MOISTURE — the wetness left by previous rain — decides how much new water the soil can still accept; a saturated catchment turns almost all further rain directly into runoff. SLOPE: steep gradients speed water downhill into channels before it can infiltrate, which is why mountain catchments flood fastest. ROCK AND SOIL: impermeable rock and thin or baked soils reject water; deep sandy soils drink it. VEGETATION: plants intercept rain on their leaves, slow its journey, open the soil for infiltration and drink from it; bare ground does none of this. And CATCHMENT SHAPE: a compact, round catchment delivers all its water to one point at nearly the same time, stacking the flow into a single high peak, while a long narrow catchment spreads arrivals out.

Take this section's questions now: intensity against duration, the cut-off low, and the catchment factors that turn the same storm into different rivers.

## Subtopic: The Human Hand in Flooding

Floods are natural, but many flood DISASTERS are manufactured — by what people do to catchments and where people choose to live.

The first and greatest human cause is URBANISATION. A city seals its ground: tar roads, paved yards, rooftops and parking lots are all IMPERMEABLE surfaces, and rain that would have soaked into veld now runs off almost entirely — and fast, because gutters and stormwater drains are engineered precisely to remove water quickly. The result, established by measurement in catchments around the world: urban streams receive more of the storm's water, sooner, than the rural streams they replaced. The flood that a natural catchment would have absorbed becomes, in a city, a wall of water in a canal.

Second, DEFORESTATION AND OVERGRAZING strip the vegetation whose interception and root channels once slowed the water, and the exposed soil erodes — which adds a second injury, because the eroded silt settles in river channels and dams, raising riverbeds and shrinking the very capacity meant to contain floods. Third, WETLAND DESTRUCTION: a wetland is a natural sponge, storing floodwater and releasing it slowly; drain it for farmland or bury it under a suburb and the sponge is gone. Fourth, failing infrastructure: blocked or undersized stormwater drains, litter-choked culverts, and poorly maintained dams turn manageable events into disasters.

And fifth — the deadliest — SETTLEMENT ON FLOODPLAINS. A floodplain is not merely land near a river; it is land the river built and periodically claims. Development presses onto it anyway: formal suburbs because flat land is cheap to build on, informal settlements because the poor take whatever land remains — and the land that remains unclaimed in South African cities is so often the flood-prone land along the streams. When the Umgeni and its sister rivers rose in April 2022, it was informal housing on steep, saturated slopes and low riverside land that suffered most of the more than 400 deaths. Exam answers should state the pattern plainly: flood risk in South Africa is distributed by inequality — those with the least choice occupy the most dangerous ground.

Pause for this section's questions: the sealed city, the lost sponges, and why floodplains fill with the people least able to escape them.

## Subtopic: The Flood Hydrograph, Described in Words

The FLOOD HYDROGRAPH is the graph geographers use to read a river's response to a single storm, and you must be able to describe and interpret it in words alone.

Build it verbally. The horizontal axis is TIME, running over hours or days. The vertical axis is DISCHARGE — the volume of water passing a measuring station each second, in cubic metres per second, called cumecs. The storm's rainfall is usually recorded as a block of bars near the start of the time axis. The river's response then draws a curve shaped like a wave: it starts low, climbs, peaks, and subsides.

Name every part of that wave, in order. Before the storm, the line runs low and nearly level: BASE FLOW, the river's everyday water, fed not by rain but by groundwater seeping steadily from the soil into the channel. Then, some time after the rain begins, the line turns upward into the RISING LIMB — the storm's water arriving, first as rapid surface runoff, swelling the discharge hour by hour. The rising limb is characteristically STEEP, because surface runoff moves fast. The curve tops out at PEAK DISCHARGE, the river's maximum flow — the moment of greatest flood danger. The time gap between the heaviest rainfall and this peak has a name you must know: LAG TIME. Lag time exists because water needs time to travel from where it fell, across slopes and down tributaries, to the measuring point. A short lag time means a dangerous river: the flood arrives before anyone can react. After the peak, the FALLING LIMB — also called the recession limb — descends as the surface water drains away. It falls more GENTLY than the rising limb climbed, because the last water arrives by slower routes: through the soil, and from the furthest corners of the catchment. Finally the curve settles back onto base flow — usually slightly higher than before the storm, because the soil, now recharged, feeds the channel more generously.

Interpreting a hydrograph is then a matter of three readings. How high is the peak? How long is the lag? How steep are the limbs? A high peak, a short lag and steep limbs describe a FLASHY river — one that answers rain with sudden, dangerous floods. A low peak, a long lag and gentle limbs describe a subdued, absorbent catchment. Every factor from the earlier sections — slope, soil, vegetation, urbanisation, saturation — leaves its signature in exactly those three readings.

Your questions on this section are ready now: the axes and the base flow, the limbs and the peak, lag time defined, and the three readings of flashiness.

## Subtopic: Reading Hydrographs — Urban Against Rural, Flash Against Regional

Now the comparison the examination loves: the same storm, two catchments, two hydrographs described side by side.

Catchment one is rural and vegetated: grassland and bush, deep soils, a wetland along the stream. Its hydrograph: a LONG lag time, because interception and infiltration delay the water; a LOW, broad peak, because much of the rain soaks in and arrives slowly through the soil; gentle limbs; and a base flow that persists between storms, because the recharged groundwater keeps feeding the channel. The catchment behaves like a sponge — absorbing the blow and releasing it slowly.

Catchment two is urbanised: the same rain now falls on roofs, tar and paving, and is hurried away by stormwater drains. Its hydrograph: a SHORT lag time, because sealed surfaces reject infiltration and drains deliver water to the channel at engineered speed; a HIGH, narrow peak — the same water, stacked into fewer hours; a steep rising limb and a steep fall; and a meagre base flow between storms, because little water ever entered the ground to sustain it. The catchment behaves like a corrugated roof — shedding the blow all at once. Urbanisation, in hydrograph language, moves the peak up, the lag down, and the limbs toward vertical.

The same reading distinguishes the two kinds of flood. A FLASH FLOOD is the hydrograph of the flashy catchment taken to its extreme: an intense thunderstorm, a steep or sealed catchment, a lag of minutes rather than hours, and a violent, short-lived peak. Laingsburg, January 1981: a cut-off low poured more than 400 millimetres in about two days onto the bare, rocky Karoo catchment of the Buffels River; the town, built on the floodplain at a river junction, was struck by water that rose faster than warning could travel, and 104 people died. A REGIONAL FLOOD is the broad hydrograph writ large: days of sustained rain over a wide area — a stalled cut-off low or a tropical cyclone — bringing rivers up over one to several days across whole provinces, slower to arrive but vast in extent and duration, as in KwaZulu-Natal in April 2022, when saturated ground and continuing rain drove rivers through Durban's settlements, killing more than 400 people and destroying infrastructure across the province.

Close the loop with management, because the hydrograph also explains every remedy: anything that lengthens lag and lowers peak reduces flood risk — restoring wetlands and vegetation, permeable paving, keeping development off floodplains, maintaining drains, early-warning systems for the lags that remain, and the honest zoning that stops the poorest from being left the most dangerous land.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the two catchments compared reading by reading, flash against regional with the two named disasters, and the remedies read off the hydrograph.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same science with a sponge, a corrugated roof and a story from the Karoo.

## Subtopic: The Sponge and the Corrugated Roof

Take a sponge and a sheet of corrugated iron into the yard and pour the same jug of water over each. Watch what happens, because the whole flood topic is in this experiment.

Pour onto the sponge, and at first — nothing. The water disappears inside. Keep pouring and eventually a slow trickle leaks from the bottom edge, continuing long after the jug is empty. Now pour onto the corrugated iron: the water is off the sheet almost before it lands, one fast rush, then nothing — the sheet is dry and done in seconds.

The sponge is a healthy catchment: veld grass catching the rain, roots opening the soil, deep earth drinking the storm, a wetland holding the surplus. Rain goes IN, travels slowly underground, and reaches the river politely, spread over days. The corrugated iron is everything that stops rain going in: rock, ground baked hard, soil already full from yesterday's rain — and, above all, the city: tar, concrete, rooftops and paving. Rain hits, nothing absorbs, everything runs — fast.

Now the flood question almost answers itself. When does a river flood? When its catchment behaves like iron instead of sponge. Some catchments are born that way: steep rocky mountains shed water like a roof, which is why they flood fastest. Some are made that way temporarily: a week of rain fills the sponge, and a full sponge IS a sheet of iron — the next storm runs straight off. And some are made that way permanently: build a city, and you have paved the sponge.

Two more pieces complete the picture. The rain itself matters: a hard hour of thunderstorm rain beats the ground's drinking speed, where the same water as soft all-day drizzle would soak away — intensity beats total. And people can destroy the sponge directly: cut the trees, overgraze the grass, drain the wetland, and a catchment that once absorbed its storms starts shedding them.

Quick check — this section's questions are coming to you now: sponge against iron, born flashy against made flashy, and why intensity beats total.

## Subtopic: A Graph You Can Hear

The hydrograph frightens people until they realise it is just the biography of one storm, told by the river downstream. Read it as a story with sound effects.

Before the storm: the river murmurs along at its everyday level. That everyday level has a name — base flow — and it is not yesterday's rain but underground water, seeping steadily from the soil into the channel. It is why real rivers still flow in the dry season.

The rain falls — drumroll — but listen to the river: for a while, nothing changes. The water needs time to travel from the hills to the gauge. That waiting gap has the name examiners test most: LAG TIME — the gap between the heaviest rain and the river's highest flow. Then the river begins to rise — the rising limb, and it rises STEEPLY, because the first water to arrive is the fast water, the surface runoff that raced downhill. The sound builds to the top of the wave: peak discharge, the maximum flow, the moment the river is most likely to leave its banks. Then, slowly, the falling limb: the flood drains away — but gently, more slowly than it rose, because the last water is the slow water, oozing through soil from the far corners of the catchment. Finally the river settles back to its murmur — a slightly fuller murmur than before, because the soaked ground now feeds it more generously.

To interpret any hydrograph, ask the three questions. How HIGH did it peak? How SOON after the rain — the lag? How STEEP was the climb? High, soon and steep spells a flashy river: dangerous, sudden floods. Low, late and gentle spells a sponge catchment that takes a storm in its stride.

And now the exam's favourite trick makes sense: same storm, two graphs. The rural, vegetated valley draws a low, lazy hill — long lag, low peak. The city drain draws a sharp spike — short lag, high peak, steep on both sides — because tar and gutters turned the sponge into iron. If you can say the city's graph rises sooner, peaks higher and climbs steeper, and explain WHY in terms of sealed ground and stormwater drains, that question is banked.

Questions on the storm's biography are up now: base flow, lag time, the two limbs and the peak, and the three readings.

## Subtopic: Laingsburg and eThekwini — Lessons From Real Floods

Two South African floods, forty-one years apart, carry every lesson of this topic between them.

Laingsburg, 25 January 1981. A Karoo town — dry country where the Buffels River usually trickles or stands empty. A cut-off low parks itself over the region and wrings out more than 400 millimetres in about two days — several YEARS of Laingsburg's normal rain, delivered onto catchments of bare rock and baked soil, ground with nothing spongy about it. The river answers like a corrugated roof: a wall of water races down the valley into the town — much of which stood on the floodplain, at the meeting of watercourses. The water rises too fast for escape; 104 people die; much of the town is simply erased. The lessons, in the topic's language: an extreme cut-off low for intensity; a catchment that could absorb nothing; a lag time of almost nothing — a true flash flood; and a town built where the river always intended to return.

eThekwini — Durban and its surrounds — April 2022. Different flood, different lessons. Here the rain fell on one of South Africa's wettest, most crowded regions — for days, over 300 millimetres in a day in places, onto ground already saturated by earlier rain: the sponge was full before the main storm began. The rivers rose across an entire metro for days — a regional flood, wide and sustained rather than a single racing wall. And the dying showed the human hand: more than 400 people, concentrated overwhelmingly in informal settlements on steep slopes and low riverside land — the flood-prone ground that remains for those with no other choice — where mudslides took houses whole and stormwater systems, blocked or overwhelmed, could not help. Roads, bridges, water and power failed across the province.

Put the two side by side and recite the topic entire. Physical causes: intensity at Laingsburg, duration-on-saturation at eThekwini — both delivered by the cut-off low, South Africa's flood weather. Human causes: floodplain settlement in both, informal settlement and failing drainage in the second. Characteristics: flash against regional — the spike hydrograph against the broad one. And the remedies follow from the failures: keep people off floodplains and give the poor safer land to hold; protect the wetlands and vegetation that keep catchments spongy; maintain the drains; and build warning systems fast enough to beat the lag time — because the rain will come again, and the only question a hydrograph ever asks is whether we will be ready when the rising limb begins.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — the two floods compared cause by cause, flash against regional, and the remedies each disaster teaches.
