**Thandi:** Questions that always come up here. Start with this one: the lesson keeps saying lag time is the dangerous number. Why lag time and not the height of the peak?

**Tutor:** Both matter, but they answer different questions, and lag answers the human one: how much time do people get? The peak tells you how big the flood will be; the lag tells you whether anyone can act before it arrives. A river with a lag of a day allows dam operators to open sluices, authorities to broadcast warnings, and families to move themselves and their belongings uphill — the flood still comes, but it finds fewer people in its path. A lag of minutes cancels every one of those options, which is exactly what happened on the Jukskei in December 2022: the storm fell upstream, the sealed catchment delivered it almost instantly, and the people at the river had no interval in which any warning could have travelled. That is also why the management strategies all chase the lag: wetlands, vegetation and permeable surfaces are really machines for buying time. When you compare two hydrographs in an exam, make the lag comparison first and translate it into warning time — that is the reading with lives attached.

**Thandi:** Next one: if base flow is groundwater and not rain, why does the hydrograph show base flow rising a little after the storm? Learners find that contradictory.

**Tutor:** It feels contradictory only if you imagine the storm's water taking a single road to the river. It takes three, at three speeds. The fastest road is over the surface — runoff that arrives within hours and builds the peak. The middle road is through the upper soil, water moving sideways downslope, arriving over a day or two on the falling limb. And the slowest road goes downward first: some of the storm soaks deep, joins the groundwater, and only then begins its patient sideways creep toward the channel. That third portion is the answer to the puzzle. Base flow is indeed groundwater — but the storm made a deposit into the groundwater account, so for days or weeks afterwards the account pays the river a slightly better allowance than before. The elevated base flow after the recession is therefore not leftover flood; it is the storm's slowest travellers still arriving. And notice the diagnostic power: a catchment whose base flow barely lifts after a storm is telling you almost nothing infiltrated — which is the signature of a sealed or degraded surface.

***

**Thandi:** Here is one that catches people: the Natal floods took days to build. With all that time, why was a regional flood still such a disaster?

**Tutor:** Because a regional flood spends its violence differently — in extent and endurance rather than suddenness. Three things make it deadly despite the slower onset. First, scale: a stalled cut-off low rains over thousands of square kilometres at once, so it is not one town's emergency but every valley's simultaneously — rescue services that could concentrate on a single flash flood are stretched across a province, and in 1987 the destruction of bridges and roads cut off the very routes help needed. Second, duration: rivers that stay high for days soak foundations, saturate slopes until they slip, and keep re-flooding the same ground with each new burst of rain, so evacuation is not one dash to safety but days of displacement. Third, the false comfort of a slow start: water that rises gradually tempts people to wait it out, and by the time the danger is undeniable the escape routes may already be underwater. So match the danger to the type: the flash flood kills by speed, the regional flood by size and persistence — and a full answer contrasts them on exactly those terms.

**Thandi:** Last one: an exam gives one hydrograph, no map, and asks whether the catchment is sealed and urban or vegetated and rural. What is the safest method?

**Tutor:** Work the three readings in order, attach a mechanism to each, then commit to a verdict — observation, explanation, conclusion. First reading, the lag: measure from the rainfall peak to the discharge peak. An hour or two says the water used fast roads — paving and stormwater pipes — so urban; many hours to a day says interception and infiltration delayed it, so vegetated. Second reading, the peak: a tall narrow spike means the storm arrived compressed, the mark of runoff that never entered the ground; a low rounded mound means the ground took a share and is releasing it gradually. Third reading, the limbs plus the quiet sections: steep rise and steep recession lean urban; gentle slopes lean rural — and check the base flow on either side of the event, because a generous base flow means groundwater is being recharged, which sealed catchments cannot do, so a thin base flow is itself evidence of paving. Then write the verdict as a chain: short lag, high narrow peak, steep limbs, weak base flow — therefore urbanised, because sealed surfaces reject infiltration and pipes deliver water at design speed. Stated that way, each observation earns its mark and the conclusion earns the rest.
