**Thandi:** A few questions that come up a lot on this topic. First: why does the rising limb climb steeply but the falling limb come down gently? People expect a symmetrical wave.

**Tutor:** Because the two limbs are drawn by two different kinds of water travelling at two different speeds. The rising limb is the fast water: surface runoff that raced downhill over the ground, plus anything the stormwater drains delivered, all arriving in a concentrated burst soon after the rain — so discharge jumps steeply. The falling limb is the slow water: once the surface rush has passed, the river is being fed by throughflow moving laterally through the soil, by water still trickling in from the most distant corners of the catchment, and finally by the groundwater that the storm recharged. Each of those routes is slower than the last, so the recession stretches out — a long tail rather than a mirror-image drop. That is also why base flow settles slightly higher after the storm than before it: the soil is now wetter and feeds the channel more generously for days. The asymmetry is diagnostic, and you can reason with it: the more of a storm's water that travels by the fast route, the steeper and taller the whole graph — which is exactly what sealing a catchment with tar does. A perfectly symmetrical hydrograph would describe a catchment where fast and slow water travel identically, and no real catchment behaves that way.

***

**Thandi:** Next: is lag time measured from the start of the rain, the end of the rain, or somewhere else? Answers keep losing marks on this.

**Tutor:** Measure it from the PEAK of the rainfall to the PEAK of the discharge — heaviest rain to highest flow — and say exactly that in your definition, because vaguer versions cost the mark. The physical meaning is travel time: the storm's water must journey from wherever it fell, across slopes, into tributaries and down the network to the measuring station, and the lag is that journey averaged over the whole catchment. That is why every factor in the topic shows up inside it. Steep slopes shorten the journey; sealed urban surfaces and engineered drains shorten it drastically; a small compact catchment has intrinsically less distance to cover; saturated ground shortens it by refusing the slow underground route and forcing water onto the fast surface one. Vegetation, deep soils, wetlands and gentle gradients all lengthen it, by intercepting, absorbing and delaying. Interpreting lag is where the danger reading lives: a river with a lag of hours gives authorities time to warn and evacuate; a Karoo mountain stream with a lag of minutes gives nobody anything — at Laingsburg the water outran any possible warning. So when an exam gives you two hydrographs, compare the lags first and translate directly into human terms: shorter lag, less warning, greater danger.

***

**Thandi:** This one seems contradictory: the lesson says a flood is natural, but also says disasters are manufactured. Which is it?

**Tutor:** Both — and separating the two is precisely the analytical skill the topic teaches. The flood is the physical event: a river receiving more water than its channel carries and spreading over its floodplain, something rivers have done for as long as rivers have existed, driven by intense or prolonged rain on catchments that cannot absorb it. The disaster is what happens when that natural event intersects with human exposure and vulnerability — and people control both of those. We manufacture exposure by placing homes, and letting the poorest place theirs, on the very land the river builds and periodically reclaims: the eThekwini deaths of April 2022 were concentrated in informal settlements on steep slopes and low riverside ground because that was the land left available to those with least choice. We manufacture intensity by sealing catchments with tar, stripping vegetation, draining the wetland sponges and letting silt raise the riverbeds — every one of which pushes the hydrograph toward the short-lag, high-peak shape. And we manufacture helplessness by failing to maintain drains or issue warnings. The exam formulation worth memorising: rain and rivers set the hazard, but settlement patterns, catchment treatment and governance decide whether the hazard becomes a disaster — which is why the same size of flood kills hundreds in one place and nobody in another.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: if a question shows one hydrograph and asks whether the catchment is urban or rural, what is the checklist?

**Tutor:** Run the three readings, attach a reason to each, and commit — the diagnosis is only worth marks when every observation carries its explanation. Reading one, the lag: measure from rainfall peak to discharge peak. A gap of only an hour or two says water is reaching the channel by fast routes — sealed surfaces and stormwater drains — so urban; a gap of many hours or a day says interception and infiltration are delaying the journey — vegetated and rural. Reading two, the peak: a high, narrow spike says the storm's water arrived compressed into a short window, the signature of runoff that never entered the ground; a low, broad hump says the catchment absorbed the blow and is releasing it gradually. Reading three, the limbs and the base flow together: near-vertical rise and steep recession point urban; gentle slopes point rural — and check the flat sections, because a healthy base flow between storms means groundwater is being recharged, which sealed catchments barely manage, so a meagre base flow is itself an urban clue. Then write the conclusion as a chain: short lag plus high narrow peak plus steep limbs plus weak base flow, therefore an urbanised catchment, because impermeable surfaces reject infiltration and drains deliver water at engineered speed. State the readings, state the mechanism, name the verdict — that sequence collects every mark the question holds.
