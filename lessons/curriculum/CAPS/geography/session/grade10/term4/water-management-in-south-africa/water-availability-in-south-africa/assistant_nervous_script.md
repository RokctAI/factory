Hey, it's Thandi — if this topic feels like a flood of river names and rainfall figures, stand still for a second — the whole thing hangs on two numbers, one direction, and one sentence, and everything else grows from those.

The two numbers: 465 and 9. South Africa gets about 465 millimetres of rain a year against the world's 860 — barely more than half. And only about 9 per cent of that rain ever reaches a river. Quote those two and any examiner knows you own the topic.

The one direction: west. Rain shrinks from east to west — over 1 000 millimetres on the KwaZulu-Natal coast, under 50 near the Orange mouth. The rivers obey the same direction: permanent in the east, seasonal in the middle, dry-bed-until-a-storm in the west. If a question asks about a river or a region you have never heard of, place it east or west and reason from there.

The one sentence: the sun takes back more than the sky gives. Evaporation beats rainfall over most of the interior — that is why our dams lose water while they store it, why we have almost no natural lakes, and why groundwater, hidden from the sun, keeps the Karoo alive.

Names to have ready — a short list, not a phone book: the Orange rising in Lesotho and reaching the Atlantic; the Vaal working for Gauteng; the Thukela; Gariep the biggest dam, Vaal Dam for Gauteng, Sterkfontein the drought reserve, Theewaterskloof and Day Zero. One river system, three or four dams, one crisis story.

For the factors essay: two columns, physical and human. Physical writes itself from the numbers above. Human: people far from water, demand growing, pollution, thirsty alien plants, leaking pipes.

Two numbers, one direction, one sentence, a short list, two columns. That is the entire lesson folded small enough for your pocket. You've got this.
