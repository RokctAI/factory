# Part 1 — Expert

South Africa is, by world standards, a dry country — among the thirty driest on earth — and nearly everything about how its people, farms and cities are arranged follows from that single fact. This session establishes the numbers behind the dryness, surveys the country's rivers, lakes, dams and groundwater, and then assembles the full list of factors — physical and human — that decide how much usable water this country actually has. The water management debates of the rest of this term all stand on the foundation laid here.

## Subtopic: A Dry Country by the Numbers

Start with the master statistic: South Africa's average annual rainfall is about 465 millimetres. The world average is about 860 millimetres. We receive barely more than half the world's average rain, and that single comparison should open any answer on South African water.

But the average, as always, conceals the real story, which is unevenness in space and in time. In space: rainfall decreases from east to west. Parts of the KwaZulu-Natal coast and the eastern escarpment receive more than 1 000 millimetres a year; the central plateau gets 400 to 600; and the west — the Karoo, Namaqualand, the Kalahari edge — receives under 200, with the coastal desert strip near the Orange River mouth under 50. Roughly two thirds of the country receives less than 500 millimetres a year, the conventional minimum for rain-fed crop farming.

In time: the rain is seasonal, and it is unreliable. Most of the country receives summer rainfall from thunderstorms; the south-western Cape receives winter rainfall from cold fronts; only a thin southern coastal strip receives rain through the year. Between years, the totals swing wildly — drought years and flood years — and the drier the region, the bigger the percentage swings. Farmers in the eastern high-rainfall zones can plan; farmers in the west can only hope.

Now subtract what the sun takes back. South Africa's evaporation rates are ferocious: over most of the interior, potential evaporation EXCEEDS annual rainfall — in parts of the Northern Cape it exceeds 2 500 millimetres a year, many times the rain that falls. Water standing in dams and pans is constantly being lifted back into the sky. The consequence appears in the runoff figure, and it is the second number worth memorising: only about 9 per cent of South Africa's rainfall ever reaches its rivers. The rest evaporates, transpires through plants, or soaks away. A thin rainfall, harvested at 9 per cent, is the country's entire surface water budget — and most of what can be economically captured has already been claimed.

Take this section's questions now: the 465 against 860 comparison, the east-west gradient, the seasonal patterns, and what evaporation does to the budget.

## Subtopic: Rivers of South Africa — Perennial and Not

A country's rivers are its water arteries, and South Africa's must be known by name, by direction and by type.

The great system is the ORANGE RIVER — also called the Gariep. It rises in the highlands of Lesotho, where the country's heaviest rain and snow fall, and flows westward across the entire width of South Africa, about 2 200 kilometres, to reach the Atlantic at Alexander Bay, forming the border with Namibia along its lower course. Its major tributary, the VAAL, drains the industrial heartland and supplies Gauteng. Together, the Orange-Vaal system drains most of the interior plateau. Note the irony examiners enjoy: the river's lower course crosses near-desert — the river carries Lesotho's mountain water through land that could never have produced such a river itself.

The other rivers organise by coast. Draining eastward to the Indian Ocean, short and relatively strong: the THUKELA from the Drakensberg through KwaZulu-Natal, whose flow is so useful it is pumped uphill into the Vaal system; the LIMPOPO along the northern border through to Mozambique; and the KOMATI and PONGOLA in the east. In the south: the BREEDE and the GOURITZ. In the winter-rain south-west: the BERG and the OLIFANTS of the Western Cape. Eastward-flowing rivers are short and steep because the escarpment stands close to that coast, but they carry the most water because they drain the wettest slopes.

The crucial classification is PERENNIAL against NON-PERENNIAL. A perennial river flows all year round; South Africa's perennial rivers rise in the east and south where rainfall is high and reliable. A non-perennial, or periodic, river flows only in and after the rainy season; an episodic river, the extreme case, flows only occasionally after rare storms — the rivers of the Karoo and Kalahari, such as the Molopo and the Auob, may lie dry for years, then run for days after a cloudburst. Move west across the country and the rivers change type in exactly the order the rainfall map predicts: perennial, then periodic, then episodic, then none.

Two more habits of South African rivers matter for management. They carry heavy silt loads — soil erosion feeds mud into the water, and that silt settles in dams, steadily shrinking their capacity. And their flow is strongly seasonal even in perennial rivers, so storage is not optional: without dams, the summer's water would be in the sea before the winter's need arrived.

## Subtopic: Lakes, Dams and Groundwater

Here is a fact that surprises learners every year: South Africa has almost NO natural freshwater lakes. The handful of exceptions proves the rule — Lake Fundudzi in Limpopo, formed where an ancient landslide dammed a valley; the pans of Chrissiesmeer in Mpumalanga; coastal lakes such as Sibaya in northern KwaZulu-Natal. Why so few? Natural lakes need either glacial scouring, which our warm history never provided, or rivers with a surplus of water, which our climate never allowed. The dryness that makes lakes scarce is the same dryness that makes storing water essential.

So South Africa built its lakes. The country has more than 500 large DAMS, among the highest totals in the world for its size, storing roughly two thirds of the entire mean annual runoff. Know the leaders by name and purpose. The GARIEP DAM on the Orange is the largest by capacity, with the VANDERKLOOF downstream of it; together they regulate the Orange for irrigation and hydro-electric power. The VAAL DAM stores Gauteng's supply. STERKFONTEIN DAM in the Free State is the country's insurance policy — a huge reserve, filled by water pumped up from the Thukela, held for drought. THEEWATERSKLOOF supplies Cape Town, and its near-emptiness in the 2017 to 2018 drought gave the country the phrase Day Zero. Dams carry costs alongside benefits: they drown farmland, they lose enormous volumes to evaporation precisely because our sun is fierce, and they silt up as the rivers deliver their mud.

Beneath the surface lies the third source: GROUNDWATER — rain that soaked underground into aquifers, layers of porous rock or sand that hold and yield water, tapped by boreholes and springs. Groundwater is the lifeline of the dry two thirds of the country: Karoo towns and farms run almost entirely on boreholes and their windpumps, and dolomite rock in the North West and Gauteng holds some of the strongest aquifers. Its virtues are that it hides from evaporation and sits where surface water is absent; its dangers are invisible ones — pump an aquifer faster than rain recharges it and the water table drops year by year, and pollution that seeps underground, such as acid drainage from old mines, is close to impossible to clean.

Your questions on this section are ready now: why natural lakes are scarce, the major dams matched to their jobs, and groundwater's virtues and dangers.

## Subtopic: The Factors Behind Water Availability

Assemble now the full answer to the question this topic exists for: what factors influence the availability of water in South Africa? Organise them as physical and human, the structure the marks reward.

Physical factors first. RAINFALL AMOUNT — 465 millimetres against the world's 860 — sets the ceiling. RAINFALL DISTRIBUTION decides who sits under that ceiling: the east has water, the west has little. SEASONALITY AND VARIABILITY mean the water arrives in bursts, in one season, in some years — so availability depends on storage, not just rainfall. EVAPORATION, exceeding rainfall over most of the interior, shrinks every open water surface. RELIEF AND GEOLOGY decide what can be kept: the escarpment gives short steep eastern rivers and good dam sites, while porous rock stores groundwater in some regions and none in others. And the LOW RUNOFF RATIO, about 9 per cent, compresses everything: little rain becomes even less river.

Human factors, second — and increasingly decisive. POPULATION AND ECONOMIC GEOGRAPHY: the country's greatest concentration of people and industry, Gauteng, sits on a watershed plateau with no major river, so water must be moved to it over enormous distances. DEMAND GROWTH: rising population, urbanisation and industrialisation push consumption upward every year, with irrigation agriculture the single largest user at around 60 per cent of the total. POLLUTION removes water from use as surely as drought: untreated sewage from failing treatment works, industrial effluent, fertiliser runoff feeding algal blooms, and acid mine drainage from the Witwatersrand's old workings all turn scarce water into unusable water. ALIEN INVASIVE PLANTS — wattles, pines and gums spreading along watercourses — drink measurably more than the fynbos and grassland they replace, stealing a significant share of runoff before people ever see it. INFRASTRUCTURE LOSSES: a large fraction of municipal water, in many towns more than a third, leaks from broken pipes or is never billed. And MANAGEMENT itself is a factor: water availability in a dry country is not found, it is made — by storage, transfer, treatment, maintenance and law.

Hold the summary sentence: South Africa's water problem is a modest rainfall, harvested at 9 per cent, evaporating fiercely, distributed against the population map, and diminished further by pollution, invasives and leaks. Every scheme in the next lesson exists because of an item on that list.

## Subtopic: The Bucket Under a Hot Sky

Picture South Africa's entire water supply as one bucket standing in the yard, refilled only by rain. The first thing to know is that the tap above the bucket runs weak: we get about 465 millimetres of rain a year while the world averages about 860. Whatever else is true, the bucket starts half full.

The second thing: the sun is working against the bucket all day. Over most of this country, the sky can evaporate MORE water in a year than it drops — in parts of the Northern Cape the sun could take back five times the rain if the water stood open to it. Every dam, every pan, every puddle is quietly steaming back into the sky.

The third thing: the tap splashes. Of all the rain that falls on South Africa, only about 9 litres in every 100 ever run into a river where people can capture them. The rest evaporates, feeds plants, or sinks out of reach. Little rain, harvested at 9 per cent — that is the whole national budget in one line.

And the fourth thing: the tap is not even over the bucket for everyone. Stand in the east — the KwaZulu-Natal coast, the Drakensberg slopes — and the tap runs strong, over 1 000 millimetres. Drive west and it weakens with every hour: 600, 400, 200, until near the west coast the sky gives less than 50 millimetres a year — one good storm's worth, per year. Two thirds of the country gets less than the 500 millimetres that ordinary crop farming needs. The east farms maize on rain; the west farms sheep on hope and boreholes.

So when anyone asks why water dominates South African planning, the bucket answers: a weak tap, a thirsty sun, a 9 per cent catch, and the water falling on the opposite side of the country from where many of the people live.

## Subtopic: One River Wearing Two Names

Follow one river across the whole country and most of this topic organises itself around it.

Start in the mountains of Lesotho, the roof of southern Africa, where storms and snow dump the region's best water. A river is born there and heads west — the Orange, also called the Gariep. It has a job no other river could do: carry mountain water across a country that gets drier with every kilometre it travels. By its final stretch it is flowing through near-desert, a green thread through Namaqualand sand, finishing in the Atlantic at Alexander Bay. The land around the lower Orange could never have made such a river; the river is Lesotho's rain, exported west.

On the way it collects its great partner, the Vaal — the hardest-working river in the country, because Gauteng, our biggest knot of people and industry, drinks from it. Remember the strange fact from the population lessons: Gauteng sits on high ground with NO big river of its own. So the Vaal must be fed like a patient — topped up by pipes and pumps from other rivers — to keep the taps of Johannesburg running.

Now sort the rest of the rivers with one simple rule: they flow the way the rain falls. The east coast rivers — Thukela, Limpopo, Komati — are short, steep and strong, because the wettest slopes of the escarpment stand close to that coast. The south has the Breede; the winter-rain south-west has the Berg. And heading west into the dry interior, the rivers themselves start to give up: first they flow only in the rainy season, and finally — in the Karoo and Kalahari — only after rare storms, dry riverbeds for years that suddenly roar for a day. Perennial in the east, seasonal in the middle, occasional in the west: the rivers are the rainfall map, drawn in water.

Questions on the river journey are up now: the Orange traced from Lesotho to the Atlantic, the Vaal's job, and the rule that sorts all the rest.

## Subtopic: The Country That Built Its Own Lakes

Try to name a natural lake in South Africa. Most people cannot — because there are almost none. A lake needs a country with spare water, and ours has never had any. The short list of true naturals — Fundudzi in Limpopo, sealed off long ago by a landslide; the Chrissiesmeer pans; coastal Sibaya — is famous precisely for being short.

So the country built its lakes instead: more than 500 big dams, one of the busiest dam-building records on earth. String the important ones like beads on a necklace. On the Orange: the Gariep, our largest, with Vanderkloof below it — storing Lesotho's exported rain for irrigation and power. On the Vaal: the Vaal Dam, Gauteng's water tank. In the Free State foothills: Sterkfontein, the country's savings account — kept full deliberately, touched only in drought. Near Cape Town: Theewaterskloof, which in 2018 nearly emptied and taught the whole world the phrase Day Zero, when a major city counted the days until its taps would run dry.

But dams in this climate come with a tax. The fierce sun takes a deep cut off every dam's surface, every day. And the rivers deliver mud along with water — our eroding soils wash downstream and settle behind every dam wall, so the dams slowly fill with silt and shrink, year by year.

Which is why the drier half of the country lives off the third source, the one you cannot see: groundwater. Rain that soaked underground centuries ago sits in aquifers — sponges of porous rock — safe from the sun. Drive through the Karoo and count the windpumps: every one is a straw into that hidden sponge, and whole towns drink through them. The two rules of the sponge: pump no faster than the rain refills it, or the water table sinks year after year; and never let poison seep down into it — filth that reaches an aquifer, like acid water draining from old mines, is almost impossible to remove.
