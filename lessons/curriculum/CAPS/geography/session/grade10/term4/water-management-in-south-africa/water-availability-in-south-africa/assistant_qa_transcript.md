**Thandi:** A few questions that come up a lot on this topic. First: everyone quotes the 465 millimetres, but why is the runoff figure of 9 per cent called the more important number?

**Tutor:** Because rainfall is what the sky offers, while runoff is what a country can actually put in a glass — and the gap between them is where South Africa's difficulty lives. The 465 millimetres is already just over half the world average, but if all of it were harvestable, we would be merely dry rather than water-stressed. The 9 per cent tells you what survives the journey: of every hundred litres that fall, around ninety-one are taken back by evaporation under our fierce sun, transpired by plants, or soaked beyond economic reach, leaving roughly nine to gather in rivers where dams can capture them. Compare that with cooler, wetter countries where a third or more of rainfall becomes river flow, and you see that our disadvantage is doubled: less rain arrives, and a far smaller share of it is keepable. Then add the third layer — most of that thin runoff has already been claimed. The major rivers are regulated and allocated, which is why every new demand triggers a transfer scheme, a tariff debate or a call to fix leaks rather than a new river to tap. In an exam, chain the three numbers: 465 against 860, then 9 per cent of that, then near-full allocation — each stage tightening the squeeze.

**Thandi:** Next: how can the death rate of a river change three times across one country? People struggle with perennial, periodic and episodic.

**Tutor:** Keep the logic rather than memorising labels: a river's regime is its rainfall's biography written in water. A perennial river flows every day of the year, which requires a catchment where rain is high and reliable enough that groundwater keeps feeding the channel between storms — that is the east and the south, the Thukela draining the wet Drakensberg slopes, the rivers of the KwaZulu-Natal coast. A periodic, or seasonal, river flows in and after its rainy season and then shrinks to pools or dries entirely; that is the middle country, where rain comes in a summer burst and the dry months give the channel nothing. An episodic river is the desert's river: it flows only after occasional heavy storms, perhaps years apart — the Molopo and Auob on the Kalahari edge lie as dry beds that a single cloudburst can turn into a torrent for a day or two. Travel west from Durban toward the Namaqualand coast and the sequence unrolls in order — perennial, periodic, episodic — precisely tracking rainfall from over 1 000 millimetres down toward 50. The Orange is the celebrated exception that proves the rule: it stays perennial while crossing the desert because its regime was written in Lesotho's mountains, not in the land it flows through.

**Thandi:** This one seems like a paradox: why does a country this dry LOSE so much water to evaporation from its dams? Should storage not be saving water?

**Tutor:** Storage does save water — but in this climate every saving pays a tax, and understanding the tax is what the question probes. The same conditions that make South Africa dry — intense sunshine, low humidity, wind — make its open water surfaces evaporate at extraordinary rates: over the interior, potential evaporation exceeds rainfall, and in parts of the Northern Cape the atmosphere could lift more than 2 500 millimetres a year off a free water surface. A dam is exactly such a surface, held in place for months so the summer flood can serve the winter demand. The arithmetic still favours the dam — water in the sea serves nobody, and without storage our strongly seasonal rivers would deliver their water at the wrong time — but a real slice of what is stored never reaches a tap. Engineers respond in recognisable ways: preferring deep dams with small surface areas relative to volume, siting the strategic Sterkfontein reserve in the cooler Free State foothills, and — the elegant solution — storing water underground where the sun cannot reach it, by protecting and recharging aquifers. Silt is the companion tax: eroding soils wash into reservoirs and settle, shrinking capacity year by year, which links this topic straight back to soil conservation. A dam, in South Africa, is a leaking, slowly filling bucket — indispensable, but never free.

**Thandi:** Last one: for the essay on factors influencing water availability, what separates a list that passes from an answer that scores full marks?

**Tutor:** Three disciplines: structure, linkage and specificity. Structure means two labelled halves — physical and human — because the memorandum allocates across both, and a brilliant answer confined to rainfall and evaporation forfeits half the marks. Under physical: rainfall amount, its east-to-west distribution, seasonality and year-to-year variability, evaporation exceeding rainfall over the interior, relief and geology controlling dam sites and aquifers, and the 9 per cent runoff ratio. Under human: the mismatch of people and water, with Gauteng on a riverless plateau; growing demand, led by irrigation at around 60 per cent; pollution — failing sewage works, acid mine drainage, fertiliser-fed algal blooms — which removes water from use as surely as drought; alien invasive plants drinking runoff along watercourses; and leaking, unmaintained infrastructure. Linkage means showing consequence, not just naming: variability matters BECAUSE it forces expensive storage; Gauteng's position matters BECAUSE it forces inter-basin transfers. Specificity means an anchor for each claim — 465 against 860, the Karoo borehole, the Vaal supplying Gauteng. And close with the synthesis sentence: availability in South Africa is not found but made — through storage, transfers, treatment and maintenance — which is exactly where the next lesson takes over.
