**Thandi:** Let me put the questions everyone whispers about this lesson. First: if the Constitution guarantees water, how can a tap legally run dry? Learners feel the two facts contradict each other.

**Tutor:** The contradiction dissolves once you read the right carefully: section 27 promises access to sufficient water, and it binds the state to work toward that promise within its resources — it is a direction of travel enforced by law, not a magic spell over every tap. That is why the policy machinery matters so much: the National Water Act makes the state the trustee of all water and ring-fences the Reserve, and the free basic water policy converts the right into a deliverable number, six kilolitres a household each month. But between the number and the water stands infrastructure, and infrastructure can fail lawfully in the sense that no court order fixes a burned-out pump by morning. So the sophisticated answer runs: the right creates enforceable duties — communities have in fact taken municipalities to court over failed supply and won — yet its everyday fulfilment depends on budgets, engineers and maintenance. When an exam asks you to evaluate the policy, mark both sides: the entitlement is real and legally grounded, and its delivery is exactly as strong as the weakest pump between the dam and the standpipe.

***

**Thandi:** Second: the rising block tariff. Why not simply make all water cheap for everyone? Would that not help the poor more?

**Tutor:** Flat cheap water sounds kind and behaves cruelly, and the tariff exists because the designers understood that. Follow the logic in three steps. First, water in this country is scarce, so the pricing must discourage waste somewhere — if every litre is cheap, the cheapest litres to waste belong to the biggest users, and consumption balloons exactly where gardens, pools and long showers live. Second, the municipality's costs are real — dams, purification, pumping, repairs — so revenue must come from somewhere; price everything low and the system slowly starves, and a starved system fails the poor first, because the wealthy can sink boreholes and buy tanks while the standpipe community cannot. Third, the block design targets the help precisely: the first block is free or nearly free, which shields exactly the households that use little because they have little, while the upper blocks climb steeply and quietly transfer money from heavy users to the basic allowance. So the staircase does three jobs at once — protects need, punishes excess, funds itself. A flat price does none of them. If you can argue that trio, the evaluation marks are yours.

***

**Thandi:** Third: with the Lesotho scheme, is South Africa not simply taking a poorer country's water? How should we answer if that gets raised?

**Tutor:** Raise it yourself before the question does — the strongest answers on a transboundary scheme always weigh both sides. The defence of the project is that it trades what each country has for what it lacks: Lesotho has water it cannot sell in any other form and terrain that delivers it by gravity; South Africa has the money and the thirst. The royalties rank among Lesotho's largest earnings, the tunnels generate hydro-electricity for a country that imported most of its power, and the dams brought roads and construction employment into remote highlands. The critique is also real, and stating it earns rather than loses marks: highland communities were displaced from valleys their families had farmed for generations, and compensation programmes have a mixed record; downstream of the dams, reduced flows change the rivers Lesotho's own villages depend on; and royalty money arriving in a treasury does not automatically become water taps in a mountain village — many Basotho still fetch water by hand within sight of the export dams. The balanced verdict: a genuinely mutual exchange on paper, whose fairness in practice depends on how well the weaker partner's people, not just its government, share the gains. That is the shape of a full-credit answer on any large scheme — benefits, costs, and who exactly carries each.

**Thandi:** Last one: for the sustainability question, learners always overweight the shower-and-tap list. What proportion should household savings take in a good answer?

**Tutor:** Give the household list its honest size: essential, visible, and third in line by volume. Structure the answer by scale. The biggest single lever is agriculture, because irrigation takes roughly 60 per cent of the nation's water — so drip lines replacing flood furrows, lined canals, and scheduling by soil moisture recover more water than any city campaign; a few percentage points of farm efficiency outweigh every shortened shower in the province. The second lever is the municipal system itself: where a third of treated water leaks away unbilled, the repair crew is the cheapest new dam available — pressure management and pipe replacement recover water at a fraction of the cost of building supply. Then comes the household, and here is the subtlety worth writing: its arithmetic is modest in normal times but decisive in crisis, because household behaviour is the only lever that can move within weeks — Cape Town proved that when its residents halved the city's daily use while no dam, pipeline or farm reform could possibly have been built in time. So the model answer ranks the levers — fields first, pipes second, homes third — and then adds that in an emergency the ranking inverts, because speed beats size when the dams are weeks from empty. Scale plus speed: that pair of ideas is what separates a list from an argument.
