# Part 1 — Expert

South Africa's constitution promises every person access to sufficient water, and government policy turns that promise into a number: a free basic amount for every poor household. Delivering it, in the thirtieth driest country on earth, is one of the state's hardest engineering and social undertakings. This session sets out the free basic water policy and the different obstacles it meets in rural and urban communities, then examines the government's large-scale responses — inter-basin transfers, dam building, and municipal purification — and closes with the strategies of sustainable water use, divided between what government must do and what individuals can.

## Subtopic: Free Basic Water — The Policy and the Promise

Begin with the legal foundation, because it anchors every mark in this topic. Section 27 of the South African Constitution gives everyone the right of access to sufficient water, and the National Water Act of 1998 rebuilt water law around a striking principle: the water in rivers and underground belongs to the nation as a whole, managed by the state as public trustee, with a RESERVE set aside before any other allocation — enough water for basic human needs, and enough to keep the rivers themselves alive.

The FREE BASIC WATER policy, introduced in 2001, converts the right into a quantity: every poor household is entitled to 6 000 litres — six kilolitres — of clean water per month, free of charge. The figure comes from an allowance of 25 litres per person per day for a household of eight. Policy also sets a distance standard: that basic supply should be available within 200 metres of the home. Twenty-five litres a day covers drinking, cooking and basic washing — set it against the roughly 150 litres a suburban resident with taps, geyser and garden uses daily, and the word basic takes on its real meaning.

Understand the funding logic, because exam questions probe it. Water is not free to produce — it must be stored, piped, purified and pumped — so somebody pays. Municipalities fund the free basic amount from government grants and from a RISING BLOCK TARIFF: the first block of a household's monthly use is free or cheapest, and each further block costs more per litre. The design is deliberately double-edged: it protects the poor, who stay within the cheap blocks, while charging heavy users a rate that discourages waste. The swimming pool subsidises the standpipe.

Delivery has come far — the share of households with access to a basic water supply has risen dramatically since 1994, to almost nine in ten — but access on paper is not water in the hand. Infrastructure breaks, taps run dry for weeks in some towns, and a household whose only tap is a communal standpipe shared with dozens of neighbours technically has access. The promise is the easy part; the rest of this lesson is about why keeping it is hard.

## Subtopic: Why Delivery Is Hard — Rural and Urban Challenges

The obstacles divide sharply by geography, and answers should treat rural and urban as separate cases.

Rural challenges first. DISTANCE AND DISPERSAL: rural settlements — especially in the former homelands of the Eastern Cape, KwaZulu-Natal and Limpopo — scatter small clusters of homes across huge, often mountainous areas. Piping water to a dispersed population costs many times more per household than serving a dense suburb, and the areas needing the most delivery are often those the apartheid state deliberately left unserved. SOURCE PROBLEMS: many rural districts lie in the drier interior, dependent on small local dams, springs or boreholes that fail in drought. INFRASTRUCTURE AND MAINTENANCE: schemes are built, then break — pumps fail, pipes burst, diesel for generators runs out — and small municipalities lack the engineers and revenue to repair them, so villages return to fetching water from rivers. And where taps fail, the burden lands on WOMEN AND CHILDREN, who walk kilometres carrying twenty-five litre containers, at a cost paid in school hours and safety. Water fetched from streams shared with cattle brings the final cost: waterborne disease, including the cholera outbreaks that South Africa still suffers.

Urban challenges are different in kind. SCALE AND SPEED: cities grow faster than pipes can follow; informal settlements appear on land that was never planned or plumbed — often on floodplains or steep ground where services are hardest to install — and their populations rely on shared standpipes and communal toilets, with all the queuing, breakage and dignity costs that follow. PAYMENT AND POVERTY: municipalities depend on water revenue to run the system, but high unemployment means many households cannot pay, and disputes over billing, credit control and disconnection are a running source of protest. LEAKS AND LOSSES: much urban infrastructure is old and under-maintained; nationally, roughly a third or more of municipal water is NON-REVENUE WATER — leaked from broken pipes, lost through faulty meters, or used but never billed. A city that loses a third of its treated water must produce half as much again as its people actually consume. VANDALISM AND THEFT of pipes, pumps and cables adds a further tax.

Behind both geographies stands a shared institutional problem: many municipalities lack the money, skills and maintenance discipline the system needs. Infrastructure is built with grant funding, then decays without an operating budget — the cycle that turns access statistics into dry taps.

Pause for this section's questions: the rural cost-and-distance trap, the urban trio of informality, non-payment and leaks, and the shared municipal weakness.

## Subtopic: Big Engineering — Transfers, Dams and Purification

Government's response operates at three scales: moving water between rivers, storing it, and cleaning it.

INTER-BASIN TRANSFERS move water from a river basin that has more than it needs to a basin that has less, and South Africa is a world leader in them out of necessity: its economic heart, Gauteng, sits on a high plateau with no major river. The flagship is the LESOTHO HIGHLANDS WATER PROJECT, one of the largest transfer schemes on earth. Its logic is elegant: the highlands of Lesotho receive the region's heaviest precipitation, and they stand HIGHER than Gauteng. Dams in the mountains — Katse and Mohale — capture that water, tunnels carry it through the mountains northward, and from the border it flows by gravity into the Vaal system, needing no pumping. Lesotho earns royalties that are among its largest revenue sources, plus hydro-electric power; Gauteng drinks. A further phase, centred on the new Polihali Dam, is under construction to enlarge the flow. The older TUGELA-VAAL scheme does the same job the hard way: it PUMPS water from the Thukela up the escarpment into Sterkfontein Dam — expensive, because it fights gravity, which is why Sterkfontein serves as a drought reserve rather than a daily source. The ORANGE-FISH TUNNEL carries Gariep Dam water southward to irrigate the Eastern Cape's Fish and Sundays valleys.

DAM BUILDING is the second response: the Gariep and Vanderkloof regulating the Orange, the Vaal Dam anchoring Gauteng's system, and hundreds more. Dams smooth the violent seasonality of South African rivers — storing summer flood for winter need — at the costs already established: evaporation, silting, drowned land, and disrupted river ecosystems downstream.

MUNICIPAL PROVISION AND PURIFICATION is the third, and its steps are a classic exam sequence. Raw water arrives from dam or river carrying mud, microbes and debris. Step one, SCREENING: metal grids catch sticks, leaves and litter. Step two, COAGULATION AND FLOCCULATION: a chemical such as aluminium sulphate is stirred in, making fine suspended particles clump together into heavier flocs. Step three, SEDIMENTATION: in still settling tanks, the flocs sink and the clear water is drawn off the top. Step four, FILTRATION: the water percolates down through beds of sand and gravel that trap the remaining fine particles. Step five, DISINFECTION: chlorine — sometimes with ozone or ultraviolet light — kills the bacteria and viruses that survived. The treated water is stored in covered reservoirs and gravity-fed or pumped into the pipe network. The Blue Drop programme audits and scores municipal drinking water quality; its counterpart Green Drop does the same for sewage works, whose failures return polluted water to the rivers the next town must drink from.

Your questions on this section are ready now: the Lesotho scheme's gravity logic, Tugela-Vaal and Orange-Fish, and the five purification steps in order.

## Subtopic: Sustainable Water Use — Government and Individuals

SUSTAINABLE water use means meeting today's needs without crippling the supply for tomorrow — using no faster than the system replenishes, and keeping what exists usable. The strategies split into two role-player lists, and the split itself is an exam question.

Government's roles. REDUCE LOSSES: the war on leaks — repairing municipal pipes, metering properly, and pressure management, which alone can save a town a substantial share of its water. PROTECT THE RESOURCE: enforce the National Water Act against polluters, fund and fix sewage treatment works, and combat acid mine drainage. CLEAR ALIEN INVASIVE PLANTS: the Working for Water programme employs thousands of people to remove thirsty wattles, pines and gums from catchments — returning significant runoff to the rivers while creating jobs, a celebrated double win. PRICE WISELY: the rising block tariff protecting basic use while penalising waste, and restrictions with escalating severity in drought. PLAN AND DIVERSIFY: maintain transfer schemes, recharge and protect aquifers, and develop new sources — desalination on the coasts, and the direct reuse of treated wastewater, both proven during the Cape Town drought. EDUCATE: national campaigns that make saving normal.

Individuals' roles — the daily arithmetic that decides whether a city survives a drought. Fix the dripping tap and the running toilet, the silent household thieves. Shower briefly rather than bath deep. Close the tap while brushing teeth or soaping dishes. Harvest rainwater from the roof in a tank for the garden. Reuse GREYWATER — the gently used water from basins, baths and washing machines — on beds and trees. Plant indigenous, water-wise gardens rather than thirsty lawns, and water them in the cool of evening so the sun cannot steal the spray. Sweep the driveway instead of hosing it. Report street leaks. And respect restrictions when they come.

The proof that behaviour matters is recent and South African: facing Day Zero in 2018, Cape Town's residents cut consumption roughly in half — from around a thousand million litres a day toward five hundred million — through exactly these measures, and the taps stayed open. Sustainability in a dry country is not a slogan; it is the sum of millions of small decisions plus a state that keeps its pipes whole.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the meaning of sustainability, government's list, the individual's list, and the Cape Town proof.

## Subtopic: The First Six Thousand Litres

Here is the promise at the centre of this topic, in kitchen terms. Every poor household in South Africa is entitled, each month, to six thousand litres of clean water, free. Six kilolitres. Why that number? Count it out: 25 litres per person per day, for a household of eight people, for a month — that is the arithmetic behind the policy, and the 25 litres covers the basics: drinking, cooking, a wash.

Feel the size of 25 litres. It is five big cooldrink bottles. A single five-minute shower under an ordinary head uses more than that. A suburban home with taps, a geyser, a washing machine and a garden runs through 150 litres per person per day. So the free basic amount is not comfort — it is a floor, the amount below which the constitution says no one should fall. Section 27: everyone has the right of access to sufficient water. The six kilolitres is that right converted into a number on a municipal account.

But free water is not free to make — it has to be dammed, piped, purified and pumped — so who pays? Look at how the bill is built and you find a quiet piece of social engineering called the rising block tariff. The first block of water each month is free or very cheap. The next block costs more per litre. The block after that, more again, climbing steeply. A small careful household never leaves the cheap blocks. A home that fills a pool and waters a lawn climbs into the expensive blocks — and those expensive litres subsidise the free ones. The design punishes waste and protects need, in one price list.

One honesty check to carry into any essay: having access on paper is not the same as water flowing. A standpipe 200 metres away, shared with forty households, counts as access. A pump that broke in March and waits for a technician counts as access. The promise is measured in policy; the reality is measured in buckets.

## Subtopic: Moving a River Over a Mountain

Gauteng has a problem no city planner would choose: the country's biggest cluster of people, mines and factories sits high on a plateau where no great river flows. The Vaal alone cannot carry the load. The solution is one of the boldest pieces of engineering in Africa: go and fetch a river from another country.

Look east to Lesotho — the mountain kingdom, the roof of southern Africa. Two things are true up there. It rains and snows more than almost anywhere else in the region. And the land stands far HIGHER than Johannesburg. Those two facts are the whole scheme. Build dams high in the Maluti mountains — Katse, curved like a shield across its valley, and Mohale — and capture the water where it falls. Then bore tunnels through the mountains themselves, and let the water run downhill, by gravity, no pumps needed, kilometre after kilometre underground, across the border, into the rivers that feed the Vaal. That is the Lesotho Highlands Water Project: Gauteng gets water; Lesotho, which has more water than money, earns royalties that rank among its biggest incomes, plus electricity from the falling water on the way. A new dam, Polihali, is being added to send even more.

Compare it with the older, harder scheme on our own side: the Tugela-Vaal. The Thukela river has plenty of water — but it flows at the BOTTOM of the escarpment, below the plateau. So its water must be pumped UP the mountain wall into Sterkfontein Dam. Pumping uphill devours electricity and money, which is why Sterkfontein is used as the emergency reserve — a full tank kept for drought — rather than an everyday source. Gravity is free; fighting gravity is expensive; the two schemes are the two sides of that law.

And once the water reaches a city, it must be cleaned. The purification plant is five steps you can picture in a kitchen: strain the soup through a sieve — screening, out with sticks and litter. Stir in a pinch that makes the fine dirt clump — coagulation. Let the pot stand so the clumps sink — sedimentation. Pour the clear top through a sand filter — filtration. And a drop of chlorine to kill anything alive — disinfection. Sieve, clump, settle, filter, chlorinate: five steps from brown river to safe tap.

Questions on the mountain river are up now: why Lesotho, why gravity matters, why Sterkfontein waits for droughts, and the five kitchen steps.

## Subtopic: The City That Counted Its Days

Summer, 2018. Cape Town — a modern city of millions — published a date and called it Day Zero: the day the dams would fall so low that the municipal taps would be turned OFF, and every resident would queue at collection points for a daily ration of 25 litres. A three-year drought had emptied Theewaterskloof, the city's main dam, to a cracked-mud fraction of itself. The world watched to see a major city run dry.

It never happened — and the reason is the most important lesson in this topic. The city and its people cut their water use roughly in HALF. From around a thousand million litres a day toward five hundred million. Not with one giant machine, but with a million small decisions. Two-minute showers, standing over a bucket — and the bucket water flushing the toilet. If it's yellow, let it mellow. Taps closed while brushing teeth. Grey water from the washing machine piped onto the garden. Rain tanks on every second house. Pools covered or empty, lawns left brown. Restaurants without table water; car washes with waterless spray. Restrictions tightened level by level, prices climbing block by block, and a weekly dam-level report the whole city read like a sports score.

Rain eventually returned, as it does. But the arithmetic had already been proven: the cheapest, fastest new dam a dry country can build is the water its people stop wasting. Cape Town found half a city's supply inside its own habits.

Now zoom the lesson out, because the exam will. Sustainability has two role-players. Government does the big half: fix the leaking pipes that lose a third of a town's water before anyone drinks it; keep sewage works working so rivers stay usable; send teams to rip out thirsty alien trees — Working for Water, which pays wages to return water to rivers; price water in rising blocks; build and maintain the transfers and dams; try new sources like desalination and reused wastewater. Individuals do the million-small-decisions half: the fixed drip, the short shower, the closed tap, the rain tank, the grey water, the indigenous garden, the evening watering, the reported leak. Neither half works alone. A saving citizenry with rotten pipes loses the water anyway; perfect pipes feeding careless households drain the dams regardless.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — what Day Zero was, how the city halved its use, and the two halves of sustainability.
