**Thandi:** Class questions before we finish! Here is the first: my uncle works in Cape Town all year and only comes home to Cofimvaba in December. Has he migrated, or is he circulating? The definitions seem to argue with each other.

**Tutor:** Your uncle is the perfect boundary case, and boundary cases are where classification marks are won. Strictly, migration requires a change of residence that is permanent or semi-permanent, while circulation returns home on a short, repeating cycle. Your uncle sits between the two: his working residence has genuinely shifted to Cape Town for eleven months at a time, which is more than a commuter's loop, yet his home base — where his family, his cattle and his December live — remains in Cofimvaba, which is more rooted than a permanent migrant. Geography has a name for exactly this pattern: circular or oscillating migration, a long-period swing between two homes, and it deserves to be named rather than forced into the wrong box. The strongest answer states the strict definitions, shows the case straddles them, and then resolves it with the precise term — that sequence demonstrates you understand the classification rather than just memorising it. And add the historical note that earns the extra mark: oscillating migration is not a curiosity here; under the contract labour system it was the dominant form of movement in southern Africa for a century, which is why so many South African families still live stretched between a city income and a rural homestead.

***

**Thandi:** Next question: push factors and pull factors often look like the same fact written twice — no jobs at home, jobs in the city. Do we really need both in an answer?

**Tutor:** You need both, because they are only sometimes mirror images, and the cases where they differ are where real explanation lives. Yes, unemployment at the origin and employment at the destination form a matched pair. But now try a drought: it pushes hard, yet the city pulls with jobs and services, not with rain — the push and pull are different in kind. Try persecution: it expels people toward whichever border is nearest and open, almost regardless of what waits beyond it — push overwhelming, pull almost irrelevant, which is precisely what distinguishes forced from voluntary migration. Or reverse it: a bursary to a university is nearly pure pull, with nothing driving the student out. So the model needs both hands because migrations differ in which hand is doing the work, and diagnosing that balance is the analytical step. Then complete the machine with its two remaining parts. The obstacles between origin and destination — fares, distance, permits, leaving family — explain why identical pressures move some people and not others. And perception explains the rest: the migrant weighs the destination as imagined from a cousin's proudest stories, not as audited by statistics. A weak answer lists factors; a strong answer runs the whole machine — push, pull, obstacles, perception — on one named stream.

***

**Thandi:** This one is about the case study: if the Eastern Cape loses its workers and Gauteng gets overcrowded, it sounds like migration makes both places worse. Is anybody actually better off?

**Tutor:** Plenty of people are better off, and the exam expects you to resist the one-sided version in either direction. Weigh it household by household first: the migrant usually earns several times what the village could offer, and the monthly remittance that flows back is groceries, school fees and electricity for people who would otherwise have almost no cash income — for many former-homeland households it is the primary income, which is a real and large gain. The origin also receives returning skills and savings when migrants come home. The losses are equally real: the village surrenders its working-age adults, so fields go unploughed and grandmothers head households, and the local economy loses exactly the people most able to build it. On the destination side, Gauteng's entire history is migrant-built — the stream delivers young, motivated labour and new businesses, which is a gain the city's economy depends on; the strain on housing, classrooms and clinics is the cost, and it is concentrated in the poorest areas. So the honest summary is not that migration makes places worse, but that it redistributes gains and costs unevenly — the migrant household usually wins, the origin gains money while losing people, and the destination gains an economy while straining its services. Answer in that four-box structure, gains and losses at both ends, and you cannot be accused of telling half the story.

**Thandi:** Last one: when a question asks about migration INTO South Africa, how much should we say about xenophobia? It feels risky to write about.

**Tutor:** Say it plainly, factually and fairly — leaving it out is the actual risk, because a consequences answer that hides an important consequence is incomplete. The safe method is the method for any sensitive material: facts, mechanism, balance. The facts: South Africa experienced major waves of xenophobic violence against foreign nationals, notably in 2008 and 2015, with people killed, shops looted and burned, and families displaced. The mechanism: the competition migrants and locals experience for jobs, housing and services is concentrated at the bottom of the economy, in communities already short of all three, and that is where frustration has ignited — an explanation, note, not an excuse. The balance: in the same answer, record what migrants contribute — labour on farms and building sites, scarce skills in hospitals, shops and businesses in townships — so the account is honest in both directions. And keep your vocabulary precise: migrants are people classified as economic migrants, refugees or asylum seekers; the violence is named xenophobia; and its costs run from lives and livelihoods to South Africa's standing in the region. Precision and balance are what make hard topics writable — and they are exactly what the marker is looking for.
