**Thandi:** A few questions that come up a lot on this topic. First: where exactly is the line between migration and just moving around? Learners boarding at school, taxi commuters — who counts?

**Tutor:** The line is drawn at residence, and applying it strictly sorts every case you named. Migration requires a permanent or semi-permanent change of the place where a person actually lives. The daily commuter's residence never changes — the movement loops home every evening — so that is circulation, not migration, and the same holds for shoppers, tourists and weekend visitors. The boarding learner is a genuinely useful borderline case: home remains the family house, the school term is fixed and return is built in, so geographers file it under circulation too, as a longer loop. Seasonal farm workers following the harvests sit at the far edge of circulation — months away, but structured around return. The mine contract system is the case that teaches the most: eleven months in a hostel, December at home, repeated for decades. Because the pattern is semi-permanent and reshapes where life is actually lived, it is treated as migration — circular or oscillating migration — rather than mere circulation. The examiner's test is always the same two-part probe: has residence changed, and is return built into the movement's design? Answer those and any example classifies itself.

***

**Thandi:** Next: in essays people list push factors and stop. Why does a push-only answer lose marks, and what does a complete explanation contain?

**Tutor:** Because a push explains why someone leaves, but never why they arrive where they do — and migration is a choice between destinations, not just an escape. Rural unemployment in the Eastern Cape pushes equally toward Durban, Cape Town and Gauteng; only the pull side — where the jobs, the networks, the opportunities actually are — explains why the dominant stream runs to Gauteng specifically. A complete explanation therefore has four parts. One: pushes at the origin, sorted into the four families — economic, social, political, environmental. Two: the mirror pulls at the destination, and the strongest answers pair them explicitly — failing farm against factory wage, weak rural school against township high school with a matric record. Three: the obstacles in between — transport costs, distance, permits and borders for international moves, and the human cost of leaving family — because migration happens only when perceived gain outweighs these. Four: perception itself. People decide on the picture of the city in their heads, painted by relatives' stories and social media, and that picture routinely outshines reality. That final point carries real explanatory power: it accounts for continued migration into cities with severe unemployment, which a purely rational push-pull sum cannot.

**Thandi:** This one comes straight from the case study: why do so many Eastern Cape migrants keep a rural home and plan to retire back there, instead of settling in Gauteng completely?

**Tutor:** Because the stream carries a century of its own history inside it, and history built the two-home pattern deliberately. Under the migrant labour system, the law itself enforced oscillation: men could work on the Witwatersrand mines but pass laws barred their families from settling with them, so the rural homestead remained the legal, social and emotional home while the city was only ever the workplace. Repeat that for three generations and the two-place life hardens into culture: land and cattle at home as security and status, ancestors and family graves anchoring identity, the December return as the year's fixed point. When movement controls ended in the 1990s, whole families could finally move — and many did — but the older pattern persists in modernised form: a worker in Gauteng renting cheaply and remitting monthly to a household that keeps the rural base alive, with retirement planned for the village. Economics reinforces sentiment — urban land is expensive, the rural home is owned, and the remittance stretches further there. For the exam, this explains two things at once: why remittances and December travel are structural features of the stream rather than quaint details, and why the origin region's population shows the deep working-age gap — the adults are counted in Gauteng but belong, in their own accounting, to the Eastern Cape.

**Thandi:** Last one: how should we handle the xenophobia part of the international case study in an exam answer without being one-sided?

**Tutor:** With the same discipline the whole topic teaches: both places, both columns, and honesty in each. Set out the destination's gains first, concretely — migrant labour in agriculture, construction, domestic work and security; businesses founded in townships by Somali, Ethiopian and Congolese traders; scarce skills filled, including nurses and teachers from Zimbabwe. Then set out the strains just as concretely: many arrivals into an economy with high unemployment concentrate competition at the very bottom of the labour market, and the pressure on housing and services lands in the poorest neighbourhoods first. Name what followed accurately — waves of xenophobic violence, notably in 2008 and 2015, with deaths, displacement and burned livelihoods — and locate its cause where the evidence puts it: in that concentrated competition and frustration, never in the migrants themselves. The strongest answers add the origin side to complete the frame — remittances that keep families and even national economies afloat, against the brain drain of exactly the professionals those countries need to recover. The marker is testing whether you can hold gains and costs in the same answer without letting either erase the other. Four boxes, filled with specifics, ending on the observation that migration is neither a disaster nor a free gift — it is a transfer with winners, losers and a bill someone pays at each end.
