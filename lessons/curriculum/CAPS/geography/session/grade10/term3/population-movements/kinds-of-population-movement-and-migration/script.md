# Part 1 — Expert

People move — daily to work, seasonally to harvests, permanently to new cities and new countries — and every one of those movements reshapes two places at once: the one left behind and the one arrived at. This session builds the vocabulary that classifies population movements, sets out the push-and-pull machinery that explains them, and then works two case studies the CAPS curriculum treats as essential: the internal stream from the rural Eastern Cape to Gauteng, and the international stream across South Africa's borders, above all from Zimbabwe.

## Subtopic: Sorting the Moves — A Vocabulary for Migration

MIGRATION, strictly defined, is a permanent or semi-permanent change of a person's place of residence. That last word does the work: a shopper, a tourist and a daily commuter all move, but none changes residence, so none is a migrant. Geographers file those non-residence movements under CIRCULATION — repeated, temporary movements that always return home: daily commuting between township and city, weekly movement of learners boarding at school, seasonal movement of farm workers following the harvests.

True migration is then sorted along three axes, and classification questions are guaranteed marks if the axes are clear.

Axis one: WHERE. INTERNAL migration happens inside one country's borders — from the Eastern Cape to Gauteng, from a farm to the nearest town. The mover is called an in-migrant at the destination and an out-migrant at the origin. INTERNATIONAL migration crosses a border, and the vocabulary changes: a person EMIGRATES from the country they leave and IMMIGRATES into the country they enter — one journey, two labels, depending on which side of the border is speaking. Internal migration has recognised streams worth naming: rural to urban, the dominant stream in developing countries, driving URBANISATION, the rising share of a population living in towns and cities; urban to rural, its small counter-stream; rural to rural, such as farmworkers moving between districts; and urban to urban, common in industrialised economies, such as moving from Bloemfontein to Johannesburg for a promotion.

Axis two: WHY — by choice or not. VOLUNTARY migration is chosen, usually for work, income, education or family. FORCED migration leaves no reasonable choice: people fleeing war, persecution or disaster. A REFUGEE has crossed an international border fleeing persecution or conflict; an ASYLUM SEEKER is applying for that recognition; an INTERNALLY DISPLACED PERSON has fled the same dangers but remains inside their own country. History adds South African examples of forced movement inside a country: the apartheid-era forced removals that emptied District Six in Cape Town and Sophiatown in Johannesburg, and moved millions into the homelands.

Axis three: HOW LONG. PERMANENT migration intends no return. TEMPORARY migration does — including the contract labour that shaped this country, where mineworkers spent eleven months on the mines and returned home each December, decade after decade. That repeating pattern is called circular or oscillating migration, and it explains families that live in two places at once.

## Subtopic: Push and Pull — Why People Move

The standard machine for explaining any migration is PUSH AND PULL. PUSH FACTORS are the negatives of the place of origin — the things that drive people out. PULL FACTORS are the attractions of the destination — the things that draw people in. Every migration is a pair: something pushing, something pulling, and full answers always give both sides.

Sort the factors into four families, each with its push and its pull. ECONOMIC: unemployment, low wages, failing farms and landlessness push; jobs, higher wages, business opportunity and the sheer size of a city's economy pull. Economic factors dominate world migration — most migrants, most of the time, are moving toward work. SOCIAL: poor schools, distant clinics, and lack of services push; good schools, universities, hospitals, electricity, water and family already settled at the destination pull. That last one matters more than it looks: existing migrant communities make the next migration easier, which is why streams strengthen over time — geographers call it chain migration. POLITICAL: war, persecution, discrimination and instability push; peace, safety and rights pull. ENVIRONMENTAL: drought, floods, soil exhaustion and climate change push; reliable rain and productive land pull.

Two refinements separate strong answers from weak ones. First, the decision is weighed, not automatic: the migrant balances pushes and pulls against the OBSTACLES between the two places — the cost of the journey, distance, borders and permits, and the wrench of leaving family. Migration happens when the perceived gain outweighs the perceived cost. Second, the word perceived is doing real work: people move on the picture of the destination in their heads, formed by relatives' stories and social media, and that picture is often brighter than the reality of a crowded job market and an informal settlement. The gap between perceived and actual pull explains why migration continues into cities that already have severe unemployment.

## Subtopic: Case Study — Rural Eastern Cape to Gauteng

Now apply the machinery to South Africa's largest internal stream: from the rural Eastern Cape — and similar former-homeland regions in Limpopo and KwaZulu-Natal — to Gauteng and the other metros.

The roots are historical, and a case-study answer must say so. Under apartheid, the homeland system confined the majority population to a small share of the land, in areas such as the former Transkei and Ciskei, with poor soils, minimal industry and few services. The migrant labour system then made movement itself the economy: men left on contracts to the gold mines of the Witwatersrand, lived in single-sex hostels, and sent money home — while pass laws blocked their families from following. When those controls ended in the 1990s, the dammed-up movement was released, and families could finally move together.

The push factors read like the homeland inheritance: rural unemployment far above the national average, tiny overworked plots that cannot support households, thin services, and few prospects for the young. The pull of Gauteng is the mirror image: the country's largest concentration of jobs, better schools and hospitals, and — decisively — relatives and home-community networks already established there, lowering every obstacle for the next arrival.

The consequences must be argued for BOTH places, positive and negative — that is the essay structure examiners reward. For the Eastern Cape origin: remittances flow home and keep households alive, and returning migrants bring skills; but the region loses its working-age adults — a brain drain and muscle drain — leaving villages of children and grandparents, with grandmothers raising grandchildren on social grants, fields going out of cultivation, and an age structure with a hole in the middle. For the Gauteng destination: a young workforce feeds the economy and migrants' spending and skills enlarge it; but arrivals outpace housing and services, so informal settlements spread, schools and clinics overcrowd, and competition for scarce jobs breeds tension. The stream is circular still: many migrants keep a rural home, send money monthly, return in December, and intend — one day — to retire back home. Money orders and December taxis are as much a part of this migration as the moving itself.

## Subtopic: Case Study — Crossing Borders into South Africa

South Africa is the great magnet of its region — the destination of the largest international migration streams in southern Africa. The biggest single stream flows from ZIMBABWE.

The causes, stated plainly. From around 2000, Zimbabwe's economy collapsed: hyperinflation at one point made the currency worthless, unemployment became the norm, and public services crumbled — a compound push of economic desperation and political fear. South Africa pulled with the region's strongest economy, a long shared border, existing family and community networks, and wages that, even at the bottom of the market, could support relatives at home. Estimates of Zimbabweans in South Africa run over a million; many entered as asylum seekers, many on work permits, many without documents at all.

The stream is mixed, and the classification vocabulary earns marks here: it contains voluntary economic migrants, refugees and asylum seekers with genuine claims of persecution, and everything between — geographers call such flows mixed migration. It joins older streams: Mozambican and Basotho mineworkers recruited for generations under formal contract systems, and refugees from further afield — the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Somalia, Ethiopia — who often become traders and shopkeepers in South African townships.

Consequences, again for both sides. For the origin countries: remittances are a lifeline — money sent home from South Africa keeps families and even national economies afloat — but the loss of teachers, nurses and skilled workers is a brain drain that slows recovery. For South Africa: migrants supply labour in agriculture, construction, domestic work and security; they found businesses and fill scarce skills, including in hospitals; but the arrival of many job-seekers into an economy with high unemployment concentrates competition at the bottom of the labour market, and pressure on housing and services grows in the poorest areas — precisely where tensions ignite. South Africa has seen repeated waves of XENOPHOBIC violence against foreign nationals, notably in 2008 and 2015, and a case-study answer should name the phenomenon and its cost honestly: lives lost, shops burned, and the country's standing in the region damaged.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the Zimbabwean stream's causes, the mixed-migration idea, and the honest balance of consequences.

## Subtopic: Every Move Answers Two Questions

Stand at a big taxi rank early on a Monday morning and watch. Some passengers ride twenty minutes to work and will be back by dark — that is not migration, just circulation, the daily loop. But some carry everything they own, headed for a city they have never seen, not planning to return. Residence is about to change. THAT is migration.

Every migrant's story is sorted by asking just two questions. Question one: did they cross a BORDER? Move inside the country — Eastern Cape to Johannesburg — and it is internal migration. Cross into another country and it is international, and the same person collects two labels at once: an emigrant in the country they left, an immigrant in the country they entered. Same journey, two points of view.

Question two: did they CHOOSE? Moving for a job, for university, for love — voluntary. Running from war, persecution or disaster — forced. And the forced category has its own words worth owning: a refugee has crossed a border fleeing danger; an asylum seeker is waiting for that status to be recognised; an internally displaced person is fleeing the same danger without leaving their country.

Add one more South African word to the kit: oscillating. For a century, mineworkers from the Eastern Cape, Lesotho and Mozambique lived a two-place life — eleven months in a hostel on the mines, December at home — year after year. Not quite gone, never fully home. That circular pattern built much of this country's economy and shaped millions of its families, and it still runs today in softer form: the security guard in Sandton whose family, and heart, and December, are in Lusikisiki.

## Subtopic: The Tug-of-War Decision

Why does anyone leave home? Picture a rope with the migrant standing in the middle. One team pulls from home — except this team pushes: no jobs, failing farm, weak school, drought. The other team pulls from the city or the new country: work, wages, university, safety, family already there. When the away team wins, a person moves. Geographers call the two teams push factors and pull factors, and a full answer always names players from BOTH teams.

The players come in four kinds, on both ends of the rope. Money reasons — no work at home against jobs in the city — and money wins most tug-of-wars on earth. People reasons — schools, clinics, and especially family who went ahead: every cousin already in Johannesburg makes the rope pull harder, because there is a couch to sleep on and someone who knows where the jobs are. That is why migration streams grow — each mover recruits the next. Safety reasons — war and persecution push harder than anything; peace pulls. And land-and-weather reasons — drought and exhausted soil push; rain and good ground pull.

But the rope has knots in it — obstacles between the two ends. The taxi fare and the bus ticket. The distance. The border and its permits, for international moves. The ache of leaving a mother, a child, a grave. People move only when the pull, minus the knots, still beats the push.

And one last honest truth: the migrant is pulling toward a PICTURE of the destination, painted by a cousin's photos and stories — and the picture is usually sunnier than the place. The real Johannesburg has unemployment and rented shacks alongside its opportunities. That gap between the imagined city and the real one explains something otherwise puzzling: why people keep arriving in cities that already have too few jobs. They are answering the picture, not the statistics.

Questions on the tug-of-war are up now: the two teams, the four kinds of players, the knots, and the too-sunny picture.

## Subtopic: The Letter Home

Here is the object that explains migration's consequences better than any diagram: the money sent home at month end. Once a letter with notes folded inside; now a banking app or a supermarket money transfer. Millions of them, every month, flowing from Gauteng to the Eastern Cape, from South Africa to Zimbabwe, Lesotho and Mozambique. Follow that money and you can answer every consequences question this topic can ask.

Start where the money lands: the village left behind. The remittance is groceries, school shoes, a grandmother's electricity — for many rural households, the main income. That is the origin area's biggest gain, along with the skills migrants bring back when they return. Now look around the village at what is missing: the working-age adults. The clinic short of nurses, the fields nobody ploughs, classrooms of children raised by grandparents. The origin gains money and loses its strongest people — both are true at once, and an exam answer must say both.

Now follow the money backwards, to the sender's city. The destination gained a worker — young, determined, often skilled: the nurse from Harare, the builder from Lusikisiki, the shopkeeper from Mogadishu. Cities are built by exactly such people, and Gauteng's economy runs on them. But the destination also strains: more arrivals than houses, so informal settlements grow; more learners than desks; more job-seekers than jobs, and the competition lands hardest in the poorest streets — where frustration has, in 2008 and 2015, exploded into xenophobic violence against foreign nationals, at a cost in lives and burned livelihoods this country is still accounting for.

So the balanced picture, both ends, gains and losses — that is the whole answer pattern: origin gains remittances and skills, loses workers and whole age groups; destination gains labour and energy, strains housing, services and social peace. Four boxes. Fill all four, attach the Eastern Cape or Zimbabwe as your example, and there is no consequences question in this topic you cannot answer.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — what the money means at each end, what the village loses, what the city gains and strains, and the four boxes.
