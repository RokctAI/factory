If migration feels like a dictionary attack — emigrant, immigrant, refugee, oscillating, internally displaced — slow down: this whole topic is two questions, one rope, and four boxes. Nothing more.

The two questions sort every single term. Did the person cross a border? Inside the country: internal. Across a border: international — and that one traveller is an emigrant to the country left and an immigrant to the country entered. Did the person choose? Yes: voluntary. No — war, persecution, disaster: forced, which is where refugee, asylum seeker and internally displaced person live. Any classification question in the paper is those two questions in disguise.

The rope is push and pull. Home pushes: no jobs, weak services, drought, danger. The destination pulls: work, schools, safety, family already there. Give BOTH sides, in pairs, and pick from the four families — money, people, safety, land. Add the knots in the rope — fares, distance, permits, leaving family — if the question asks why some people stay.

The four boxes win every consequences essay: origin gains, origin loses, destination gains, destination strains. Remittances home; working-age adults gone. Workers and businesses arriving; housing and services straining. Fill all four, always.

And your case studies are ready-made: Eastern Cape to Gauteng for internal — homelands, mine contracts, December taxis, grandmothers and grants. Zimbabwe to South Africa for international — economic collapse pushing, the region's biggest economy pulling, remittances flowing back, and the honest mention of the 2008 and 2015 xenophobic violence.

Two questions, one rope, four boxes, two case studies. Count them off and write. You've got this.
