# Part 1 — Expert

A country's population can be summed up in a handful of numbers and one picture. The numbers are the population indicators — birth rate, death rate, fertility, infant mortality and life expectancy — and the picture is the population pyramid, a graph that shows a whole nation's age and sex structure at a glance. This session defines each indicator precisely, explains what pushes it up or down, then teaches the pyramid: how it is built, how to read it, and what the three classic shapes reveal about a country's past and future. South Africa's own numbers and its own pyramid run through the whole lesson.

## Subtopic: The Vital Statistics — Birth, Death and Natural Increase

Population indicators are standardised measurements that let you compare countries of wildly different sizes, and the standardisation is the point. Nigeria has far more births than Lesotho simply because it has far more people; to compare fairly, geographers convert everything to a rate per thousand people per year.

The CRUDE BIRTH RATE is the number of live births per thousand people in a population in one year. South Africa's is currently around 19 per thousand. Niger, one of the highest in the world, is above 40 per thousand. Japan is below 7 per thousand. The word crude simply means the rate is calculated for the whole population, old men and schoolgirls alike, without any adjustment for age structure.

The CRUDE DEATH RATE is the number of deaths per thousand people per year. South Africa's sits near 9 per thousand. Read death rates carefully, because they hide a trap: some wealthy countries with excellent healthcare have HIGHER death rates than poorer ones. Germany's death rate is above 11 per thousand while Kenya's is below 8. The reason is age structure — Germany has a large elderly population, and elderly people die at a higher rate no matter how good the hospitals are. A low death rate can simply mean a young population.

Subtract one from the other and you get the RATE OF NATURAL INCREASE: birth rate minus death rate. South Africa's is roughly 19 minus 9, which is 10 per thousand — and dividing by ten converts it to a percentage, so the population grows by about 1 per cent a year from births and deaths alone. The word natural matters: this measure deliberately excludes migration. A country can have positive natural increase and still shrink if enough people emigrate.

What pushes these rates around? Birth rates rise where families depend on children for farm labour and old-age support, where infant mortality is high so parents have more children as insurance, where women marry young and have limited access to education and contraception. Birth rates fall as education — especially of women — improves, as children survive, as families urbanise and children become expensive rather than productive. Death rates fall with clean water, vaccination, food security and healthcare, and rise with epidemics, famine and war.

## Subtopic: Fertility, Infant Mortality and Life Expectancy

Three more indicators complete the toolkit, and each tells you something the crude rates cannot.

The TOTAL FERTILITY RATE is the average number of children a woman will bear in her lifetime if current patterns hold. It is the single best predictor of future growth. The magic number is 2,1 — REPLACEMENT LEVEL — the average needed for a generation to replace itself, with the extra decimal covering children who do not survive to adulthood. Above 2,1 a population will eventually grow; below it, a population will eventually shrink. Niger's fertility rate is above 6. Japan's and Italy's are near 1,3, which is why both countries are already losing people. South Africa's has fallen dramatically, from about 6 children per woman in the 1960s to around 2,3 today — just above replacement, and still falling.

The INFANT MORTALITY RATE is the number of babies per thousand live births who die before their first birthday. It is treated as the most sensitive single measure of development, because keeping a baby alive requires nearly everything to work: clean water, nutrition, vaccination, clinics, educated mothers. Sierra Leone's infant mortality is above 70 per thousand; Japan and Singapore are below 2. South Africa has cut its rate to roughly 25 per thousand, a major improvement on the 1990s, though still many times the level of the wealthiest countries.

LIFE EXPECTANCY is the average number of years a newborn can expect to live under current conditions. Japan is above 84 years. South Africa is around 65 — and its recent history is a lesson in how fast the number can move. In the early 2000s, at the height of the HIV and Aids epidemic before antiretroviral treatment was widely available, South African life expectancy fell to around 53 years. The mass rollout of treatment — now the largest such programme in the world — raised it by more than a decade. Life expectancy is an average dragged heavily by infant deaths: where many babies die, the average falls sharply even if adults live long lives.

One caution for exam answers: never call a country developed or developing off one indicator alone. Use two or three together — a high fertility rate plus high infant mortality plus low life expectancy is a consistent picture; one number by itself can mislead.

## Subtopic: Reading a Population Pyramid

A population pyramid packs a country's entire age and sex structure into one graph, and you must be able to describe it in words, because that is how exams ask about it.

Here is the construction, stated verbally. Imagine a vertical line running up the middle of the page. On each level of that line sits a pair of horizontal bars, back to back: the bar stretching to the LEFT shows males, the bar stretching to the RIGHT shows females. Each level is an AGE COHORT of five years: the bottom pair of bars is ages nought to four, the pair above it five to nine, then ten to fourteen, and so on, level by level, up to the very old at the top. The LENGTH of each bar shows how many people — or what percentage of the population — falls in that age and sex group. So the youngest people form the base of the graph, the oldest form the peak, and the profile that results is read like a shape.

Three reading habits unlock any pyramid. First, look at the BASE. A very wide base means the recent birth rate is high — the country is producing many children. A base NARROWER than the bars above it means births are declining. Second, look at the SLOPE of the sides. Sides that taper in sharply from one cohort to the next mean many people die between age groups — high mortality. Sides that rise almost vertically mean most people survive into old age. Third, look for IRREGULARITIES: a notch, meaning one cohort is unusually small, points to a past war, famine or epidemic that killed people or suppressed births; a bulge points to a baby boom or to immigration of people in that age band; a longer bar on the female side at the top reflects women outliving men, which is near-universal.

The pyramid also gives you the DEPENDENCY RATIO. The population divides into the YOUNG DEPENDENTS, aged nought to fourteen, the ECONOMICALLY ACTIVE, fifteen to sixty-four, and the AGED DEPENDENTS, sixty-five and over. Add the two dependent groups, divide by the economically active group, and multiply by a hundred: the result says how many dependants every hundred workers must support. A wide-based pyramid carries a heavy YOUTH dependency load, which strains schools and clinics; a top-heavy pyramid carries a heavy AGED dependency load, which strains pensions and hospitals.

## Subtopic: Three Pyramid Shapes and South Africa's Own

Examiners work with three classic shapes, and each is a stage in a country's story.

The EXPANSIVE pyramid has a very wide base and sides that taper quickly to a narrow peak — a true triangle. Verbally: each cohort is noticeably smaller than the one below it. This is the shape of high birth rates and high or recently high death rates: many children, few elderly, rapid growth, low life expectancy. It is typical of the least developed economies — Niger, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Mozambique. Its future is built in: the huge child cohorts will soon reach child-bearing age themselves, so growth continues even if fertility starts falling. That momentum is why populations keep growing for decades after birth rates decline.

The STATIONARY pyramid has almost vertical sides: the bars from childhood through middle age are roughly equal in length, narrowing only near the top as old age takes its toll. Births roughly equal deaths, growth is near zero, life expectancy is high. The United States and Sweden approximate this shape.

The CONSTRICTIVE pyramid is the surprising one: the BASE is narrower than the bars above it, so the graph is pinched at the bottom, bulging in the middle, heavy near the top. Verbally: there are fewer young children than young adults. Birth rates have fallen below replacement, the population is ageing and will shrink. Japan, Germany and Italy are the standard examples, and their problem is the inverted dependency load — ever fewer workers supporting ever more pensioners.

Now South Africa. Our pyramid is TRANSITIONAL, between expansive and stationary. The base is still fairly wide — birth rates remain above replacement — but it has stopped widening and the lowest cohorts are no longer much bigger than those above them, because fertility has fallen to about 2,3. There is a strong bulge through the twenties and thirties. The sides still taper faster than a wealthy country's, reflecting mortality including the long shadow of the Aids epidemic, which cut deeply into the adult cohorts now in middle age. Females outnumber males in the upper bars. The overall message: South Africa is moving from a young, fast-growing population toward a slower-growing, ageing one — and it currently enjoys a large working-age share relative to dependants, a window sometimes called a demographic dividend, which pays off only if those workers find work.

## Subtopic: A Country's Report Card

Think of the indicators as a report card for a country, with five subjects on it.

Births per thousand people per year — that is the birth rate. Deaths per thousand per year — the death rate. Subtract deaths from births and you get natural increase, which is just the speed the population grows on its own, before anyone packs a suitcase. South Africa: about 19 births, about 9 deaths, so about 10 per thousand — one per cent a year.

Then the three that go deeper. Fertility rate: how many children the average woman has in her lifetime. Remember one number for the rest of your life: 2,1. Two children replace their two parents, and the small extra covers children who do not make it to adulthood. Above 2,1, the country grows. Below it, the country shrinks — no matter how rich it is. Japan sits near 1,3, and Japan is losing people every year.

Infant mortality: of every thousand babies born alive, how many die before their first birthday. This is the harshest and most honest subject on the report card, because a baby's survival tests everything at once — the water, the food, the clinic, the mother's education. If a country scores well here, most other things are working.

Life expectancy: how long a newborn can expect to live. And here is the trap question teachers love: a low life expectancy does NOT mean nobody grows old. It is an average, and every baby who dies drags that average down hard. A country where half the people die as infants and half live to 80 has a life expectancy around 40 — yet plenty of grandparents.

One warning before the questions: never judge a country on one number. Germany has a higher death rate than Kenya — not because German hospitals are worse, but because Germany is full of old people and Kenya is full of young ones. Always ask WHO is in the population before you trust the rate.

## Subtopic: The Pyramid as a Family Photograph

Picture a huge family photograph of the entire country — but instead of standing in rows by height, everyone stands in rows by AGE. All the babies and toddlers in the front row. Behind them, the five-to-nines. Behind them, the ten-to-fourteens. Row after row, all the way back to the great-grandparents in the last row. Men stand on the left half of each row, women on the right.

Now fly a drone straight overhead and look down. The SHAPE of that crowd is the population pyramid. A long front row and short back rows: a country full of children. Rows all roughly the same length: a country where people are spread evenly across the ages. A SHORT front row with longer rows behind it: a country that has almost stopped having babies.

That is the entire trick of the graph. Each pair of bars is one row of the photograph — one five-year age group — with males drawn to the left of the centre line and females to the right, youngest at the bottom, oldest at the top. Longer bar, more people of that age and sex. Nothing more mysterious than that.

And the photograph holds history. See a row that is strangely SHORT compared with its neighbours — a bite taken out of the crowd? Something happened to that generation: a war, an epidemic, a famine in the years they were born or young. See a row that bulges longer than its neighbours? A baby boom, or a wave of immigrants who arrived at that age. See the women's side longer than the men's in the oldest rows? Women outlive men almost everywhere on earth.

Last piece: split the photograph into three blocks. Front block, under fifteen — too young to work. Back block, sixty-five and over — retired. The big middle block does the working and supports both ends. Geographers call the front and back blocks the dependants, and the question that decides budgets is simple: how many dependants must every hundred workers carry?

Questions on the photograph are up now: who stands where, what a short row means, and the three blocks.

## Subtopic: Wide Base, Narrow Base — What the Shape Tells You

Three shapes to recognise on sight, described as stacks of bricks.

Shape one: a proper triangle — a very wide bottom layer, every layer above it smaller, ending in a point. That is the EXPANSIVE shape. Translation: babies everywhere, few old people, the population growing fast. This is Niger, the DRC, Mozambique. And notice what is loaded inside it: today's enormous rows of children become tomorrow's enormous rows of parents, so the growth keeps rolling for decades even if families start shrinking today. Geographers call that momentum.

Shape two: a tower — layers of almost equal width from bottom to middle, narrowing only near the top. That is the STATIONARY shape. Births roughly equal deaths, the population coasting at a steady size, most people living long lives. Think Sweden or the United States.

Shape three is the strange one: a stack that is NARROWER at the bottom than in the middle — pinched at the base, fat in the middle, heavy at the top. That is the CONSTRICTIVE shape, and it should stop you in your tracks, because it means the country has fewer small children than young adults. Families have shrunk below the replacement level. Japan, Germany, Italy. Fast forward that stack thirty years: the fat middle becomes the heavy top, the pinched base becomes a thin middle, and you have many pensioners resting on few workers. That is why those governments beg their citizens to have children.

Where is South Africa? Between shape one and shape two, and moving. Our base is still wide-ish — we are above replacement — but it has stopped growing wider, because fertility has fallen from about six children per woman in our grandparents' generation to about 2,3 now. We have a big bulge of people in their twenties and thirties, and a taper above that which still carries the scar of the Aids years in the adult rows. For the moment, South Africa has an unusually large share of people of working age compared to children and pensioners — a once-off opportunity, but only if the economy gives that bulge jobs.
