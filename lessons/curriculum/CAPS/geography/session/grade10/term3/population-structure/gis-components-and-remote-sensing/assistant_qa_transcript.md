**Thandi:** A few questions that come up a lot with this topic. First: is the map application on a phone REALLY a GIS, or is that just a friendly comparison?

**Tutor:** It genuinely is one, and checking it against the formal definition is a useful exercise rather than a loose analogy. The definition demands a computerised system that captures, stores, analyses and displays georeferenced information, and the application does all four: every feature in its database carries coordinates, the data sits on servers, the routing engine performs real spatial analysis when it computes the shortest path through the road network, and the screen renders the result as a map. All five components are present too — the phone and servers are hardware, the application is software, the road and business database is the data, you are the person, and the company's update rules are the procedures. What separates it from a professional planning GIS is not kind but degree of control: a municipal planner using a package such as QGIS or ArcGIS can load their own layers, run their own overlay queries and interrogate attribute tables directly, while the phone application only answers the questions its designers anticipated. So in an exam, define a GIS formally, but know that the strongest way to show understanding is to demonstrate the definition living inside an everyday example.

***

**Thandi:** Next: why do experts insist DATA is the most important component when the software seems to do all the clever work?

**Tutor:** Because the software's cleverness is entirely conditional on what it is fed, and the data is the only component that touches reality. Hardware and software are commodities — replaceable, purchasable, improving every year. The data is the model of the actual world: every road, clinic, contour and household that the analysis will treat as true. If the clinic layer is five years old, the overlay analysis will run flawlessly and recommend building a clinic where one already stands; the error is invisible precisely because the output looks authoritative. Data is also the expensive component in a very practical sense — collecting it means surveys, GPS capture in the field, digitising paper records, buying imagery, and above all keeping everything current, which never ends. South Africa's census is a good scale reference: mobilising enumerators to visit every dwelling in the country is a massive undertaking, and its product is, in GIS terms, one enormous attribute dataset. That is also why procedures earn their place among the five components: they are the discipline — update cycles, accuracy checks, change control — that stops the most valuable component from quietly rotting.

***

**Thandi:** This one comes up every time satellites are mentioned: if a satellite can photograph every house in the country, why do we still pay thousands of people to walk around doing a census?

**Tutor:** Because the satellite and the enumerator answer different halves of the population question, and the half the satellite cannot touch is the half a government needs most. Remote sensing is superb at the WHERE: it sees every roof, including shacks that appeared last month on a city's edge, it revisits on a fixed cycle so growth becomes measurable, and it reaches places field teams cannot. But a sensor measures reflected energy, so it registers structures, not lives. It cannot determine how many people sleep under a roof, their ages, sexes, languages, incomes, or whether the dwelling is a home, a spaza shop or a storeroom — and every one of those attributes drives real allocations of school places, clinic staff and municipal budgets. So the two systems are deliberately combined: before the count, Statistics South Africa uses aerial and satellite imagery to carve the country into enumeration areas and confirm that every visible dwelling falls inside one, which protects the census from missing exactly the fast-growing informal areas that most need counting. Afterwards, the answers collected at the door become the attribute data attached to those mapped areas in the GIS. The imagery guarantees completeness; the knock on the door supplies the humanity.

**Thandi:** Last one: satellite images and aerial photographs blur together in people's minds. What is the cleanest way to keep them apart in an answer?

**Tutor:** Anchor them to three axes: altitude, area and detail — and then attach one South African use to each so the answer cannot collapse into vagueness. The satellite operates from orbit, hundreds of kilometres up, so a single image covers an enormous area at coarser detail, and because the platform is in orbit it returns over the same ground on a fixed cycle for years, which is what makes change detection possible — Landsat has been building that record since 1972. Its natural jobs are the big and the repeated: tracking drought across provinces, watching dam levels, measuring the outward creep of a city's informal edge. The aircraft flies a few kilometres up, so its photograph covers a small patch in fine detail — individual buildings, roads, field boundaries. Corrected into orthophotos, those photographs become the base of South Africa's 1 to 10 000 orthophoto map series used in mapwork, where a single house is visible. A memorable compression: the satellite is the wide-angle time-lapse of the whole country; the aerial photograph is the sharp close-up of one neighbourhood. If the question asks which to choose for a task, reason from the task's area and detail needs, and say so explicitly — that reasoning is what the marks reward.
