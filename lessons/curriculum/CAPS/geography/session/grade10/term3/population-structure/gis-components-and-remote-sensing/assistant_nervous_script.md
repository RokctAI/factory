Hey, it's Thandi — if GIS feels like a computer-science topic that wandered into geography, relax — you already use a GIS every time you search for the nearest chips on your phone, and the exam only wants three things: a definition, a list of five, and one big idea.

The definition, in one breath: a GIS is a computerised system that captures, stores, analyses and displays geographically referenced information. Four verbs and one adjective. Practise saying it until the four verbs come out in order, because markers tick each one.

The list of five: hardware, software, data, people, procedures. Make it physical — the phone, the app, the map information inside it, you, and the company's update rules. If asked which matters most, say data, and give the reason: wrong data makes perfect software give convincing wrong answers.

The big idea is layers. Rivers on one transparent sheet, roads on another, clinics, population. Stack them and questions answer themselves — dense settlement with no clinic nearby jumps off the page. That stacking has a name, overlay analysis, and writing the name earns the mark.

Remote sensing is even friendlier: information about the earth's surface gathered from a distance, no touching — sensors on satellites and aircraft. Satellite: huge area, less detail, comes back again and again, perfect for watching change. Aerial photograph: small area, fine detail, the source of our 1 to 10 000 orthophoto maps. And the limit examiners love: a satellite counts roofs, not people — that is why the census still knocks on doors.

One definition, five components, one big idea, two platforms, one limit. Count them on your fingers on the way into the exam. You've got this.
