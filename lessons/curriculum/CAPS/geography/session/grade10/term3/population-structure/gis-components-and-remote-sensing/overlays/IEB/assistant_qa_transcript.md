**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First up: the definition of a GIS is a mouthful — captures, stores, analyses, manages, displays. Is there a way to remember it that will not fall apart under pressure?

**Tutor:** Anchor it to a story instead of a string of verbs, because stories survive pressure and strings do not. Use the pharmacy search from the lesson: you needed medicine on a Sunday and your phone found an open pharmacy and routed you there. Now narrate the verbs through that story. The pharmacy's position and details were CAPTURED once by surveyors and data teams; they are STORED on the provider's servers; when you searched, the system ANALYSED — filtered for open now, ranked by distance, computed a route; the result was DISPLAYED as the map on your screen; and behind it all, the database is MANAGED so yesterday's closures and new streets keep flowing in. Then finish with the phrase that makes it geography rather than computing: all of that information is GEOREFERENCED — every record carries its position on the earth. If an answer requires the formal definition, write the full sentence; if it requires understanding, show the two halves — it is a database, and the database knows where everything is. A list of pharmacies is admin; a list of pharmacies with coordinates is geographical information. That contrast is the definition in working clothes.

**Thandi:** Next: of the five components, everyone remembers hardware and software and then blanks on the other three. Why do people and procedures even count as components of a computer system?

**Tutor:** Because a GIS is not a computer system — it is a decision system with computers inside it, and the last three components are where decisions succeed or fail. Take data first, since it outranks everything: the most powerful workstation running the finest software, fed a school layer that is six years old, will confidently recommend building a school where one already stands. The machine did nothing wrong; the fuel was stale. That is why professionals call data the most valuable and most expensive component — collecting and verifying every road, boundary and dwelling costs more than all the equipment. Now people: someone must decide which layers even exist, spot that a cluster of new roofs is an informal settlement and not a farm shed complex, and translate an overlay result into a budget line for standpipes. The system computes; only humans conclude. And procedures are what keep the first four honest over the years — the update schedule that retires the stale school layer, the accuracy checks on newly captured data, the record of who changed what. Here is a memory frame that holds all five: hardware and software are the engine, data is the fuel, people steer, procedures are the service manual. Lose the manual and the engine still runs — for a while, in the wrong direction.

**Thandi:** This one is from our mapwork group: satellite images and aerial photographs seem to do the same job. When would you actually choose one over the other?

**Tutor:** Choose by asking two questions: how much ground, and how much detail? The satellite wins on ground. It sweeps entire provinces in a pass, reaches places no survey aircraft is going — open ocean, remote mountains, disaster zones with closed airspace — and above all it comes back, on a fixed schedule, for years. That return visit is the satellite's superpower, because two images of the same place at two dates equal change: the shoreline of Theewaterskloof Dam stepping backwards through the drought, new roof clusters appearing on a metro's fringe between censuses. No ground survey can rival that rhythm. The aircraft wins on detail. Flying low, it resolves individual buildings, fences and footpaths, which is why corrected aerial photographs — orthophotos — are the base of the 1 to 10 000 map series you use in mapwork: you can see single rooftops on them. So: monitoring a province's crops or a dam through time, satellite; mapping every dwelling in one valley precisely, aerial. And for full marks, add the shared limits — optical sensors are blind under cloud, images demand skilled interpretation, and neither platform can tell you a single fact about the people under the roofs it photographs. Both are collectors; the GIS is where their harvest becomes answers.

**Thandi:** Last one: the lesson kept saying the satellite counts roofs, never people. If that is true, why does Statistics South Africa bother with satellites at all — why not just knock on doors?

**Tutor:** Because before you can knock on every door, you must know where every door is — and that is precisely the question imagery answers best. Think of the census as two problems stacked. Problem one is coverage: the country must be divided into small enumeration areas whose boundaries together contain every dwelling in South Africa, including the shack built on a metro's edge three months ago that no municipal record mentions yet. Aerial photographs and satellite images solve that, showing every roof so that each one lands inside somebody's counting route and nobody is invisible to the count. Problem two is content: who lives under each roof, their ages, languages, work, schooling. Only the enumerator at the door can collect that, which is why the fieldwork remains irreplaceable. So the technologies are sequenced, not competing: imagery guarantees no roof is missed, fieldwork fills each roof with facts, and afterwards the GIS binds them — census counts become the attribute data behind density maps drawn on the very enumeration boundaries the imagery helped define. That partnership is the exam point worth memorising: remote sensing sees everywhere, the census asks everything, and the GIS is where seeing and asking finally meet on one map.
