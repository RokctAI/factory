**Thandi:** A few questions that come up a lot on this topic. First: if rich countries have the best hospitals, why can their death rates be higher than poor countries' rates?

**Tutor:** Because the crude death rate is calculated across the whole population without adjusting for age, and age structure dominates it. Death is concentrated among the elderly, so a country whose pyramid is heavy at the top will record many deaths per thousand people even with superb healthcare — Germany, above eleven per thousand, is the standard example. A country whose pyramid is nearly all young people records few deaths per thousand even where clinics are scarce, which is why Kenya sits below eight. The word crude in the name is the warning label: the rate is honest about how many people died, but silent about why. That is exactly why geographers keep several indicators in play at once. Infant mortality and life expectancy are much harder to fool, because they are anchored to specific ages: no age structure can disguise babies dying before their first birthday, and life expectancy already builds the whole mortality pattern into one number. So when a question asks you to compare development levels, reach for infant mortality and life expectancy first, and treat the crude death rate as a number that needs its age structure read alongside it.

**Thandi:** Next one: why does the fertility number 2,1 matter so much more than the birth rate for predicting the future?

**Tutor:** The birth rate tells you what is happening this year; the fertility rate tells you what each generation is doing to the next, and that is where the future lives. A birth rate can look healthy simply because a country has a bulge of young adults at child-bearing age — many parents produce many births even if each family is small. The total fertility rate strips that illusion away by asking how many children the average woman bears in her lifetime. Set it against replacement level, 2,1, and the long-term arithmetic becomes mechanical: above 2,1, each generation is larger than the last and the population must eventually grow; below it, each generation is smaller and the population must eventually shrink, whatever this year's birth count says. The delay between the two is population momentum. South Africa is a perfect worked example: fertility has fallen from about six in the 1960s to about 2,3 today, yet the population still grows by around one per cent a year, because the large cohorts born in the high-fertility decades are now the parents. The growth we see today was loaded into the pyramid twenty and thirty years ago.

***

**Thandi:** This one confuses people every year: how can a pyramid's base be NARROWER than the bars above it? Surely there are always more babies than adults.

**Tutor:** Only while fertility stays high — and in a growing list of countries it no longer does. The base of the pyramid is simply the children born in the last five years. If families shrink below about two children each, then the newest cohort comes out smaller than the cohort of its own parents, and the graph pinches at the bottom. That is the constrictive shape, and Japan is the cleanest case: fertility near 1,3 means each generation is roughly a third smaller than the one before, so the bars for small children are visibly shorter than the bars for people in their forties. Nothing morbid is happening to the babies — they simply were never born. The examination skill is to read the pinch as a prediction. Slide the whole stack upward in your imagination: in thirty years the bulging middle bars become the elderly bars, the pinched base becomes the working-age bars, and the dependency arithmetic inverts — many pensioners supported by few workers, with pressure on pensions, hospitals and the retirement age. That is why governments in constrictive countries pay child grants and parental leave to coax the base wider, and why some rely on immigration to refill the working-age bars directly.

**Thandi:** Last one: for South Africa's pyramid, what are the specific features worth naming in an exam answer?

**Tutor:** Name five, in order from the base upward, and attach a reason to each. First, a base that is still fairly wide but no longer widening: fertility, at about 2,3, sits just above replacement, so young cohorts are large but no longer growing from one cohort to the next. Second, a pronounced bulge through the twenties and thirties — the cohorts born in the higher-fertility 1990s and early 2000s, now of working age. Third, a taper above the bulge that is steeper than a wealthy country's, carrying the mark of the Aids epidemic that raised adult mortality in the 2000s before antiretroviral treatment was rolled out at scale. Fourth, longer female bars in the upper cohorts, because women outlive men. Fifth, the classification itself: transitional, between expansive and stationary, moving toward stationary as fertility keeps easing. Then close with the interpretation examiners reward: the current structure gives South Africa a large economically active share relative to dependants — a demographic dividend — but the dividend is only potential. It pays out if the bulge generation finds employment, and it expires as that bulge ages upward and the country's aged dependency begins to climb.
