**Thandi:** A few questions that come up a lot on this topic. First: if the growth rate has been falling since the 1960s, why does the population keep going UP?

**Tutor:** Because a falling growth rate is still a positive growth rate — the world is climbing more slowly, not descending. Picture a car: the growth rate is the speed, and the population is the distance travelled. Easing off the accelerator from two per cent to under one per cent means the car covers ground more slowly, but it is still moving forward, so the odometer keeps rising. On top of that sits population momentum, which is the deeper answer examiners want. The high-fertility decades of the twentieth century created enormous cohorts of children, and those cohorts are now adults of child-bearing age. Even with small families averaging near two children each, an enormous number of parents produces an enormous number of births, so the total keeps climbing for decades after fertility falls. That is why the projections show the curve flattening only late this century, somewhere above ten billion — the J-curve bending into an S-curve. The two statements the exam wants you to hold together: the growth RATE peaked around 1968 and has fallen ever since, and the population TOTAL has risen throughout and will rise for decades more. Both are true, and confusing them is the classic error.

**Thandi:** Next: why divide 70 by the growth rate for doubling time? Where does the 70 actually come from?

**Tutor:** The 70 is a mathematical shortcut baked into how compounding works — the same arithmetic as compound interest at the bank. When something grows by a fixed percentage each year, each year's increase is calculated on a total that already includes all previous increases, so the growth accelerates in absolute terms even at a constant rate. Working through the exact mathematics of repeated multiplication, the time any quantity needs to double comes out very close to 70 divided by its percentage growth rate, and the approximation is accurate for the small rates populations actually have. For this course, you use it rather than derive it, and the value lies in what it exposes. Rates of one, two and three per cent sound like neighbours; the rule shows they are different worlds — doubling in 70 years, 35 years and 23 years respectively. A country at three per cent must duplicate every school, clinic, house and water scheme within about 23 years just to keep the same standard per person, before any improvement. And run it on South Africa: at roughly one per cent, doubling would take about 70 years — but since our fertility is still falling toward replacement, the honest reading is that the doubling may never arrive at all. Always pair the calculation with that kind of interpretation; the number alone is half the marks.

**Thandi:** This one causes arguments: how can a nearly empty desert be overpopulated while the packed Netherlands is not? It sounds backwards.

**Tutor:** It sounds backwards only while you are measuring the wrong thing — people per square kilometre instead of people per unit of what the land can actually provide. Overpopulation is defined against carrying capacity: the number of people an area's resources and technology can support at a decent standard of living. The Netherlands packs more than 500 people onto every square kilometre, but each square kilometre is backed by fertile soils, abundant water, advanced technology and a wealthy trading economy — a very large boat, in the lifeboat picture, so the crowd rides comfortably. A stretch of the Sahel carries perhaps 20 people per square kilometre, but each square kilometre offers erratic rain and thin soil that regenerates almost nothing — a tiny boat, overloaded even by that sparse population, which is why the symptoms appear: overgrazing, stripped fuelwood, erosion, hunger in drought years, and young people leaving. Two further points lift an answer into the top range. First, carrying capacity moves: irrigation and improved seed enlarge the boat, while erosion and exhausted groundwater shrink it, so a region can slip into overpopulation with no growth at all, purely because a drought cut the resource base. Second, the symptom list is the evidence: never assert overpopulation from a density figure — argue it from strain on land, jobs and services.

**Thandi:** Last one: China cut its birth rate with force and Kerala cut its birth rate with schools. If both worked, why do we judge them so differently?

**Tutor:** Because the two approaches left completely different countries behind, and demography always sends the bill later. China's one-child policy, enforced with fines and penalties from 1979 to 2015, did accelerate a fertility decline — though much of that decline was already underway before the policy began. The costs are now structural. Cutting one generation of births that sharply guarantees, decades later, a small working generation supporting a huge elderly one — ageing arriving before the country grew rich. Son preference under a one-child limit skewed the sex ratio, leaving millions more men than women. And when the state reversed course and began urging larger families, births barely responded, because forty years of urban life had rebuilt what families want. Kerala and Thailand reached comparable fertility levels in a similar timeframe through girls' education, primary healthcare that cut infant mortality, and voluntary family planning — no coercion, no skewed sex ratio, and a decline rooted in what parents themselves chose, which makes it stable. The exam-ready principle: measures that change the conditions under which families decide — education, child survival, security in old age — outperform measures that override the decision. South Africa is your local proof: six children per woman in the 1960s to about 2,3 today, entirely by the voluntary route.
