**Thandi:** Class questions before we wrap up! First: everyone says the population explosion is over because the growth rate is falling — but the world keeps adding people. Which is it?

**Tutor:** Both statements are true at once, and holding them together is the mark of a real answer. Think of a kettle coming off the boil: the temperature is falling, but the water is still hot. The growth rate is the temperature — it crested near two percent in the late 1960s and has cooled below one percent — while the population total is the water, still enormous and still rising, just more slowly each decade. The deeper mechanism is population momentum. The decades of high fertility produced gigantic generations of children, and those children are now adults having children of their own. Even if every couple in that gigantic generation has only two children, the sheer number of couples produces a flood of births, so the total keeps climbing for decades after family size falls. That is why the projections only flatten late this century, somewhere past ten billion. So when a question shows you a graph of the growth rate falling and asks why the population line still rises, give both layers: a positive rate always means growth, however small, and momentum keeps the base of parents enormous long after fertility drops.

**Thandi:** Next one: the rule of 70 feels like a magic trick. Why seventy, and why should we trust it?

**Tutor:** It is compressed mathematics rather than magic — the same compounding you meet with bank interest. When a quantity grows by a fixed percentage each year, every increase is calculated on a base that already contains all the earlier increases, so absolute growth accelerates even while the rate stands still. If you work through that repeated multiplication properly, the time to double lands very close to seventy divided by the percentage rate, and the approximation is excellent for the low single-digit rates real populations run at. You are welcome to use it as a given; the value is what it reveals. Rates of one, two comma five and three and a half percent sound like small steps apart — the rule translates them into seventy years, twenty-eight years and twenty years to double, which are entirely different national futures. A country doubling in twenty years must duplicate every classroom, clinic, house, pipe and power line in twenty years just to stand still per person. Then always close with interpretation, because the number alone is only half an answer: South Africa at roughly one percent implies seventy years to double, but our fertility is still drifting down, so the honest conclusion is that the doubling will probably never happen. A calculation followed by its meaning — that is the full-marks pattern.

**Thandi:** This one started a debate in class: how can Singapore, with thousands of people per square kilometre, be fine, while a nearly empty piece of Madagascar is overpopulated? It feels upside down.

**Tutor:** It feels upside down because instinct measures the wrong quantity — people per square kilometre instead of people against what the place can provide. Overpopulation is defined against carrying capacity: the population a region's resources and technology can support at a decent standard of living. Singapore's land grows almost nothing, but the island's true resource base is a wealthy trading economy — it imports food and water, exports services and manufactured value, and sustains its dense population in comfort, so its capacity towers above its headcount. The dry south of Madagascar holds the reverse position: rainfall is scarce and erratic, soils are exhausted, and there is little economy beyond subsistence farming, so its capacity is tiny — and in drought years even a sparse population overshoots it, which is when hunger and outmigration appear. Two refinements complete the answer. First, capacity moves: irrigation and better seed raise it, erosion and emptied aquifers lower it, so a district can become overpopulated with zero population growth simply because drought shrank the resource base beneath it. Second, evidence: never declare a region overpopulated from a density figure — argue it from symptoms of strain, shrinking plots, degraded land, jobless growth, oversubscribed services. Density is a clue at best; the strain is the proof.

**Thandi:** Last question: if China's one-child policy and Bangladesh's clinics both cut births, why does the lesson score one as a failure and the other as a success?

**Tutor:** Because the outcomes a generation later are not remotely alike, and demography always presents its bill with a delay. China compressed one generation of births by force, with fines and penalties, from 1979 to 2015 — and much of the fertility fall was underway before the policy even began, driven by urbanisation. The structural costs are now locked in: a small working generation is carrying a vast elderly one, so the country ages before it grows rich; the workforce is shrinking; and son preference under a one-child limit produced millions more boys than girls, a distortion that will echo for decades. Worse, the tool cannot be reversed — the state now pleads for larger families and the birth rate barely twitches, because forty years of city living rebuilt what parents actually want. Bangladesh reached a similar fertility level over similar decades with the opposite toolkit: village health workers delivering family planning door to door, girls kept in school, infant deaths cut by immunisation and clean water. Botswana ran the same voluntary play through clinics and classrooms. Neither carries the skewed sex ratio, and their declines are stable precisely because parents chose them. The principle to write down: policies that change the conditions of the family's decision — education, child survival, security in old age — achieve what coercion achieves, minus the wreckage. South Africa's fall from six children per woman to about two comma three is the local demonstration.
