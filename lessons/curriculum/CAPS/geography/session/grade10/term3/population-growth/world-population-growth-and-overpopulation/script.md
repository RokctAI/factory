# Part 1 — Expert

For almost all of human history the world's population barely grew. Then, in the space of two centuries, it multiplied eight times. This session traces that curve and explains the machinery behind it, sets up the measurements of growth — natural increase and doubling time — then confronts the concept examiners phrase carefully: overpopulation, which is about people measured against resources, not raw numbers. It closes with the strategies that actually manage population growth, separating what works from what fails, with South Africa's own falling growth rate as the running example.

## Subtopic: Ten Thousand Years of Slow, Two Centuries of Fast

Plot world population against time over ten thousand years and the line tells a two-act story.

Act one is astonishingly flat. From the first farming villages until about the year 1800, population crept upward, held down by a brutal balance: birth rates were high, but death rates were nearly as high. Famine, epidemic disease and constant infant death cancelled most births. It took all of human history until about 1804 for the world to hold its first ONE BILLION people.

Act two is the explosion. The second billion took only 123 years, arriving around 1927. The third took 33 years, by 1960. Then the gaps shrank to little more than a decade each: four billion in 1974, five billion in 1987, six billion in 1999, seven billion in 2011, and eight billion in 2022. Described as a shape, the graph is a J-CURVE: a long, almost horizontal handle stretching across the centuries, then a bend, then a nearly vertical climb through the twentieth century.

What bent the curve? Not a rise in births — birth rates barely moved at first. What changed was DEATH. The agricultural revolution grew more food; the industrial revolution moved and stored it; clean water, sewerage, vaccination and, in the twentieth century, antibiotics attacked the great killers. Death rates collapsed, birth rates stayed high for a generation or three, and the gap between them — natural increase — blew the population upward. The fastest growth came after 1950, when medical advances reached Asia, Africa and Latin America within a single generation, far faster than they had spread through Europe.

Now the part exam answers often miss: the growth RATE has already peaked. It topped out around two per cent a year in the late 1960s and has fallen steadily since, to below one per cent today, because birth rates worldwide are falling. Population still climbs — momentum guarantees that — but the curve is bending again, and United Nations projections see it levelling out somewhere above ten billion late this century. The J-curve is becoming an S-curve.

Take this section's questions now: the billion milestones, what bent the curve, and the difference between population still rising and the growth rate falling.

## Subtopic: Measuring Growth — Natural Increase and Doubling Time

Two calculations turn the story into numbers you can use.

The first is NATURAL INCREASE, carried over from population structure work: birth rate minus death rate, both per thousand per year. Divide the answer by ten and you have the growth rate as a percentage. A country with 30 births and 10 deaths per thousand grows at 20 per thousand, which is 2 per cent a year. The word natural still means migration is excluded: total population change is natural increase plus net migration.

Two per cent a year sounds modest. The second calculation shows why it is not. DOUBLING TIME is the number of years a population needs to double at its current growth rate, and the shortcut is the RULE OF 70: divide 70 by the percentage growth rate. At 2 per cent, doubling takes 70 divided by 2 — 35 years. A country growing at 2 per cent must build twice the schools, houses, clinics and water supply within 35 years merely to stand still per person. At 3 per cent, doubling takes about 23 years. At 1 per cent, 70 years. At half a per cent, 140 years. Small differences in the rate compound into utterly different futures — that is the point examiners want you to make.

The reason compounding behaves this way is that growth feeds on itself: each year's increase adds people who themselves produce the next year's increase, the same mathematics as compound interest on savings.

Anchor the numbers with real cases. Niger, with births above 40 per thousand and deaths around 8, grows at roughly 3,7 per cent — doubling in under 20 years, one of the fastest on earth. Japan's deaths now exceed births: natural increase is negative and the population shrinks each year. South Africa sits near 1 per cent — about 19 births minus about 9 deaths per thousand — giving a doubling time around 70 years, and the rate is drifting downward as fertility falls toward replacement.

Pause for this section's questions: the natural increase calculation, the rule of 70 applied, and the three country cases.

## Subtopic: Overpopulation — People Measured Against Resources

OVERPOPULATION is one of the most misused words in geography, and the exam rewards precision. It does NOT mean many people, and it does not mean high density. A region is overpopulated when its population exceeds its CARRYING CAPACITY — the number of people the area's resources and technology can support at a decent standard of living. Overpopulation is a RATIO between people and resources, not a headcount.

Two comparisons make the definition bite. The Netherlands is one of the most densely populated countries on earth, above 500 people per square kilometre, yet it is not overpopulated: fertile land, advanced technology and a strong economy support its people at a high standard of living. Parts of the arid Sahel, with fewer than 20 people per square kilometre, can be overpopulated: rainfall is so scarce and soils so thin that even that sparse population presses beyond what the land can regenerate. Density measures crowding; overpopulation measures strain. A place can be crowded and comfortable, or empty and overstretched.

The signs of overpopulation are the symptoms of that strain, and they make a ready-made paragraph: land pressure — plots subdivided smaller with each generation, farming pushed onto steep or marginal ground; environmental degradation — overgrazing, deforestation for fuelwood, soil erosion, falling water tables; economic symptoms — unemployment and underemployment, because the workforce grows faster than jobs; service symptoms — overcrowded schools and clinics, housing shortages, informal settlements growing faster than services reach them; and, at the extreme, hunger and outmigration, as the young leave because the land cannot hold them.

Carrying capacity itself is not fixed, and saying so earns higher-order marks. Technology raises it: irrigation, fertiliser and improved seed let the same hectare feed several times more people than a century ago. Abuse lowers it: eroded soil and exhausted groundwater feed fewer people permanently. So a region can slide into overpopulation from two directions — the population rising, or the resource base shrinking — and droughts, in particular, can tip a marginal region over the line without a single extra birth.

## Subtopic: Managing Population Growth

If growth strains resources, how does a country slow it? The record of the last half-century gives a clear and sometimes surprising answer: the measures that respect people's choices work better and last longer than the measures that force them.

Start with the strongest lever on the list: the EDUCATION OF GIRLS AND WOMEN. Everywhere on earth, as girls stay in school longer, fertility falls. Educated women marry later, start families later, have wider choices beyond child-bearing, earn incomes that make each child's schooling an investment worth protecting, and use family planning effectively. No single policy has ever cut birth rates as reliably.

Second: REDUCE INFANT MORTALITY. This sounds like a paradox — saving babies to slow growth — but it works, because parents in high-mortality regions have many children partly as insurance against losing some. When clinics, vaccination and clean water make child survival normal, parents choose smaller families within a generation. Falling death rates for children precede falling birth rates.

Third: ACCESS TO FAMILY PLANNING — affordable contraception, honest information, and health systems that deliver both. Fourth: POVERTY REDUCTION AND SOCIAL SECURITY. Where old age is protected by pensions rather than by sons, and where children attend school instead of herding and hauling, the economic logic of the large family dissolves. Birth rates track women's rights, child survival and economic security far more closely than they track slogans.

Now the cautionary case: CHINA'S ONE-CHILD POLICY, enforced from 1979 to 2015 with fines and penalties. It did cut births steeply — but at heavy cost: an ageing population arriving before the country grew rich, a shrinking workforce, and a skewed sex ratio with millions more boys than girls born under son preference. China now urges couples to have more children, and the birth rate barely responds. Contrast Thailand and the Indian state of Kerala, which achieved comparable fertility declines through education, healthcare and voluntary family planning — without coercion and without the demographic scars.

South Africa fits the voluntary pattern: fertility fell from about six children per woman in the 1960s to about 2,3 today through urbanisation, rising education of women, clinic-based family planning and falling infant mortality — no compulsion required. Growth near one per cent and easing is the result.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the four levers, the paradox of saving babies, and China against Kerala.

## Subtopic: The Stadium That Fills in the Last Minute

Here is a picture for the whole of human population history. Imagine filling a giant stadium with everyone who has to be seated — but the gates work strangely.

The stadium is open for 24 hours. For the first 23 hours and about 45 minutes, people trickle in — a family here, a village there. By quarter to midnight, after nearly the whole day, there is about one billion people inside. That is humanity from the first farms to the year 1800: ten thousand years to seat the first billion.

Then, in the last fifteen minutes, the other seven billion arrive. The second billion is seated by 1927. The third by 1960. After that a new billion pushes through the gates roughly every twelve years — 1974, 1987, 1999, 2011, and eight billion by 2022. Almost everyone in the stadium came through the door in the final sliver of the day.

Why did the crowd suddenly surge? Not because people started having more babies — they had always had many babies. Because babies STOPPED DYING. For thousands of years, high births were cancelled by high deaths: famine, disease, dirty water. Then came more food, clean water, sewerage, vaccines, antibiotics — and the deaths collapsed while the births, for a generation or two, carried on as before. Births minus deaths is growth, and that gap swung wide open. The population did not explode because humanity sped up; it exploded because death slowed down.

And the last twist, which most people outside this classroom do not know: the surge is already easing. The growth rate peaked around two per cent in the late 1960s and has fallen to below one per cent, because families everywhere are shrinking. The crowd is still entering — but the queue outside is getting shorter every year, and the stadium is expected to stop filling somewhere above ten billion.

## Subtopic: Too Many for What? The Lifeboat Question

Say the word overpopulated and most people picture a packed street. Wrong picture. The right picture is a lifeboat.

A lifeboat rated for 20 people is fine with 15 aboard and sinking with 30. The number that matters is not 15 or 30 by itself — it is the comparison with 20, the capacity. Overpopulation works the same way: a place is overpopulated when it holds more people than its resources — its water, soil, food, jobs — can support decently. Geographers call the boat's rating the carrying capacity.

That is why the two standard examples seem upside down until the lifeboat clicks. The Netherlands is jam-packed — over 500 people on every square kilometre — yet NOT overpopulated, because its rich land and strong economy are a huge boat that carries the crowd comfortably. A stretch of the Sahel desert may have 20 people per square kilometre — nearly empty — and BE overpopulated, because with almost no rain and paper-thin soil, its boat is rated for fewer than are aboard. Crowded is not the same as overloaded. Density is a headcount; overpopulation is a comparison.

How do you recognise an overloaded boat? It shows strain everywhere at once. The land: family plots split smaller with each generation, cattle grazing hillsides bald, trees stripped for firewood, soil washing away. The economy: too many hands for too few jobs. The services: classrooms with too many learners, clinics with too many patients, settlements growing faster than pipes and power lines can follow. And finally the young simply climb out of the boat — leaving for the cities or for other countries, because the land cannot hold them.

One more move separates the top answers: the boat's rating can CHANGE. Irrigation, fertiliser and better seeds make the boat bigger — the same land carries more people. Erosion, exhausted wells and drought make the boat smaller — and a region can become overpopulated without a single person being added, simply because the resources shrank beneath them.

Questions on the lifeboat are up now: capacity against headcount, the upside-down examples, the signs of strain, and the boat that changes size.

## Subtopic: What Actually Slows Growth Down

Suppose a country decides its population is growing too fast. What actually works? The honest answer from half a century of evidence starts at a surprising place: the school gate.

The single most powerful brake on population growth ever found is keeping girls in school. Follow the chain: a girl who finishes school marries later, so her child-bearing years start later. She has plans of her own alongside motherhood. She can earn, so each child's schooling becomes something worth investing in — and quality replaces quantity. She understands and uses family planning. Multiply that by a generation of girls and the birth rate drops without anyone being ordered to do anything.

The second brake sounds like a mistake until you think it through: save more babies. In places where many infants die, parents have extra children as insurance — expecting to lose some. When vaccines, clean water and clinics make survival normal, the insurance logic dies instead, and families shrink within a generation. Lower infant deaths first, lower births after: that is the sequence, every time.

Third and fourth: make contraception affordable and available, and reduce poverty — especially with pensions. Where old people are supported by a pension, children are no longer a retirement plan, and the strongest old reason for a large family quietly disappears.

Now the tale of two approaches. China forced the issue: one child per couple, enforced with fines from 1979 to 2015. Births did fall — and the bill arrived later: a country ageing before it grew rich, a shrinking workforce, and millions more boys than girls. Today China pleads for babies, and the birth rate barely moves. Thailand and Kerala took the other road — schools, clinics, voluntary family planning — and their birth rates fell just as far, with none of the scars. Choice outperformed force.

And South Africa? We are the quiet success story in the room: from about six children per woman in our grandparents' time to about 2,3 now — done through education, urbanisation, clinics and choice, never compulsion. Growth is around one per cent and easing. The school gate worked here too.
