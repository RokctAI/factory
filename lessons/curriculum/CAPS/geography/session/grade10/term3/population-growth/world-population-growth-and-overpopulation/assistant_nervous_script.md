If this topic feels like a blur of billions and percentages, breathe — population growth is one shape, two calculations, one comparison and one list, and you can carry all of it into the exam in your head.

The shape first: the J-curve. Flat for ten thousand years, then a bend around 1800, then almost straight up. One billion by 1804, eight billion by 2022. And the cause is a single sentence that answers half the questions in this section: death rates fell while birth rates stayed high. Not more babies — fewer funerals. Say that sentence and the explosion is explained.

The two calculations are friendly. Natural increase: births minus deaths, per thousand, and divide by ten for the percentage. Doubling time: 70 divided by the growth rate. Two per cent means 35 years. That is genuinely all the maths this topic contains.

The comparison is the one that trips everyone, so nail it now: overpopulation is NOT high density. The Netherlands is packed and fine; parts of the Sahel are empty and overstretched. The test is people against carrying capacity — the lifeboat and its rating. If a question shows a dense place, do not shout overpopulated until you have checked the resources.

The list is the management strategies, and there is a story to hold them together: educate girls, save babies, provide family planning, reduce poverty. Then one contrast for the discussion marks — China forced it and inherited an ageing, skewed population; Kerala schooled it and inherited stability. And our own example: South Africa fell from six children per woman to about 2,3 by choice, not force.

One shape, two sums, one lifeboat, four levers, one contrast. Walk in with those five things and this topic cannot touch you. You've got this.
