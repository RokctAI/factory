**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: if density is just people divided by area, why do geographers say it can be misleading? A number is a number.

**Tutor:** The number is accurate; what misleads is what people read into it. Three problems sit inside every density figure. The first is averaging. A density is a mean, and a mean flattens extremes: South Africa's national figure of roughly fifty people per square kilometre is the arithmetic result of joining an almost empty Karoo to a densely built Witwatersrand, and it describes neither. The larger the area you calculate over, the less the answer means. The second problem is that arithmetic density counts all land equally — mountain, desert, salt pan and prime farmland alike. Two countries with the same density can be under completely different pressure if one is largely arable and the other largely desert, which is why geographers also calculate density per unit of arable land to show how many people each productive hectare must support. The third is that density says nothing about pattern, so it cannot distinguish a landscape of evenly spread villages from one of empty veld with a single city. Use density to compare, then always describe the distribution alongside it — the number and the picture together, never the number alone.

***

**Thandi:** Next: you said climate is the strongest physical factor, but Gauteng has no special climate and it is the most crowded place in the country. Does that not contradict the whole list?

**Tutor:** It qualifies it rather than contradicting it, and understanding the qualification is exactly what separates a good answer from an average one. Physical factors set the broad canvas; human factors then paint on it, and where the two disagree, human factors usually win in the short run. At the scale of the whole country, climate is clearly in charge: rainfall declines westward, and so does population, which is why the Karoo, Kalahari and Namaqualand are almost empty. But at the scale of a single region, one economic fact can override the physical picture entirely. Gold on the Witwatersrand was a resource that could not be moved, so people had to come to it, and once they arrived the settlement developed its own momentum — workers needed housing, shops and schools, mines needed machinery, factories needed banks, and roads and railways were built to serve all of it. That accumulated investment is why Gauteng remains dominant even though the gold is largely worked out. The general principle worth writing down is that physical factors explain where people CAN live, while human factors explain where they DO live, and the second increasingly overrides the first as an economy industrialises.

***

**Thandi:** This one confused a few of us. Why are there such crowded rural areas in the Eastern Cape when the farming there cannot support that many people? Nothing about the land explains it.

**Tutor:** Nothing about the land does explain it, and that is precisely the point — the cause is political history. Under twentieth-century land legislation and the apartheid homeland system, the majority of South Africa's population was legally confined to a small fraction of the country's land, designated as reserves and later as homelands such as Transkei and Ciskei. Those areas were chosen for political convenience rather than agricultural quality, so they combined poor soils, uncertain rainfall and almost no industry with very large numbers of people. The consequences are still visible on a population map: rural densities far higher than the resource base could ever justify, with heavy pressure on land, overgrazing and soil erosion, and households that survive on remittances rather than on farming. It also explains one of the biggest flows on any South African migration map, the steady movement of working-age people from those areas to Gauteng, Cape Town and Durban, which in turn shapes the age structure left behind. So when a question asks you to account for South Africa's distribution, a full answer has three layers: rainfall for the east-west divide, economic opportunity for the urban clusters, and land policy for the crowded rural pockets. Leaving out the third layer leaves the map genuinely unexplained.

**Thandi:** Last one: how would I actually describe a population distribution from a map in an exam? I can see the pattern but I never know what to write.

**Tutor:** Use a fixed four-move structure and it becomes routine. Move one: give the overall character in a single sentence — the distribution is uneven, with population concentrated in the east and sparse in the west. Move two: locate the concentrations by name and by direction, using the map's own evidence — dense settlement in the north-eastern interior around the main urban area, and along the coast at the three port cities. Move three: locate the empty areas the same way — very low densities across the dry western interior, with settlement limited to scattered points. Move four: give a reason attached to each observation, one physical and one human where possible — the west is dry, the east is better watered; the interior concentration reflects mining and industry, the coastal ones reflect harbours and trade. Two extra habits raise the mark. Quote figures if the map supplies them, because a described class such as 'over 100 people per square kilometre' shows you actually read the key. And use compass directions rather than vague words like 'here' and 'over there' — an examiner cannot see where you are pointing, and directions are how geographers write.
