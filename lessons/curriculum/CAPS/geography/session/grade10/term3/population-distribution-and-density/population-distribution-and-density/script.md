# Part 1 — Expert

People are not spread evenly over the earth, and they never have been. Roughly nine out of every ten human beings live north of the equator, and vast areas of the planet carry almost nobody. This session establishes the two technical terms used to describe that unevenness, works the density calculation, sets out the physical and human factors that explain the pattern, and applies all of it to South Africa's own map — where the biggest concentration of people sits inland, on ground with no river and no port, for a reason worth understanding.

## Subtopic: Distribution and Density — Two Different Ideas

These two terms are constantly confused, and the confusion is expensive because questions almost always ask for one specifically.

POPULATION DISTRIBUTION is the PATTERN — the way people are spread across an area. It is described in words, not numbers: even or uneven, clustered or dispersed, concentrated along the coast, strung out in a line along a road or river, absent from the interior. A distribution answer paints a picture.

POPULATION DENSITY is a NUMBER — the average number of people per unit of area, normally people per square kilometre. It measures crowding, not pattern.

Prove to yourself that they are different. Take two districts of exactly 1 000 square kilometres, each holding 50 000 people. Both have a density of 50 people per square kilometre — identical. But in the first the people are spread evenly across the farms, roughly the same handful in every valley. In the second, every one of those 50 000 lives in a single town in one corner, and the remaining 950 square kilometres are empty. Same density, completely different distribution. Density tells you how many; distribution tells you where.

The vocabulary for describing distribution is worth having ready. DENSELY POPULATED means many people in a small area. SPARSELY POPULATED means few people spread over a large area. UNINHABITED means nobody at all — the ice sheets, the driest deserts, the highest mountains. And the standard descriptive shapes are CLUSTERED or nucleated, where settlement gathers at a point; LINEAR, where it follows a route, a river or a coast; and DISPERSED or scattered, where homesteads stand apart across the countryside.

Take this section's questions now: the two definitions kept apart, the worked example of equal density with different distribution, and the vocabulary for describing a pattern.

## Subtopic: Calculating Density — and What It Hides

The formula is simple, and the marks are in the units and the interpretation.

POPULATION DENSITY equals TOTAL POPULATION divided by TOTAL AREA, giving people per square kilometre.

Work an example. A municipality has 240 000 people and covers 1 600 square kilometres. Divide 240 000 by 1 600: the density is 150 people per square kilometre. Always write the unit; a bare number earns nothing.

Now a national example. South Africa has a population above 60 million and an area of about 1,22 million square kilometres, which gives an average of roughly 50 people per square kilometre. Compare that with the extremes inside the country. Gauteng occupies under two per cent of South Africa's land area yet holds around a quarter of its people, giving a density well above 800 people per square kilometre. The Northern Cape covers close to a third of the country's area and holds only about two per cent of its population, giving a density of roughly three people per square kilometre. Same country, a difference of more than two hundred times.

That comparison exposes the weakness of the measure, and stating it earns higher-order marks. Density is an AVERAGE, and an average conceals everything inside it. South Africa's figure of 50 per square kilometre describes nowhere in South Africa: it is the average of empty Karoo and crowded Soweto. The smaller the area you calculate it for, the more meaningful it becomes; calculated for a whole country it can be actively misleading.

There is a second limitation. Simple, or arithmetic, density divides people by TOTAL land, including mountain, desert and rock on which nobody could live. Two countries with the same arithmetic density can be under quite different pressure if one is mostly farmland and the other mostly desert. That is why geographers sometimes divide population by ARABLE land instead, which shows how many people each unit of productive land must support.

## Subtopic: Physical Factors Affecting Distribution

Factors are sorted into physical and human, and every answer should be organised under those two headings.

RELIEF. Flat or gently undulating land attracts people: it is easier to farm, to build on, and to cross with roads and railways. Steep, rugged mountains repel settlement — thin soils, difficult access, high construction costs. High altitude also brings cold and thinner air, though in the tropics highlands can be more attractive than the lowlands because they are cooler and freer of disease.

CLIMATE. This is the strongest single physical control. Moderate temperatures and reliable rainfall support agriculture and comfortable living, and the world's great population clusters sit in such zones. Extremes repel: hot deserts such as the Sahara and the Kalahari because of drought, polar and tundra regions because of cold and a frozen growing season, and equatorial rainforests because of leached soils, dense vegetation and disease.

WATER SUPPLY. Fresh water is non-negotiable. Settlement follows rivers, springs, lakes and reliable groundwater, which is why the world's oldest dense populations lie along the Nile, the Ganges and the great Chinese rivers, and why in dry regions the settlement pattern is often linear, strung along a watercourse.

SOILS. Deep, fertile soils support intensive agriculture and therefore dense rural populations. Alluvial soils on floodplains and deltas, and weathered volcanic soils, carry some of the densest farming populations on earth. Thin, sandy or saline soils carry few people.

NATURAL RESOURCES. Minerals and energy attract population to places that would otherwise be unattractive, because the resource cannot be moved to the people — the people must come to it.

VEGETATION AND DISEASE. Very dense forest hinders settlement and transport, while the presence of disease vectors such as the tsetse fly has historically kept people and their cattle out of large areas of tropical Africa.

ACCESSIBILITY. Coasts with natural harbours, navigable rivers and low mountain passes concentrate people, because trade concentrates there.

## Subtopic: Human Factors and South Africa's Pattern

HUMAN and ECONOMIC factors increasingly outweigh physical ones, and in South Africa they explain almost everything.

ECONOMIC OPPORTUNITY is the dominant one: people move to where work is. Mining, manufacturing and services concentrate employment, and population follows. TRANSPORT AND COMMUNICATIONS concentrate settlement along corridors — a national road, a railway line, a port. SERVICES matter: schools, clinics, hospitals, electricity, water and internet make an area liveable, and their absence drives people out. POLITICAL DECISIONS AND HISTORY shape distribution powerfully, through land policy, forced removals, border controls and the location of capitals. And SOCIAL factors — family ties, language, religion, community — hold people in places economics alone cannot explain.

Now read South Africa's map, and describe it in the order examiners reward: the pattern first, then the evidence, then the explanation.

The pattern is strikingly uneven, with a broad divide between a wetter, more densely settled east and a drier, emptier west, roughly along the line where average annual rainfall falls below about 500 millimetres. Four major urban concentrations stand out. GAUTENG is the largest and it is the anomaly: no coast, no navigable river, ordinary highveld grassland — but gold beneath it. The Witwatersrand goldfields drew labour and capital from the 1880s, industry followed the labour, services followed the industry, and today a small province holds about a quarter of the national population. The DURBAN corridor grew around the country's busiest port and the industry attached to it. CAPE TOWN grew from the oldest port and now from services, tourism and administration. The GQEBERHA-EAST LONDON area grew on harbours and manufacturing, particularly motor assembly.

Then the pattern that surprises learners: high RURAL densities in parts of the Eastern Cape, Limpopo, Mpumalanga and the eastern seaboard, on land whose farming potential does not justify them. These are the former homelands — Transkei, Ciskei and others — where apartheid-era policy confined the majority population to a small share of the land. The density there is a political inheritance, not a physical one, and it explains much of the migration to the cities that continues today.

Sparse areas complete the picture: the Karoo, the Northern Cape, the Kalahari and Namaqualand, all limited by low and unreliable rainfall, with settlement scattered on huge farms and clustered at the few water points and mining towns.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now: the human factors listed, the four urban concentrations explained, and the former-homeland densities accounted for.

## Subtopic: The Classroom and the Stadium

Put forty learners in a normal classroom. Now put the same forty learners in a soccer stadium. Same number of people. Completely different feeling — and that feeling is DENSITY. It is just people divided by space: crowded in the classroom, lost in the stadium.

Now a second experiment, and this is the one people miss. Keep everyone in the stadium, but arrange them two ways. First, spread all forty evenly across the stands, one here, one there, all the way round. Then bring all forty together into a single huddle in one corner, leaving the rest of the stadium empty.

The density has not changed at all — forty people, same stadium. But the PATTERN has completely changed. That pattern is DISTRIBUTION.

So: density is a NUMBER, and it answers "how crowded?" Distribution is a PICTURE, and it answers "where are they?" A question asking for density wants people per square kilometre. A question asking for distribution wants words — clustered here, empty there, strung along the coast, packed into the corner.

The words to use for the picture are few and worth knowing. Dense means many people in a small space. Sparse means few people spread thin. Clustered means they gather in one spot. Linear means they follow a line — a road, a river, a coastline. Dispersed means scattered apart, like farmhouses across the veld.

One more warning about the number, because it fools people. A density is an average, and averages lie about extremes. If you work out the average for the whole of South Africa you get about fifty people per square kilometre, which describes precisely nowhere — it is empty Karoo and packed Soweto stirred into one figure.

## Subtopic: Why Nobody Builds a City in the Kalahari

Ask yourself a simple question: if you were choosing where to live with nothing but the land to guide you, what would you look for? Your answer is the list of physical factors, and you already know it.

WATER, first and always. No water, no settlement — it is that blunt. This is why towns line up along rivers, why you find a village at every spring, and why in dry country the settlement pattern is a thin string of dots following a watercourse rather than a spread.

FLAT GROUND. It is easier to plough, easier to build on, easier to run a road across. Steep mountainsides give you thin soil, hard access and expensive building, so mountains hold few people — think of how empty the high Drakensberg is compared with the land below it.

DECENT WEATHER. Not too hot, not too cold, and enough rain to grow something. The places people avoid are the ones where the weather says no: the Sahara and the Kalahari, too dry; the polar regions, too cold to grow anything; and the thick equatorial rainforests, where the soil is poor once the trees go and disease is a constant.

GOOD SOIL. Rich soil feeds more people from the same hectare, so farming areas with deep soils carry dense rural populations. Sandy or salty soil carries very few.

Then two extras that pull people to places that otherwise look unpromising. RESOURCES: if there is gold or coal or platinum under the ground, people come, because the mineral cannot be carried to them. And ACCESS: a natural harbour, a river mouth, a gap through the mountains — trade collects there, and people collect around trade.

Look at South Africa's rainfall and the picture snaps into focus. Draw an imaginary line down the middle of the country: the east gets decent rain, the west is dry. Now look at where people live — mostly in the east. The land itself wrote most of that map.

Your questions on the land are up now: water, relief, climate and soil as attractors and repellers, and the two extras that override them.

## Subtopic: Follow the Jobs

Here is the thing the land alone cannot explain, and it is the biggest fact on South Africa's population map.

The largest cluster of people in this country is Gauteng. And Gauteng has no coast, no big river, no harbour, and perfectly ordinary highveld grass. By the rules of the previous section, nobody should be there. So why are around a quarter of all South Africans living on less than two per cent of the land?

Gold. In the 1880s the Witwatersrand reef was found, and everything followed from that one fact. Mines need workers, so workers came from across the country and the region. Workers need food, housing, transport, shops and schools, so towns grew. Mines need machinery, so factories opened to make it. Factories need banks and offices, so a financial centre grew. Roads and railways were built to serve the mines, and everything that came later used those roads. Today the gold is largely worked out, but the city built on it is the economic heart of the continent — because once people, roads and jobs are in a place, they hold each other there.

That is the master human factor: people go where the work is, and work goes where people already are. Everything else follows — the port cities of Durban, Cape Town and Gqeberha grew because harbours mean trade, trade means factories, and factories mean jobs.

And then there is a pattern on our map that neither jobs nor rainfall explains. Parts of the rural Eastern Cape, Limpopo and the eastern seaboard have very high densities of people on land that cannot really feed them. That is not geography — it is history. Under apartheid, the majority of the population was confined to the homelands, a small fraction of the country's land, with the poorest resources and almost no industry. The crowding is a political inheritance, and it is the engine behind the steady flow of people from those areas to the cities today.

So when you describe South Africa's population map, say three things and you will have it all: dense in the east and empty in the dry west, because of rainfall; four big city clusters, because of gold and harbours; and crowded rural pockets in the former homelands, because of history.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now — why Gauteng exists where it does, why the port cities grew, and why the rural east is crowded.
