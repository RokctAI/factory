Hey, it's Thandi — volcanoes come with a long list of names: shield, composite, cinder, dome, caldera, batholith, laccolith, lopolith, sill, dyke, neck. It reads like a spelling test, and it is completely normal to feel buried by it. Let me hand you the two questions that sort every one of those words.

Question one: did it come out on top, or did it stay underground? Out on top is EXTRUSIVE — cones, craters, calderas, lava flows, lava plateaux, hot springs. Stayed underground is INTRUSIVE — batholith, lopolith, laccolith, sill, dyke, neck. That single split organises the whole list, and questions almost always ask you to classify before they ask anything else.

Question two, for the eruption itself: was the magma runny or thick? Runny means gas escapes, so gentle eruptions and a wide low shield. Thick means gas is trapped, so explosions and a tall steep composite cone. Runny-gentle-wide, thick-explosive-steep. Say it once and you have half the topic.

For the intrusive family, remember only sill and dyke properly and you will survive: a sill lies flat BETWEEN the layers, a dyke cuts ACROSS them. Sill sounds like sill of a window — flat and horizontal. That one image carries a lot of marks.

The habit that protects marks: attach a real place to every term. Shield — Hawaii, or Marion Island, which is ours. Composite — Fuji. Caldera — Ngorongoro. Lava plateau — the Drakensberg. Sill — the Karoo koppies. Lopolith — the Bushveld and its platinum. Neck — the Kimberley diamond pipes. Named examples are free marks, and half of them are in your own country.

Two sorting questions, one sill-and-dyke image, seven named places. That is the whole topic, and it fits in your pocket. You've got this!
