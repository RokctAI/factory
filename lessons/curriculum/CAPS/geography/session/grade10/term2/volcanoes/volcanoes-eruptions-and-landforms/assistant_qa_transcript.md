**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: how can a hill in the Karoo be a volcanic landform when there is obviously no volcano anywhere near it?

**Tutor:** Because you are not looking at a volcano — you are looking at the plumbing of one, with the volcano itself long gone. Roughly 180 million years ago, as Gondwana began to pull apart, enormous volumes of magma forced their way through the Karoo rocks. Some reached the surface and erupted; a great deal did not. That trapped magma squeezed sideways between the flat-lying layers of shale and mudstone and cooled slowly into a hard, dark rock called dolerite. It cooled slowly because it was insulated by kilometres of rock above it, and slow cooling gives a tough, crystalline rock. Then time did the rest. Rain, wind, heat and frost stripped away everything softer, and shale weathers far faster than dolerite. Wherever a dolerite sheet lay, it acted as an umbrella, protecting the shale directly beneath it while the unprotected shale on either side was carried away. What remains is a flat-topped koppie with a hard dark lid and steeper slopes below — exactly the profile you see for hundreds of kilometres along the N1. So the landform is not built by eruption; it is built by differential erosion around something the eruption left underground. That is the whole idea of an intrusive landform.

**Thandi:** Next: if lava is molten rock at over a thousand degrees, why do you keep saying lava flows hardly ever kill anyone? That sounds wrong.

**Tutor:** It sounds wrong and it is nevertheless true, and the reason is speed rather than temperature. Most lava flows advance at walking pace or slower — often only metres per hour — because as soon as lava is exposed to air it begins to crust over, and that crust slows the flow further. People in volcanic regions routinely watch flows approach and leave with their possessions. Lava is a hazard to property, not usually to life: it burns and buries houses, roads, crops and pipelines, and what it covers is unrecoverable. The killers are the fast hazards. A pyroclastic flow is a dense cloud of gas, ash and rock fragments at several hundred degrees that pours down the slope at speeds no vehicle on a mountain road can match, and it kills by heat and asphyxiation in seconds. A lahar is a mudflow with the consistency of wet concrete that races down river valleys and can bury a town tens of kilometres away, long after people thought they were at a safe distance. Ash is the quiet third killer: its weight collapses roofs onto the people sheltering under them. So in an answer, rank the hazards honestly — lava for property, pyroclastic flows and lahars for lives — and you will be saying exactly what volcanologists say.

***

**Thandi:** This one troubled us: if scientists can see an eruption coming, why do people ever get caught? It feels like the warnings should be enough.

**Tutor:** The warnings are usually good; the difficulty lies between the warning and the action. Three problems recur. First, timing is imprecise. Instruments detect that magma is moving — earthquake swarms, ground swelling, more gas, hotter ground — but the volcano may erupt in three days or settle down for another two years. A false alarm that empties a town for weeks costs people their income and their trust, and the next warning is taken less seriously. Second, the decision is political and economic, not scientific. Evacuating farming communities means abandoning livestock and crops that represent a family's entire wealth, and many people will not leave without a guarantee that the animals will be fed and the land will still be theirs. Third, the danger zone is often where the good soil is, so the population density is highest exactly where the hazard maps are reddest. That is why the best programmes combine four things: continuous monitoring, clearly graded alert levels that everyone understands, evacuation routes and shelters prepared and rehearsed in advance, and compensation or support that makes leaving affordable. Where those four are in place, eruptions with high hazard have produced remarkably few deaths. Where any one is missing, warnings arrive and are not acted on.

**Thandi:** Last one: South Africa has no active volcanoes, so why does the syllabus spend so long on them here?

**Tutor:** Three reasons, and all of them are examinable. The first is that South Africa's landscape is volcanic, even though its volcanoes are extinct. The Drakensberg is a pile of basalt lava sheets from fissure eruptions during the break-up of Gondwana; the Karoo's flat-topped koppies are capped by dolerite sills from the same event; the Bushveld Igneous Complex north of Pretoria is the largest layered intrusion on earth and holds the world's greatest platinum and chromium reserves; Paarl Rock is exposed granite; and the diamond pipes at Kimberley and Cullinan are kimberlite volcanic necks that brought stones up from the mantle. Almost the whole mining economy of this country rests on ancient volcanic processes. The second reason is that South Africa is not entirely without present-day activity — Marion Island, in the sub-Antarctic and part of our territory, is a shield volcano that has erupted within living memory. The third is conceptual: volcanoes are the clearest demonstration of the idea the whole term is built on, that the interior of the earth shapes the surface. Once you can explain why the Andes have composite cones, why the mid-ocean ridges have fissure eruptions and why Hawaii sits over a hot spot, you have shown that you understand plate tectonics rather than merely memorised it — and that is what the higher-mark questions are really testing.
