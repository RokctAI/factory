# Part 1 — Expert

A volcano is an opening in the crust through which molten rock, gas and fragments reach the surface. This session traces the material from its origin deep underground to the landform it finally builds, explains why one eruption oozes and another explodes, distinguishes the features made above ground from those made below it, and weighs the hazards against the benefits that keep millions of people living on volcanic slopes. South Africa has no active volcanoes on its mainland, but its landscape is written in old volcanic rock — the Drakensberg, the Karoo koppies and the diamond fields all belong to this topic.

## Subtopic: From Magma to Lava — and Why Some Eruptions Explode

Get the two words right first. MAGMA is molten rock BELOW the surface. LAVA is the same material once it has reached the surface. Both carry dissolved gases — mainly water vapour, carbon dioxide and sulphur dioxide — and those gases drive the eruption.

Magma forms in three settings, each already familiar. At DIVERGENT boundaries the plates part, pressure on the mantle beneath is reduced, and rock that was solid under pressure melts. At CONVERGENT boundaries water driven out of the descending slab lowers the melting point of the mantle above it, generating magma that rises into the overriding plate. And at HOT SPOTS, columns of unusually hot mantle rise beneath a plate far from any boundary, burning through it — Hawaii is the classic example, and Réunion in the Indian Ocean is the nearest one to us.

Because magma is less dense than the surrounding rock it rises, collecting in a MAGMA CHAMBER a few kilometres below the surface until pressure forces it up a pipe to the surface.

Now the property that decides everything about the eruption: VISCOSITY, the resistance of the magma to flowing, which is controlled mainly by its SILICA content.

Magma LOW in silica is BASALTIC. It is very hot, around 1 100 to 1 200 degrees Celsius, and extremely runny, so dissolved gas escapes easily as it rises. Eruptions are EFFUSIVE — the lava pours out, runs for many kilometres and builds gently sloping landforms.

Magma HIGH in silica is andesitic or rhyolitic. It is cooler and thick — stiff enough to plug its own vent. Gas cannot escape, so pressure builds behind the plug until the whole thing is blown apart. Eruptions are EXPLOSIVE, throwing out PYROCLASTIC material: ash, cinders and volcanic bombs.

Classify the volcano too. ACTIVE means it has erupted in recorded history and may erupt again; DORMANT means it has not erupted recently but is capable of doing so; EXTINCT means it is no longer supplied with magma.

## Subtopic: The Structure of a Volcano and the Main Types

Build the structure from the bottom up, in the order a labelling question runs. The MAGMA CHAMBER is the reservoir at depth. From it the main VENT, or pipe, leads upward. At the summit the vent opens into the CRATER, a funnel-shaped depression. Around the vent, alternating or repeated layers of erupted material form the CONE. Branching off the main pipe, side vents feed SECONDARY or PARASITIC CONES on the volcano's flanks. If an enormous eruption empties the magma chamber, the summit loses its support and collapses inward, producing a CALDERA — a vast basin far wider than any crater. Ngorongoro in Tanzania is a caldera; its floor is now a wildlife-filled plain many kilometres across.

Four main types follow directly from the magma.

SHIELD VOLCANOES are built by runny basaltic lava. Because the lava flows far before solidifying, the volcano spreads wide and rises gently: picture an upturned soup plate or a warrior's shield laid on the ground, hundreds of kilometres across but with slopes of only a few degrees. Mauna Loa in Hawaii is the type example, and Marion Island — South African territory in the sub-Antarctic — is a shield volcano that has erupted in living memory.

COMPOSITE VOLCANOES, also called stratovolcanoes, are built at subduction zones by alternating explosive and effusive activity. Each explosion lays down a layer of ash; each quieter phase lays down a layer of lava that seals the ash in place. The result is a tall, steep-sided, symmetrical cone with concave slopes — steeper near the summit, flattening outward. Mount Fuji, Vesuvius and the volcanoes of the Andes are composite cones, and these are the dangerous ones.

CINDER or ASH CONES are small and simple: loose pyroclastic fragments piled around a vent, forming a steep-sided cone, seldom more than a few hundred metres high, with a large crater for its size.

DOME volcanoes are made of lava so viscous that it barely flows at all, piling up over the vent into a steep, bulging mound that frequently collapses and reforms.

## Subtopic: Extrusive and Intrusive Landforms

The syllabus divides volcanic landforms into two families, and the division is simply whether the magma reached the surface.

EXTRUSIVE landforms are built at the surface. The cones and craters already described belong here, along with LAVA FLOWS and LAVA PLATEAUX. A plateau forms when very fluid basaltic lava erupts not from a single cone but from long FISSURES, flooding the countryside in sheet after sheet until a thick, flat-topped upland is built. South Africa's own example is spectacular: about 180 million years ago, as Gondwana began to break apart, fissure eruptions poured basalt over the subcontinent. What survives is the Drakensberg, whose highest peaks and the whole Lesotho highland are stacked basalt flows — which is why the summit skyline is a wall of flat-topped, stepped ramparts rather than a row of peaks. Hot springs and geysers are also extrusive features, and South Africa has plenty of hot springs, at Bela-Bela, Caledon, Montagu and Aliwal North, where groundwater circulates deep enough to be heated and returns to the surface.

INTRUSIVE landforms form where magma cools and solidifies UNDERGROUND, and they become landforms only after erosion has stripped the covering rock away. Learn five.

A BATHOLITH is an enormous mass of magma, usually granite, solidified deep in the crust; exposed by erosion it forms rounded, domed hills — Paarl Rock is granite of this kind.

A LOPOLITH is a saucer-shaped intrusion, sagging in the middle. The Bushveld Igneous Complex north of Pretoria is the largest such body on earth, and it holds the world's greatest reserves of platinum and chromium.

A LACCOLITH is a dome-shaped intrusion that has arched the overlying strata upward into a blister.

A SILL is a sheet of magma that has forced its way BETWEEN existing layers, so it lies horizontally, parallel to the bedding. A DYKE is a sheet that has cut ACROSS the layers, so it stands vertically or steeply. Both are everywhere in the Karoo as dolerite, from the same event that built the Drakensberg. Dolerite is far harder than the shale and mudstone around it, so where a sill is exposed it caps a hill and protects it from erosion — which is exactly why the Karoo landscape is a plain studded with flat-topped koppies, each wearing a dolerite lid. Where a dyke is exposed it weathers into a long, straight wall of rock running across country.

A VOLCANIC NECK or PLUG is the solidified magma left in the pipe itself; once the softer cone around it erodes away, the plug stands alone as a steep tower. South Africa's most valuable pipes are the KIMBERLITE pipes at Kimberley and Cullinan, which brought diamonds up from the mantle.

## Subtopic: Hazards, Benefits and Management

Sort the hazards by what does the killing. LAVA FLOWS destroy property but rarely kill, because most move slowly enough to outwalk. PYROCLASTIC FLOWS are the true danger: avalanches of superheated gas, ash and rock that race down the slopes far faster than anyone can run and incinerate everything in their path. ASH FALLS collapse roofs under their weight, smother crops and pasture, contaminate water, damage lungs, and shut down air traffic across whole continents. LAHARS are volcanic mudflows, formed when ash mixes with heavy rain or melted snow, and they follow valleys, burying settlements far from the volcano. Add poisonous gases, landslides, tsunamis from coastal eruptions, and the cooling of world climate for a year or two after very large eruptions when ash and sulphur reach the stratosphere.

Then the question worth asking: why do people stay? Because volcanic land is valuable. Volcanic ash and lava weather into some of the most FERTILE SOILS on earth, rich in potassium, phosphorus and magnesium, which is why densely farmed populations live on the slopes of Java, Sicily and the Ethiopian highlands. GEOTHERMAL ENERGY comes free — Iceland heats most of its buildings this way, and Kenya draws a large share of its electricity from geothermal plants in the Rift Valley. MINERALS concentrate in volcanic settings: platinum and chromium from the Bushveld, copper, gold and sulphur elsewhere, and diamonds from kimberlite pipes. Volcanic rock supplies building stone and aggregate; hot springs and dramatic scenery support TOURISM.

Management is more successful here than for earthquakes, because volcanoes give warning. MONITORING tracks swarms of small earthquakes as magma forces its way up, ground swelling measured by tiltmeters and satellites, rising gas emissions, and changes in ground temperature. From that, scientists issue graded alert levels. HAZARD MAPS show which valleys lahars would follow and which slopes lie in the path of pyroclastic flows. Planning then keeps permanent settlement out of the highest-risk zones, plans evacuation routes and shelters in advance, and rehearses them. Education matters as much as instruments: communities that know the alert levels and the routes evacuate in hours rather than days.

## Subtopic: Shaking the Cooldrink

Take a two-litre bottle of fizzy cooldrink. The gas is dissolved in the liquid, invisible, under pressure. Open it gently and it hisses. Shake it first, then open it, and it goes everywhere.

Every volcano is that bottle. Molten rock underground — magma — has gas dissolved in it, and the gas wants out. The only question is how easily it can escape, and that depends on how RUNNY the melt is.

Some magma is thin and runny, like warm honey off a spoon. Gas bubbles slide up through it and out with no trouble, so there is no build-up. That volcano does not explode; it oozes. Red rivers of lava pour out, travel a long way, and cool into wide, gently sloping ground.

Other magma is thick, like cold, stiff pap that resists the spoon. Bubbles cannot get through, and the stuff is so stiff it plugs its own pipe like a cork. Pressure builds behind the cork for years. When it finally gives, the whole top of the mountain is blown apart, and what comes out is not a river of lava but a blast of ash, cinders and rock.

So: runny magma means gentle eruptions and wide, flat volcanoes; thick magma means violent eruptions and steep, dangerous ones. What makes it thick or thin is mostly how much silica is in it, and that in turn depends on where the magma came from — plates pulling apart give runny stuff, plates diving under each other give thick stuff.

Two more words to keep straight, because they are the same rock at different addresses. Underground it is called magma. The moment it reaches the surface it is called lava. Same material, new name.

## Subtopic: Pancake Volcanoes and Layer-Cake Volcanoes

Volcanoes come in shapes, and each shape gives away what came out of it.

The PANCAKE. Pour runny batter into a pan and it spreads out wide and thin. Runny lava does exactly this: it flows kilometres before it cools, so the volcano ends up enormously wide and very low, with slopes so gentle you could drive up them. Geographers call it a SHIELD volcano, because it looks like a warrior's shield lying face-up on the ground. Hawaii is made of them, and so is Marion Island, which belongs to South Africa and sits far south in the ocean.

The LAYER CAKE. Now imagine building a cake in alternating layers: a layer of loose crumbs, then a layer of icing to hold the crumbs down, then more crumbs, then more icing. That is a COMPOSITE volcano. It explodes, laying down loose ash; then it oozes, laying down lava that glues the ash in place; then it explodes again. Hundreds of alternating layers build a tall, steep, beautiful cone — steepest near the top and flattening out at the bottom. Mount Fuji is one. So is Vesuvius. These are the postcard volcanoes and also the killers.

The RUBBISH HEAP. Throw loose material into a pile and it forms a small steep cone with a big hollow on top — that is a cinder cone, made only of the fragments an eruption threw out.

Inside, all of them share the same plumbing. Deep down there is a magma chamber, the store room. Up from it runs a pipe, the vent. At the top the pipe opens into a bowl, the crater. And sometimes side-pipes break out through the flanks and build small extra cones on the mountain's shoulders.

One last shape to know. If a giant eruption empties the store room completely, the mountain top has nothing left underneath it and it collapses inward, leaving a huge basin many kilometres wide — a caldera. Ngorongoro in Tanzania is one, and its floor is now a plain full of wildlife.

## Subtopic: What the Volcano Leaves Behind

Here is the part that makes this topic South African, because you have almost certainly seen these landforms.

Some volcanic rock cools on top of the ground. About 180 million years ago, when Gondwana started tearing apart, the crust here cracked open in long splits and basalt lava poured out — not from a single mountain but from cracks kilometres long — flooding the country in sheet after sheet. Those stacked sheets are the Drakensberg and the high ground of Lesotho, and that is why the skyline up there is a wall of flat steps and ramparts instead of pointed peaks. You are looking at cooled lava, layer on layer.

Other magma never made it out. It stopped underground and cooled slowly in place — and then, over millions of years, the softer rock covering it was worn away and the hard lump was left standing. That is why the Karoo looks the way it does. Magma squeezed sideways between the flat layers of Karoo shale, cooled into a hard rock called dolerite, and because dolerite resists weathering far better than shale, it now sits like a lid on top of hundreds of hills. Every flat-topped koppie you pass on the N1 is a hill wearing a dolerite hat, and the plain around it is where the unprotected shale has already gone. Where the magma squeezed upward across the layers instead of sideways, it now stands out as a long straight wall of rock running over the veld.

And one more, worth billions. Deep pipes of a rock called kimberlite blasted up from the mantle, and they carried diamonds with them. Kimberley and Cullinan are those pipes.

So the ledger, both sides. The dangers are real: lava wrecks property, ash collapses roofs and ruins crops, mudflows bury towns down-valley, and the true killer is the pyroclastic flow — a hurricane of scorching gas and ash that outruns any vehicle. But people stay, and for good reasons: volcanic soil is among the richest farmland on earth, the ground heat runs power stations in Iceland and Kenya, and the minerals are extraordinary — our own platinum north of Pretoria, our own diamonds at Kimberley.

The good news is that volcanoes warn you. Before an eruption the ground swells, small tremors swarm, gases increase and the ground warms — all measurable. So scientists watch the instruments, draw maps of which valleys the mud will come down, keep housing out of the worst zones, and practise the evacuation. Watch, map, plan, practise.
