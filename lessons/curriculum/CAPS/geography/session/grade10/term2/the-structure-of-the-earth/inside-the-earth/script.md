# Part 1 — Expert

Nobody has ever been inside the earth. The deepest borehole ever drilled reached about twelve kilometres, and the earth's radius is 6 371 — the equivalent of scratching the skin of an orange. Yet we can state the composition, thickness, temperature and physical state of every layer with confidence. This session builds that picture, distinguishes the two kinds of crust, and — the part most learners skip — explains exactly how we know, because "seismic evidence" is a question in its own right and the whole of plate tectonics rests on this foundation.

## Subtopic: The Layers by Composition — Crust, Mantle, Core

Three layers by composition, and two rules that run through all of them: temperature and pressure both increase with depth, and so does density.

The CRUST is the thin, rigid, rocky outer shell, and it is astonishingly thin — between about 5 and 70 kilometres, which is less than one per cent of the earth's radius. Its underside is marked by a sharp boundary called the MOHOROVIČIĆ DISCONTINUITY, universally shortened to the Moho, detected because seismic waves suddenly speed up when they cross it.

The MANTLE lies beneath, some 2 900 kilometres thick, and it is by far the largest layer — around 84 per cent of the earth's volume. It is made of dense silicate rock rich in iron and magnesium, chiefly peridotite. Get its physical state right, because this is the topic's most punished error: the mantle is SOLID rock, not a sea of liquid lava. But within its upper part, roughly 100 to 250 kilometres down, lies the ASTHENOSPHERE, where rock is close enough to its melting point to behave plastically — it deforms and flows extremely slowly, like exceptionally stiff toffee. That plastic layer is what makes plate movement possible.

The CORE begins at about 2 900 kilometres depth, at the GUTENBERG DISCONTINUITY, and is composed of iron and nickel. It has two parts. The OUTER CORE, roughly 2 900 to 5 150 kilometres down, is LIQUID; its circulating molten metal generates the earth's magnetic field. The INNER CORE, from 5 150 kilometres to the centre, is SOLID — and here is the counter-intuitive fact examiners love: it is the hottest place in the earth, in the region of 5 000 to 6 000 degrees Celsius, comparable to the sun's surface, yet it is solid because the pressure there is so immense that it forces the iron to remain in a solid state.

Take this section's questions now: the three layers with their thicknesses, the two discontinuities named, and the physical state of each layer given with its reason.

## Subtopic: Continental Versus Oceanic Crust

The crust is not one material. It comes in two kinds, and every difference between them matters later.

CONTINENTAL crust — traditionally called SIAL, after its dominant silica and aluminium — is thick: 30 to 50 kilometres typically, and up to 70 beneath great mountain ranges. It is granitic in composition, comparatively LIGHT with a density around 2,7 grams per cubic centimetre, and it is ancient: the oldest continental rocks are close to four billion years old, and South Africa's own Kaapvaal Craton is among the oldest and best-preserved fragments of early crust anywhere on earth.

OCEANIC crust — SIMA, after silica and magnesium — is thin: 5 to 10 kilometres. It is basaltic, DENSER at around 3,0 grams per cubic centimetre, and it is young. No ocean floor anywhere on earth is older than about 200 million years, because oceanic crust is continuously created at mid-ocean ridges and continuously destroyed at subduction zones. The continents are the earth's memory; the ocean floors are its most recent draft.

That density contrast has one enormous consequence: where oceanic and continental crust converge, the DENSER oceanic plate sinks beneath the lighter continental one. Subduction is not an arbitrary rule — it is buoyancy.

Now the second way of slicing the earth, and both are examinable. The layers above were defined by COMPOSITION. Defined by MECHANICAL BEHAVIOUR the earth divides differently: the LITHOSPHERE is the rigid outer shell comprising the crust PLUS the uppermost solid mantle, about 100 kilometres thick, and it is broken into the tectonic plates. Beneath it the plastic asthenosphere allows those plates to move. So a plate is not the same thing as the crust — it is crust and upper mantle together, riding on plastic rock below.

Your questions on this section are with you now: sial against sima on thickness, composition, density and age; why the ocean floor is young; and lithosphere distinguished from crust.

## Subtopic: How We Know — Seismic Evidence

Now the question that separates a good answer from an excellent one. We cannot drill there, so how do we know?

Earthquakes send waves through the entire planet, and those waves behave differently in different materials. Two types of BODY WAVE matter. P-WAVES, primary waves, are compressional — they push and pull the rock along their direction of travel. They are the fastest, they arrive first, and crucially they travel through SOLIDS, LIQUIDS AND GASES alike. S-WAVES, secondary waves, are transverse — they shake the rock sideways across the direction of travel. They are slower, arrive second, and they CANNOT travel through liquid at all, because a liquid has no rigidity to transmit a sideways shear.

Now the evidence itself. Seismic stations all over the world record every large earthquake. What they find is a SHADOW ZONE: beyond about 103 degrees of angular distance from the epicentre, on the far side of the earth, no S-waves arrive anywhere. Not weakened — absent. The only explanation is that they encountered a LIQUID layer and were stopped by it. That is the proof that the outer core is molten.

The P-waves tell the rest. They are not stopped, but they are sharply REFRACTED — bent — when they pass from the solid mantle into the liquid outer core, which leaves a ring between roughly 103 and 143 degrees where no direct P-waves arrive either. The size of that ring gives the depth of the core-mantle boundary with real precision. And within the P-wave shadow zone faint arrivals appear that can only be explained by a further refraction at a boundary deep inside the core — the LEHMANN DISCONTINUITY — which is how we know the inner core is solid.

Three supporting lines of evidence complete the case. Density: the whole earth averages about 5,5 grams per cubic centimetre while surface rocks average only 2,7, so the interior must be far denser than anything we see — which points to metal. The magnetic field: a planetary magnetic field requires circulating liquid metal, exactly what the outer core provides. And meteorites: iron meteorites are thought to be fragments of the cores of shattered planetary bodies, and their composition matches what the earth's core must be.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: P-waves against S-waves, the shadow zones and what each proves, and the three supporting arguments.

## Subtopic: Drawing the Interior in Words — and the Exam Traps

Because you will be asked to label or describe a cross-section, build the description in a fixed order: name, depth range, composition, physical state, one distinguishing feature. Crust — 5 to 70 kilometres, silicate rock, solid, in two varieties. Moho. Mantle — to 2 900 kilometres, iron-magnesium silicates, solid but plastic in the asthenosphere, the largest layer by volume. Gutenberg discontinuity. Outer core — to 5 150 kilometres, iron and nickel, liquid, source of the magnetic field. Lehmann discontinuity. Inner core — to 6 371 kilometres, iron and nickel, solid under enormous pressure, hottest at 5 000 to 6 000 degrees.

Then the traps, each of which costs marks every year. TRAP ONE: describing the mantle as molten. It is solid rock; only the asthenosphere behaves plastically, and even that flows rather than pours. Magma exists in localised pockets, not as a global ocean. TRAP TWO: assuming the inner core must be liquid because it is hottest. Pressure, not temperature alone, determines state — and at the centre pressure wins. TRAP THREE: using "crust" and "plate" as if they were synonyms. A plate is lithosphere: crust plus rigid upper mantle. TRAP FOUR: reversing sial and sima. Continental crust is thick, light, granitic, old; oceanic crust is thin, dense, basaltic, young — and it is the density that sends it downward at a subduction zone.

Hold this structure and next week's plate tectonics becomes almost self-explanatory: plates of lithosphere, driven by heat from a core we have never seen, sliding on plastic rock we have never touched — and every claim there resting on the seismic evidence you have just learned to cite. The final questions of this part are with you now: the cross-section described in order, and each of the four traps stated correctly.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same planet from an avocado, a swimming pool and a knock on a wall.

## Subtopic: The Earth Is an Avocado

Cut an avocado in half and you are holding a model of the earth, and a surprisingly accurate one.

The SKIN is the crust. Look how thin it is compared to everything else — that is exactly right. The crust is between five and seventy kilometres deep, and the earth is six thousand three hundred and seventy-one kilometres to the middle. If the earth were the size of that avocado, the crust would be thinner than the skin. Everything we know — every mine, every borehole, every mountain and ocean trench — happens in that skin.

The GREEN FLESH is the mantle, and just like the avocado it is by far the biggest part: about two thousand nine hundred kilometres of it, roughly eighty-four per cent of the whole earth by volume. Now the thing people get wrong constantly: the mantle is NOT a lake of lava. It is solid rock — very hot solid rock, and in one band near the top it is soft enough to ooze slowly. Think of fridge-cold butter against butter that has sat in a Durban kitchen all afternoon. Still butter, still solid, but it sags. That soft band is what lets the plates move.

The PIP is the core, and this pip has two parts. The outside of it is LIQUID iron and nickel, sloshing around, and that sloshing metal is what makes the earth's magnetic field — the reason a compass works at all. The very middle is SOLID iron, five to six thousand degrees, as hot as the surface of the sun. Why does the hottest place not melt? Because it is squeezed so hard by everything above it that the iron simply cannot spread out into a liquid. Pressure beats heat.

Skin, flesh, pip. Crust, mantle, core. Questions on the avocado are with you now — the three parts, their thicknesses, and which ones are solid and which is liquid.

## Subtopic: Two Kinds of Raft on One Pool

The skin of the earth is not one material — it comes in two, and the difference decides which one goes under when they meet.

Picture a swimming pool. Floating on it is a thick block of polystyrene, and next to it a thin sheet of steel. The polystyrene block is much thicker, but it is LIGHT, so it floats high with most of itself out of the water. The steel sheet is thin, but it is HEAVY, so it sits low, barely at the surface.

The polystyrene is continental crust. Thirty to fifty kilometres thick, up to seventy under big mountain ranges, made of granite-type rock, light at about two point seven, and ancient — some of it nearly four billion years old. The Kaapvaal Craton under much of northern South Africa is one of the oldest surviving pieces of it anywhere on the planet.

The steel sheet is oceanic crust. Only five to ten kilometres thick, made of basalt, heavier at about three point zero, and shockingly young — nowhere on earth is the sea floor older than about two hundred million years, because it is constantly being manufactured at one place and swallowed at another.

Now push the two together in the pool and watch what happens. The steel sheet slides under the polystyrene. Every time. Not because of a rule someone invented, but because it is denser. That is subduction, and it is why the great ocean trenches sit right against continents.

One more thing while we are here: when geographers say "plate", they do not mean crust alone. A plate is the crust PLUS the rigid top slice of the mantle glued underneath it, about a hundred kilometres of solid rock all together, sliding on the soft layer below. Crust is the skin; the plate is the skin with a layer of flesh still attached. Your questions on the two rafts are up now: thick and light against thin and heavy, which one dives, and what a plate actually includes.

## Subtopic: Knocking on the Wall

Here is the honest problem: nobody has ever been down there. The deepest hole ever drilled went about twelve kilometres, and the middle is over six thousand away. So how does anyone dare to say the outer core is liquid?

The same way you find a stud behind a wall — you knock, and you listen to what comes back. Except our knock is an earthquake, and our ears are thousands of seismic stations spread across the world.

Every big earthquake sends two kinds of wave straight through the planet. The P-wave is a push-and-pull, a squeeze travelling forward, and it goes through anything at all: solid, liquid, gas. The S-wave shakes sideways, and here is the crucial part — sideways shaking cannot travel through a liquid. Wiggle a rope and the wave travels along it; try to wiggle water sideways and the water just slides. No rigidity, no sideways wave.

So now the discovery. After a large earthquake, stations on the far side of the earth report something extraordinary: the S-waves never arrive. Not faint, not late — completely absent across a huge zone. Something down there swallowed them. The only thing that stops an S-wave is liquid, and that is how we know the outer core is molten. It is the silence that proves it.

The P-waves finish the job. They do get through, but they BEND sharply when they cross into the liquid core — the way a straw looks broken where it enters a glass of water. That bending leaves its own quiet ring on the surface, and the size of the ring tells us exactly how deep the core starts. Faint extra arrivals inside that ring, bent one more time, are how we know there is a solid ball at the very centre.

Three more clues back it all up. The whole earth weighs far more per bucket than surface rocks do, so the inside must be metal. A compass works, and only sloshing liquid metal makes a magnetic field. And iron meteorites falling out of space look exactly like the cores of smashed-up planets.

Knock, listen, work it out. The final questions of the lesson are coming to you now — the two waves and what each can pass through, what the silence proves, and the three clues that back it up.
