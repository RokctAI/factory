**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: if the mantle is solid rock, where does lava come from? Volcanoes clearly pour out liquid, so something down there must be melted.

**Tutor:** Something is melted, but only in patches, and the patches are made rather than found. The mantle sits below its melting point almost everywhere, because although it is extremely hot, it is also under enormous pressure, and pressure raises the temperature at which rock melts. Three things can tip a piece of it over that line. First, DECOMPRESSION: where plates pull apart at a mid-ocean ridge or a rift valley, the pressure on the mantle beneath is released, the melting point drops, and rock that was solid a moment ago becomes partly liquid without getting any hotter. Second, ADDED WATER: at a subduction zone, water carried down in the sinking oceanic slab is released into the overlying mantle, and water lowers the melting point sharply — which is why subduction volcanoes exist at all. Third, EXTRA HEAT, from a rising plume of unusually hot material, which is what feeds hot-spot volcanoes such as those in Hawaii and on Réunion. In every case only a small percentage of the rock melts; that melt is less dense than its surroundings, so it squeezes upward, collects in magma chambers in the crust, and only then erupts. So the honest answer is that the mantle is a solid that manufactures liquid locally, not an ocean of it.

***

**Thandi:** Next: how can rock be solid and still flow? Those sound like opposites, and the word 'plastic' in the notes did not help anybody.

**Tutor:** They sound like opposites because of the timescale we live on. "Solid" in physics means the material resists a sideways force — it has rigidity — and mantle rock certainly does; if you hit it with a hammer it would shatter, and seismic S-waves pass through it perfectly happily, which they could never do through liquid. "Flow" here means deformation over geological time, at rates measured in centimetres a year. Two everyday examples make it click. Cold candle wax snaps if you bend it fast, yet a candle left leaning against a warm window sags over weeks without ever melting. Glacier ice is unambiguously a solid, and yet a glacier flows downhill. Both are solids deforming slowly because their crystals slip past one another under sustained stress. The asthenosphere is the same idea: rock close enough to its melting point, and under enough load from everything above it, that over millions of years it deforms like exceptionally stiff toffee. In an answer, be careful with your wording — say the asthenosphere is "solid but plastic, deforming and flowing very slowly", never "molten". Examiners mark that phrase closely, because the whole mechanism of plate movement depends on getting it right.

***

**Thandi:** This one confused us badly: what is the actual difference between the crust and a plate? People seem to use the words as if they mean the same thing.

**Tutor:** They are not the same, and the difference is a favourite one-mark question. The crust is defined by COMPOSITION: it is the outermost shell of relatively light silicate rock, five to ten kilometres thick under the oceans and thirty to seventy under the continents, and its base is the Moho, where seismic waves suddenly speed up as they enter the denser mantle rock beneath. A plate is defined by MECHANICAL BEHAVIOUR: it is a slab of LITHOSPHERE, which means the crust together with the uppermost part of the mantle that is cold enough to be rigid — around a hundred kilometres of solid rock in total. So the mantle appears on both sides of the boundary. Its cold rigid top belongs to the plate; the warmer plastic asthenosphere beneath does not, and that is precisely the layer the plate slides on. Picture it as skin with a layer of flesh still attached, floating on something softer. The practical consequence is real: when a plate subducts, it is not just a sliver of crust going down but a hundred-kilometre-thick slab of cold dense lithosphere, and the weight of that sinking slab is one of the main forces dragging the rest of the plate along behind it.

**Thandi:** Last one: this all rests on earthquake waves. But how do scientists know a wave travelled through the core and did not just bounce along the surface or take some other route?

**Tutor:** Because every path through the earth has a distinctive TIMING, and timing is what seismology actually measures. P-waves and S-waves travel at different speeds, and their speed changes predictably with the density and rigidity of the rock they pass through — faster in denser mantle rock, slower in the crust, and sharply slower again in the liquid outer core. So a wave that has crossed the core arrives at a very different moment from one that skimmed round the outside, and its arrival time can be calculated in advance for any assumed structure. Seismologists compare the predicted arrival times for thousands of earthquakes recorded at hundreds of stations against what the instruments actually record, and adjust the assumed structure until the predictions match. That is how the depths of the Moho, the Gutenberg discontinuity and the Lehmann discontinuity were pinned down. Two features clinch the picture. The S-wave shadow zone is complete rather than partial — a bounce or a detour would produce weak signals, not silence, and only a liquid barrier removes them entirely. And the P-wave shadow ring has sharp edges at predictable angles, exactly where a sudden change in wave speed would refract the waves aside. Consistent, worldwide, and reproducible with every large earthquake — which is why we can be confident about a place no one will ever visit.
