Hey, it's Thandi — earthquakes can feel like a topic with a hundred separate facts: waves, scales, depths, effects, engineering. And if you found the wave section slippery, you are in very good company. Let me shrink it for you.

There are really only four moves in this whole topic. One: what causes it — rock bends, friction holds, rock snaps back. Two: where — focus underground, epicentre on the surface directly above. Three: how we measure it — Richter for the earthquake, Mercalli for the experience. Four: why some places suffer more — buildings, ground, money, preparation. Four moves. Everything else hangs off them.

The waves sound scary but they are just three speeds. P is fastest and goes through everything. S is slower and stops at liquid. Surface waves come last and do the damage. If you remember nothing else, remember the order: P, S, surface — and that the LAST one is the destroyer, which feels backwards but is true.

The mark-losing trap is Richter versus Mercalli. Say it once in your own words: Richter is one number for the whole earthquake; Mercalli is a different number in every town. And when a question gives you two magnitudes, do the arithmetic out loud — each step is ten times the shaking and about thirty-two times the energy, so two steps is a hundred and about a thousand.

The habit that protects your marks in the long questions: whenever you are asked why one earthquake killed more people, never answer with geology. Answer with buildings, ground type, density, wealth, preparedness and time of day. Six words, six marks, every single time that question appears.

Four moves, three waves, two scales, six vulnerability words. That is the whole lesson, and it fits in your pocket. You've got this!
