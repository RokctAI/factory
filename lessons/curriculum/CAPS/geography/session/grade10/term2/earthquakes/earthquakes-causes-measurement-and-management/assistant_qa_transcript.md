**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First: if scientists can measure strain building up on a fault, why can they still not tell us when an earthquake will happen?

**Tutor:** Because knowing that a fault is loaded is not the same as knowing when it will fail, and the difference is genuine physics rather than a lack of effort. Strain accumulates steadily and measurably, so seismologists can say with real confidence which faults are dangerous and roughly how often they have failed in the past — that is why hazard maps exist and why they are trustworthy over decades. What nobody can measure is the exact strength of the rock and the friction along every square metre of a fault surface tens of kilometres long and equally deep. A rupture begins at one small point where the strain first exceeds the local strength, and whether that point gives way this year or in ninety years depends on details buried far beyond reach. Every claimed precursor — foreshocks, ground tilt, changes in groundwater level, radon gas, animal behaviour — appears before some earthquakes and not others, and appears at times when no earthquake follows. So the honest scientific position is probabilistic, not predictive, and the whole strategy shifts to preparedness: build so that a surprise is survivable, because the surprise is guaranteed.

***

**Thandi:** Next: how can two towns the same distance from the epicentre get completely different damage? That feels unfair, and I would have expected the same shaking.

**Tutor:** The distance is the same but the ground is not, and the ground can multiply or reduce the shaking dramatically. Seismic waves travelling through solid bedrock move fast and with relatively small amplitude. When those waves pass into soft, loose material — river sediment, old lake beds, reclaimed land, engineered fill — they slow down, and to carry the same energy at a lower speed they must swing much further. The result is that soft ground shakes harder and for longer than the bedrock next door. Worse, a soft layer of a particular thickness can resonate, matching the natural sway of buildings of a particular height and amplifying them severely. And there is a second effect: if the loose material is saturated with groundwater, the shaking can push the grains apart until the mixture briefly behaves like a liquid — liquefaction — so foundations lose all support and buildings tilt or sink. That is why intensity is mapped as many values rather than one, why geotechnical surveys matter before building, and why land-use zoning that keeps hospitals and schools off soft ground is one of the cheapest protective measures a municipality can take.

**Thandi:** This one bothered a lot of us: South Africa is nowhere near a plate boundary, so is any of this actually relevant to us?

**Tutor:** Relevant in three concrete ways. First, we do have tremors. The goldfields of Gauteng, the Free State and the North West produce mining-induced seismic events regularly, because taking rock out from kilometres underground leaves a void that the surrounding rock adjusts into. Those events are very shallow, so people feel them strongly and buildings near the mines can be damaged, and communities on the reef live with them as a genuine hazard. Second, we have natural faults from ancient tectonics that can still move; the Ceres-Tulbagh earthquake of 1969 in the Western Cape caused deaths and heavy structural damage, and it is the standard South African case study. Third, and this is the exam-relevant one, an earthquake does not have to happen here to reach us: a large undersea earthquake anywhere around the Indian Ocean rim can send a tsunami toward the East African and South African coasts, which is why South Africa participates in the Indian Ocean tsunami warning system. So the correct framing is that South Africa has low but not zero seismic hazard, dominated by induced events, and a real coastal exposure to distant earthquakes.

**Thandi:** Last one: everyone says "get under a table" but that sounds like the worst possible place if the building falls. Why is that the advice?

**Tutor:** Because the advice is aimed at the thing that actually injures most people, which is not total collapse but falling objects and flying debris. In the great majority of earthquakes, buildings are damaged rather than flattened, and the injuries come from ceilings shedding plaster and light fittings, from bookshelves, cupboards and televisions toppling, and from window glass. A solid table absorbs exactly those hazards, and holding on matters because the table itself will slide across a moving floor. The alternatives are worse than they look. Running outside during the shaking puts you in the zone where masonry, glass and roof tiles land, and stairwells during violent shaking are dangerous. The old advice about standing in a doorway comes from an era of unreinforced adobe houses where the door frame was the strongest element, and in a modern frame building a doorway offers no protection while exposing you to a swinging door. So the rule is: drop before the shaking drops you, cover your head and neck under something solid, and hold on until the shaking stops — then, once it has stopped, leave the building carefully and stay away from walls, and expect aftershocks. It is worth adding that this advice assumes reasonably sound construction; the same physics is exactly why enforcing building standards is the single most effective way to reduce earthquake deaths.
