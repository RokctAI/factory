# Part 1 — Expert

An earthquake is the sudden release of energy stored in rock, felt at the surface as shaking. This session establishes where that energy comes from and where it is released, the three kinds of wave that carry it, the two entirely different scales used to measure it, and why an earthquake of a given size kills a few hundred people in one country and a quarter of a million in another. That last question is the one the syllabus cares about most, and the one worth the most marks.

## Subtopic: Focus, Epicentre and Elastic Rebound

Start with the mechanism, because everything else follows from it. Tectonic forces load rock with STRAIN. The rock deforms elastically and stores that energy, exactly as a ruler stores energy when you bend it. Friction holds the two sides of a fault locked together, sometimes for centuries. Eventually the accumulated strain exceeds the strength of the rock or the friction holding it, the fault slips, the rock snaps back toward its original shape, and the stored energy is released as vibrations. That is ELASTIC REBOUND, and it is the standard explanation the syllabus expects.

Two locations must be distinguished precisely, because examiners test the pair every year. The FOCUS, also called the hypocentre, is the point WITHIN the crust where the rupture actually begins and energy is first released. The EPICENTRE is the point on the earth's SURFACE directly above the focus. Shaking is normally strongest at the epicentre and weakens outward.

Focus depth is classified in three bands: SHALLOW, from the surface to 70 kilometres; INTERMEDIATE, 70 to 300 kilometres; and DEEP, 300 to 700 kilometres. Depth matters enormously. A shallow-focus earthquake releases its energy close to the surface, so less is absorbed on the way up and the shaking is far more destructive. Most of the world's damaging earthquakes are shallow. Deep-focus earthquakes occur only along subduction zones, where a cold slab descends far into the mantle.

Earthquakes rarely come alone. Smaller FORESHOCKS may precede the main event, and AFTERSHOCKS follow it for days or months as the crust adjusts — frequently completing the collapse of buildings already weakened by the main shock.

Where do they occur? Overwhelmingly along plate boundaries: the Ring of Fire around the Pacific, the Mediterranean-to-Himalaya belt, the mid-ocean ridges and the East African Rift. South Africa lies deep inside the African plate, so natural earthquakes are rare — the Ceres-Tulbagh earthquake of 1969 in the Western Cape being the most damaging on record — while most tremors felt in Gauteng, the Free State and the North West goldfields are induced by deep mining.

Take this section's questions now: elastic rebound stated in sequence, focus distinguished from epicentre, and why depth governs destruction.

## Subtopic: Seismic Waves and Locating the Epicentre

Energy leaves the focus as SEISMIC WAVES of three kinds, and they arrive in a fixed order that is itself a measuring instrument.

P-WAVES — primary waves — are compressional: they push and pull the rock along their line of travel, like the movement running down a stretched spring that is tapped at one end. They are the fastest, travelling roughly six kilometres per second in the crust, they arrive first, and they pass through solids, liquids and gases alike.

S-WAVES — secondary waves — are transverse: they shake the rock sideways, across their direction of travel, like the ripple that runs along a skipping rope flicked at one end. They travel at roughly half the speed of P-waves, arrive second, and cannot pass through liquid at all, because liquids have no rigidity to transmit a sideways shear.

SURFACE WAVES — also called L-waves — travel along the ground rather than through the earth. They are the slowest and arrive last, but they have the largest amplitude and do most of the damage, rolling and swaying the ground beneath buildings.

The instrument is a SEISMOGRAPH, and its trace is a SEISMOGRAM: a line that is quiet, then jumps at the P-wave arrival, jumps again at the S-wave arrival, and swings widest when the surface waves come through.

Now the calculation. Because P and S waves leave the focus at the same instant but travel at different speeds, the TIME GAP between their arrivals grows with distance — precisely as the gap between a lightning flash and its thunder grows with distance. A station reading a two-minute gap is much further from the epicentre than one reading twenty seconds. That gap converts to a distance, but distance alone does not give a position: it only says the epicentre lies somewhere on a circle of that radius around the station. So you need THREE stations. Draw a circle around each, and the three circles intersect at exactly one point. That is TRIANGULATION, and it is how every epicentre in the world is located.

## Subtopic: Richter and Mercalli — Two Different Measurements

Learners lose marks here by treating these as two versions of the same thing. They measure different quantities.

The RICHTER SCALE measures MAGNITUDE: the amount of energy released at the focus. It is calculated from the amplitude recorded on a seismograph, corrected for distance. One earthquake has ONE Richter magnitude, whatever happens at the surface, and the scale has no upper limit.

Critically, it is LOGARITHMIC. Each whole number step up means TEN TIMES the wave amplitude and approximately THIRTY-TWO TIMES the energy released. Work the standard example: a magnitude 7 earthquake compared with a magnitude 5. That is two steps, so the ground movement is ten times ten — one hundred times greater — and the energy released is roughly thirty-two times thirty-two, about ONE THOUSAND times greater. Say "a thousand times more energy", not "slightly stronger". Below about magnitude 3 an earthquake is generally not felt; above 7 it is major; the largest ever recorded was 9,5 in Chile. Modern seismology reports large events on the moment magnitude scale, which is calibrated to agree with Richter but describes very large earthquakes better.

The MERCALLI SCALE measures INTENSITY: the observed effects at a particular place. It runs from I to XII in Roman numerals and is based on what people felt and what was damaged — I, not felt; IV, windows rattle and parked cars rock; VII, difficult to stand, poorly built structures damaged; X, most masonry structures destroyed, ground badly cracked; XII, total destruction, objects thrown into the air.

The consequence is the examinable point: ONE earthquake produces MANY Mercalli values — high near the epicentre, lower further away. And two places at the same distance can record different intensities, because intensity depends on the local ground, the depth of the focus and the quality of the buildings. Loose sediment or reclaimed land amplifies shaking dramatically, while solid bedrock transmits it far less. Richter describes the earthquake; Mercalli describes the experience.

## Subtopic: Effects, Vulnerability and Preparedness

Sort the effects into primary and secondary. PRIMARY effects are caused directly by the shaking: ground rupture along the fault, and the collapse of buildings, bridges, roads and pipelines. SECONDARY effects follow from those: fires from ruptured gas mains and fallen power lines; landslides and rockfalls on steep slopes; LIQUEFACTION, in which saturated loose sediment loses its strength and behaves like a liquid, so buildings tilt and sink; flooding from failed dams; contaminated water and disease outbreaks; homelessness; and enormous economic loss. Where the focus is beneath the sea floor and the sea bed is displaced vertically, the water column above is lifted and a TSUNAMI is generated — a series of waves that cross an ocean at jet-aircraft speed and rear up on reaching shallow coastal water.

Now the question that carries the highest marks: why does the same magnitude kill so differently? The answer is VULNERABILITY, not geology.

BUILDING QUALITY AND ENFORCEMENT. It is buildings that kill people, not shaking. Where earthquake-resistant codes exist and are enforced, structures sway and survive; where informal or poorly built housing crowds a steep hillside, it collapses.

POPULATION DENSITY AND URBANISATION. A large city near the epicentre multiplies casualties.

WEALTH AND PREPAREDNESS. Wealthier societies fund monitoring networks, drills, trained rescue services, hospitals with backup power and insurance for rebuilding. Poorer communities have less of all of these, and recovery takes far longer because rebuilding competes with every other unmet need.

TIME OF DAY AND WARNING. A night-time earthquake catches people asleep indoors.

GROUND CONDITIONS. Settlement built on soft sediment or fill suffers more than settlement on bedrock.

Then reduction. PREDICTION remains unreliable — geologists can identify high-risk zones and estimate probabilities over decades, and they monitor foreshocks, ground tilt, groundwater levels and radon gas, but nobody can name a date. So the strategy is PREPAREDNESS. Engineering: base isolators of rubber and steel that let the ground move while the building stays comparatively still, cross-bracing, deep foundations anchored to bedrock, lighter roofs and flexible frames. Planning: land-use zoning that keeps hospitals and schools off active faults and off unstable ground, and building codes that are actually inspected. People: regular drills, the drop-cover-and-hold-on routine, emergency kits with water and a torch, knowing how to shut off gas, and a family meeting point. Systems: seismometer networks that give seconds of warning ahead of the surface waves — enough to stop trains and close gas valves — and, for coasts, tsunami warning systems.

The final questions of this part are in front of you now: primary and secondary effects separated, the vulnerability factors listed, and preparedness set out under engineering, planning and people.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same shaking from a bent ruler, a thunderstorm and two ways of scoring the same song.

## Subtopic: Bending the Ruler Until It Goes

Hold a plastic ruler at both ends and start bending it. It does not break immediately. It bends, and it stores your effort inside itself — you can feel it pushing back against your hands. Bend further. Further. Then SNAP: the ruler breaks and both halves whip back straight, and your hands feel the jolt.

That is an earthquake, exactly. Plates push against each other, but the fault is stuck fast by friction, so the rock just bends and stores the energy — sometimes for hundreds of years. Then one day the rock cannot hold any more, the fault slips, the rock snaps back toward its old shape, and all the stored energy leaves at once as shaking. Geographers call it elastic rebound. The rock does not "explode"; it springs back.

Two words come out of this picture and the exam always wants both. The FOCUS is the point underground where the snap actually happened. The EPICENTRE is the spot on the surface directly above it — if the snap happened three kilometres beneath a soccer field, the epicentre is the middle of that field.

And here is why the focus depth matters so much. Shout from the bottom of a deep mine shaft and people at the top barely hear you; shout from just below the surface and they hear you clearly. A shallow earthquake, less than seventy kilometres down, delivers its energy almost undiluted to the surface, and those are the killers. The deep ones, hundreds of kilometres down, arrive tired.

One more thing to expect: the aftershocks. The ground keeps settling for days or weeks afterwards, and those smaller shakes finish off buildings that the first one only cracked. That is why nobody is allowed back into a damaged building straight away.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the ruler are coming to you right now: what gets stored and what gets released, focus against epicentre, and why shallow is worse.

## Subtopic: Thunder, Lightning and Three Friends with Phones

When the energy leaves the focus it travels as three different waves, and they do not run at the same speed.

First to arrive is the P-wave. It is a push-and-pull, a squeeze travelling forward like the bump you send down a stretched spring. It is the fastest, and it goes through anything — rock, water, air.

Second is the S-wave. It shakes sideways, the way a ripple runs along a skipping rope when you flick it. It moves at about half the speed of the P-wave, and it cannot travel through liquid at all.

Last is the surface wave, which rolls along the ground rather than through it. It is the slowest — and it is the one that wrecks things, because it swings the ground furthest.

Now the clever part. Think about a thunderstorm. You see the flash and then you wait for the bang, and the longer the wait, the further away the storm is. Same trick here. The P-wave and the S-wave leave the focus at the same instant, but the P-wave gets there first. So the GAP between them tells you how far you are from the earthquake. A short gap means it was close; a long gap means it was far.

But a distance is not a position. If you know you are two hundred kilometres from the earthquake, it could be north of you, south of you, anywhere on a big circle around you. So we use three stations. Each one draws its own circle, and there is only one point where all three circles cross. That point is the epicentre. Three friends with phones, three circles, one answer — that is triangulation.

## Subtopic: Two Ways to Score the Same Song — and Staying Alive

People mix up Richter and Mercalli constantly, so use this picture and you never will.

Imagine a sound system at a stadium. RICHTER is the size of the amplifier — one number, decided at the source, the same no matter where you are standing. MERCALLI is how loud it actually sounded where YOU were: deafening at the front, background noise on the hill outside, silent three suburbs away. One earthquake, one Richter number, but a different Mercalli number in every town.

Richter has one habit that traps people. Each step up the scale is not one bit bigger — it is ten times bigger in ground movement and about thirty-two times more energy. So a 7 next to a 5 is not "a bit worse". Two steps means ten times ten, a hundred times more shaking, and thirty-two times thirty-two, about a thousand times more energy. Never write "slightly stronger" about a two-point difference.

Mercalli runs from I to XII and is written in Roman numerals, because it is based on what people saw and felt: hanging lights swinging, then windows cracking, then chimneys falling, then whole buildings down.

Now the part that saves lives, and the reason this topic is in the syllabus at all. Earthquakes do not kill people; falling buildings do. Two places can get exactly the same shaking and lose completely different numbers of people, and the difference is not luck. It is whether the houses were built properly and inspected. Whether the town sits on solid rock or on soft filled-in ground that wobbles like jelly. Whether there is money for rescue teams, hospitals and rebuilding. Whether anyone practised what to do.

Nobody can tell you the date of the next earthquake — that is honestly beyond science right now. So the whole game is being ready. Engineers put big rubber-and-steel pads under buildings so the ground can move while the building stays fairly still, brace walls with diagonal steel, and anchor foundations into bedrock. Town planners keep schools and hospitals off the fault line and off soft ground. And ordinary people practise: drop, take cover under something solid, hold on; keep water, a torch and shoes near the bed; agree where the family will meet.
