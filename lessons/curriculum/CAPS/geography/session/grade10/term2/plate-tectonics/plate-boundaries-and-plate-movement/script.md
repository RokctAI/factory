# Part 1 — Expert

The continents are not fixed. They have moved, they are moving now, and their movement assembles every great mountain range and triggers almost every major earthquake on the planet. This session sets out the evidence for continental movement, identifies the engine that drives it, and works through the three kinds of plate boundary with the landforms each one manufactures — the framework on which folding, faulting, earthquakes and volcanoes all rest for the rest of this term.

## Subtopic: Continental Drift and the Evidence for It

In 1912 the German scientist Alfred Wegener proposed that all present continents were once joined in a single supercontinent, PANGAEA — "all the earth" — which began fragmenting about 200 million years ago into a northern mass, LAURASIA, and a southern mass, GONDWANA. South Africa sat deep inside Gondwana, wedged against what is now South America to the west and Antarctica to the south.

Wegener assembled four independent lines of evidence, and the syllabus expects all four by name.

The FIT of the coastlines. The Atlantic coast of South America nests into the western bulge of Africa with a precision no coincidence explains — and the fit improves when the true edge of the landmass, the CONTINENTAL SHELF, is matched rather than today's shoreline.

FOSSIL evidence. Mesosaurus, a small freshwater reptile that could not possibly have crossed an ocean, is found in Karoo rocks in South Africa and in identical rocks in Brazil and Paraguay. The seed fern Glossopteris, whose seeds were far too heavy to be blown across water, occurs in South Africa, South America, India, Australia and Antarctica. Lystrosaurus, a land reptile, turns up in the Karoo, in India and in Antarctica.

GEOLOGICAL evidence. Rock sequences and mountain chains stop at one coast and resume at another. The CAPE FOLD MOUNTAINS continue as the Sierra de la Ventana in Argentina and into the Ellsworth Mountains of Antarctica — the same rocks, the same age, the same folding event.

CLIMATIC evidence. The Dwyka tillite at the base of the Karoo Supergroup is rubble dumped by a continental ice sheet, in a country that now bakes. Glacial deposits of the same age lie in South America, India and Australia, and the scratch marks the ice carved radiate outward from a single centre once the continents are reassembled. Meanwhile coal, which forms in warm swamps, is found in Antarctica.

Wegener's weakness was mechanism: he could not say what moved continents, and his theory was dismissed for half a century. The questions for this section are with you now — the four categories of evidence, one worked example of each, and the reason the idea was rejected.

## Subtopic: The Engine — Convection Currents and Sea-Floor Spreading

The mechanism Wegener lacked lies in the mantle. Heat from radioactive decay, together with heat left over from the planet's formation, keeps the deep interior at thousands of degrees. That heat drives CONVECTION CURRENTS in the plastic asthenosphere: hot, less dense mantle rock rises slowly toward the lithosphere, spreads sideways beneath it, cools, becomes denser and sinks again — a closed circulation cell turning at roughly the speed at which fingernails grow.

Word this carefully, because it is a marking point: the mantle is SOLID rock that flows plastically. It is not a lake of lava, and its currents move centimetres per year.

The lithosphere — crust plus rigid upper mantle, about 100 kilometres thick — is broken into some fifteen major TECTONIC PLATES riding on that circulation. Two forces do most of the actual work: RIDGE PUSH, the gravitational sliding of newly formed, elevated crust away from a spreading ridge, and SLAB PULL, the weight of a cold dense plate sinking into the mantle and hauling the rest of the plate behind it.

The decisive proof came from the ocean floor. In the 1960s Harry Hess proposed SEA-FLOOR SPREADING: molten material rises along a mid-ocean ridge, solidifies and becomes new oceanic crust, carried away from the ridge on both sides. Two observations confirmed it. First, AGE: the sea floor is youngest at the ridge and grows systematically older toward the continents, and nowhere on earth is it older than about 200 million years, while continental rock approaches four billion. Second, PALAEOMAGNETISM: the earth's magnetic field reverses at irregular intervals, and iron minerals in cooling lava lock in whichever direction prevailed as they solidified. The result is magnetic stripes running parallel to the ridge and MIRRORED symmetrically on both sides of it — a tape recording of spreading.

The Atlantic widens at roughly two to five centimetres a year; over 200 million years that is an ocean. Take this section's questions now: convection stated correctly, ridge push distinguished from slab pull, and the two proofs of sea-floor spreading.

## Subtopic: Divergent and Transform Boundaries

A plate boundary is classified by RELATIVE MOVEMENT — apart, together, or past. Take the two simpler cases first.

DIVERGENT boundaries are also called CONSTRUCTIVE, because crust is created there. Beneath an ocean, rising magma builds a MID-OCEAN RIDGE: a submarine mountain chain with a narrow rift groove along its crest where the plates part. Eruptions are FISSURE eruptions of runny basaltic lava building gentle shield volcanoes; where the ridge rises above sea level it makes islands — Iceland, and in the South Atlantic, Tristan da Cunha. Earthquakes are frequent but SHALLOW and moderate, because the crust here is thin, hot and weak.

On a continent, divergence produces a RIFT VALLEY on a grand scale. The EAST AFRICAN RIFT runs some 6 000 kilometres from the Afar region of Ethiopia southward toward the Zambezi. Build the picture in your head: the crust stretches, two long parallel faults form, and the whole strip of land between them sinks like a lift, leaving a broad flat valley floor walled by steep fault scarps hundreds of metres high. Long, deep, narrow lakes fill the floor — Tanganyika and Malawi — block mountains stand on the shoulders, and volcanoes rise along the fractures, Kilimanjaro and Mount Kenya among them. Continue the process and the rift floods: the Red Sea is exactly this, one stage further on.

TRANSFORM boundaries are also called CONSERVATIVE, because crust is neither created nor destroyed. Two plates slide horizontally past each other along a great fault — the San Andreas Fault in California, where the Pacific plate grinds north-westward past the North American plate. There are no volcanoes, because nothing sinks and nothing melts, and no fold mountains either. The landform signature is a long, remarkably straight fault line with features offset sideways where they cross it — streams bent, fences and roads displaced by metres. But the earthquakes are severe: the plates lock by friction, strain accumulates for decades, and its sudden release produces shallow, violent shaking directly beneath settlements.

Constructive and conservative dealt with — destructive comes next. Pause for this section's questions: both boundary types with their alternative names, the landforms of each, and why one has volcanoes and the other has none.

## Subtopic: Convergent Boundaries and Their Landforms

At a CONVERGENT — destructive — boundary, plates move toward one another and lithosphere is consumed. Three cases, distinguished by what is colliding.

OCEANIC MEETS CONTINENTAL. Oceanic crust is denser, about 3,0 grams per cubic centimetre against 2,7, so it SUBDUCTS beneath the continent along a sloping plane of earthquake foci that deepen inland. The landform set is fixed: a deep OCEAN TRENCH offshore; a chain of FOLD MOUNTAINS thrust up on the continental edge by compression; and COMPOSITE VOLCANOES along that chain, fed by magma generated where water driven out of the sinking slab lowers the melting point of the mantle above it. The Nazca plate diving beneath the South American plate built the Peru-Chile Trench and the Andes from one process.

OCEANIC MEETS OCEANIC. The older, colder, denser plate subducts beneath the younger, giving a trench — the Mariana Trench, deepest place on earth at about eleven kilometres — and an ISLAND ARC, a curved chain of volcanic islands built up from the sea floor. Japan and the Philippines are island arcs.

CONTINENTAL MEETS CONTINENTAL. Neither plate sinks, because both are light, buoyant continental crust, so the lithosphere has nowhere to go but upward and the rock crumples into the highest FOLD MOUNTAINS on earth. India has been driving into Eurasia for roughly 50 million years and is still advancing; the Himalayas are still rising. Note the point examiners test: continental collision produces enormous mountains and powerful earthquakes but essentially NO volcanoes, because no slab descends to generate magma.

Plot the world's earthquake and volcanic belts and they trace these boundaries exactly: the RING OF FIRE encircling the Pacific, the belt from the Mediterranean through Iran to the Himalayas, and the mid-ocean ridges. South Africa lies near the middle of the African plate, far from any boundary — which is why the country records few natural earthquakes, and why most tremors felt in Gauteng and the Free State are induced by deep mining rather than by tectonics.

Three traps: a plate is lithosphere, not crust alone; subduction follows DENSITY, not size; and continental collision has no volcanic activity. The final questions of this part are in front of you now — the three convergent cases with their landforms, and the world pattern explained.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same moving earth from a torn newspaper, a pot on the stove, and three taxis in a rank.

## Subtopic: The Torn Newspaper

Tear a page of the Sunday paper in half and hand the halves to two different people. Years later both pieces turn up. How do you prove they were once one page? Not by the ragged edge alone — you check whether the SENTENCES run across the tear. If a headline continues and a photograph completes, the argument is over.

That is exactly the case for the continents. The ragged edge is the coastline fit: South America slots neatly into the bulge of Africa. But the real proof is the sentences running across.

The fossils. A small reptile called Mesosaurus lived in fresh water and could no more swim the Atlantic than you could. Its bones lie in the Karoo, and identical bones lie in Brazil. A plant called Glossopteris had heavy seeds that no wind carries far, and it grew in South Africa, South America, India, Australia and Antarctica. Same sentence, both halves of the page.

The rocks. Stand in the Cape Fold Mountains near Worcester and you are standing in a range that carries on — same age, same squeeze, same rock — in Argentina and in Antarctica. Mountain chains do not stop at the sea and restart three thousand kilometres away by accident.

The ice. At the base of the Karoo rocks lies the Dwyka tillite: rubble dumped by a glacier. An ice sheet, in the Northern Cape. And the scratches that ice carved point outward from a centre that sits nowhere sensible — until you push the continents back together, and then every scratch mark agrees.

Wegener said all of this in 1912 and was laughed at for one reason: he could not say what pushed. Your questions on the evidence are with you now — the fit, the fossils, the rocks, the ice, and why nobody believed him.

## Subtopic: The Pot on the Stove

Put a pot of thick pap on the stove and watch it before it boils. The stuff at the bottom gets hot. Hot means lighter, so it rises up the middle. At the top it can go no further, so it spreads sideways across the surface, cools, gets heavy again, and sinks down the sides. Round and round: that is a convection current, and you can watch one in any kitchen.

The mantle is doing the same thing, unbelievably slowly. The heat comes from the core and from radioactive decay in the rock. The "pap" is solid rock that flows like extremely stiff toffee, moving about as fast as your fingernails grow.

Now lay a skin of cooled porridge on the surface. That skin is the lithosphere — the crust plus the hard top of the mantle, a hundred kilometres of solid plate. The circulation underneath drags the skin along. Where the current rises, the skin is pulled apart and new material fills the gap; where it sinks, the skin is dragged down with it.

Two extra shoves finish the job. New hot rock piles up high at a ridge, and gravity makes the plate slide off that hump — a push from behind. And once a cold heavy plate has started sinking, its own weight hauls the rest along, like a tablecloth sliding off a table when enough of it has gone over the edge. Push at the ridge, pull at the slab.

Best of all, the ocean floor kept the receipt. Rock at the ridge is brand new and gets steadily older the further you travel from it — never older than about 200 million years, while continents run to billions. And because the earth's magnetic field flips every so often, and cooling lava freezes in whichever direction was current at the time, the sea floor carries magnetic stripes running along the ridge and matching perfectly on both sides. A conveyor belt that printed its own receipt, twice.

Questions on the pot and the conveyor belt are coming to you right now — what makes the current turn, the two extra shoves, and how the sea floor proves the whole story.

## Subtopic: Three Ways Plates Meet

Everything plates do to each other is one of three moves, and a taxi rank teaches all three.

MOVE ONE: pulling apart. Two taxis reverse away from each other and a gap opens. Underground, molten rock fills that gap and hardens, so new land is being made — geographers call it constructive. Under the sea it builds a long ridge of underwater mountains with a groove down the middle, poking above the water at Iceland. On land it builds a rift valley, and ours is the biggest on earth: the East African Rift, from Ethiopia down toward the Zambezi. Picture the ground splitting along two long parallel cracks, the whole strip between them dropping like a lift, leaving a flat valley floor with steep cliff walls on either side, deep narrow lakes lying along it — Malawi, Tanganyika — and volcanoes standing on the cracks, Kilimanjaro among them.

MOVE TWO: crashing together. Destructive, because something gets destroyed. When a sea-floor plate meets a continent, the sea floor is HEAVIER, so it dives underneath: a deep trench out at sea, mountains folded up along the coast, volcanoes on the mountains. South America shows all three in one picture — trench offshore, Andes above, volcanoes on top. When two sea-floor plates meet, one dives under the other and a curved string of volcanic islands rises: Japan. And when two CONTINENTS meet, neither will go under, because both are too light, so the rock has nowhere to go but up. That is the Himalayas, still growing. Here is the exam gift: continent against continent gives huge mountains and big earthquakes but no volcanoes, because nothing is sinking to melt.

MOVE THREE: scraping past. Two taxis squeeze past each other in a narrow street, side against side. Nothing is made and nothing is destroyed, so it is called conservative — and there are no volcanoes at all. But the sides catch, pressure builds, and when they finally jerk free everything shakes. That is California's San Andreas Fault, and the giveaway on the ground is a long straight crack with roads, fences and even streams shifted sideways where they cross it.

Apart, together, past. Master those three and the rest of the term follows. The last questions of the lesson are with you now — the three moves, their proper names, the landforms each one makes, and the one that never gets volcanoes.
