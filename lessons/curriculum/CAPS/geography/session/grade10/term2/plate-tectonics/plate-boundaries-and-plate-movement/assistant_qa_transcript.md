**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it bothered a lot of us: if the mantle is solid rock, how can anything "flow" in it? Solid things don't circulate.

**Tutor:** They do, if you give them enough time and enough heat. Solid does not mean rigid forever; it means the material keeps its own shape rather than taking the shape of its container. Rock deep in the mantle sits at temperatures close to its melting point and under crushing pressure, and in that state the mineral grains slowly deform and slide past one another. Geologists call it plastic flow, and the ordinary example is a glacier: ice is unquestionably solid — you can walk on it — yet a glacier creeps downhill, bending and folding as it goes. Cold tar behaves the same way; so does a thick block of toffee left in the sun. The mantle flows at a few centimetres a year, which is meaningless in a lifetime and enormous over millions of years. Hold two things apart in your answer: the mantle is solid and flows plastically; magma, which is genuinely molten, exists only in localised pockets, mostly near plate boundaries. Writing "the plates float on molten mantle" loses marks every single year.

***

**Thandi:** Next: why does the heavier plate always dive under the lighter one? A heavy thing falling under a light thing feels obvious, but plates are lying flat next to each other, not stacked.

**Tutor:** The word to reach for is buoyancy, not weight. Both plates rest on the plastic asthenosphere below, and each floats on it at a level set by its density — exactly as a block of pine and a block of hardwood sit at different depths in the same water. Oceanic lithosphere is basaltic and dense, around three grams per cubic centimetre, and it gets colder and denser still as it ages away from the ridge. Continental lithosphere is granitic and light, around two comma seven, and it is thick and buoyant. When the two are pushed together, the dense one is already sitting lower and is nearly ready to sink on its own; the convergence simply tips it over the edge, and once its leading end is descending, its own weight drags the rest down — slab pull. The lighter continent cannot be forced under, because buoyancy pushes it back up. That is why continental collisions build mountains instead: neither plate will go down, so the rock has nowhere to go but up. It is density that decides, never size or speed.

**Thandi:** This one puzzled us. You said the East African Rift will one day become an ocean. How does a valley on dry land turn into a sea?

**Tutor:** By continuing to do exactly what it is already doing. Divergence stretches continental crust, and stretched crust thins and drops. Along the rift, blocks of land have already sunk between parallel faults, which is why the floor lies hundreds of metres below the shoulders on either side. As stretching goes on, that floor drops further and the crust beneath it thins until it is no longer continental in thickness at all; magma rising into the gap begins to make new basaltic crust — oceanic crust. At that point the rift is a narrow sea floor sitting below the level of the ocean, and the moment the sea finds a way in, it floods. That is not a prediction from theory alone: the Red Sea is the same rift a few million years further on, a long narrow sea with new oceanic crust down the middle, and the Afar region of Ethiopia is the exact hinge where the process is switching over right now. Run the film backwards and the Atlantic began as a rift valley through the middle of Gondwana, splitting Africa from South America. Every ocean starts as a crack in a continent.

**Thandi:** Last one: South Africa is nowhere near a plate boundary, so why do we still feel earth tremors here — and does that break the theory?

**Tutor:** It does not break it; it fills in a detail the simple map leaves out. Plate interiors are quieter, not silent. Three things happen away from boundaries. First, old fault lines from ancient collisions never fully heal, and stress transmitted across the plate can occasionally move them — the Ceres-Tulbagh earthquake of 1969 in the Western Cape was a genuine natural event on such a structure, and it did serious damage. Second, and this is the big one locally, most tremors felt on the Witwatersrand and in the Free State goldfields are mining-induced: removing rock from three kilometres underground changes the stresses around the void, and the rock adjusts suddenly. Those events are shallow, so people feel them strongly even when the energy released is modest. Third, the far southern rift structures reach further south than most maps show. So the honest answer in an exam is layered: South Africa lies in a stable plate interior, which is why it has no volcanoes and no great earthquakes; the tremors it does experience are mostly human-induced, with occasional natural events on ancient faults. That is the theory working exactly as it should — boundaries explain the world pattern, and local geology explains the exceptions.
