**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: how can solid rock possibly bend? Every rock I have ever picked up breaks when you bend it.

**Tutor:** Every rock you have picked up was cold, dry, small and at the surface — the four conditions that guarantee brittle behaviour. Take it down ten kilometres and everything changes. The temperature rises steadily with depth, so the rock is hot, though not molten. The weight of all the rock above squeezes it from every direction at once, which is called confining pressure, and material squeezed equally on all sides cannot open a crack, because a crack needs space to open into. And the force is applied unimaginably slowly — a few centimetres a year for millions of years. Under those three conditions the mineral grains inside the rock deform and creep past one another, and the rock takes a new permanent shape without ever fracturing. Geologists call it plastic deformation. The everyday parallel is a glacier: ice is undeniably solid, yet a glacier bends around a valley wall and folds visibly as it creeps. So when you see folded strata in a road cutting, remember you are looking at rock that was bent deep underground and only later lifted and stripped bare by erosion. It did not bend where you are standing.

**Thandi:** Next: hanging wall and footwall. I can never remember which is which, and the question always asks.

**Tutor:** Use the old miners' picture, because that is exactly where the words come from. Imagine a fault plane that is not vertical but tilted, and imagine yourself standing inside a tunnel driven along that tilted surface. The rock above your head is the block you would hang a lantern from — that is the HANGING wall. The rock under your boots is the block your feet rest on — that is the FOOTWALL. Lantern above, feet below. Now attach the movement. Tension stretches the crust, so the roof block slides DOWN the slope: hanging wall down, and that is a normal fault. Compression shortens the crust, so the roof block is shoved UP the slope: hanging wall up, and that is a reverse fault. Two more words while we are here: the vertical distance the rock has shifted is the throw, and the horizontal distance is the heave. If a question gives you a labelled cross-section, find the tilted fault plane first, name the two blocks, then say which way the hanging wall went — and you have identified the fault and its causal force in one sentence.

**Thandi:** This one confused a lot of us: you said an anticline can end up as a valley. If the rock was pushed UP, how does it become the low ground?

**Tutor:** Because being pushed up and staying up are different things, and erosion gets the final say. Think about what happens to material on the outside of a bend. Take a thick paperback and bend it: the pages on the outside of the curve are stretched, and if you bend far enough that outer surface splits. Rock on the crest of an anticline is in exactly that position — stretched over the arch, so it develops countless small cracks and joints. Cracks are where water gets in, where frost prises, where plant roots wedge, where chemical weathering works. So the arch is the weakest part of the whole structure, and rivers and weathering attack it hardest. Meanwhile the rock down in the syncline was compressed rather than stretched, so it is tightly packed and comparatively resistant, and it is also protected by lying lower. Over millions of years the crest is stripped away and the trough survives, so the finished landscape has a valley where the arch was and a ridge where the trough was. That is inversion of relief. The important idea underneath it is that landscape is always a contest between the structure that tectonic forces build and the erosion that immediately begins to take it apart — and in old mountains like the Cape ranges, erosion has had a quarter of a billion years to win arguments.

**Thandi:** Last one: South Africa has no plate boundary, so why do we have the Cape Fold Mountains at all — and could new mountains form here again?

**Tutor:** The mountains are there because the boundary used to be. Around 250 to 280 million years ago southern Africa was not in the middle of a plate: it lay near the southern edge of the assembling supercontinent, and compression from that collision crumpled the Cape Supergroup rocks into folds. The mountains are the fossil evidence of a plate margin that has since gone quiet. This is a general truth worth carrying into any answer: the tectonic setting of a place changes over geological time, so old structures record old boundaries. It also explains why the Cape ranges are lower and more rounded than the Andes or the Himalayas — those are still being pushed up, while the Cape ranges stopped growing long ago and have been eroding ever since. What survives is only the most resistant rock, the hard quartzitic sandstone, which is why the ridges are sandstone and the valleys are cut in softer shale. As for the future: on human timescales, no. South Africa sits deep inside the African plate, so it experiences no mountain building and no volcanic activity, and its occasional tremors come from ancient faults adjusting or from deep mining. But the African plate is splitting along the East African Rift, and if that split continues southward for tens of millions of years, the tectonic story of this subcontinent will open a new chapter — with tension, not compression, doing the work.
