# Part 1 — Expert

Plate movement supplies the force; this session traces what that force does to the rock it acts on. Rock that bends produces folds and fold mountains; rock that snaps produces faults, block mountains and rift valleys. Both processes are examined by name, by diagram description and by South African example — and the Cape Fold Mountains, which most of this country's wine and fruit is grown between, are the worked case.

## Subtopic: Compression, Tension and Shearing

Three tectonic forces act on rock, and each is tied to a plate boundary you already know.

COMPRESSION squeezes rock from opposite sides, shortening and thickening it. It operates at convergent boundaries. TENSION pulls rock apart from opposite sides, stretching and thinning it; it operates at divergent boundaries. SHEARING pushes rock in opposite directions along the same line, so that one side slides past the other; it operates at transform boundaries. Compression, tension, shearing — squeeze, stretch, slide.

Whether rock bends or breaks under those forces depends on three conditions, and this is the part learners most often omit.

DEPTH and TEMPERATURE. Deep in the crust, under heat and enormous confining pressure, rock behaves plastically: it deforms permanently without fracturing. Near the surface it is cold and brittle, so it snaps.

RATE. Force applied slowly, over millions of years, allows rock to creep and bend. Force applied suddenly exceeds the rock's strength and it fractures.

ROCK TYPE. Well-bedded sedimentary strata such as shale and sandstone bend comparatively readily; massive igneous rock such as granite tends to fracture.

There is a sequence in this. Rock first deforms ELASTICALLY, springing back if the force is removed; push further and it passes its ELASTIC LIMIT and deforms PLASTICALLY, keeping the new shape — that is folding; push further still, or push suddenly, and it FRACTURES, and if the two sides then move relative to each other you have a fault. Elastic, plastic, fractured, displaced — the same order that will explain earthquakes in the next lesson.

## Subtopic: Folds — Anticlines, Synclines and the Fold Family

FOLDING is the bending of rock strata under compression. Picture a cross-section — a vertical slice through the ground, as though the earth had been cut with a bread knife and you are looking at the cut face. Horizontal layers of sedimentary rock, one above another like the layers of a cake, are squeezed from left and right. Having nowhere to go sideways, they buckle into a series of arches and troughs, like a rug pushed against a wall.

Name the parts. An ANTICLINE is an upfold — an arch, with the layers curving upward to a CREST and dipping away on both sides. A SYNCLINE is a downfold — a trough, with the layers sagging to a low point and rising on both sides. The sloping sides of any fold are its LIMBS, and neighbouring anticlines and synclines share limbs. The imaginary line running along the highest points of an anticline is the AXIS, and the plane containing it is the AXIAL PLANE. One diagnostic detail: in an anticline the OLDEST rocks are exposed at the centre once erosion cuts in, because the deepest layers are lifted highest.

Increase the compression and the fold family develops in order. A SIMPLE or SYMMETRICAL fold has limbs dipping at equal angles either side of a vertical axis. An ASYMMETRICAL fold has one limb steeper than the other, the axial plane leaning. An OVERFOLD has one limb pushed beyond the vertical so it overhangs. A RECUMBENT fold lies almost flat, folded right over on itself. And when the pressure exceeds what the rock can absorb, the fold snaps along its axis and one limb is driven over the other: an OVERTHRUST FOLD, or nappe — the point where folding becomes faulting.

The great landform product is FOLD MOUNTAINS: long, parallel ranges running at right angles to the direction of compression, with valleys between them. But do not assume the anticlines are always the mountains. Rock at the crest of an anticline is stretched over the arch and therefore cracked and weakened, so weathering and erosion attack it hardest; rock in a syncline is compressed and tightly packed. Given time, the anticline may be worn into a valley while the syncline stands up as a ridge. That is INVERSION OF RELIEF, and it is a favourite higher-order question.

## Subtopic: Faults and the Landforms They Make

A FAULT is a fracture in the crust along which rock has MOVED. Fracture alone is a joint or a crack; the movement makes it a fault.

Name the parts, because labelling questions use them. The FAULT PLANE is the surface along which movement occurs. Where that plane is inclined, the block resting above it is the HANGING WALL and the block beneath it is the FOOTWALL. THROW is the vertical displacement, HEAVE the horizontal displacement. The side that ends up higher is the upthrow side; the side that ends up lower is the downthrow side.

Three fault types, each produced by one of the three forces.

A NORMAL fault is produced by TENSION. The crust is stretched, and the hanging wall slides DOWN relative to the footwall. On the surface it leaves a FAULT SCARP: a steep, remarkably straight step in the landscape, often running for many kilometres.

A REVERSE fault is produced by COMPRESSION. The crust is shortened, and the hanging wall is forced UP over the footwall. Where the fault plane is at a low angle and rock is pushed a long distance over the top of other rock, it is called a THRUST fault.

A SHEAR fault — also called a tear or strike-slip fault — is produced by SHEARING. Movement is horizontal, with almost no vertical throw, so features crossing the fault are offset sideways. The San Andreas Fault is the type example.

Now the landforms, built from combinations. Where two roughly parallel normal faults form and the block between them drops, the result is a GRABEN, seen at the surface as a RIFT VALLEY — a wide, flat-floored valley with steep fault scarps on both sides, the shape of the East African Rift. Where the block between two faults instead stands proud, either because it was pushed up or because the land on either side dropped, the result is a HORST, seen as a BLOCK MOUNTAIN: a mountain with steep straight sides and a flattish top, quite unlike the folded, curved profile of a fold mountain. The Ruwenzori Mountains on the Uganda-Congo border are a horst standing in the East African Rift. And where a series of parallel faults each drop a little further, the land descends in a giant staircase — STEP FAULTING. Tilted blocks give a third profile: one steep faulted face and one long gentle back slope.

## Subtopic: The Cape Fold Mountains — and Describing Structures in Words

South Africa's own worked example is the CAPE FOLD MOUNTAINS, and every element of the topic appears in them.

The rocks are the Cape Supergroup, laid down as sand and mud in a shallow sea and hardened into extremely resistant quartzitic sandstone. Roughly 250 to 280 million years ago, while Gondwana was being assembled, compression from the south crumpled these strata into a magnificent series of folds. The result is exactly what theory predicts: long, narrow, parallel ranges separated by long, narrow valleys. Two trends meet in the Western Cape — one set running roughly north to south, including the Cederberg and the Hex River Mountains, and another running roughly west to east, including the Langeberg, the Swartberg and the Outeniqua — and they converge in the tangle of ranges around Ceres and Worcester.

Read the human geography off the structure. The valleys between the folds are sheltered, have deep soils washed down from the ridges, and are watered by rivers rising in the mountains: the Hex River Valley grows table grapes, the Breede River Valley wine, the Langkloof deciduous fruit. The passes that cross the ranges — Bain's Kloof, Du Toitskloof, the Swartberg Pass — exist because the folded ridges are genuine barriers, and the road pattern of the Western Cape still bends around them. The Cape's mountains are old and no longer growing, so erosion has had ample time to work, and in several places the crests of anticlines have been stripped away while synclines stand as high ground.

Now the skill that carries the marks: describing structures in words, since an examiner may give a description and ask for the term, or the reverse. Build every answer in the same order — the force, the response, the shape, the landform. For a rift valley: tension pulled the crust apart, two parallel normal faults formed, the central block subsided, leaving a flat valley floor with steep straight scarps facing each other across it. For a block mountain: faulting on both sides left a raised block with steep, straight edges and a level summit, unlike a fold mountain's curved crest. For an anticline: compression buckled the strata upward into an arch, the oldest rocks lie at the core, and the limbs dip away on either side.

## Subtopic: Push the Rug, Pull the Rug

Put a rug flat on a smooth floor and push one end toward the other. It rides up into ridges and hollows — ridge, hollow, ridge, hollow — running across the direction you pushed. You have just made fold mountains. That push is COMPRESSION, and it happens wherever two plates are moving toward each other.

Now grab both ends of the rug and pull. It stretches, thins, and eventually tears. That pull is TENSION, and it happens where plates move apart. And if you slide one half of the rug forward while the other stays put, that is SHEARING — the sideways scrape at a transform boundary.

Squeeze, stretch, slide. Three forces, three boundaries, and they cause everything in this lesson.

So why does rock sometimes bend like the rug and sometimes snap like a biscuit? Three things decide. How deep it is — deep down the rock is hot and squeezed from all sides, so it behaves like warm Prestik and bends; near the surface it is cold and brittle and it cracks. How fast the force comes — press a slab of chocolate slowly with warm hands and it sags; hit it and it shatters. And what the rock is made of — layered rocks like sandstone and shale bend far more willingly than a solid lump of granite.

Bending is called FOLDING. Snapping and then sliding is called FAULTING. Same forces, different rock conditions, completely different landscapes.

## Subtopic: Hills, Hollows and the Surprise Valley

Back to the rug, and now we name the bumps.

Every up-bump is an ANTICLINE. Picture an arch, or the shape of a bridge, or your hand held palm-down with the fingers curved to the table. Every down-dip between two arches is a SYNCLINE — a trough, a valley shape, a smile instead of a frown. The sloping sides of any of them are called LIMBS, and two neighbouring folds share a limb, so the picture is one continuous wavy line: up, down, up, down.

Push harder and the folds get more dramatic, in a fixed order worth memorising as a story. Push gently and both sides slope equally — a simple fold. Push harder from one side and it leans over — asymmetrical. Push harder still and the top flops over past upright so it overhangs — an overfold. Keep going and it lies down flat — recumbent. Push beyond what the rock can take and it tears along its own axis and one half rides over the other — an overthrust. Gentle, leaning, overhanging, lying down, torn. That's the whole family.

Now the twist that examiners love, and it is genuinely surprising. You would expect the arch to be the mountain and the trough to be the valley. Often, in the end, it is the other way round. Think about bending a stack of newspaper: the outside of the bend gets stretched and starts to crack. That is exactly what happens on top of an anticline — the rock is stretched over the arch, so it is full of cracks, and rain, frost and rivers go to work on it. Meanwhile the rock in the syncline was squashed tight. Give it a few million years and the arch has been worn down into a valley while the trough stands up as a ridge. Geographers call it inversion of relief — the landscape ends up upside down compared to the structure.

## Subtopic: The Broken Chocolate Slab

Take a slab of chocolate and snap it. Now slide the pieces around, and you have every fault landform there is.

Snap it and pull the two halves apart, and one piece drops lower than the other along the break. That drop is a NORMAL fault, caused by TENSION, and in the landscape it leaves a long, dead-straight step called a fault scarp — the kind of unnaturally straight cliff that tells a geographer immediately that a fault runs along it.

Snap it and push the halves together, and one piece rides up over the other. That is a REVERSE fault, caused by COMPRESSION. And if you slide the halves past each other sideways without either rising, that is a SHEAR fault — the San Andreas kind, where the fences and streams crossing it get shifted sideways.

Now break it in two places instead of one, and watch what the middle piece does. If the middle piece drops between the two breaks, you get a wide flat-bottomed valley with steep straight walls on both sides — a rift valley. Stand on the floor of the East African Rift and that is exactly what you see: flat ground running away in front of you and a long straight wall of cliff on your left and another on your right, with a deep lake lying between them. If instead the middle piece stands up while the outer pieces drop, you get a block mountain: steep straight sides and a flat top, like a table. And if you make many breaks, each one dropping a bit further, the land steps down like a staircase.

Here is how to spot the difference in real life. Fold mountains are curved and rounded, running in long parallel waves — drive from Worcester through the Hex River Valley and you have mountains on your left, mountains on your right, a fertile valley of vineyards down the middle, and the same pattern repeating for a hundred kilometres. That is folding, from a squeeze that happened while Gondwana was being built, in rock so hard it has survived a quarter of a billion years. Block mountains, by contrast, look like something was cut with a knife: straight edges, steep faces, flat tops.

Curved and wavy means folded. Straight and stepped means faulted. The final questions of the lesson are with you now — the three ways a broken slab can move, the rift valley and the block mountain described, and the one look that tells folding from faulting.
