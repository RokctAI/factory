**Student:** The wind direction thing breaks my brain. If the arrow flies INTO the circle from the north-west, why isn't it called a south-easterly? It's going toward the south-east!

**Thandi:** You are 100% right about the geometry — and that's exactly why the naming feels backwards! Here's the convention: winds are named after where they come FROM, not where they're heading. Think of how we talk about people: "she's from Durban" tells you her origin. A north-westerly wind is a wind *from* the north-west. Why do we care about origin? Because origin tells you what the wind is carrying — a north-westerly ahead of a front carries warm, moist air; a south-westerly behind it drags in cold polar air. Name the source, know the cargo.

**Student:** Why does a HIGH pressure mean nice weather? I'd have guessed high pressure squashes clouds into rain.

**Thandi:** Great guess, but the secret is in which way the air moves vertically. In a high, air slowly *sinks* toward the ground. Sinking air gets compressed and warms up — and warm air can hold *more* moisture, so instead of forming clouds, it evaporates them away. Result: clear, settled weather. In a low, air *rises*, cools as it climbs, can't hold its moisture anymore, and the moisture condenses into cloud and rain. Just remember: sink = clear, rise = rain. Two words each!

**Student:** How do I know if the cold front has already passed a station or is still coming? The question gave me one station and I froze.

**Thandi:** Use the three-witness test — any station carries three witnesses that all tell the same story. Witness one: wind direction. North-westerly = front still coming; south-westerly = front has passed. Witness two: temperature. Mild (like 18°) = ahead of the front; suddenly cold (8-10°) = behind it. Witness three: pressure — falling as the front approaches, rising after it passes. In our lesson's station: NW wind, 18 degrees, humid and clouding over — all three witnesses say "the front is on its way." Check all three, because one witness alone can occasionally mislead.

**Student:** What even is dew point? I know the definition but I don't get what it's FOR.

**Thandi:** Forget the definition for a second — dew point is a *moisture meter* in disguise. The real move is always comparing it to the air temperature. Picture the air as a sponge: the dew point tells you the temperature at which the sponge becomes completely full. If the air is at 18° and the dew point is 16°, the air only needs to cool 2 degrees to overflow — that sponge is already nearly saturated, so expect mist, cloud or rain soon. If the dew point were 3°, the sponge is mostly dry — clear skies. So never report dew point alone in an answer: report the *gap*. Small gap, wet story; big gap, dry story.
