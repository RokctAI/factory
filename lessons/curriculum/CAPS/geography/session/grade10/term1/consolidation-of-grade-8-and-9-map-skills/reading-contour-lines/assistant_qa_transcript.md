**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it is the classic: I can never remember whether the V in the contours points upstream or downstream. Is there something better than a memory trick?

**Tutor:** There is, and it starts with understanding why the V exists at all. A contour line is not allowed to gain or lose height — it stays at, say, 840 metres for its whole length. Now send it along a hillside until it meets a valley. The floor of that valley is lower than 840 metres, so the line cannot cross straight over; it would have to drop. Instead it turns and runs up the valley, hugging one side, until it reaches the place where the far bank has climbed back to 840 metres. It crosses there and comes back down the other side. Seen from above, that detour makes a V, and the point of the V sits at the furthest place upstream the line had to travel — so the V points towards the high ground, upstream. The stream itself runs the other way, out through the open mouth of the V. And you never have to trust the memory at all, because the contour numbers settle it: trace the river and read the values it crosses. If they run 840, then 820, then 800, you are moving downstream. Water cannot flow uphill, and the numbers do not forget.

***

**Thandi:** Next one is about steepness, but with a twist. Someone found two contour lines that looked like they were touching, and she was convinced her map was printed badly. Was it?

**Tutor:** Almost certainly not — she had found a cliff, and the map was working exactly as it should. Hold on to what the gap between two lines actually measures. The height difference is fixed at 20 metres by the contour interval, so the only thing the gap can tell you is the horizontal distance you must travel to gain those 20 metres. A wide gap means a long, easy walk for 20 metres of climbing — a gentle slope. A narrow gap means the same 20 metres in a short walk — steep. Now push that to the extreme. At a cliff the land goes up almost vertically, so you gain 20 metres with practically no horizontal travel at all, and the two lines are pressed against one another until they appear to merge. On a truly vertical face the mapmaker draws them as a single thickened line with a special symbol. So the correct response is to name the feature and quote the evidence: this is a cliff or near-vertical slope, shown by contour lines packed so tightly that the 20 metre rise occupies almost no horizontal distance.

***

**Thandi:** Here is one that lost somebody marks. She was asked whether a slope was concave or convex and she guessed. How do you actually tell, and does it matter?

**Tutor:** It matters, because examiners attach a consequence to it that you can be asked about directly. Build the picture as a walk. On a concave slope the contour lines are crowded near the top and spread out lower down, which means the climb is severe as you leave the summit and eases as you approach the foot — imagine leaving a cliff-edge and finishing on gentle ground. Because the ground falls away sharply first and then flattens in front of you, someone standing on the summit can see the entire slope right down to its base. On a convex slope the opposite holds: the lines are spread out near the top and crowded near the bottom, so you set off across gentle ground and the slope steepens as you descend, like the shoulder of a dome. That gentle upper ground bulges into your line of sight, so from the summit the lower part of the slope is hidden — a blind spot, exactly like the way the bonnet of a car hides the road immediately in front of it. So read the spacing, name the shape, and then state the visibility, because that is usually where the second mark lives.

***

**Thandi:** Last one, and it is a complaint about marking. She identified the steepest slope correctly, pointed at the right place, and still only got half the marks. What was missing?

**Tutor:** The evidence. In mapwork, identifying a feature is only ever half of the answer; the other half is proving it from the map itself. A bare "the steepest slope is here" is a claim with nothing under it, and a marker cannot separate a learner who reasoned it out from one who guessed. The full-mark version has three moves. Name the place precisely: on the western side of the hill marked by spot height 913. Quote the map evidence: the contour lines from 820 through to 880 are packed closer together there than anywhere else on the extract. Then explain what that evidence means: the land rises 60 metres over a very short horizontal distance, which is the steepest gradient on the sheet. Name it, quote it, explain it. That three-part shape works for every mapwork identification you will be asked to justify — valleys, spurs, saddles and cliffs included — and it is the single habit that separates a mapwork answer that scores from one that merely happens to be right.
