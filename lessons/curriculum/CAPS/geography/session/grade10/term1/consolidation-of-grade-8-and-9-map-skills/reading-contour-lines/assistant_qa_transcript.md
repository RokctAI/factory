**Student:** I can never remember if the V points upstream or downstream. Is there a trick that actually sticks?

**Thandi:** Here's the one that sticks for most people: the V points to where the river was BORN — up in the high ground. Rivers are born in the mountains and run away downhill, and the contour Vs all point back home. But honestly? The better habit is to not rely on memory at all: look at the numbers on the contours the river crosses. If you trace the river and the contours go 840, 820, 800 — you're walking downstream, no memory trick needed. Water never flows uphill, and the numbers never lie.

**Student:** What's the actual difference between a spot height and a contour line? Both give heights.

**Thandi:** Think of it as a crowd versus a name tag. A contour line speaks for thousands of points at once: "everyone on this line is at exactly 900 metres." But between lines, and above the highest ring, there's uncertainty — a summit inside the 900 contour could be 901 or 919, the contours can't say. A spot height is one point wearing a precise name tag: that little dot with 913 next to it says "THIS exact spot is 913 metres." So contours give you the shape of the land, and spot heights pin down the exact special points — usually summits.

**Student:** On my practice map, two contour lines looked like they were touching. Is that a mistake in the map?

**Thandi:** Almost certainly not a mistake — you found a cliff! Remember why: contours are 20 metres apart in height, and the horizontal gap between them shows how far you walk to climb those 20 metres. At a cliff, the land climbs 20 metres almost straight up — nearly zero walking distance — so the lines get squeezed until they practically touch. On a truly vertical cliff face they'd merge into one thick line. So when you see contours kissing, write "cliff / near-vertical slope" and quote the crowded spacing as your evidence. Free marks!

**Student:** The question asked me to "identify the steepest slope and justify". I pointed at the right place but only got half the marks. Why?

**Thandi:** Because in mapwork, the pointing is only ever half — the *evidence* is the other half! The marker wants to hear the reasoning in map language: "The steepest slope is on the western side of the hill at spot height 913, because the contour lines there are closest together — the land rises 20 metres over the shortest horizontal distance." See the three moves? Name the place, quote the spacing, explain what the spacing means. Do those three every time and the second half of the marks comes home with you.
