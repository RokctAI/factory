# Part 1 — Expert

Contour lines are the language a topographic map uses to describe the shape of the land, and mapwork — Question 3 on both Geography papers, every year — begins with reading that language fluently. This session works on a 1:50 000 map extract with a 20 metre contour interval. It builds from the definition of a contour line and the rules that govern it, to the principle that turns line spacing into gradient, to the V-shaped signature that identifies a river valley and separates it from a spur, and finally to a full landscape reading expressed in the language of evidence that markers reward.

## Subtopic: What a Contour Line Actually Is

A contour line is a line on a map joining all points that are the same height above sea level. Walk along a contour line in the real landscape and you would neither climb nor descend — you would stay level the whole way, tracing a path around the hillside.

On our 1:50 000 extract, the contour interval is 20 metres. That means each contour line is 20 metres higher or lower than its neighbours: 800, 820, 840, 860 and so on. The interval is fixed for the whole map — check the map key, because some maps use 10 metres or 50 metres, and every calculation you ever do starts from knowing the interval.

Three rules govern all contour lines, and they let you catch your own mistakes. First, contour lines never cross each other — a point cannot be two heights at once. Second, a contour line never simply ends in the middle of the map — it either closes into a loop or runs off the edge. Third, every fourth or fifth line is drawn thicker and labelled with its height — these index contours are your anchors when counting.

To find the height of any point between two contours, read the lines on either side. A point between the 820 and 840 lines is somewhere between 820 and 840 metres — closer to whichever line it lies nearer. And when contours enclose a summit, the map often marks the exact top with a dot and a number: a spot height. If our extract shows a spot height of 913, that hilltop stands at exactly 913 metres above sea level — more precise than the highest contour, which can only say "above 900".

The questions for this section are with you now. Answer them with the three rules in mind, and be ready to say precisely what a spot height adds that a contour line, by its nature, can never give you.

## Subtopic: Spacing Is Gradient — Steep and Gentle Slopes

Now the single most useful sentence in mapwork: the closer together the contour lines, the steeper the slope; the further apart, the gentler the slope.

Why must this be true? Reason it out — do not memorise it. Between any two neighbouring contours the land climbs exactly 20 metres; that is fixed by the interval. What varies is the horizontal distance you walk to gain those 20 metres. If the lines sit a kilometre apart on the map, you stroll a kilometre to rise 20 metres — a gentle slope. If the lines crowd within 100 metres of each other, you gain the same 20 metres in a tenth of the walking distance — a demanding climb. Same rise, shorter run, steeper gradient. Gradient is nothing more than rise compared with run, and contour spacing shows you the run at a glance.

So, to find the steepest slope on our extract: scan for the zone where contour lines bunch most tightly together — where 820, 840, 860 and 880 squeeze into the smallest space. That crowded staircase is your steepest slope. To defend your answer in an exam, say precisely this: "the contour lines are closest together here, so the land rises 20 metres over the shortest horizontal distance — the steepest gradient on the extract."

Learn to recognise the standard slope patterns too. Evenly spaced contours: a uniform slope — the same gradient the whole way down. Contours crowded near the TOP of the slope and spread out near the bottom: a CONCAVE slope — steep at the summit, easing towards the foot, and the whole slope is visible from the top because the ground falls away and then flattens. Contours spread out near the top and crowded near the BOTTOM: a CONVEX slope — gentle at first, then steepening, so the shoulder of the hill creates a blind spot and a person standing on the summit cannot see the foot of the slope. Examiners love asking you to name these from the spacing pattern alone, and to state the visibility consequence.

Take this section's questions now. Each one wants the reasoning rather than the slogan — that a fixed 20 metres of rise gained over a shorter horizontal distance is what "steep" actually means — and two of them turn on telling concave from convex by spacing.

## Subtopic: Finding the River Valley — the V Rule

Now the river valley. Water always flows downhill, and over time it carves the land into a valley. On a contour map, a valley announces itself with one unmistakable signature: contour lines bend into a V shape, and the V points uphill — toward the higher ground, upstream.

Why uphill? Picture a contour line trying to stay level as it meets a valley. The valley floor is lower than the land on either side, so to keep its height the line must detour up the valley, cross at the level point, and come back down the other side. That detour draws a V — and the tip of the V reaches toward the high ground where the river comes from.

So, on our extract: find the blue river line, or if none is printed, look for a chain of V-shaped kinks in successive contours. The V tips tell you the upstream direction; the river flows the opposite way, out of the open end of the Vs. Test yourself with the heights: the contours crossing the river must decrease in the direction of flow — a river cannot flow from 820 up to 840.

Beware the mirror-image trap: a spur. A spur is a ridge of high land jutting out between two valleys, and its contours also bend — but the bend points downhill, away from the summit. Valley: V points uphill, often with a river. Spur: the bulge points downhill, never with a river. Confusing these two is the most common contour error in Grade 10, and the cure is always the same: read the contour heights on either side and ask which way is up.

Put together, our extract now tells a story: the river valley threads between the hills, the spot height 913 crowns the summit, and the steep slope drops where the contours crowd.

This section's questions are in front of you. Keep the valley and the spur side by side as you answer them, and settle every case with the contour heights rather than with the shape alone — the shape can be mimicked, the numbers cannot.

## Subtopic: Reading the Whole Landscape — and the Exam Method

Let us assemble the full method you will use in Question 3, feature by feature.

A round hill: closed, roughly circular contours, values rising toward the centre, often a spot height at the top. A ridge: a long, narrow band of high ground — closed contours stretched like a loaf of bread. A saddle or col: the dip between two summits, where contours of the two hills face each other. A cliff: contour lines so close they almost touch — height gained with nearly no horizontal distance. A plain: hardly any contours at all, the land almost flat.

Now the exam method, five steps, in order. Step one: read the contour interval from the key — on our map, 20 metres. Step two: find the index contours and establish which direction the land rises. Step three: identify features by pattern — Vs for valleys, downhill bulges for spurs, closed rings for hills, crowded lines for steep slopes. Step four: confirm with heights — every claim you make must survive the question "do the numbers on the lines agree?" Step five: answer in the language of evidence: quote the spacing, quote the heights, name the feature.

Here is a model answer for our extract, the standard you should copy. "The river valley runs from the north-east to the south-west: the contour lines form V shapes pointing north-east, toward higher ground, so the river flows south-west. The steepest slope lies on the western side of the hill marked by spot height 913, where the 820 to 880 contours are closest together — the land rises 60 metres in a very short horizontal distance. The spot height tells us the exact summit altitude, 913 metres, which the contours alone could not give."

Every sentence names a feature, points to the evidence, and reads the heights. That is contour literacy — and next lesson in the mapwork sequence, gradient calculation, will turn this eye for spacing into an exact number. Practise on any 1:50 000 sheet you can find: the land will start rising off the paper at you.

The closing questions of this part are with you now. They range across the whole landscape — hills, ridges, saddles, cliffs and plains — and they reward the same three-part answer every time: name the feature, quote the spacing, read the heights.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same map read as a walk instead of a set of lines — same 20 metre steps, same hill with the 913 on top, same river, told the way you would describe the ground to somebody who has to hike it.

## Subtopic: Walking the Hill — What the Lines Really Are

Picture a koppie behind a township on a hot afternoon, and picture a footpath that goes around it rather than up it. You walk the whole way round and come back to where you started without ever climbing or dropping — your legs feel nothing, because you stayed at the same height the whole time. That path is a contour. That is all a contour line is: a level walk around the hill.

Now imagine the koppie wearing a stack of those paths, one above the other, each one exactly 20 metres higher than the one below it. From the side you would see a hill wrapped in rings, like a stack of tyres. From above — which is exactly what a map is — you would see those rings drawn as lines. That 20 metres between one ring and the next is called the contour interval, and it stays the same everywhere on one map, so once you know it, every line on that sheet tells you its height.

Once you see the lines as level paths, the rules stop being rules and become obvious. Two contour lines cannot cross, because a single patch of ground cannot be 820 metres and 840 metres at the same time. And a contour line cannot just stop in the middle of nowhere, because if you keep walking a level path around a hill you must eventually arrive back where you began — or walk right off the edge of the map. Nothing to memorise; just think about the walk.

One more thing on the map, and it is the friendliest symbol there is. Sometimes there is a little dot with a number beside it, like 913. That is a spot height, and it is the exact height of that one spot — usually the very top of the hill. The rings around it can only tell you the summit is somewhere above 900. The dot tells you it is exactly 913 metres. Think of the rings as a crowd all wearing the same badge, and the dot as one person wearing a name tag.

Good — the questions for this piece are with you now. Hold on to the picture of the level footpath while you answer them, because almost every question about contours is really a question about that walk.

## Subtopic: Squeezed Lines, Sore Legs

Here is the sentence that carries the most marks in the whole of mapwork, and here is why it is true rather than just something to learn.

Every step from one line to the next costs you the same 20 metres of climbing — the map has already decided that. What changes is how far you have to walk sideways to earn those 20 metres. Think of a shopping centre. There is a long, lazy ramp up to the parking, and there is a staircase. Both take you up one level. The ramp spreads that climb over a long stroll and you barely notice it. The staircase gives you the same height in a couple of metres of floor space, and your legs know all about it.

That is exactly what contour spacing shows you. Lines far apart mean the ramp: 20 metres of climb spread over a long walk, a gentle slope. Lines squeezed together mean the staircase: the same 20 metres gained in almost no distance at all, a steep slope. So when the exam asks where the steepest slope is, you are hunting for the place where the lines are most tightly packed — and when it asks you to justify your answer, you say the reason out loud: the lines are closest together there, so the land rises 20 metres over the shortest sideways distance.

Push that idea to its limit and you get a cliff. If the ground goes almost straight up, you gain 20 metres with hardly any sideways distance at all, so the lines get pressed against each other until they look as if they are touching. Learners often think that is a printing mistake. It is not — it is the map shouting "cliff".

And two slopes worth being able to name, because the walk feels different on each. On a concave slope the climb is brutal at the start and eases off near the top — the lines are crowded low down where you began and spread out higher up. Stand on the summit of one and you can see all the way down to the bottom, because the ground drops away and then flattens out in front of you. A convex slope is the opposite: gentle where you start at the top, then steepening as you go down — lines spread out near the top, crowded near the bottom. Stand on that summit and part of the slope hides from you behind the bulge of the hillside, the way the roof of a car hides the ground right in front of the bonnet. Farmers know that blind spot; so do search-and-rescue teams, and so should you.

Nice going — take the questions on this section now, and every time one asks about steepness, answer with the staircase and the ramp before you answer with the words.

## Subtopic: Finding the River Without Looking for Blue

Last piece, and it is the one that catches most people in the exam. You have to find a river valley, and the map is in black and white, or the blue line is too faint to see. Can you still find the water? Easily.

Think of a contour line as a very stubborn walker. She refuses to climb and refuses to descend — she stays at exactly 840 metres, always. Now she is walking along a hillside and she reaches a valley: a dip in the ground with a stream in the bottom of it. She cannot walk straight across, because the middle of that dip is lower than she is allowed to go. So what does she do? She turns and walks up into the valley, keeping to the side of it, until she reaches the point where the ground on the far side is back at 840. She crosses there and comes back out again.

Draw her path from above and it makes a V. And here is the important bit: the pointy end of that V is the furthest she walked up the valley — which means it points towards the high ground, back up towards where the stream is coming from. So the V points upstream. Water then runs out the open end of the V, downhill, away from the point.

Now the trap that costs marks. A spur is the opposite kind of ground: a nose of high land sticking out from a hill, with valleys either side. Our stubborn walker meeting a spur has to go around the outside of the nose, so her path bulges the other way — outwards, downhill, away from the summit. Same bending lines, opposite direction, and no water at the bottom, because water runs off a spur rather than along it.

If you never want to depend on remembering which is which, use the numbers instead, and this is the honest professional's method. Look at the contour values on either side of the bend. Whichever side has the higher numbers is uphill. If the bend points towards the higher numbers, it is a valley. If it points away from them, towards the lower numbers, it is a spur. Water cannot flow uphill and numbers do not forget, so the heights settle every argument. And when you trace a river across contours reading 840, then 820, then 800, you are walking downstream — no memory tricks required.

Those are the last questions of the lesson, and they are with you now. Take them with the stubborn walker in your head and the contour heights under your finger, and the shape of the land will come up off the map at you for the rest of the year.
