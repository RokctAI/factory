# Part 1 — Expert

Everything the atmosphere does — every wind, every cloud, every front that crosses the Cape in July — is driven by unequal heating. This session establishes the engine. Where the energy comes from, what happens to it on the way in, the four processes that move heat through the air, why greenhouse gases change the balance, and the five factors that decide the temperature of one place rather than another.

## Subtopic: Insolation and the Earth's Heat Budget

INSOLATION is INcoming SOLar radiATION — the short-wave energy the sun delivers to the earth. Track 100 units of it, because the syllabus expects the budget quantified.

Roughly 30 units never enter the system at all: they are REFLECTED straight back to space, chiefly by cloud tops, partly by dust and gas molecules in the atmosphere, and partly by bright surfaces — snow, ice, desert sand, the Karoo's pale soils. That reflected proportion is the ALBEDO. Fresh snow reflects most of what strikes it; a dark ocean or a forest reflects very little.

About 20 units are ABSORBED on the way through — by ozone in the stratosphere, by water vapour, dust and cloud in the troposphere. The atmosphere is a poor absorber of short-wave radiation, which is exactly the point.

That leaves about 50 units reaching and warming the EARTH'S SURFACE. The surface then radiates that energy back upward, but transformed: the earth is far cooler than the sun, so it emits LONG-WAVE infrared — TERRESTRIAL radiation. And here the atmosphere behaves in the opposite way: it is an excellent absorber of long-wave radiation. The energy that passed straight in is caught on the way out.

Now the budget itself. Over a year, the earth as a whole loses to space exactly as much energy as it receives — otherwise it would heat or cool without limit. But the balance holds only for the planet as a total. Between about 40 degrees north and 40 degrees south there is a permanent SURPLUS, and poleward of those latitudes a permanent DEFICIT. That imbalance is not corrected by radiation; it is corrected by TRANSFER — winds carrying warm air poleward, and ocean currents such as the Agulhas doing the same in water. Global circulation exists to settle an energy debt.

The questions for this section are with you now: the 30-20-50 split named and explained, albedo defined, short-wave in against long-wave out, and the surplus-deficit reason for global circulation.

## Subtopic: How Heat Moves — Radiation, Conduction, Convection, Advection

Four processes, and the examiner wants each defined and located.

RADIATION is energy travelling as electromagnetic waves, requiring no medium — the only way heat crosses the vacuum between sun and earth. Two kinds matter: incoming short-wave solar radiation, and outgoing long-wave terrestrial radiation.

CONDUCTION is transfer by direct contact, molecule to molecule. Air is a poor conductor, so this matters only in the lowest few metres, where air touching hot ground is warmed — and near the surface on a still night, where the ground chills the air lying on it.

CONVECTION is vertical transfer by the movement of the air itself. Air warmed at the surface expands, becomes less dense, and rises; cooler denser air sinks to replace it, and a CONVECTION CURRENT is established. This is how heat is carried up through the troposphere, and it is the mechanism behind afternoon thunderstorms.

ADVECTION is horizontal transfer by moving air. A sea breeze advecting cool air inland at Muizenberg on a summer afternoon, or a berg wind advecting hot dry air down to the coast, are both advection.

Now the single most important consequence in this whole topic. The atmosphere is heated FROM BELOW, not from above. Insolation passes largely through the air, warms the ground, and the ground then heats the air by conduction, convection and long-wave radiation. That is why temperature falls with height in the troposphere — you are moving away from the heat source — and it is the answer to a great many questions that appear to be about something else.

Take this section's questions now: the four processes defined, one example each, and the reason the atmosphere is heated from below.

## Subtopic: The Greenhouse Effect and Enhanced Warming

The mechanism, in strict order, because marks follow the sequence. One: short-wave solar radiation passes through the atmosphere with little absorption. Two: it is absorbed by the earth's surface, which warms. Three: the surface re-radiates the energy as LONG-WAVE terrestrial radiation. Four: greenhouse gases absorb that long-wave radiation. Five: they re-radiate it in all directions, including back down to the surface — COUNTER-RADIATION. The result is that the lower atmosphere is warmer than it would otherwise be.

The greenhouse gases: water vapour, the most abundant; carbon dioxide; methane; nitrous oxide; ozone; and CFCs. This is a NATURAL and necessary process — without it the global average surface temperature would be about minus 18 degrees Celsius rather than the roughly 15 degrees we live with. Thirty-three degrees of habitability.

The problem is the ENHANCED greenhouse effect: human activity adding greenhouse gases faster than natural sinks remove them. The sources are examinable — burning fossil fuels for electricity, industry and transport; deforestation, which removes the carbon sink and releases stored carbon; agriculture, especially livestock and flooded rice, releasing methane; landfills; and industrial processes such as cement manufacture. South Africa's own emissions are unusually carbon-intensive because coal dominates electricity generation.

The consequences for Africa, which the syllabus asks about specifically. Higher average temperatures, with the continent warming faster than the global average. Rainfall becoming less reliable and more variable, with longer droughts in the south and west — Cape Town's recent severe water restrictions are the shape of things to come. Crop yields falling for staples such as maize, threatening food security in a continent where a large share of households farm for subsistence. Sea-level rise pressing on coastal cities and low-lying settlements. Shifts in the range of disease vectors, notably malarial mosquitoes moving into higher and cooler areas previously free of them. And greater vulnerability overall, because adaptation costs money that many African economies do not have.

The precision that earns marks — and the questions on this section are in front of you now — is separating the natural greenhouse effect, which we depend on, from the enhanced greenhouse effect, which we caused.

## Subtopic: The Five Factors That Set Temperature

Why is Upington hot, Sutherland freezing and Durban humid and mild? Five factors, each with a mechanism.

LATITUDE. At the equator the sun's rays strike near vertically, so a given beam is concentrated on a small area and passes through the least atmosphere. Toward the poles the same beam strikes obliquely, spreads over a far larger area, and travels a longer, more absorbing path through the atmosphere. Concentration and path length together: temperature falls from equator to poles.

ALTITUDE. Temperature falls with height at roughly 6,5 degrees per 1 000 metres, because the atmosphere is heated from below and because thinner high air holds less water vapour and dust to absorb heat. Johannesburg sits near 1 750 metres and Durban at sea level, at almost the same latitude — and Johannesburg is distinctly cooler. Work the arithmetic when asked: 1 750 metres of ascent at 6,5 degrees per 1 000 metres gives a fall of about 11 degrees.

DISTANCE FROM THE SEA — CONTINENTALITY. Water heats and cools far more slowly than land, because it is transparent so radiation penetrates deeper, because currents and waves mix warmth downward, because evaporation consumes heat, and because water simply requires more energy per degree. So the sea moderates: coastal Durban has a small annual temperature range, while inland Upington and Kimberley swing violently between summer and winter. Learn the phrase "annual temperature range" — it is what the factor actually controls.

OCEAN CURRENTS. The warm Agulhas Current flows southward down our east coast, warming the air above it and supplying moisture — hence Durban's warmth, humidity and high summer rainfall. The cold Benguela Current flows northward up our west coast, chilling the air above it — hence Port Nolloth's cool, dry conditions, the fog that rolls onto the coast, and ultimately the Namib Desert. One country, two coasts, opposite currents: the classic South African comparison.

CLOUD COVER AND ASPECT complete the set. Cloud reduces daytime maxima by reflecting insolation and raises night minima by trapping outgoing radiation, so cloudy places have small daily ranges and clear desert skies produce enormous ones. Aspect matters in the southern hemisphere as the mirror of the north: NORTH-facing slopes receive more direct sun, so they are warmer and drier, which is why they carry different vegetation and why farmers site orchards accordingly.

The last questions of this part are with you now: the five factors, the mechanism behind each, and the two currents and their coasts kept the right way round.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same energy story from a hundred rand of sunshine, a pot on the stove, and a car parked in the sun in Polokwane.

## Subtopic: One Hundred Rand of Sunshine

Say the sun sends the earth one hundred rand every day. Follow the money.

Thirty rand never even gets spent — it bounces straight back off the tops of clouds, off dust in the air, off bright ground like snow and pale desert sand. That is the bounce-back, and geographers call it ALBEDO. Wear a white shirt on a Kimberley afternoon and you are raising your own albedo; wear black and you have lowered it.

Twenty rand gets taken on the way in — a small commission charged by the ozone, the water vapour, the dust and the clouds as the energy passes through them.

That leaves fifty rand landing on the ground. The ground is the one that actually gets paid — and the ground is therefore the one that gets warm.

Here is the twist that explains the whole topic. The ground does not keep the money. It spends it back upward as heat — but in a different currency. Sunlight comes in as short-wave; the earth is much cooler than the sun, so it sends its heat back out as long-wave. And the atmosphere, which barely noticed the short-wave passing in, grabs long-wave greedily on the way out.

One more thing about the budget. Across the whole planet the books balance every year — what comes in goes out. But not everywhere equally: the tropics collect a surplus and the poles run a shortfall, permanently. Winds and ocean currents exist to carry the surplus to where the shortfall is. Wind, in other words, is the planet settling a debt. Your questions on the money are up right now — the thirty, the twenty and the fifty, and why the currency changes on the way out.

## Subtopic: The Pot on the Stove

The atmosphere is heated from underneath, like a pot of pap on a stove — and once you see that, half of geography stops being confusing.

The stove plate is the ground. It is the ground that soaks up the sun, and the ground then warms the air above it. So the air nearest the plate is hottest, and the higher you go, the colder it gets. That is why there is snow on the Drakensberg while Durban is warm, and why climbers get colder as they get closer to the sun.

Now the four ways heat travels, in kitchen terms. RADIATION is standing next to a braai and feeling the heat on your face — nothing touches you and nothing carries it; the warmth arrives on its own. That is how the sun reaches us across empty space. CONDUCTION is grabbing the metal handle of the hot pot — touch passing heat straight along. Air is bad at this, so it only matters for the very lowest metres of air actually touching the ground. CONVECTION is the pap itself churning in the pot: hot stuff rises from the bottom, cooler stuff sinks to take its place, round and round. That circle is why hot afternoons on the Highveld build thunderclouds. And ADVECTION is heat carried sideways — the cool sea breeze pushing inland over Muizenberg at three in the afternoon, or a hot berg wind coming down off the plateau and roasting the coast in winter.

Four words: braai, handle, churn, breeze. Radiation, conduction, convection, advection. And the questions on the pot are with you now — the four movers, and the one big idea that the stove is under the pot, not above it.

## Subtopic: Why Upington Bakes and Sutherland Freezes

Same country, wildly different thermometers. Five reasons, and none of them is luck.

FIRST, the torch angle. Shine a torch straight down at a table and you get a small bright circle; tilt it and the same light smears across a wide dim patch. The equator gets the straight-down torch; the poles get the smear. That is latitude, and it also explains why summer beats winter.

SECOND, height. Climb and it cools, about six and a half degrees for every kilometre up. Johannesburg sits nearly two kilometres above the sea and Durban sits on it — almost the same distance from the equator, and about eleven degrees of difference just from the climb. Sutherland is higher still, which is a large part of why it records our coldest nights.

THIRD, the sea. Water is a slow cooker and land is a frying pan. The sea takes forever to warm and forever to cool, so the coast never goes to extremes — Durban is mild in July and not brutal in January. The deep interior has nothing to slow it down: Upington bakes in summer and Kimberley can frost in winter. The word for that swing between the seasons is the annual range, and the sea is what shrinks it.

FOURTH, the two currents — and this one is pure South Africa. Down the east coast runs the warm Agulhas, carrying tropical water south: warm, wet air, and Durban's humidity and summer storms. Up the west coast runs the cold Benguela: chilly, dry air, sea fog rolling into Port Nolloth, and, further north, the Namib Desert sitting beside an ocean. Two coasts, two currents, opposite weather.

FIFTH, cloud and which way the slope faces. Cloud is a blanket that works both shifts — it keeps the day cooler by blocking sun and the night warmer by holding heat in, so cloudy places have gentle days and clear Karoo skies give scorching days and freezing nights. And down here in the southern hemisphere it is the NORTH-facing slope that catches the sun — warmer, drier, and where the farmers put the orchards.

Torch, height, sea, currents, sky. Five reasons, one thermometer. The final questions of the lesson are coming to you now — the five factors, and the Agulhas and Benguela kept on their own coasts.
