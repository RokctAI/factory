**Thandi:** Class questions before we go! First one: if the sun is highest at twelve o'clock, why is the hottest part of the day around three in the afternoon? Should noon not be the peak?

**Tutor:** It would be, if the sun heated the air directly — but it does not, and the delay is the proof. Remember the chain: insolation warms the GROUND, and the ground then warms the air by conduction, convection and long-wave radiation. At noon the ground is receiving its maximum delivery, but it keeps receiving more energy than it loses for another two or three hours, so the surface — and the air fed by it — goes on warming after the sun has passed its peak. The maximum arrives only when outgoing finally catches incoming, typically between two and four in the afternoon. The same lag runs on the yearly calendar. The longest day in the southern hemisphere falls in late December, yet the hottest weeks in most of South Africa arrive in January and into February, because the land is still banking more heat than it spends. Write it as one principle: air temperature follows the surface's energy balance, not the sun's position, and it follows it with a lag. Any question about the timing of maximum temperatures — daily or seasonal — is answered by that sentence.

**Thandi:** Next: my gran says frost only comes on clear, still nights, and she is always right about it. What does she know that the textbook says in harder words?

**Tutor:** Your gran is running the whole radiation budget in her head. Two conditions, two mechanisms. Clear skies first: at night the ground loses heat as long-wave radiation, and cloud is the blanket that intercepts that loss and counter-radiates part of it back down. Strip the cloud away and the ground radiates straight out to space with nothing returned — the surface temperature drops fast and far. Still air second: on a windy night, moving air keeps stirring warmer air down to the surface, replacing the layer the cold ground has chilled. On a calm night there is no stirring, so the thin layer of air touching the ground is cooled by conduction, stays put, and keeps cooling until water vapour in it freezes onto the grass. So clear plus calm equals maximum radiation loss plus no mixing — frost. That is also why fruit farmers in the Langkloof run big fans in their orchards on cold nights: they are manufacturing the wind your gran knows is missing, mixing warmer air downward to save the blossoms. One budget, one blanket, one stir — and a grandmother ahead of the textbook.

**Thandi:** Third one: if dark surfaces absorb more heat, why do people in hot towns paint their roofs white — and does the same idea work at the scale of the whole planet?

**Tutor:** It is albedo, working at every scale. A dark roof absorbs most of the short-wave radiation landing on it and turns it into heat, which conduction then delivers into the rooms below; a white-painted roof reflects a large share of that energy away before it ever becomes heat, and the house below stays noticeably cooler for the price of a coat of paint. Now scale it up, because the planet runs the same experiment with ice. Snow and sea ice are the earth's white roof: they reflect most incoming radiation. But here the story gains a dangerous twist called feedback. Warm the planet slightly and some ice melts, exposing dark ocean or dark rock; the dark surface absorbs what the ice would have reflected, so the region warms further, which melts more ice, which lowers the albedo again. The loop feeds itself. That is why the Arctic is warming far faster than the global average, and why scientists watch ice cover so anxiously — it is not just a symptom of warming but an amplifier of it. So one idea joins your roof to the poles: bright surfaces refuse energy, dark ones bank it, and changing a surface changes the budget.

**Thandi:** Last one: the lesson said the tropics run an energy surplus. If the extra heat is always being exported by winds and currents, why does the equator never actually cool down to match everywhere else?

**Tutor:** Because the export never stops arriving. Think of a basin with the tap running and the plug half open: water flows out continuously, yet the basin stays full, because inflow continues at the same rate as the draining. The tropics are that basin. Every single day the near-vertical sun delivers a fresh surplus of energy; every single day the Hadley circulation, the great wind systems and warm currents like the Agulhas drain some of it poleward. What the transfer achieves is not equality but a steady state — a permanent temperature gradient from warm equator to cold poles that would be far steeper if the transfer stopped. And notice what lives inside that word "transfer": the drained heat does not vanish, it does work along the way. It powers the trade winds, feeds tropical storms, drives the rain-bearing systems that water the continents, and keeps the mid-latitudes far milder than their sunshine alone could manage. So do not picture the circulation as a repair that will one day be finished. It is a permanent job — the planet's heat economy in motion — and every wind you will study for the rest of this course is part of the workforce.
