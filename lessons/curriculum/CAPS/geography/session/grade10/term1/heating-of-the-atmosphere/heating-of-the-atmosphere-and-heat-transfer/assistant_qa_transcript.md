**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! This one bothers everybody: if you climb a mountain you get closer to the sun, so why does it get colder?

**Tutor:** Because the sun is not the thing warming you. In terms of distance, climbing the highest mountain on earth shortens your journey to the sun by less than one part in seventeen thousand — utterly negligible. What matters is that the atmosphere is heated from below. Insolation is short-wave radiation, and the air is nearly transparent to it, so most of that energy passes straight through and is absorbed by the ground. The ground warms, and only then does it heat the air touching it — by conduction into the lowest metres, by convection lifting warmed parcels, and by emitting long-wave radiation that the air absorbs well. So the heat source in the troposphere is the surface beneath you, and every metre you climb takes you further from it. Two extra reasons pile on. Air pressure falls with height, so a rising parcel expands and cools simply by doing that work. And high air is thinner, drier and dustier-free, so it holds fewer of the particles and less of the water vapour that absorb heat. Put together, temperature falls about 6,5 degrees for every thousand metres — which is why Sutherland and the high Drakensberg record our coldest nights.

**Thandi:** Next: everyone talks about the greenhouse effect as if it is a disaster. But the lesson said we would freeze without it. So which is it?

**Tutor:** Both statements are true, and telling them apart is exactly what the examiner is testing. The natural greenhouse effect has operated for the whole of earth's history. Water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane and the rest absorb the long-wave radiation leaving the surface and send some of it back down, holding the global average surface temperature near 15 degrees. Remove those gases and the average would sit around minus 18 — oceans frozen, agriculture impossible. So the effect itself is not merely harmless, it is the condition for life. The problem is the ENHANCED greenhouse effect: our additions of carbon dioxide from coal, oil and gas, methane from livestock, landfills and rice fields, and the carbon released by clearing forests, arriving faster than natural sinks can absorb them. A blanket keeps you comfortable; the complaint is not about blankets, it is about the fourth blanket in February. Write it that way in an answer — name the natural effect, state its benefit, then name the enhanced effect and its human sources — and you will collect marks that learners who condemn "the greenhouse effect" as a whole simply lose.

**Thandi:** This one had us arguing: Durban and Johannesburg are almost the same distance from the equator, but Durban is hot and humid and Johannesburg is not. If latitude is the biggest factor, why does it lose here?

**Tutor:** Latitude is the biggest factor at the global scale — it explains why the tropics are hot and the poles are frozen. But between two places at the same latitude, latitude has nothing left to say, and the other four factors take over completely. Here two of them do the work. Altitude: Johannesburg sits near 1 750 metres above sea level and Durban sits on the sea. At 6,5 degrees per thousand metres, that height difference alone accounts for roughly eleven degrees of cooling, which is most of the gap. Distance from the sea and the current running along it: Durban is coastal and the warm Agulhas Current flows south past it, so the air above is warmed and loaded with moisture, giving mild winters, sticky summers and heavy summer rainfall. Johannesburg is far inland with no moderating water body, so it heats and cools quickly and has a much larger daily range and drier air. The lesson for exam technique is that these factors work together, and a good comparison answer says which one dominates and why the others do not. Same latitude, different altitude, different sea influence — that sentence is the whole answer.

**Thandi:** Last one: why does the west coast have a desert right next to the ocean? An ocean should mean rain, surely.

**Tutor:** It should, and the reason it does not is the cold Benguela Current, which flows northward up our west coast carrying cold water from the Southern Ocean. Rain requires air that is both moist and RISING. Air sitting over cold water is chilled from below, and chilled air near the surface is dense and stable — it will not rise. Worse, cold water evaporates far less readily than warm water, so the air above the Benguela picks up much less moisture in the first place. What moisture it does hold condenses close to the surface as the sea-air is cooled to its dew point, which is why Port Nolloth and the Namib coast get thick advection fog rather than rain. Plants and animals there famously drink that fog. Compare the east coast, where the warm Agulhas heats the air above it, encourages evaporation and destabilises the lower atmosphere, so moist air rises readily and Durban records heavy summer rainfall. Same country, two coasts, opposite currents, opposite climates — and it is one of the most reliable comparison questions in the whole grade, so learn the pair as a matched set.
