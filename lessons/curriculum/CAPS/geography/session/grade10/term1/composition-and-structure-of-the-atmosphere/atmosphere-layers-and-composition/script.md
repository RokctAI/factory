# Part 1 — Expert

Every weather and climate topic in the FET phase stands on this one session. The atmosphere is not empty space above the ground — it is a measured mixture of gases, arranged in four layers with sharply different behaviour, holding a shield of ozone that decides how much ultraviolet radiation reaches the Karoo, the Highveld and the coast. Composition, structure, ozone, and the atmosphere's functions: four blocks of content that reappear in every paper from here to matric.

## Subtopic: The Composition of the Atmosphere

State the figures for DRY air by volume, because that is the standard the syllabus uses. NITROGEN — 78 per cent. OXYGEN — 21 per cent. ARGON — 0,93 per cent. CARBON DIOXIDE — roughly 0,04 per cent, which is four parts in every ten thousand. The remainder is traces of neon, helium, methane, krypton and hydrogen. Those four numbers — 78, 21, 0,93, 0,04 — earn marks on their own.

Then the division examiners actually test: PERMANENT gases against VARIABLE components. The permanent gases — nitrogen, oxygen, argon — sit in the same proportions everywhere in the lower atmosphere, over Cape Town and over the Sahara alike. The variable components change from place to place and hour to hour: WATER VAPOUR, ranging from almost nothing over Upington in August to nearly four per cent over the Mozambique Channel; DUST and other aerosols — smoke, sea salt, pollen, volcanic ash, industrial particles; and OZONE, concentrated far above in the stratosphere.

Now the functions, because "state the composition" is a two-mark question and "explain the importance of each component" is a ten-mark one. Nitrogen DILUTES oxygen, so combustion proceeds at a controllable rate, and it is the raw material that fixing bacteria convert into the compounds plants need for protein. Oxygen sustains respiration and drives all burning. Carbon dioxide is the input for photosynthesis and, despite that tiny percentage, a principal greenhouse gas. Water vapour is the source of every cloud and every millimetre in the rain gauge, carries latent heat around the planet, and is the most abundant greenhouse gas of all. Dust particles matter more than their size suggests: each one is a CONDENSATION NUCLEUS, a surface for water vapour to condense onto. Without them raindrops would not readily form.

The trap here is quoting the percentages as if they applied to the air actually in the room. They apply to dry air. Note that in your answer and the mark is secure — as are the questions waiting for you on this section right now: the four figures, the permanent-versus-variable split, and one function per component.

## Subtopic: The Four Layers — Structure by Temperature

The atmosphere is divided into layers by ONE criterion: the direction in which temperature changes as you climb. Learn the layers in order upward, and for each one give the height range, the direction of temperature change, the REASON, and a distinguishing feature.

The TROPOSPHERE runs from the surface to about 18 kilometres over the equator and only about 8 kilometres over the poles — average roughly 12. It holds around three-quarters of the atmosphere's total mass and virtually all its water vapour. Temperature FALLS with height, at a normal lapse rate averaging 6,5 degrees Celsius per 1 000 metres. The reason is decisive: the atmosphere is heated from BELOW, by long-wave radiation from the earth's surface, so the further you move from that source, the colder it gets. All weather happens here. It ends at the TROPOPAUSE, near minus 55 degrees, where temperature stops falling.

The STRATOSPHERE runs from the tropopause to about 50 kilometres, and here temperature RISES with height — a permanent inversion. The reason is the ozone layer at roughly 20 to 30 kilometres, absorbing ultraviolet radiation and converting it to heat. Because the warm air lies ABOVE the cool air, nothing rises, there is no convection and almost no water vapour: no cloud, no weather, superb stability. That is precisely why jet aircraft cruise in its lowest reaches. It closes at the STRATOPAUSE, near 0 degrees.

The MESOSPHERE, 50 to about 80 kilometres, has no ozone to warm it, so temperature FALLS again — to about minus 90 degrees at the MESOPAUSE, the coldest place in the entire atmosphere. Meteoroids burn up here; those are shooting stars.

The THERMOSPHERE begins near 80 kilometres and temperature RISES steeply, into the hundreds and beyond, because the sparse remaining molecules absorb the most energetic solar radiation. The air is so thin that this heat could not warm a body. It contains the ionosphere, which reflects radio waves back to earth, and the auroras.

Picture the graph properly, because it is examined: height up the vertical axis, temperature along the horizontal. The line leans to the LEFT as it climbs the troposphere, swings RIGHT through the stratosphere, leans LEFT again through the mesosphere, then swings RIGHT in the thermosphere — a zigzag with three turning points, and each turning point is a pause. Take the questions on this section now: layer order, height ranges, and the reason behind each temperature reversal.

## Subtopic: The Ozone Layer — Shield, Damage and Repair

Ozone is oxygen wearing three atoms instead of two. It forms in the stratosphere when ultraviolet radiation splits an ordinary oxygen molecule and the freed atoms attach to other oxygen molecules. Concentrated between about 20 and 30 kilometres, it is startlingly thin: compressed to sea-level pressure the whole ozone layer would be a film roughly three millimetres deep. Its function is single and vital — it absorbs most incoming ultraviolet-B and ultraviolet-C radiation before it reaches the surface.

The agents of depletion are CHLOROFLUOROCARBONS — CFCs — released for decades from aerosol propellants, refrigerator and air-conditioner coolants, foam-blowing agents and solvents. Their danger lies in their stability: they do not dissolve in rain or react at low levels, so they drift upward for years. In the stratosphere ultraviolet radiation finally breaks them apart and liberates CHLORINE. A single chlorine atom destroys ozone catalytically — it is not consumed in the reaction, so one atom goes on to destroy many thousands of molecules.

The Antarctic ozone hole is a seasonal thinning that opens each southern spring, because the polar vortex seals off that air through winter and polar stratospheric clouds provide the surfaces on which the chlorine reactions run. South Africa's position matters: we lie relatively near that thinning, already receive intense solar radiation, and have skin-cancer rates among the highest in the world.

The effects, kept separate for the mark scheme: more skin cancer and premature skin ageing; cataracts and other eye damage; suppressed immune systems; damage to phytoplankton, the base of the marine food web supporting our west-coast fisheries; reduced yields in sensitive crops; and faster degradation of plastics and paint. The remedies: the Montreal Protocol of 1987 phased out CFC production internationally — the most successful environmental treaty yet signed, and the layer is now measurably recovering. Individually: have old fridges and air-conditioners degassed by a technician rather than dumped, and buy ozone-friendly products.

One separation must be airtight, and the questions on this section are with you now to test it: ozone depletion lets radiation IN through the stratosphere; the greenhouse effect traps heat in the troposphere. Different gases, different layer, different mechanism.

## Subtopic: Why the Atmosphere Matters — and the Exam Method

Close the topic where the syllabus closes it: the importance of the atmosphere itself. It supplies the oxygen of respiration and the carbon dioxide of photosynthesis. It acts as an insulating blanket — the natural greenhouse effect holds the global average surface temperature near 15 degrees Celsius instead of roughly minus 18. It shields the surface with ozone and burns up meteoroids before they strike. It holds and moves the water that drives the hydrological cycle, and transports surplus heat from the tropics toward the poles. It prevents the ferocious daily temperature swings of an airless world — the Moon's surface passes from above 100 degrees by day to below minus 150 at night. And it scatters light for a blue sky, carries sound, and makes flight and radio communication possible.

Then the method, three disciplines that hold across every question on this topic. FIRST: answer structure questions in order, from the surface upward, and for each layer give four things — height range, temperature direction, reason, distinguishing feature. A layer named without its reason is half an answer. SECOND: when a graph of temperature against height is supplied, work from the TURNING POINTS. Each reversal is a pause — tropopause, stratopause, mesopause — and identifying them locates every layer without memorising a single height. THIRD: when the question turns to ozone, write three separate sentences — layer, cause, consequence — because the marks are usually split exactly that way.

Finally, guard your terminology. The ozone LAYER is a natural, beneficial part of the stratosphere. Ozone at ground level, produced by traffic and industry, is a pollutant. Same molecule, opposite verdict, decided entirely by altitude. The last questions of this part are in front of you now: the functions of the atmosphere listed, the four-part layer description practised, and ground-level ozone kept apart from the stratospheric shield.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same air, the same four layers and the same shield — told with a bag of marbles, a block of flats, and a bottle of sunscreen.

## Subtopic: The Air Recipe

Imagine a bag holding exactly one hundred marbles, and that bag is a mouthful of dry air. Seventy-eight of them are grey: nitrogen. Twenty-one are blue: oxygen. One marble covers everything else — mostly argon, with carbon dioxide as a mere speck of it. Carbon dioxide is four marbles in every ten thousand, a pinch of salt in a very big pot of pap. And yet that pinch feeds every plant on earth and helps hold the planet's heat.

Nitrogen looks boring and is not. Think of Oros concentrate: nobody drinks it neat. Nitrogen is the water you add — it dilutes the oxygen so that fire burns steadily instead of tearing away. In pure oxygen a match would not light a candle; it would light everything.

Now the ingredient that never appears in the recipe, because it refuses to stay still: WATER VAPOUR. On a February afternoon in Durban the air is soaked with it; in Upington in midwinter there is barely any. Because it changes by the hour, the textbook percentages always say "dry air" — and that little phrase is worth a mark on its own.

Last, the bits you can see floating in a sunbeam: dust, smoke, sea salt, pollen. Each speck is a tiny seat for water vapour to sit down on when it cools, and without those seats raindrops struggle to form. The same specks scatter sunlight — which is why the sky is blue, and why the sun goes deep orange dropping behind the Karoo. Small check before we climb: the questions on the recipe are with you right now — the four numbers, the ingredient that varies, and why the dust matters.

## Subtopic: Four Storeys of One Strange Building

Picture a block of flats four storeys high, with heating that makes no sense until you know where the heaters are.

GROUND FLOOR — the troposphere. The heater is the FLOOR itself: the ground soaks up sunshine and radiates the warmth upward. So the higher you climb, the colder it gets. That is exactly why the Drakensberg can carry snow on a day when Durban is swimming weather. All the weather lives on this floor, and so does about three-quarters of all the air in the building.

FIRST FLOOR — the stratosphere. Now the heater is on the CEILING: ozone up there catches ultraviolet and turns it into warmth, so it gets WARMER as you climb. Warm air sitting on top of cool air is a lid; nothing rises, nothing churns, no clouds form. Dead calm. That is why aeroplanes climb up into the bottom of this floor — smooth flying, above all the weather.

SECOND FLOOR — the mesosphere. No heater at all up here, so it gets cold again, and the ceiling of this floor is the coldest place in the whole building: around minus ninety. Shooting stars are meteors burning up in this floor's thin air.

TOP FLOOR — the thermosphere. A fierce heater and almost no air. The thermometer reads thousands of degrees, and you would still freeze, because there is almost nothing up there to touch you and pass the heat on. This is the floor that bounces radio signals back to earth and where the auroras glow.

Say the order out loud once — troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere — and remember the temperature going DOWN, UP, DOWN, UP as you climb. Your questions on the building are up now: the four floors in order, where each heater sits, and the down-up-down-up pattern.

## Subtopic: The Sunscreen With a Hole In It

Ozone is the country's sunscreen, smeared about twenty-five kilometres up. Squeeze it all down to sea level and it would be a film about three millimetres thick — the edge of a two-rand coin standing between us and the worst of the sun's ultraviolet.

CFCs are the chemical that eats it. They leaked for decades out of old fridges, air-conditioners, aerosol cans and foam. Their trick is that they are chemically lazy: rain will not wash them out, so they drift upward for years. Then, high in the stratosphere, ultraviolet cracks them open and the chlorine gets loose — and one chlorine atom does not destroy one ozone molecule and stop. It destroys thousands, one after another, like a single person with a master key unlocking every door down a corridor.

Each southern spring a thinning opens over Antarctica — the "ozone hole" — because the winter air down there gets sealed off and turns into a perfect chemistry lab. We are the country living nearest that thinning with a great deal of sun already, and our skin-cancer rates sit among the highest anywhere.

Here is the good part, and it is rare: this is the environmental problem the world genuinely fixed. Countries signed the Montreal Protocol, CFCs were phased out, and the layer is slowly healing. What still helps: get an old fridge degassed properly instead of dumping it — and wear the hat between ten and three.

One last thing to keep straight, because examiners bank on the mix-up: the ozone problem lets too much radiation IN through the first floor; the greenhouse problem traps too much heat DOWN on the ground floor. Two different problems, two different storeys. The final questions of the lesson are with you now — the sunscreen, the chemical that eats it, the treaty that worked, and the two problems kept firmly apart.
