**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it comes up every year: if the thermosphere is thousands of degrees, why do astronauts and satellites not simply burn up in it?

**Tutor:** Because temperature and heat are not the same quantity, and this layer is where the difference becomes obvious. Temperature measures how fast individual molecules are moving. Heat is the total energy actually transferred to an object, and that depends on how MANY molecules strike it. In the thermosphere the few remaining oxygen and nitrogen molecules absorb the most energetic solar radiation and are flung about at enormous speeds, so a thermometer reading of the molecular motion is genuinely in the hundreds or thousands of degrees. But the air there is so rarefied that a satellite is struck by almost nothing. Very few collisions means very little energy delivered, so the object loses heat by radiating into space faster than it gains it — a spacecraft's problem there is staying warm on its shaded side, not cooling down. Contrast that with the troposphere, where the air is dense: plenty of molecules, plenty of collisions, and a thirty-degree day genuinely feels like thirty degrees. So the exam answer is one sentence: high temperature but negligible heat transfer, because the density is almost nothing.

**Thandi:** Next: carbon dioxide is only four parts in ten thousand. How can something that tiny run the greenhouse effect and the whole climate argument?

**Tutor:** Because effect depends on what a gas DOES, not on how much of it there is. Nitrogen and oxygen make up ninety-nine per cent of the air and are almost transparent to long-wave radiation — the earth's outgoing heat passes straight through them as if they were not there. Carbon dioxide, water vapour, methane and nitrous oxide have molecular structures that absorb precisely those long-wave wavelengths, then re-radiate the energy in all directions, including back down. So the ninety-nine per cent does nothing to the heat budget and the fraction of a per cent does everything. Think of a window: the glass is a small part of the wall's mass, yet it decides entirely how much light gets through. There is a second reason the small number matters. Because the base is so small, human additions move it proportionally a very long way — adding a fixed amount of carbon dioxide changes its concentration by a large percentage, whereas the same mass of nitrogen added to the atmosphere would be undetectable. Small stock, big leverage. Never dismiss a gas in an exam answer because its percentage looks trivial; say what it absorbs.

***

**Thandi:** This one confused half of us: the ozone layer is high up in the stratosphere, but people also say ozone is a pollutant near the ground. Which is it?

**Tutor:** Both, and altitude decides. The molecule is identical — three oxygen atoms — and its property is identical too: it absorbs ultraviolet radiation and it is chemically aggressive. What changes is where it sits. In the stratosphere, twenty to thirty kilometres up, that aggressive absorbing molecule stands between the sun and every living thing, soaking up ultraviolet-B and ultraviolet-C long before they reach a person, a crop or the plankton off our west coast. That is the shield, and we want it thick. At ground level, ozone forms when sunlight acts on vehicle exhaust and industrial emissions — nitrogen oxides and unburnt hydrocarbons. Now that same chemical aggression is applied to the tissue of your lungs, to the leaves of crops and to rubber and paint, and it is a major component of photochemical smog over big cities. So the summary line for an answer is: good ozone is up high and thinning where we need it; bad ozone is down low and thickening where we do not. And note the sting — the two problems have completely separate causes. Fixing traffic emissions will not repair the layer, and phasing out CFCs did nothing for city smog.

**Thandi:** Last one: if the atmosphere gets thinner as you go up, why does the boundary between layers not just fade away? What actually marks the tropopause?

**Tutor:** Density does fade smoothly — there is no wall anywhere in the atmosphere, and pressure simply decreases as you rise. But the layers are not defined by density. They are defined by the DIRECTION in which temperature changes, and that direction changes abruptly, which gives a genuinely sharp boundary. Through the troposphere the temperature falls steadily because the heat source is the ground below. Climb far enough and the influence of the surface fades while the influence of the ozone above begins; at a particular level the falling stops. That level is the tropopause, and it is real enough to be detected by any weather balloon: temperature drops to about minus fifty-five, holds steady over a stretch, then begins to rise. Above the pause, warm air sits on cool air, which is a lid — rising thunderclouds hit it and spread sideways, which is exactly why a cumulonimbus develops a flat anvil top instead of continuing upward. The tropopause is also not the same height everywhere: it stands near eighteen kilometres over the equator, where strong heating pushes it up, and only around eight over the poles. So the honest answer is that the layers are temperature zones, and their boundaries are the levels where the temperature trend reverses.
