**Thandi:** Time for the class questions! Here is the first: if oxygen is always 21 per cent of the air, why do climbers on Everest carry oxygen bottles? Surely the percentage is the same up there?

**Tutor:** The percentage is the same — and that is exactly the trap. Twenty-one per cent is a PROPORTION, not an amount. What changes with altitude is the total amount of air available: pressure and density fall the higher you climb, because there is less atmosphere pressing down from above. At sea level a lungful of air contains a certain number of oxygen molecules. Near the top of Everest, at almost nine kilometres, air pressure is roughly a third of the sea-level value, so a lungful still holds 21 per cent oxygen — but 21 per cent of far fewer molecules altogether. The body counts molecules, not percentages, so each breath delivers about a third of the oxygen it would at the coast, and the climber suffocates slowly without a bottle. This is also why aircraft cabins are pressurised, and why athletes training in Johannesburg at seventeen hundred metres already feel a thinner bite in the air than they do at sea level in Durban. So keep the two ideas separate: composition stays fixed through the lower atmosphere; density and pressure do not.

**Thandi:** Next one: the lesson said ultraviolet light actually MAKES ozone in the stratosphere. If the sun keeps making more, why was everyone so worried about CFCs destroying it?

**Tutor:** Because the layer was never a stockpile — it is a balance. Ozone is being created and destroyed naturally all the time: ultraviolet splits oxygen molecules, the fragments build ozone, and other reactions break it down again. For thousands of years production and natural destruction ran at matching rates, so the total held steady, the way a dam stays full when inflow equals outflow. What CFCs did was open a second outlet. Chlorine destroys ozone catalytically, without being used up, so the destruction rate suddenly outran the production rate — and the sun cannot simply make ozone faster, because its ultraviolet supply is fixed. The dam level fell. That is also why recovery is slow even now: the treaty closed the tap on new CFCs, but the chlorine already in the stratosphere lingers for fifty years or more, so the extra outlet is closing gradually, not instantly. When you write about this, use the word balance — depletion means destruction exceeding production, not ozone production switching off.

**Thandi:** This one caused an argument in class: is the greenhouse effect a good thing or a bad thing? The lesson called it a blanket we need, but the news calls it a crisis.

**Tutor:** Both statements are true, because they describe two different things sharing one name. The NATURAL greenhouse effect is unambiguously good: water vapour, carbon dioxide and methane intercept outgoing long-wave radiation and return part of it, holding the planet's average near fifteen degrees instead of minus eighteen. Without it there is no liquid water and no agriculture — no us. The crisis in the news is the ENHANCED greenhouse effect: human activity thickening the blanket by adding extra carbon dioxide and methane, which traps extra heat and shifts climates faster than farming and ecosystems can adjust. The analogy is medicine. A prescribed dose keeps you alive; a triple dose is poison; nobody concludes that medicine is bad. In your answers, always attach the adjective — natural or enhanced — before praising or blaming the greenhouse effect, and never mix it up with ozone depletion, which is a different problem in a different layer with different gases.

**Thandi:** Last question: the troposphere was given as eighteen kilometres at the equator but only eight at the poles. Why is it not the same thickness everywhere?

**Tutor:** Because the troposphere is inflated from below, and the strength of the inflation depends on heating. Over the equator the surface receives concentrated, near-vertical sunshine all year, so the ground is hot, convection is vigorous, and powerful rising currents punch warm air to great heights — the layer gets pumped up like a well-blown balloon, reaching about eighteen kilometres. Over the poles the sun's rays arrive at a low slant and spread thin, the surface is bitterly cold, air hugs the ground and barely rises, so the layer slumps to about eight kilometres — a balloon with most of the air let out. There is a consequence worth remembering: the equatorial tropopause is actually COLDER than the polar one, because air cooling at six and a half degrees per thousand metres has eighteen kilometres to cool over the equator and only eight over the poles. So more heating below produces a higher, colder ceiling — a lovely example of how one process, heating from below, explains both the layer's existence and its shape.
