# Part 1 — Expert

Rain is not a mystery; it is a sequence. Air takes up water vapour, is forced to rise, cools as it rises, reaches the temperature at which it can hold no more, condenses onto tiny particles, and eventually releases the water it can no longer carry. This session establishes that sequence precisely — humidity and dew point, the condensation level and the cloud families it produces, and the three mechanisms that lift air over South Africa.

## Subtopic: Humidity, Saturation and Dew Point

Water enters the atmosphere by EVAPORATION from oceans, dams and soil, and by TRANSPIRATION from plants; together, EVAPOTRANSPIRATION. Once airborne it is invisible: water VAPOUR is a gas, and the visible white in a cloud is already liquid.

HUMIDITY is the amount of water vapour in the air. Two measures. ABSOLUTE humidity is the actual mass of vapour in a given volume, in grams per cubic metre. RELATIVE humidity is the useful one: the amount of vapour present expressed as a percentage of the maximum that air could hold at that temperature.

The governing rule: warm air can hold more water vapour than cold air, and the relationship is steep. Air at 30 degrees holds far more than double what air at 10 degrees can hold.

Work an example. Suppose air at 20 degrees Celsius can hold at most 17 grams of water vapour per cubic metre, and it actually contains 8,5 grams. Relative humidity is 8,5 divided by 17, times 100 — exactly 50 per cent. Now cool that same air, adding no water whatsoever. At 10 degrees the maximum it can hold has dropped to roughly 8,5 grams, and 8,5 divided by 8,5 is 100 per cent. The air is SATURATED. Nothing was added; the container simply shrank.

That temperature — the temperature to which air must be cooled for it to become saturated — is the DEW POINT. It is a moisture measurement disguised as a temperature, and its power lies in comparison. When air temperature and dew point are close, the air is nearly saturated: humid, with condensation imminent. When they are far apart, the air is dry. On a station model, never report either number alone; report the GAP.

## Subtopic: Condensation — From Dew to the Condensation Level

CONDENSATION is water vapour changing to liquid, which happens when air is cooled to its dew point and CONDENSATION NUCLEI are available — dust, smoke, pollen, sea salt. Without those surfaces, condensation is very difficult.

The forms depend on where and how the cooling occurs. On a clear, still night the ground radiates heat away rapidly, chills the air lying on it, and moisture condenses onto cold surfaces as DEW — provided the dew point is above freezing. If the dew point lies below zero, the vapour deposits directly as ice and you get FROST, which is why Free State farmers watch for clear windless winter nights rather than cold ones alone. When a whole layer of surface air is cooled below its dew point, the droplets stay suspended as MIST, or FOG when visibility falls below one kilometre. Radiation fog forms in valleys on cold still mornings; ADVECTION fog forms when moist air moves horizontally over a cold surface — precisely what happens when maritime air drifts over the cold Benguela Current and rolls into Port Nolloth and the west-coast towns.

Now the vertical case, which drives every cloud. A parcel of air forced upward expands as pressure falls around it, and expansion cools it — ADIABATIC cooling, meaning cooling without heat being removed. Unsaturated air cools at the DRY ADIABATIC LAPSE RATE, 1 degree per 100 metres. Once saturated, condensation releases latent heat, so the parcel cools more slowly — the SATURATED ADIABATIC LAPSE RATE, roughly 0,5 degrees per 100 metres.

The height at which the rising parcel reaches its dew point is the CONDENSATION LEVEL: the cloud base. Calculate it. Surface temperature 25 degrees, surface dew point 15 degrees: the parcel must cool 10 degrees, and at 1 degree per 100 metres that takes 1 000 metres. The cloud base stands at 1 000 metres above the surface. This is why cumulus clouds over the Highveld have flat bottoms all at the same level — every parcel in that air mass reaches saturation at the same height.

Your questions on this section are with you now: dew, frost, mist and fog distinguished by cause, adiabatic cooling explained, and a condensation level calculated.

## Subtopic: Clouds — Naming Them and Reading Their Weather

Cloud names are built from four Latin words, and once you know them the classification writes itself. CIRRO means wispy and high. ALTO means middle. STRATO means layered or spread out. CUMULO means heaped. And NIMBUS means the cloud is raining.

HIGH clouds, above roughly 6 000 metres, are made of ice crystals. CIRRUS are white feathery streaks — fair weather at present, but often the first sign of an approaching front, a warning arriving a day early. CIRROSTRATUS is a thin milky sheet that produces a halo around the sun or moon, another classic pre-frontal signal. CIRROCUMULUS forms the rippled "mackerel sky".

MIDDLE clouds, roughly 2 000 to 6 000 metres, carry the prefix alto. ALTOSTRATUS is a grey sheet through which the sun appears as if through frosted glass; it commonly thickens ahead of steady rain. ALTOCUMULUS is a layer of rounded white and grey patches.

LOW clouds lie below about 2 000 metres. STRATUS is a uniform grey layer bringing drizzle and dull conditions — fog is essentially stratus at ground level. STRATOCUMULUS is a lumpy low sheet. NIMBOSTRATUS is thick, dark and shapeless, and delivers steady prolonged rain rather than showers.

VERTICALLY DEVELOPED clouds are the dramatic ones. CUMULUS is the flat-based, cauliflower-topped fair-weather cloud of a Highveld morning. CUMULONIMBUS is what it becomes when the uplift continues: a towering mass from near the surface to the tropopause, flattening into an ANVIL where it strikes that ceiling, and producing heavy showers, thunder, lightning and the hail that damages Free State and Gauteng crops.

The examinable skill is reading weather from the cloud, and the questions on this section are in front of you now: name the family, name the height, and state the weather each cloud brings.

## Subtopic: The Three Rainfall Types

Every type of rainfall requires the same four steps — air rises, cools adiabatically, reaches dew point, condenses. The types differ ONLY in what forces the air upward. Name the mechanism and the marks follow.

RELIEF or OROGRAPHIC rainfall. Moist air moving inland from the sea meets a mountain barrier and is forced to rise over it. It cools, condenses and precipitates on the WINDWARD slope. Having crossed the crest, the air descends the LEEWARD slope, is compressed, warms, and its relative humidity falls, so the leeward side is dry — a RAIN SHADOW. South African examples are exact: moist easterly air off the Indian Ocean rises against the Drakensberg escarpment, drenching the eastern slopes while the interior beyond lies drier; and in the Western Cape, moist air rising over the Hottentots Holland and Langeberg keeps the seaward slopes wet while the Little Karoo sits in their rain shadow, arid enough for ostrich farming.

CONVECTIONAL rainfall. Intense heating of the ground makes the air above it expand and rise in strong thermals. It cools, condenses, and if the uplift is vigorous the cloud grows into cumulonimbus. The signature is the late-afternoon thunderstorm: it builds through the heat of the day, breaks between mid-afternoon and evening, and is short, heavy and localised, often with lightning and hail. This is the summer rainfall of the Highveld — Gauteng, the Free State and Mpumalanga — and it is why our interior rainfall arrives in violent bursts rather than steady drizzle.

FRONTAL or CYCLONIC rainfall. Two air masses of different temperature meet, and the denser cold air wedges beneath the warm moist air and forces it to rise along the frontal surface. In a COLD front the cold air advances aggressively, lifting the warm air steeply and producing a narrow band of cumulonimbus with heavy showers and a sharp temperature drop behind. In a WARM front the warm air rides gently up over retreating cold air, producing a wide band of layered cloud and prolonged, gentler rain. South Africa's decisive example is the winter rainfall of the Western Cape, delivered by cold fronts sweeping in from the SOUTH-WEST across the Atlantic, one behind another, between roughly May and August.

The examiner's favourite trap, and the last questions of this part are with you now to test it: identify the type by the LIFTING MECHANISM, not by how heavy the rain is, and then name the region and the season.

## Subtopic: The Bag That Grows When It's Warm

Think of air as a shopping bag carrying water vapour, and remember one rule: WARM air is a big bag, COLD air is a small bag.

A big warm bag can carry plenty of water without any trouble. Now cool that same bag down. You have not added a single drop — but the bag itself is shrinking. Keep cooling and eventually the bag is completely full. That moment has a name: the air is SATURATED. And the temperature at which it happened is the DEW POINT.

That is the whole idea, and it explains something you have seen a hundred times. Take a cold Coke out of the fridge on a summer day and it sweats. Nobody poured water onto that can. The air touching the cold metal got chilled, its bag shrank below what it was carrying, and the extra had to come out as liquid. Same thing with a bathroom mirror after a shower, and the same thing with the inside of a taxi window on a winter morning in Bloemfontein.

Now the number version, quickly. Say air at twenty degrees can carry seventeen grams of water and is actually carrying eight and a half. It is half full — relative humidity fifty per cent. Cool it to ten degrees, where the bag only holds eight and a half, and now it is a hundred per cent full without anything being added.

The exam habit that comes out of this: never look at the dew point on its own. Look at the GAP between the temperature and the dew point. Small gap, the bag is nearly full and rain is nearby. Big gap, plenty of room, dry day. Quick check before we carry on — the questions on the bag are with you now: warm bag, cold bag, what saturated means, and why the gap is what matters.

## Subtopic: Reading the Sky Like a Menu

Cloud names look like Latin homework and are actually four words glued together. Learn the four and you can name any cloud in the sky.

CIRRO means high and wispy. ALTO means middle. STRATO means spread flat, like a blanket. CUMULO means heaped up, like cotton wool. And NIMBUS is the one that matters most in the exam: it means this cloud is actually raining.

Now stick them together. High up, way above the aeroplanes, sit the ice-crystal clouds: CIRRUS, thin white feathers brushed across the blue. Beautiful, and a warning — in the Cape in winter, cirrus streaming in often means a front is a day away. If they thicken into a milky sheet and you see a ring around the sun, that is CIRROSTRATUS and the front is closer.

In the middle sit the alto clouds — ALTOSTRATUS, a grey sheet with the sun showing through like frosted glass, usually thickening before steady rain.

Low down live the greys. STRATUS is a flat dull blanket with drizzle; fog is just stratus that came all the way down to the ground. NIMBOSTRATUS is the thick dark one that rains steadily all day and ruins the washing.

And then the tall ones. CUMULUS is the fair-weather cotton-wool cloud with a flat bottom — every one of them sitting on the same invisible shelf, which is the height where the air reached its dew point. Let a summer afternoon keep pushing it up and it becomes CUMULONIMBUS: a tower reaching almost to the top of the troposphere, spreading into a flat anvil where it hits the ceiling, throwing down thunder, lightning and Highveld hail.

Four words, one sky. Your questions on the cloud menu are up now: the four building blocks, the three heights, and which cloud brings which weather.

## Subtopic: Three Ways to Push Air Up a Hill

Every drop of rain that has ever fallen came from the same four steps: air goes up, air cools, air fills up, water falls out. Only one thing changes between the three rainfall types — WHAT pushed the air up. Get that and the topic is finished.

PUSH NUMBER ONE — the mountain. Wet air comes in off the sea, meets a mountain and has nowhere to go but up. It cools on the climb, fills up, and dumps its rain on the side facing the wind. Then it comes down the far side, warms up again and arrives dry. That is RELIEF rainfall, and it is why the eastern slopes of the Drakensberg are green and the interior behind is not — and why the Little Karoo, tucked behind the Cape mountains, is dry enough for ostriches.

PUSH NUMBER TWO — the hot ground. On a January afternoon in Gauteng the ground bakes, the air above it swells and shoots upward like smoke off a fire. It builds all day, towers up into cumulonimbus, and around four or five o'clock the sky opens: short, violent, loud, sometimes with hail, and often over your street while the next suburb stays dry. That is CONVECTIONAL rainfall, and it is the Highveld's summer rain in one sentence.

PUSH NUMBER THREE — the cold bulldozer. A mass of cold air comes marching in from the south-west, and because cold air is heavier it shoves underneath the warm air and lifts it right off the ground. The lifted air cools, condenses, and rains along the boundary. That is FRONTAL rainfall — and it is Cape Town's whole winter, one front after another rolling in off the Atlantic from May through August, which is why the Western Cape gets its rain in the months the rest of the country stays dry.

Mountain, hot ground, bulldozer. Say those three and you never confuse the types again — and note the trick the examiner uses: do not decide the type by how hard it is raining. Decide it by what did the pushing. Then say where in the country and in which season, because those are separate marks.
