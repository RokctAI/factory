Hey, it's Thandi — relative humidity, absolute humidity, dew point, condensation level, dry and saturated lapse rates, and then a whole shelf of Latin cloud names. If this topic feels like it has more vocabulary than actual ideas, you are not imagining it. But underneath the vocabulary there are only two ideas, and they are both easy.

Idea one: warm air is a big bag, cold air is a small bag. Everything about humidity falls out of that. Cool the air and the bag shrinks; when the bag is exactly full, the air is saturated, and the temperature where that happened is the dew point. That is why a cold Coke sweats and why the taxi window fogs up in winter. Nobody added water — the bag just got smaller.

Idea two: rain always needs air to be pushed UP. Up means cooling, cooling means the bag shrinks, and a shrinking bag has to let water out. Three things do the pushing: a mountain, a hot piece of ground, or a wedge of cold air. Relief, convectional, frontal. Mountain, hot ground, bulldozer.

The habit that saves marks: when a question asks for a rainfall type, do not judge by how heavy the rain sounds. Judge by what forced the air upward — that is the definition. And then add the region and the season, because those are separate marks that people leave on the table every year. Drakensberg slopes, Highveld summer afternoons, Cape winter fronts.

For clouds, do not memorise a list. Learn four words — cirro is high and wispy, alto is middle, strato is spread flat, cumulo is heaped — and nimbus means it is raining. Then build any name you need. Cumulonimbus is literally "heaped and raining", which is exactly what a thunderstorm is.

Two bags, three pushes, four Latin words. That is honestly the whole topic. You've got this!
