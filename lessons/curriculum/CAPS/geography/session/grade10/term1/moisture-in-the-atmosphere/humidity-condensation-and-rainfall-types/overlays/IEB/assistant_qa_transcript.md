**Thandi:** Class questions before we wrap the boards! First: why can I see my breath on a cold winter morning but never in summer? It is the same breath.

**Tutor:** It is the same breath, but it is not the same receiving air — and that difference is this whole lesson in miniature. The air leaving your lungs is warm, around body temperature, and nearly saturated with moisture. On a summer morning it mixes into warm outside air with a generous capacity for vapour, so your breath's moisture is absorbed without ever reaching saturation — nothing to see. On a frosty morning that same moist breath is dumped into air whose capacity is tiny. The mixture is chilled within a fraction of a second to below its dew point, the vapour has nowhere to stay, and it condenses instantly into a small cloud of droplets — a genuine, personal cloud, manufactured by the identical process that builds cumulus over the mountains. Watch it closely and you will even see it vanish a moment later, as the droplets mix into drier surrounding air and evaporate again. So your breath is a complete water-cycle demonstration lasting two seconds: evaporation in your lungs, condensation at your lips, evaporation again in the wind. Cold air is a small sponge — that is the entire answer.

**Thandi:** Next one: the berg wind. The lesson said air coming DOWN a mountain arrives warm and dry. But it was cold up on the mountain, so how does descending make it hot?

**Tutor:** By exactly the same mechanism that cools rising air — run in reverse. Going up, a parcel expands into thinning pressure and cools without losing heat. Coming down, the atmosphere squeezes the parcel back together, and compression warms it, without any heat being added — adiabatic warming, one degree for every hundred metres of descent while it stays unsaturated. Now put numbers on a real berg wind. Air sitting on the interior plateau at, say, ten degrees spills off the escarpment and drops two and a half kilometres to the coast. Two and a half thousand metres at one degree per hundred is twenty-five degrees of warming: the air arrives at around thirty-five. And it arrives THIRSTY, because warming enlarges its capacity for moisture while its actual vapour content never changed — relative humidity collapses. That is why berg wind days on the coast feel hot, dry and electric, why they arrive in winter when the plateau drains seaward, and why fire services dread them: any spark meets pre-dried vegetation and running hot wind. Same physics as the rain shadow — descending air is nature's hair dryer.

**Thandi:** Third: why does hail come with summer thunderstorms? Hail is ice, and surely ice weather belongs to winter?

**Tutor:** It feels backwards until you remember where the ice is made — not down here in the season, but kilometres up in the cloud, where it is far below freezing in January and July alike. What hail actually needs is not a cold month; it is a cloud with a violent elevator, and only the summer cumulonimbus has one. Convectional heating drives updraughts strong enough to catch a falling droplet and hurl it back up above the freezing level, where a shell of ice hardens around it. It falls, is caught again, rises, gains another coat — around and around, growing a layered skin like an onion, until it is finally too heavy for the updraught and drops out as a hailstone. Cut a big hailstone open and you can count the rings, one for each ride up the tower. Winter weather cannot do this: frontal rain comes from layered nimbostratus with gentle uplift — no elevator, no recycling, no hail, just steady rain. So hail is not a sign of cold weather; it is a signature of violent VERTICAL motion, which is why it belongs to the Highveld's hottest afternoons and why the strongest storms throw down the biggest stones.

**Thandi:** Last one: the weather report says "morning fog patches in the valleys, clearing by mid-morning." Why the valleys, and why does it always clear at more or less the same time?

**Tutor:** Both halves come from the same clear-night physics. After sunset the ground radiates its heat away, and the air touching it cools by conduction. Cold air is denser than warm air, so it behaves like water: it flows downhill off the slopes and ponds on the valley floor, the lowest point available. By the small hours the valley holds a pool of the night's coldest air, and if that pooled air is cooled below its dew point, its moisture condenses into suspended droplets — radiation fog, filling the valley like milk in a bowl while the ridge tops above stand in clear air. Farmers on hillsides often watch sunrise above a white lake with their neighbours invisible beneath it. The clearing is just the budget reversing. Once the sun rises it begins reheating the ground through the fog, the ground warms the air, the air's capacity grows, and the droplets evaporate back to invisible vapour — usually two to three hours after sunrise, which is why the forecast can promise mid-morning with such confidence. Notice the deeper lesson: fog did not blow away, it was dissolved in place by warming. Condensation and evaporation are the same door, swinging in opposite directions with temperature.
