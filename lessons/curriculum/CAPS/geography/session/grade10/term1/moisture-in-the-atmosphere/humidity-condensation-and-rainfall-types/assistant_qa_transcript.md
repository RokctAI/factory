**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: if relative humidity is 100 per cent, does that mean it must be raining? Because it is 100 per cent in fog and no rain falls at all.

**Tutor:** A very good separation to make, and it is worth marks. One hundred per cent relative humidity means the air is saturated — it holds all the vapour it can at that temperature — and it guarantees only that CONDENSATION will occur. Condensation makes droplets, and droplets are cloud, mist or fog. What it does not guarantee is PRECIPITATION, because a cloud droplet is minute and is held up easily by the gentlest rising air. For rain, those droplets must grow perhaps a million times in volume, by colliding and coalescing with one another or, in cold clouds, by ice crystals growing at the expense of surrounding droplets. That growth needs depth and time and, above all, sustained uplift. So fog is saturated air with essentially no uplift: the droplets form and simply sit there. A cumulonimbus is saturated air with violent uplift: droplets are carried up and down through kilometres of cloud, collide constantly and grow heavy enough to fall. Saturation is the doorway; uplift is what carries you through it. In an answer, always give both.

***

**Thandi:** Next: the lesson said air cools as it rises even though nothing takes heat away from it. That still sounds like something for nothing. Where does the warmth actually go?

**Tutor:** It goes into work, and this is genuinely one of the more elegant ideas in the syllabus. Atmospheric pressure falls with height, so a parcel of air that rises finds itself surrounded by less pressure and expands, pushing the surrounding air outward. Pushing is work, and work costs energy. The only energy available to the parcel is the energy of its own molecules — which is what temperature measures. So the parcel spends its internal energy on expanding, and its temperature drops. Nothing was removed by contact or radiation; the heat was converted into the work of expansion. That is why it is called ADIABATIC, meaning without heat transfer. The reverse happens exactly as neatly on the way down: sinking air is compressed by the increasing pressure around it, the surroundings do work on it, and it warms — which is why the leeward side of a mountain is hot and dry, and why berg winds arriving at the coast can be so unpleasantly warm. Two rates to quote: 1 degree per 100 metres while unsaturated, and about 0,5 degrees per 100 metres once condensation begins, because the latent heat released by condensing water partly offsets the cooling.

**Thandi:** This one caused a real argument: everybody says Cape Town gets winter rain and the rest of the country gets summer rain. Why would the seasons be opposite in one country?

**Tutor:** Because the two regions are fed by completely different rain-bringing systems, and those systems move with the sun through the year. The interior and the east are watered by convection and by moist air drawn in from the warm Indian Ocean. Both of those need heat: strong summer heating of the land drives the thermals that build the afternoon thunderstorms, and the low pressure that develops over the hot interior in summer pulls that moist air inland. In winter the land is cool, the thermals never build, and the interior is dominated by dry, sinking, stable air — so the Highveld winter is famously clear and rainless. The south-west is watered by something quite different: cold fronts embedded in the westerly wind belt of the mid-latitudes. In summer that belt sits far to the south, and the South Atlantic high-pressure cell sits over the Cape keeping it dry and sunny. As winter comes, the whole system shifts northward, the fronts sweep across the Cape one after another, and the winter rains arrive. One country, two engines: heat-driven rain in the interior in summer, front-driven rain in the south-west in winter. Learn it as a pair and you will never mix the seasons up.

**Thandi:** Last one: why does a thunderstorm on the Highveld hit one street and completely miss the next one? Rain from a front seems to fall on everybody.

**Tutor:** Because the two are lifted by mechanisms of completely different size and shape. Convectional uplift begins as a thermal — a bubble of air rising off a particularly hot patch of ground, perhaps a bare field, a tarred road or a dark rocky outcrop. That bubble is a few kilometres across at most, and the cumulonimbus it builds is correspondingly narrow, so the storm cell it produces might drop thirty millimetres on one suburb in twenty minutes while the next suburb records nothing at all. The cells also drift, which is why the rain arrives, hammers and stops. A front is a completely different geometry: it is a boundary between two air masses that can stretch a thousand kilometres or more across the country, and the uplift occurs in a long band along the whole of that line. So frontal rain arrives as a broad sweep, is generally lighter and lasts longer, and it genuinely does fall on everybody as the line passes. That contrast is examinable in itself: convectional rain is short, intense and patchy in summer afternoons; frontal rain is widespread, moderate and prolonged, and it comes with a clear wind and temperature change once the front has gone through.
