# Part 1 — Expert

The synoptic chart is the weather map behind every forecast, and Grade 10 is where a learner stops looking at it and starts reading it. This session completes one full examination task on it: a station model reporting a temperature of 18 degrees Celsius, a dew point of 16 degrees, cloud cover of six-eighths and a north-westerly wind at 20 knots, plotted on a chart that also carries a cold front. The session builds from isobars and pressure cells, through the symbol-by-symbol decoding of that station model, to identifying the front and describing — in observations rather than impressions — the weather it leaves behind it.

## Subtopic: What a Synoptic Chart Shows

A synoptic chart is a snapshot of the atmosphere over a region at one exact moment — "synoptic" means seen together. Every element on the chart was observed at the same time at weather stations across the country, then plotted so the whole pattern becomes visible at once.

What lives on the chart? First, isobars: smooth lines joining places with equal atmospheric pressure, measured in hectopascals — hPa. They work exactly like the contour lines you know from mapwork, except they map pressure instead of height. Around the letter H — a high-pressure cell — pressure rises toward the centre. Around the letter L — a low-pressure cell, or depression — pressure falls toward the centre.

Why does pressure matter so much? Because air behaves predictably around these cells. In a high, air sinks — and sinking air warms, dries out, and gives clear, settled weather. In a low, air rises — and rising air cools, its moisture condenses, and you get cloud and rain. High: calm and clear. Low: unsettled and wet. That single pairing unlocks half of every synoptic question.

The isobar spacing carries information too, just like contour spacing: isobars packed close together mean a steep pressure gradient — strong winds. Wide spacing, gentle winds.

And scattered across the chart you will see little circles with symbols branching off them — one for each weather station. Those are the station models, and each one compresses a full weather report into a thumbnail. Decoding them is our next job.

The questions for this section are with you now. Hold on to the two pairings they test: sinking air with clear settled weather and rising air with cloud and rain, and packed isobars with strong wind.

## Subtopic: Decoding the Station Model

The station model packs at least five observations around a small circle. Let us decode our example symbol by symbol.

The circle itself reports cloud cover, measured in eighths of the sky — oktas. The fraction of the circle shaded is the fraction of sky covered. Ours is six-eighths shaded: three-quarters of the sky is cloudy. Fully shaded means overcast, eight-eighths; an empty circle means clear sky.

To the upper left of the circle sits the air temperature: 18 degrees Celsius for our station — a mild day.

Below it, the dew point temperature: 16 degrees. The dew point is the temperature at which the air would become saturated — the temperature at which dew or cloud starts forming. Here is the key skill: compare dew point with air temperature. Ours are only two degrees apart. Air at 18 degrees needing to cool just two degrees to saturate is air already nearly full of moisture — humid air, with condensation, mist or rain close at hand. A wide gap — say 25 degrees and dew point 5 — means dry air. Narrow gap, moist air; wide gap, dry air. Write that comparison into every answer.

The wind comes as a shaft with feathers. The shaft flies INTO the circle from the direction the wind blows FROM. Our shaft comes from the north-west, so this is a north-westerly wind — and note carefully, wind is always named for its origin. The feathers on the shaft give speed: a full feather counts 10 knots, a half feather 5. Two full feathers: 20 knots — a fresh breeze, roughly 37 kilometres per hour.

Read our station in one breath now: mild at 18 degrees, very humid with the dew point at 16, sky three-quarters clouded, fresh north-westerly wind at 20 knots. Moist air streaming in from the north-west with thickening cloud... this station feels like weather is coming. Let us find out what.

This section's questions are in front of you. Every element of the model is worth naming precisely — the okta shading, the air temperature, the size of the dew point gap, and the wind's origin and speed — so answer each one in those terms.

## Subtopic: Finding the Cold Front

On South African charts, especially in winter, the troublemaker is the cold front — the leading edge of a mass of cold air pushing in from the south-west, usually attached to a low-pressure depression tracking south of the country.

On the chart, a cold front is drawn as a bold line studded with triangles — teeth. The triangles sit on the side the front is moving TOWARD: they point in the direction of movement, north-eastward across the country. (Its partner, the warm front, wears semicircles instead — rare over South Africa itself, but know the symbol.)

How do you confirm you have the right line? Look for the tell-tale signatures on either side of it. Ahead of the front — the north-eastern side — warm, moist air is being lifted: stations show exactly what ours showed. That north-westerly wind at 18 degrees with a high dew point and thickening cloud is the classic pre-frontal picture — warm air flowing in ahead of the advancing cold wedge.

At the front itself, the change is sharp: the cold air, being denser, drives under the warm air like a wedge and forces it violently upward. That forced ascent builds towering cumulonimbus clouds along the front line — the thunderheads that bring the front's heaviest weather.

The isobars kink sharply where they cross the front — another fingerprint to confirm your identification. And behind the front, everything transforms — which is precisely what the exam asks us to describe next.

Take this section's questions now. They ask you to justify an identification rather than merely make one, so answer each with the evidence found on both sides of the line, not with the symbol alone.

## Subtopic: The Weather Behind the Front — and the Exam Method

The question says: describe the weather BEHIND the cold front. Behind means the south-western side — the territory the front has already swept through, now occupied by the invading cold air. Describe it observation by observation, the way a station model would report it.

Temperature: distinctly colder — the warm air has been shoved up and away, and polar air has taken its place. A station that read 18 degrees before the front may read 8 or 10 after it.

Wind: swings around with the front's passage — the north-westerly ahead becomes a south-westerly behind. That wind swing, from NW to SW, is often the clearest sign in the data that the front has passed a station.

Pressure: rising. The front is attached to a low; once it passes, the low moves away eastward and the next high builds in — the barometer climbs.

Precipitation and cloud: showers in the front's immediate wake — cold air moving over a relatively warm surface stays unstable, giving cumulus build-ups and scattered showers — then progressively clearing skies as the high-pressure air settles in. Cold, showery, clearing: that is the post-frontal trio.

So a full-marks answer reads: "Behind the cold front the air is much colder, the wind has backed to south-westerly, pressure is rising, and showery weather is giving way to clearing skies as cold, stable air moves in."

Now the general method, your checklist for any synoptic question. One: find the pressure cells — label the highs and lows and recall sinking-clear, rising-wet. Two: read the isobar spacing for wind strength. Three: decode the station models — temperature, dew point gap, cloud okta, wind direction and speed. Four: locate fronts by their symbols and confirm with the evidence: warm moist NW flow ahead, cumulonimbus at the line, cold SW showers behind. Five: answer in observations, not vibes — quote the numbers, name the directions.

The synoptic chart returns in Grade 11 with Africa's weather and again in Grade 12 with mid-latitude and tropical cyclones — the biggest single questions on Paper 1. The chart never changes its language; you now speak it. Practise with today's real chart from the weather service — read a station near the coast and one inland, and tell yourself the story of tomorrow.

The final questions of this part are with you now. Answer them the way a memorandum reads: temperature, wind direction, pressure tendency and cloud, each one stated as an observation with its value or direction attached.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same chart from the pavement outside — same 18 degrees, same north-westerly, same front coming — told as the weather you actually feel arriving.

## Subtopic: Highs, Lows and Which Way the Air Is Going

Forget pressure as a number for a moment. Air has weight, and pressure is simply how heavily the air is leaning on you where you stand. A high-pressure cell is a place where the air is leaning down hard. A low is a place where it is leaning down less.

What matters is not the leaning, though — it is what the air is doing vertically, because that is what makes weather. In a high, air is slowly sinking towards the ground. Sinking air gets squashed and warms up, and warm air holds its moisture instead of letting it go, so clouds evaporate away and you get those clean, cold-nights-and-blue-skies winter days over the Karoo. In a low, air is rising. Rising air cools as it climbs, and cool air cannot hold its water, so the water lets go and becomes cloud — and then rain.

You have watched this happen in your own kitchen. Boil a kettle and look just at the spout: nothing visible, because the water vapour there is still hot. A few centimetres up, where it has cooled, it turns into a visible white cloud. That is a low-pressure system on a small budget. Rising and cooling makes cloud; sinking and warming clears it. Two words each: sink, clear; rise, rain.

The lines curling around the H and the L are called isobars, and they join up places where the air is leaning equally hard. Read them the way you read contour lines on a hill: they draw a pressure landscape, and wind is simply air rolling down that landscape from the high ground of the H towards the low ground of the L. So when those lines are squeezed together, the pressure hill is steep and the air runs down it fast — strong wind. Spread far apart, gentle slope, gentle breeze.

Good start — the questions for this piece are with you now. Whenever one asks about weather, first ask yourself which way the air is going, up or down, and the answer will usually be waiting there.

## Subtopic: The Little Circle That Holds a Whole Weather Report

Now those small circles dotted across the chart. Each one is a weather station, and each one is a whole weather report squeezed into something the size of a thumbnail. Take ours apart piece by piece.

The circle itself is the sky. Imagine cutting the sky above the station into eight slices, like a pizza, and counting how many slices have cloud in them. However many slices are cloudy, that much of the circle gets shaded in. Ours is shaded six slices out of eight — so three-quarters of the sky is covered, a properly grey day with some blue still showing. All eight shaded means overcast; an empty circle means not a cloud anywhere.

The number at the upper left is the plain air temperature: 18 degrees. Mild.

The number below it, 16 degrees, is the dew point, and this one is worth slowing down for, because it is really a wetness meter in disguise. You know what happens when you take a cold cooldrink out of the fridge on a summer day: the bottle sweats, because the air touching the cold glass has been cooled until it cannot hold its water any more. The dew point is the temperature at which that happens to the air around you. So the question that matters is never "what is the dew point" — it is "how far is the dew point from the temperature". Ours are 18 and 16, only two degrees apart, which means this air needs to cool by just two degrees before it starts making water. That is very humid air, and mist, cloud or rain is not far away. If the temperature were 25 and the dew point 5, the air would have twenty degrees of cooling to do before anything happened — bone dry, clear skies.

Last, the little arrow flying into the circle with feathers on its tail. It comes in from the direction the wind is coming from — ours flies in from the north-west, so we call it a north-westerly. Winds are named for where they come from, the same way you would say a person is from Gqeberha. That is not fussiness; where a wind comes from tells you what it is carrying. The feathers count the speed: a full feather is 10 knots, a half feather 5. Ours has two full feathers, so 20 knots, about 37 kilometres per hour — enough to move the washing on the line properly.

Well done — take the questions on this section now, and read every station the same way: sky, temperature, the gap, then the wind and where it is from.

## Subtopic: Before, During and After the Cold Front

Now put the two together, because our station has been telling us a story all along. Mild, very humid, sky filling with cloud, and a north-westerly wind pushing in. Something is on its way.

That something is a cold front. Picture a wedge of cold air coming up from the deep south-west, out of the cold ocean below the country, and shoving its way north-eastward across the land. Cold air is heavier than warm air, so it does not mix politely — it slides underneath, like pushing a spade under a heap of sand, and lifts the warm moist air straight up off the ground. Air that gets lifted like that cools fast, dumps its moisture, and builds those enormous piled-up thunder clouds that stand in a line along the front. On the chart, that line is drawn with little triangles along it, and the triangles point the way the front is travelling.

So the day comes in three acts, and you can feel every one of them. Before the front: warm for the season, sticky, a north-westerly breeze, and cloud thickening through the afternoon — exactly our station. At the front: the sky goes dark, the wind gusts, and the rain arrives in a serious burst rather than a drizzle. Behind the front: the temperature drops away sharply, from something like 18 degrees down to 8 or 10; the wind swings right round to a cold south-westerly coming off the sea; the barometer starts climbing again as the next high builds in; and the heavy rain breaks up into scattered showers before the sky finally clears. This is the Cape winter in a single sentence, and a day or two later the same front reaches Bloemfontein and Gauteng as a cold snap.

That gives you a test you can use on any single station, even when nobody tells you where the front is. Ask three witnesses. The wind: north-westerly means the front is still coming, south-westerly means it has already gone through. The temperature: mild means ahead of it, suddenly cold means behind it. The pressure: falling means it is approaching, rising means it has passed. Our station has a north-westerly wind, 18 degrees and thickening cloud — all three witnesses agree that the front is on its way.

Those are the last questions of the lesson, and they are with you now. Answer them with actual observations — the number of degrees, the direction of the wind, whether pressure is climbing or falling — because that is exactly what the exam pays for.
