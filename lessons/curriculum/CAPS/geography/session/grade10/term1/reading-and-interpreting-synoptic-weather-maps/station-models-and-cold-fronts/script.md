Welcome, student. Today we learn to read the weather map you see behind every TV forecaster: the synoptic chart. Our task comes straight from a typical exam: decode a station model showing a temperature of 18 degrees Celsius, a dew point of 16 degrees, cloud cover of six-eighths, and a north-westerly wind at 20 knots — then find the cold front on the chart and describe the weather behind it. By the end, that cluster of symbols will read as easily as a sentence.

## Subtopic: What a Synoptic Chart Shows

A synoptic chart is a snapshot of the atmosphere over a region at one exact moment — "synoptic" means seen together. Every element on the chart was observed at the same time at weather stations across the country, then plotted so the whole pattern becomes visible at once.

What lives on the chart? First, isobars: smooth lines joining places with equal atmospheric pressure, measured in hectopascals — hPa. They work exactly like the contour lines you know from mapwork, except they map pressure instead of height. Around the letter H — a high-pressure cell — pressure rises toward the centre. Around the letter L — a low-pressure cell, or depression — pressure falls toward the centre.

Why does pressure matter so much? Because air behaves predictably around these cells. In a high, air sinks — and sinking air warms, dries out, and gives clear, settled weather. In a low, air rises — and rising air cools, its moisture condenses, and you get cloud and rain. High: calm and clear. Low: unsettled and wet. That single pairing unlocks half of every synoptic question.

The isobar spacing carries information too, just like contour spacing: isobars packed close together mean a steep pressure gradient — strong winds. Wide spacing, gentle winds.

And scattered across the chart you will see little circles with symbols branching off them — one for each weather station. Those are the station models, and each one compresses a full weather report into a thumbnail. Decoding them is our next job.

## Subtopic: Decoding the Station Model

The station model packs at least five observations around a small circle. Let us decode our example symbol by symbol.

The circle itself reports cloud cover, measured in eighths of the sky — oktas. The fraction of the circle shaded is the fraction of sky covered. Ours is six-eighths shaded: three-quarters of the sky is cloudy. Fully shaded means overcast, eight-eighths; an empty circle means clear sky.

To the upper left of the circle sits the air temperature: 18 degrees Celsius for our station — a mild day.

Below it, the dew point temperature: 16 degrees. The dew point is the temperature at which the air would become saturated — the temperature at which dew or cloud starts forming. Here is the key skill: compare dew point with air temperature. Ours are only two degrees apart. Air at 18 degrees needing to cool just two degrees to saturate is air already nearly full of moisture — humid air, with condensation, mist or rain close at hand. A wide gap — say 25 degrees and dew point 5 — means dry air. Narrow gap, moist air; wide gap, dry air. Write that comparison into every answer.

The wind comes as a shaft with feathers. The shaft flies INTO the circle from the direction the wind blows FROM. Our shaft comes from the north-west, so this is a north-westerly wind — and note carefully, wind is always named for its origin. The feathers on the shaft give speed: a full feather counts 10 knots, a half feather 5. Two full feathers: 20 knots — a fresh breeze, roughly 37 kilometres per hour.

Read our station in one breath now: mild at 18 degrees, very humid with the dew point at 16, sky three-quarters clouded, fresh north-westerly wind at 20 knots. Moist air streaming in from the north-west with thickening cloud... this station feels like weather is coming. Let us find out what.

## Subtopic: Finding the Cold Front

On South African charts, especially in winter, the troublemaker is the cold front — the leading edge of a mass of cold air pushing in from the south-west, usually attached to a low-pressure depression tracking south of the country.

On the chart, a cold front is drawn as a bold line studded with triangles — teeth. The triangles sit on the side the front is moving TOWARD: they point in the direction of movement, north-eastward across the country. (Its partner, the warm front, wears semicircles instead — rare over South Africa itself, but know the symbol.)

How do you confirm you have the right line? Look for the tell-tale signatures on either side of it. Ahead of the front — the north-eastern side — warm, moist air is being lifted: stations show exactly what ours showed. That north-westerly wind at 18 degrees with a high dew point and thickening cloud is the classic pre-frontal picture — warm air flowing in ahead of the advancing cold wedge.

At the front itself, the change is sharp: the cold air, being denser, drives under the warm air like a wedge and forces it violently upward. That forced ascent builds towering cumulonimbus clouds along the front line — the thunderheads that bring the front's heaviest weather.

The isobars kink sharply where they cross the front — another fingerprint to confirm your identification. And behind the front, everything transforms — which is precisely what the exam asks us to describe next.

## Subtopic: The Weather Behind the Front — and the Exam Method

The question says: describe the weather BEHIND the cold front. Behind means the south-western side — the territory the front has already swept through, now occupied by the invading cold air. Describe it observation by observation, the way a station model would report it.

Temperature: distinctly colder — the warm air has been shoved up and away, and polar air has taken its place. A station that read 18 degrees before the front may read 8 or 10 after it.

Wind: swings around with the front's passage — the north-westerly ahead becomes a south-westerly behind. That wind swing, from NW to SW, is often the clearest sign in the data that the front has passed a station.

Pressure: rising. The front is attached to a low; once it passes, the low moves away eastward and the next high builds in — the barometer climbs.

Precipitation and cloud: showers in the front's immediate wake — cold air moving over a relatively warm surface stays unstable, giving cumulus build-ups and scattered showers — then progressively clearing skies as the high-pressure air settles in. Cold, showery, clearing: that is the post-frontal trio.

So a full-marks answer reads: "Behind the cold front the air is much colder, the wind has backed to south-westerly, pressure is rising, and showery weather is giving way to clearing skies as cold, stable air moves in."

Now the general method, your checklist for any synoptic question. One: find the pressure cells — label the highs and lows and recall sinking-clear, rising-wet. Two: read the isobar spacing for wind strength. Three: decode the station models — temperature, dew point gap, cloud okta, wind direction and speed. Four: locate fronts by their symbols and confirm with the evidence: warm moist NW flow ahead, cumulonimbus at the line, cold SW showers behind. Five: answer in observations, not vibes — quote the numbers, name the directions.
