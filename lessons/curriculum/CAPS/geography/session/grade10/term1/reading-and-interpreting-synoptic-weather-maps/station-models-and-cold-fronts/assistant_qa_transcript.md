**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! The first one breaks people's brains every year. If the wind arrow flies into the circle from the north-west, the air is clearly travelling towards the south-east. So why on earth do we call it a north-westerly?

**Tutor:** Because winds are named for where they come from, not where they are going, and that convention exists for a very practical reason. A wind's origin tells you what it is carrying. Over South Africa, a north-westerly ahead of a front has travelled over warmer land and ocean, so it arrives warm and moist — that is why our station reads 18 degrees with a dew point of 16 and a sky filling with cloud. A south-westerly behind the same front has come off the cold southern ocean, so it arrives cold and much drier, and the temperature at that station may fall to 8 or 10 degrees. Same station, two different winds, two completely different days. Name the source and you know the cargo. As for reading the symbol itself: the shaft is drawn flying inwards, so follow it outwards from the circle and see which compass direction it stretches towards — that is the origin, and that is the name. Then count the feathers on it: each full feather is 10 knots, each half feather 5, so two full feathers is 20 knots.

**Thandi:** Next question, and it is a fair guess that turns out to be wrong. Someone assumed high pressure must mean bad weather, because surely all that pressure squashes the clouds until it rains. Why is it the opposite?

**Tutor:** The answer is not in how hard the air is pressing down, but in which way it is moving vertically — that is what makes or unmakes cloud. In a high-pressure cell the air is slowly sinking towards the ground. As it sinks it is compressed and it warms, and warmer air can hold more moisture as invisible vapour, so any cloud in it evaporates away. The result is clear, settled, often windless weather, the sort that gives the interior its cold nights and cloudless winter mornings. In a low-pressure cell the air is rising. Rising air expands and cools, and cooling air reaches a point where it cannot keep its moisture in vapour form, so that moisture condenses into droplets — first cloud, then rain. Your kettle demonstrates the whole thing: right at the spout the vapour is hot and invisible, but a few centimetres higher, where it has cooled, it becomes a visible white cloud. Sinking and warming clears the sky; rising and cooling fills it. Sink, clear. Rise, rain.

**Thandi:** Here is a panic question. The exam gave one station and asked whether the cold front had already passed it. She froze, because how are you supposed to know from a single station?

**Tutor:** You can know, because a single station carries three separate witnesses and they all testify to the same thing. Witness one is the wind direction. Ahead of a South African cold front the wind is north-westerly, drawing warm moist air in from over the land and the warmer ocean; once the front has swept past, the wind swings round to south-westerly, dragging cold air up from the southern ocean. Witness two is the temperature. Mild for the season — our station's 18 degrees — means the front is still coming; a sharp drop to 8 or 10 degrees means it has already gone through. Witness three is the pressure tendency. Pressure falls as the low and its front approach, and rises again afterwards as the low moves away eastward and the next high builds in. Test our own station against all three: north-westerly wind, 18 degrees, dew point of 16, cloud thickening to six-eighths, and falling pressure. Three witnesses, one verdict — the front has not arrived yet. Always check all three, because any single witness can occasionally mislead you.

**Thandi:** Last one, and it is more honest than it sounds. She knows the definition of dew point off by heart and still has no idea what it is actually for.

**Tutor:** Then drop the definition and treat it as a moisture meter, because that is its whole job. The dew point is the temperature to which the air must cool before it starts turning its invisible water vapour into visible water. You have seen it work every summer: a cold bottle out of the fridge collects beads of water on the outside, because the air touching that cold glass has been chilled past its dew point and can no longer hold what it was carrying. Now here is the part that earns marks. The dew point on its own tells you almost nothing — the useful figure is the gap between it and the air temperature. Our station reads 18 degrees with a dew point of 16, a gap of only two degrees, so this air needs to cool by just two degrees before it starts making cloud. That is very humid air with rain close behind it, which is exactly what you expect ahead of a front. Compare a station reading 25 degrees with a dew point of 5: a twenty-degree gap, dry air, clear skies. So never quote the dew point alone in an answer. Quote the gap, and say whether it is small or large and what that means.
