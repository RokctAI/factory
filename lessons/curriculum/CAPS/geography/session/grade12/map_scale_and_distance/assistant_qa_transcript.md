**Student:** I always mess up the conversion — sometimes I divide by 1 000 to get metres and my answer is out by ten times. How do I stop doing that?

**Mandy:** You need the ladder chant, said the same way every time: "centi to metres, divide by a hundred; metres to kays, divide by a thousand." ÷100, then ÷1 000 — in that order, always. The slip happens when you rush and grab the wrong rung, so make the two rungs physical: write "cm → m → km" above your working and cross each arrow as you use it. And then let the sense check catch anything that slips through: 430 000 cm should land at 4,3 km. If you see 43 km, you know instantly a rung was skipped. The chant prevents; the sense check catches.

**Student:** Why is 1:10 000 called a LARGER scale than 1:50 000? Fifty thousand is obviously the bigger number!

**Mandy:** It trips everyone, because we're secretly comparing fractions, not whole numbers. A scale is really 1/10 000 versus 1/50 000 — and one ten-thousandth is the *bigger* fraction, the way one slice of a cake cut into 10 000 pieces beats a slice from 50 000 pieces. Bigger fraction = larger scale. Here's the payoff for remembering it: larger scale means everything on the map is drawn bigger, so you see a smaller area in more detail — that's your orthophoto map. Small ratio number, large scale, lots of detail. Chain those three together and the exam can't touch you.

**Student:** For the winding road question, my string measurement was 12,1 cm but my friend got 12,6 cm. Whose is right? That's a 250-metre difference!

**Mandy:** Welcome to the honest truth of practical mapwork: string measurements genuinely vary, and markers know it. Memos for curved-distance questions accept a *range* — usually a few millimetres either side of the official measurement — precisely because of this. What you CAN control: press the string firmly into each bend rather than cutting corners (corner-cutting always under-measures), mark your end point on the string with your thumbnail before lifting it, and measure the straightened string twice. Get your technique tight and you'll land inside the accepted range every time — that's what "right" means here.

**Student:** If they give me the real distance and ask for the map distance, do I need a whole new formula?

**Mandy:** No new formula — just drive the same machine in reverse! Forwards: map to ground, multiply by 50 000. Backwards: ground to map, divide by 50 000. The only discipline is converting the real distance into centimetres *first*, so the division hands you centimetres of map. Try it with our numbers: 4,3 km → 430 000 cm → ÷ 50 000 → 8,6 cm. You end exactly where the original question started, which is also a lovely way to check the forward calculation: run it backwards and see if you land on your own measurement.
