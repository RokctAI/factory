**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and I promise it comes up every year: why do tropical cyclones never hit Cape Town? We have an ocean right there!

**Tutor:** Check Cape Town against the birth conditions and it fails two of them outright. Condition one is the fuel: sea surface temperature of at least twenty-six and a half degrees. The Atlantic off Cape Town is chilled by the cold Benguela current flowing up from the Southern Ocean — on a warm day it struggles to reach sixteen degrees. That is a fuel tank running on empty; no latent heat, no engine. Condition two is position: cyclones are born in the tropics and steered westward by the trade winds, then curve poleward and die once they cross onto cooler water beyond about thirty degrees of latitude. Cape Town sits at thirty-four degrees south on the wrong ocean entirely — the storms of the south-west Indian Ocean track toward Madagascar, Mozambique and sometimes northern KwaZulu-Natal, where the warm Agulhas current keeps the water hot enough to feed them. So the exam answer has two clean sentences: the west coast's cold Benguela water cannot supply the latent heat a cyclone needs, and the cyclone tracks lie in the tropical Indian Ocean far to the north-east. Cape Town's storms are mid-latitude cyclones — a different machine from a different lesson.

***

**Mandy:** Next: I wrote that the eye is the most dangerous part of the storm because it is the centre, and I lost the marks. What should I have said?

**Tutor:** The examiner wants the physics, and the physics says the centre is paradoxically the calmest place in the storm. In the eye, air is slowly sinking, and sinking air compresses, warms and dries — cloud evaporates, wind falls away, sometimes the sky clears completely. The violence lives one step outward, in the eye wall, that ring of towering cumulonimbus where all the inward-spiralling air finally turns upward: strongest winds, heaviest rain, lowest visibility. So on the cross-section diagram, label the calm sinking core as the eye and the furious rising ring as the eye wall, and never swap them. Now, where your instinct was not entirely wrong: the eye is dangerous, but socially rather than meteorologically. People feel the calm, believe the storm has ended, and leave shelter — then the far side of the eye wall arrives within the hour at full strength, with the wind blowing from the opposite direction because they are now on the other side of a rotating system. Structures and people caught in the open by that reversal suffer badly. So write it as two ideas: meteorologically the calmest zone, behaviourally a deadly trap. That pairing is exactly what a full-mark answer looks like.

***

**Mandy:** Here is one that confused the group: why does the storm get weaker over land but Malawi still drowned? Surely weaker means safer?

**Tutor:** Weaker refers to wind, and wind was never the storm's deadliest weapon. When a cyclone makes landfall, the engine loses its fuel line — no warm ocean beneath it means no evaporation and no latent heat — and surface friction grinds the winds down within a day or so. But the storm arrives carrying an enormous cargo of moisture, and a dying cyclone often slows down, which means all of that moisture falls on the same place for longer. That is precisely what happened with Freddy in March twenty twenty-three: by the time its remnants reached Malawi the winds were modest, but it unloaded months' worth of rain in days. On steep slopes stripped of trees, the water had nothing to hold it — flash floods and mudslides tore through settlements around Blantyre, and that is where most of Freddy's hundreds of deaths occurred, far from the coast the storm first struck. The exam lesson is to separate the hazards: wind and surge dominate at the coast during landfall, while inland the killer is rainfall — floods and mass movement — sometimes days later. An answer that tracks the danger inland as the storm decays shows the examiner genuine understanding of the system's whole life.

**Mandy:** Last one: for the case study, how much detail do I actually need? Names and dates, or the whole story?

**Tutor:** Build it on a five-finger skeleton, because that matches how the marks are allocated. Finger one: name and date — Tropical Cyclone Freddy, February to March twenty twenty-three. Finger two: track — formed in the eastern Indian Ocean, crossed the entire basin, struck Madagascar, then Mozambique, looped back over the warm Mozambique Channel to refuel, struck Mozambique a second time near Quelimane, then drenched Malawi. Finger three: environmental impacts — catastrophic flooding, mudslides on deforested slopes, crops and topsoil stripped, rivers re-routed. Finger four: human impacts — hundreds of deaths, the majority in Malawi, hundreds of thousands displaced, cholera outbreaks in flooded settlements, roads, power and clinics destroyed. Finger five: responses — early warnings from the regional forecasting centre, evacuations, international relief, and the longer rebuild with stronger housing and reforestation. Two marks of insight lift it further: mention that Freddy's channel loop demonstrates the warm-water fuel principle in action, and close with the vulnerability point — the same storm produces a far larger disaster where housing is informal, slopes are bare and warning systems are weak. Five fingers plus those two sentences and the case-study question is fully banked, whichever way it is phrased.
