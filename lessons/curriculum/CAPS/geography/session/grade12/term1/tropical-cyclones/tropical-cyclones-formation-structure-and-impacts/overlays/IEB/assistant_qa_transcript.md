**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it is the big one: why do these storms hammer Mozambique and Madagascar every single season but never make it down to Durban? Durban's sea is warm.

**Tutor:** Warm, but not warm enough, and that difference is the entire answer. A tropical cyclone needs a sea surface above roughly twenty-six and a half degrees, warm to fifty metres down, because the storm drinks its energy from evaporation. The Mozambique Channel and the ocean east of Madagascar sit above that threshold all summer — a permanently full fuel tank. Off KwaZulu-Natal, the Agulhas current is warm by swimming standards but usually hovers just below the cyclone threshold, and it narrows against a steep shelf, so a storm tracking south finds its fuel gauge dropping with every hour of travel. Add two more brakes: as the storm moves poleward the Coriolis parameter and the upper-air westerlies change, and wind shear increases — the shear that decapitates the rising towers. So the usual life story is: a system rides down the Channel, weakens as the water cools, and dies or curves away east before reaching South African shores. What DOES reach us is the storm's outer envelope: tropical moisture flung inland, which is why a cyclone parked over southern Mozambique can flood the Lowveld and northern KwaZulu-Natal without its eye ever crossing the border. Say it in an exam as a fuel argument — the storm follows the warm water, and the warm-enough water runs out north of us — and you have both the pattern and the mechanism.

***

**Mandy:** Next: I get the latent heat idea in words, but what is actually happening in the eye wall? Why does condensation make the pressure DROP? It feels like adding rain should make things heavier.

**Tutor:** Beautiful confusion, because untangling it is the whole engine. Follow one parcel of air. Over the hot sea it loads up with water vapour — that vapour carries hidden energy, the energy that was spent tearing water molecules free of the ocean. The parcel spirals inward and, at the eye wall, turns upward. As it rises it cools, the vapour condenses into droplets, and at that instant the hidden energy is handed back as heat. So the rising column in the eye wall is being continuously reheated from within, like a hot-air balloon with a burner that never switches off. A heated column is a light column: it weighs less, so the pressure at its base — the surface pressure under the eye wall — falls. Falling surface pressure steepens the pressure gradient, which drags the surrounding air inward faster, which delivers more vapour to the chimney, which releases more heat, which lightens the column further. That is the self-feeding loop, and it is why these storms can deepen explosively over a warm sea. The rain itself does fall out, but its weight is trivial beside the buoyancy of the heated column. One sentence to carry: condensation does not add weight to the storm — it adds heat to the chimney, and a hotter chimney means a deeper low.

**Mandy:** Third one: in the case study, why did so many of Idai's deaths happen hundreds of kilometres from the sea, up in the Zimbabwean mountains? I thought cyclones were a coastal problem.

**Tutor:** That assumption is precisely what made Idai so deadly, and correcting it is worth real marks. A landfalling cyclone loses its wind first — friction and the cut fuel line see to that — but it keeps its water. Idai came ashore carrying moisture evaporated from an entire ocean crossing, then slowed to a crawl, and a slow storm is a stationary rain machine: the same catchments were hosed for days. Now put mountains in the path. When that saturated air is forced up the eastern highlands around Chimanimani, orographic lifting wrings even more rain out of it — the slopes concentrate the downpour exactly where the terrain is steepest. Then the human layer: hillsides stripped of trees for farming and fuel had lost the root systems that bind soil, so saturated slopes liquefied into mudslides that buried villages in the night; rivers funnelled the runoff into valleys where settlements stood on the flood line. So the full chain is: dying wind, undiminished moisture, stalling track, mountain lifting, deforested slopes, valley settlement — natural hazard stacked on human vulnerability. In any cyclone answer, give the coast its surge and wind, but follow the water inland: with these storms, the rain frequently out-kills the sea.

**Mandy:** Last one: if I get a satellite image in a test and panic, what is the fastest safe way to tell a tropical cyclone from a mid-latitude one?

**Tutor:** Use a three-question drill that works even with a shaking hand. Question one: what is the shape? A tropical cyclone is compact and nearly circular — a tight pinwheel of solid white cloud, and in a mature system a dark dot at the dead centre, the eye. A mid-latitude cyclone is an open comma: a curled head with a long tail of cloud streaming away along the cold front, and never an eye. Question two: where is it sitting? Read the latitude off the image or the surrounding coastlines. Tropical systems live equatorward of about thirty degrees, over warm ocean — for us, east of Madagascar or in the Mozambique Channel. Mid-latitude systems live poleward of thirty degrees, south of the country. Question three: what season does the question imply? Tropical cyclones in our region belong to the warm months, November to April; the great frontal commas batter the Cape chiefly in winter. Shape, address, season — any two of the three settle it, and writing all three earns the justification marks. And if fronts are drawn on an accompanying chart, the case is closed instantly: fronts mean mid-latitude, because a tropical cyclone has no fronts at all — it is one air mass spinning, not two air masses fighting.
