# Part 1 — Expert

A tropical cyclone is the most violent weather system on Earth, and for southern Africa it is not an abstraction: Idai flattened Beira, and Freddy crossed the Mozambique Channel twice. This session covers the system exactly as the DBE examines it: the birth conditions and regional names, the staged growth from disturbance to full cyclone, the anatomy of eye, eye wall and rain bands with the weather sequence they deliver, and finally the southern African case study, the satellite identification and the management strategies that close the question. Keep one idea burning underneath everything: a tropical cyclone is a machine for converting warm ocean water into wind, and every fact in this lesson follows from that single conversion.

## Subtopic: Birth Conditions, Regions and Names

Tropical cyclones form over warm oceans between roughly five and thirty degrees of latitude, on either side of the equator — but never on the equator itself. Each condition earns marks, so take them one at a time.

Condition one: sea surface temperature of at least twenty-six and a half to twenty-seven degrees Celsius, and warm water reaching some fifty metres deep. The warm ocean is the fuel tank. Evaporation loads the air with water vapour; when that vapour condenses, it releases latent heat, and latent heat is the engine's power stroke.

Condition two: a location at least five degrees from the equator. The Coriolis force is zero on the equator and strengthens poleward; without it, converging air cannot be set spinning. That is why no cyclone ever crosses the equator and none is born on it.

Condition three: converging surface winds — usually within the easterly trade-wind belt — to start air flowing inward and upward, often beginning as a cluster of thunderstorms called a tropical disturbance.

Condition four: weak wind shear, meaning winds that do not change much with height. Strong upper winds decapitate the growing storm by shearing its rising towers apart; calm upper air lets the chimney build straight and tall.

The same machine carries different regional names, and examiners test the vocabulary: hurricanes in the North Atlantic and eastern Pacific, typhoons in the north-western Pacific, and cyclones in the Indian Ocean and around Australia. Our storms form in the south-west Indian Ocean, east of Madagascar, in the warm season from November to April, and they are named alphabetically from a prepared list each season — so a storm called Ana came early in the season and a storm called Freddy came later. In the Southern Hemisphere they rotate clockwise around their intense low-pressure centre, and they drift from east to west in the trade winds before curving poleward.

Take this section's questions now: the four birth conditions and the job each performs, the regional names matched to their oceans, and the season and rotation for our part of the world.

## Subtopic: Stages of Growth and the Anatomy of the Storm

Growth is classified by sustained wind speed, in four stages. Stage one: a tropical disturbance — an unremarkable cluster of thunderstorms over warm water. Stage two: a tropical depression — the cluster organises around a weak low, winds below about sixty-three kilometres per hour. Stage three: a tropical storm — winds from sixty-three to one hundred and eighteen kilometres per hour; at this point the system receives its name. Stage four: a tropical cyclone proper — sustained winds above one hundred and eighteen kilometres per hour, and the signature eye appears. Beyond that, intensity categories climb with wind speed to the catastrophic category five.

Now the anatomy, built from the centre outward, because the cross-section diagram is a standing exam favourite. At the very centre sits the eye: a core of calm, twenty to fifty kilometres wide, where air is sinking, not rising. Sinking air warms and dries, so the eye is eerily clear and almost windless — the deadly deception that lures people out of shelter mid-storm.

Wrapped tightly around it stands the eye wall: a ring of the tallest cumulonimbus towers, and the most violent real estate on the planet. Here the inward-spiralling air finally turns upward in a furious rising sleeve; the strongest winds, often above two hundred kilometres per hour, and the heaviest rain live in the eye wall.

Beyond the eye wall, spiral rain bands curve outward for hundreds of kilometres like the arms of a galaxy — bands of thunderstorm cloud separated by relatively quieter gaps, so the storm delivers rain in pulses, heavier and more frequent toward the centre.

Feel the engine running through this anatomy: warm ocean evaporates; moist air spirals inward, clockwise, gathering speed; in the eye wall it rockets upward, condensing and releasing latent heat, which lowers the central pressure further, which pulls the inward spiral harder — a self-feeding loop. Air exhausted at the top spreads outward, and a little sinks down the middle to form the calm eye. Central pressure can fall below nine hundred and sixty hectopascals — far deeper than any mid-latitude low on a South African chart.

Attend to this section's questions before moving on: the four stages and the wind speeds that separate them, the eye, eye wall and rain bands each matched to its weather, and the latent-heat loop that powers the whole machine.

## Subtopic: The Weather Sequence and Why the Storm Dies

An exam question loves to park you in a coastal town — say Beira in Mozambique — and march the cyclone over you. Learn the sequence as an ordered experience.

First, the approach: a day or more of rising swell on the sea, high thin cloud thickening, pressure beginning to slide, winds freshening in pulses as the outer rain bands sweep through — squall, gap, stronger squall.

Then the front half of the storm: the bands come faster and harder, wind climbs to destructive strength, rain becomes blinding, and the sea rises into a storm surge — the dome of ocean pushed up by low pressure and driven ashore by the wind. The surge, not the wind, is historically the biggest killer in tropical cyclones, drowning low-lying coasts under metres of seawater.

Then the eye: within an hour, near-calm and open sky. Pressure is at its minimum. The temptation to emerge is lethal, because the eye passes in twenty minutes to an hour and the far eye wall arrives with the full violence resumed — and with the wind blowing from the opposite direction, since you are now on the other side of a clockwise-rotating system. Structures loosened by the first half are torn off by the reversed second half.

Then the back half and departure: a mirrored retreat — eye wall, rain bands weakening, pressure climbing, seas easing over a day or two, but rivers now rising inland as the rainfall drains.

Why does the storm eventually die? Cut the fuel line. Landfall is the classic death: over land there is no warm ocean to evaporate, friction with the surface slows the winds, and the engine starves — though the dying storm can still dump catastrophic rain far inland, which is exactly what drowned the Zimbabwean highlands during Idai. Moving over cold water poleward of thirty degrees kills the storm the same way, and strong wind shear aloft can tear a storm apart even over warm seas.

The questions on this section are with you now: the ordered sequence from swell to second eye wall, the storm surge and why it kills, the false calm of the eye with the wind reversal, and the three ways the engine starves.

## Subtopic: Case Study, Satellite Identification and Management

The DBE requires one recent southern African case study; Cyclone Freddy is the strongest current choice. Freddy crossed almost the entire southern Indian Ocean in February and March of twenty twenty-three, one of the longest-lived tropical cyclones ever recorded. It made landfall over Madagascar, then over Mozambique — and then looped back over the warm Mozambique Channel, refuelled, and struck a second time near Quelimane, before dumping extreme rainfall over Malawi. The refuelling loop is the perfect exam illustration of the fuel principle: back over warm water, the dying engine restarted. Impacts: hundreds of deaths, the worst in Malawi from flooding and mudslides on deforested slopes; hundreds of thousands displaced; cholera outbreaks in flooded settlements; roads, crops and power systems destroyed in a region with little capacity to absorb the loss. If your class prepared Idai of twenty nineteen instead — Beira devastated, massive inland flooding in Mozambique and Zimbabwe — the analytical skeleton is identical: name, date, track, impacts on environment and people, responses.

Identification on imagery and charts: on a satellite image a tropical cyclone is a dense, near-circular mass of brilliant white cloud with spiral arms and, when mature, a dark central dot — the eye. Distinguish it from a mid-latitude system in one look: the tropical cyclone is a tight circle with an eye, found in the tropics over warm ocean; the mid-latitude cyclone is an open comma with trailing fronts, found poleward of thirty degrees. On a synoptic chart the tropical cyclone appears as tightly packed circular isobars around an intense low east of Madagascar or in the Mozambique Channel, marked with its name; South African coastal charts show them approaching from the north-east.

Precaution and management, the mark-rich finish: before the storm — early-warning systems and forecast tracks from regional centres, cyclone-resistant building codes, mangrove belts preserved as natural surge breaks, evacuation plans and drills, emergency kits. During — move to shelters on high ground, stay indoors through the eye, keep listening to official broadcasts. After — clean water first to prevent cholera, restore power and clinics, replant and rebuild with better standards. Grade the answer up by noting vulnerability: informal housing, deforested slopes and floodplain settlement turn the same storm into a far larger disaster in poor regions — hazard is natural, disaster is partly social.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: Freddy's track and impacts, the one-look satellite distinction, and two strategies each for before, during and after.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same monster, rebuilt from a pot of boiling water, a spinning netball player and a dangerous quiet moment — the pictures that hold the facts still.

## Subtopic: The Pot, the Spin and the Birth Rules

Start in a kitchen. A pot of water boils on the stove, and steam rises. Where does the energy in that steam come from? From the plate, through the water. Now stretch the picture: the tropical ocean in summer is the pot, heated for months until the top fifty metres sits above twenty-six and a half degrees. The steam is water vapour rising off that warm sea. And here is the physics in one line: when steam turns back into water — when vapour condenses into cloud — it gives back all the heat it took to evaporate. That returned heat is called latent heat, and it is the cyclone's petrol. No warm ocean, no petrol, no cyclone. That single sentence explains almost everything else in this lesson.

But a pot of steam is not a storm; it needs spin. Picture a netball player pirouetting with arms stretched out, then pulling her arms in — she spins faster. Air rushing inward toward low pressure does the same, but only if something bends its path into a curve first. That something is the spin of the Earth itself, the Coriolis effect, and it has one dead zone: the equator, where it is zero. So cyclones are never born on the equator and never cross it — the dance floor right at the middle line is closed.

Two smaller rules finish the birth certificate. The upper winds must be gentle, or they knock the growing tower over like a bully flicking a stack of blocks. And the whole thing starts from an ordinary cluster of thunderstorms that happens to sit over that hot water long enough to organise.

Same beast, different passports: hurricane in America's ocean, typhoon in Asia's, cyclone in ours — the Indian Ocean, east of Madagascar, in our summer, November to April, each one named from an alphabetical list so Ana comes before Freddy.

Your questions for this section are with you now: the pot and the petrol, the netball spin and the closed equator, and the names on the passports.

## Subtopic: The Doughnut With the Hole in the Middle

Now build the grown storm as a shape you can hold: a giant doughnut of cloud, hundreds of kilometres wide, with a strange quiet hole in the middle.

The hole is the eye. Inside it, air is gently sinking, and sinking air eats cloud, so the eye is clear, almost windless, sometimes even sunny — twenty to fifty kilometres of impossible calm in the middle of chaos.

The ring of dough around the hole is the eye wall, and it is the worst place on Earth. All the air that has been spiralling inward across the ocean finally turns upward here, in one furious sleeve of rising cloud. Strongest winds, heaviest rain, tallest thunderheads — all in the eye wall.

Trailing off the doughnut, long curved arms of cloud spiral outward — the rain bands. They cross a coastal town like the arms of a slowly turning galaxy: squall, breather, bigger squall, shorter breather, each band worse than the last as the centre closes in.

March that shape over a town by the sea and you get the famous sequence. First the sea grows angry before the sky does — big swells arriving from a storm still far away. Then the arms: rain in pulses, wind climbing each time. Then the eye wall: the roof-test, the worst hours. Then, suddenly — silence. Blue sky. This is the trap the lesson begs you to remember: it is the hole in the doughnut, not the end of the storm. In twenty minutes to an hour the other side of the ring arrives, just as violent, with the wind now blowing the opposite way — so the wall your house braced against the first half gets hit from behind in the second half.

One more sea-monster travels with the storm: the storm surge. The low pressure lets the sea bulge upward like fizzy drink rising in a shaken bottle when the cap loosens, and the wind shovels that bulge onto the land — metres of ocean walking ashore. More people drown in the surge than die in the wind. Low coasts like Beira fear the water, not the gale.

Take the questions for this section now: hole, ring and arms each matched to their weather, the trap of the quiet middle, and the wall of sea that does the real killing.

## Subtopic: Freddy, the Fuel Gauge and Being Ready

Hold one storm as your story for the exam: Freddy, twenty twenty-three. Freddy was born far out in the Indian Ocean and travelled west for weeks like a slow missile, all the way to Madagascar and then Mozambique. Then it did the thing that makes it unforgettable: it wandered back out over the warm Mozambique Channel — back over the hot pot — refuelled, and hit a second time, before drowning huge parts of Malawi in rain. Hundreds died, most of them in Malawi, where floods and mudslides tore down hillsides stripped of trees. Whole villages lost crops, roads, clinics; cholera followed the dirty water. Learn Freddy as a fuel story: over warm water the gauge fills and the storm strengthens; over land or cold water the gauge drains and the storm dies. Freddy simply found a filling station on its way out.

How do you spot the monster from space? A tropical cyclone on a satellite image is a tight white spiral pinwheel with a dark dot in the centre — the eye. Its cousin from the Cape lessons, the mid-latitude storm, looks completely different: an open comma with a long tail, no eye, much further south. Circle with a dot: tropical. Comma with a tail: mid-latitude. That one-line comparison is worth marks nearly every year.

And because we can see it days away, nobody needs to be surprised — which is where management earns its marks. Before: listen to the warnings and the forecast track, know your evacuation route to high ground, keep an emergency kit, protect the mangroves that break the surge the way a thorn hedge breaks wind. During: stay in shelter, and do not step outside in the quiet middle — wait for the second half. After: clean water first, because cholera kills after the wind is gone; then clinics, power, replanting, and rebuilding stronger. And say the wise sentence that lifts an answer to full marks: the storm is natural, but the size of the disaster is human — houses on floodplains, hills without trees and weak warning systems turn the same wind into a far bigger tragedy.

Here come the final questions of the lesson, right now: Freddy's double landfall and what refuelled it, the pinwheel against the comma, and the before-during-after list with the golden rule about the quiet middle. Take them — this is the topic where preparation literally saves lives, including your marks.
