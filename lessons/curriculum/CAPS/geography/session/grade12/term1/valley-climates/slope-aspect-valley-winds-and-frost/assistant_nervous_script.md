Hey, it's Mandy — valley climates has a reputation for being fiddly, all those Greek wind names and layer diagrams. Here is the secret: this is the most common-sense topic on the whole paper. It is literally about which side of the braai is warm and where the cold air sinks.

Two pictures carry everything. Picture one: your palm. Hold it flat toward a heater and it cooks; hold it edge-on and it barely warms. That is aspect — the north-facing slope holds its palm flat to our northern midday sun. Picture two: the bathtub. At night cold air drains down both walls like water from two taps and fills the valley floor. Cold bath at the bottom, warm shelf partway up — that shelf is the thermal belt, and everything valuable in the valley sits on it.

The vocabulary is just labels on those pictures. Anabatic: the day breeze going UP — anabatic ascends. Katabatic: the night drain going down. Inversion: cold below, warm above — the bath. Frost pocket: the bath below zero. Radiation fog: the bath reaching its dew point.

Tonight, draw one cross-section of a valley. Shade the sunny wall, arrow the night drainage, colour the cold pond blue and the thermal belt orange, and plant tiny orchards on the orange band. One drawing, five exam answers.

You have felt every piece of this topic in real life — the cold dip in the road at night, the fog in the low stretch, the vineyards halfway up the slope. Trust what you already know, put names on it, and go collect these marks. You've got this!
