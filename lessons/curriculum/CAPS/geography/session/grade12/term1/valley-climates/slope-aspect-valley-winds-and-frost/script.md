# Part 1 — Expert

Valley climates are the smallest weather systems on the syllabus and among the most reliably examined, because everything in them follows from two mechanisms you can reason out in the exam room: which slope faces the sun, and which way air drains at night. This session builds the topic in four moves: slope aspect and the temperature difference it creates in South African valleys, the daily rhythm of anabatic and katabatic winds, the night-time trio of inversions, frost pockets and radiation fog with the thermal belt above them, and finally the human geography — why the vineyard, the orchard, the farmhouse and the informal settlement each sit where they do.

## Subtopic: Slope Aspect — Which Side Gets the Sun

Aspect is the direction a slope faces. That single word decides how much solar energy a slope receives, and in a valley running east to west, it splits the two sides into different climates.

Work the geometry for South Africa. We live in the Southern Hemisphere, so the midday sun stands in the NORTHERN half of the sky. A slope that faces north therefore leans toward the sun: rays strike it at a high angle, close to perpendicular, so each square metre of ground catches a concentrated beam. A slope facing south leans away from the sun: the same rays strike at a glancing angle, smeared across a wider area, so each square metre receives less energy. Add the daily bonus — the north-facing slope also catches sunlight for more hours — and the verdict is fixed: in the Southern Hemisphere, north-facing slopes are the warm slopes; south-facing slopes are the cool, shaded, moister slopes. Reverse it for the Northern Hemisphere, and be ready for the exam to test exactly that reversal.

The consequences are visible from the road anywhere in the Cape fold mountains or a Karoo poort. The warm north-facing slope: drier soil, faster evaporation, grassier and more sparsely vegetated, snow melting first. The cool south-facing slope: damper soil, denser and greener vegetation, sometimes forest in the kloofs, snow lying longest. Farmers read aspect like a menu — vineyards and peach orchards on sun-facing slopes, timber and dairy pasture on the shaded side.

A precision point worth marks: aspect matters most in winter, when the sun is lowest and the angle difference between the two slopes is greatest, and it matters most in east-west valleys, where one wall faces due north and the other due south. A north-south valley shares the sun more equally between its walls.

Take this section's questions now: the definition of aspect, the Southern Hemisphere geometry that warms the north-facing wall, and the visible differences between the two slopes.

## Subtopic: The Valley's Daily Breathing — Anabatic and Katabatic Winds

A valley breathes on a daily cycle: air flows up the slopes by day and drains down them by night. The two winds carry Greek names, and the exam wants both the names and the mechanisms.

Daytime: the anabatic wind, the upslope breeze. After sunrise, the valley SIDES heat faster than the air above the valley centre, because the sun strikes the slopes directly. Air touching the warm slopes heats by conduction, becomes less dense, and slides upward along the slope like a bubble following a tilted ceiling. By early afternoon a steady upslope breeze blows on the sunny wall — strongest on the north-facing slope, naturally, because that wall is hottest. Anabatic air is rising air, and rising air can build cloud: the puffs of cumulus that crown ridgelines on summer afternoons are anabatic winds made visible.

Night-time: the katabatic wind, the downslope drainage. After sunset the slopes lose heat rapidly by long-wave radiation to a clear sky. The air touching them chills, becomes denser and heavier than the free air at the same height, and begins to slide downhill under gravity — a cold, gentle, persistent creep down both walls, gathering in the valley floor. Katabatic flow is strongest on clear, calm winter nights, when radiation loss is fastest and no regional wind stirs the pot.

Hold the asymmetry: the anabatic wind is driven by heating and rises; the katabatic wind is driven by cooling and gravity, and sinks. A memory anchor from the lesson: anabatic ascends — both start with A; katabatic collapses downhill.

Where does the cold katabatic air go? It pools. The valley floor fills with cold air from both walls the way a basin fills with water from two taps, and that ponded cold air manufactures the night-time weather of the next section — the inversion, the frost pocket and the fog.

Attend to this section's questions before moving on: each wind matched to its time of day, its driving mechanism and its direction, and where the night's cold air finally collects.

## Subtopic: Night in the Valley — Inversion, Thermal Belt, Frost and Fog

Follow one clear winter night to its morning, because this sequence is the heart of every valley-climate exam question.

Evening: katabatic drainage fills the valley floor with cold, dense air. By the small hours, the coldest air in the entire valley lies at the BOTTOM, with warmer air resting on top of it. Temperature now increases with height above the floor — the normal pattern is upside down. This is a valley temperature inversion, a surface inversion born from radiation cooling and cold-air drainage.

Climb the slope in your mind and feel the layers. The floor: coldest, often below freezing before dawn. The mid-slope: strikingly warmer — this band, sitting above the ponded cold air but below the radiatively cooling upper slopes, is the thermal belt, the warmest zone of the night-time valley. Above it, the upper slopes and ridge cool again toward the free-air temperature.

The floor's cold pond breeds two hazards. First, the frost pocket: where ponded air drops below zero, water vapour freezes onto every cold surface, and the valley bottom whitens while the mid-slope stays frost-free. Any hollow that traps drainage air — a depression, a dip behind a road embankment, a bend in the valley — becomes a frost pocket. Second, radiation fog: chill the ponded moist air to its dew point and its vapour condenses into droplets — a fog formed by radiative cooling, hugging the valley floor, thickest at dawn, burning off from the top down as the morning sun works on it. Anyone who has driven the N1 through a Boland winter morning, dropping out of sunshine into a river of white in each valley, has seen radiation fog mapping the inversions.

The inversion also traps whatever enters it: smoke from morning fires and dust accumulate under the warm lid, which is why valley towns wear a grey blanket on winter mornings — a miniature of the pollution behaviour the urban-climates lesson develops at city scale.

The questions on this section are with you now: the inversion and how drainage builds it, the thermal belt's position and why it stays warm, and the recipes for frost pockets and radiation fog.

## Subtopic: People in the Valley — Farming, Settlement and Sensible Planning

Every mechanism so far converts directly into human decisions, and the exam's favourite phrasing — explain the location of the orchard, the vineyard, the town — is answered by mapping people onto the physics.

Farming first. Frost-sensitive crops — deciduous fruit, citrus, vineyards — belong on the THERMAL BELT, the frost-free mid-slope shelf. Plant them on the valley floor and one clear June night in a frost pocket kills the blossom and the season's income; plant them too high and the growing season shortens. That is why the peach blocks and vine rows of the Hex River and Elgin valleys stripe the lower-middle slopes, and why aspect chooses the side: table grapes ripen on the warm north-facing wall, while cooler-climate varieties and orchards needing winter chill can use the southern aspect. Hardy crops — pasture, lucerne, vegetables that tolerate cold — take the risky floor, where the deep alluvial soils and the river compensate. Some farmers fight the frost directly: wind machines that stir the inversion's warm upper air down into the orchard, orchard heaters, even helicopters over high-value blocks on critical nights — each one a deliberate assault on the inversion structure, which is exactly how to justify it in an exam answer.

Settlement follows the same logic. Traditional farmhouses and many historic Cape towns sit on the lower slopes above the flood line and above the worst frost — on or near the thermal belt, sheltered from regional winds by the valley walls. Roads and railways naturally follow the flat floor, accepting the fog risk; that is why winter pile-ups happen on low-lying freeway stretches, and why fog warnings concentrate there.

Planning failures make the sharpest exam material. Informal settlements and low-cost housing pushed onto cheap valley-floor land sit in the coldest, foggiest, most flood-prone and most smoke-trapped zone of the entire landscape — higher heating costs, higher respiratory illness, higher flood losses, borne by those least able to carry them. Sensible planning reads the valley: housing on the mid-slope, industry and smoky activities OUT of the inversion pond, floor reserved for fields, parks and the river's floodplain.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: crops matched to floor, belt and slope with reasons, the settlement logic of the thermal belt, and one planning failure with its fix.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole valley again, using nothing but a braai, a bathtub and Gran's electric blanket — the version that answers exam questions at midnight.

## Subtopic: The Sunny Wall and the Shady Wall

Stand in a valley that runs east to west, early on a winter morning, and feel the two walls. One wall is already warm in the sun; the other is still cold in shadow. Nothing in this topic is more complicated than working out which is which.

Here is the trick. In South Africa the midday sun is never overhead — it hangs in the NORTHERN sky. Face north at noon and the sun is in front of you. So the slope that FACES north is the one leaning toward the sun, catching the rays full in the face, like holding your palm up flat to a heater. The slope facing south leans away, catching the same rays at a skimming angle, like holding your palm edge-on to the heater — same heater, far less warmth on the skin. Palm flat, palm edge-on: that is the whole physics of aspect.

So the north-facing wall is the braai side of the valley: warm, dry, quick to lose its frost, covered in grass and vines and sun-loving crops. The south-facing wall is the fridge side: cool, damp, green, holding its snow for weeks, good for forests and pasture. Farmers have never needed the textbook for this — look at any Boland valley and read the answer planted in rows: vineyards sunning themselves on one wall, pine plantations keeping cool on the other.

Two small print items for full marks. The difference between the walls is biggest in winter, when the sun is low and the skimming angle is most extreme. And if the valley runs north-south instead, both walls get their share of morning and afternoon sun, so the contrast fades. And always say WHICH hemisphere you are in — flip the answer for Europe, and expect the exam to test the flip.

Your questions for this section are with you now: palm flat against palm edge-on, braai side against fridge side, and the winter and direction small print. Feel the heater on your hand and you cannot go wrong.

## Subtopic: The Bathtub That Fills With Cold Air

Now give the valley its daily rhythm, and let a bathtub explain the night.

Daytime first. The sun cooks the valley walls, the walls warm the air touching them, and warm air climbs — so all day, gentle breezes slide UP the slopes, like the shimmer of hot air rising off a tar road, but leaning against the mountainside. That upslope day breeze is the anabatic wind. Anabatic ascends — both start with A. It is the reason little cauliflower clouds sit on the peaks by afternoon: the slopes are pumping warm air to the summit all day.

Then the sun sets, and the valley flips. On a clear night the slopes lose their heat fast, radiating it out to the stars. The air lying against them goes cold, and cold air is heavy air — it does not float, it FLOWS, downhill, like water. All night, thin sheets of cold air dribble down both walls into the bottom of the valley. That downhill night drain is the katabatic wind. And the valley floor? The valley floor is the bathtub. Cold air from both walls pours in like water from two taps, and by three in the morning the tub is full: a deep, still pond of the coldest air in the landscape, lying on the valley floor with warmer air floating on top of it.

Notice what has happened to the temperatures: cold at the bottom, warm above — upside down from normal. That upside-down arrangement is the temperature inversion. And the warm layer floating above the cold pond has a name you now care about deeply if you farm: the thermal belt, the cosy shelf partway up the slope that stays warm all night, sitting above the cold bath like the rim of the tub above the water line.

Take the questions for this section now: which wind blows when and why, the bathtub filling from two taps, and where the warm shelf sits. If you can see the tub, you can answer anything they ask about valley nights.

## Subtopic: Frost on the Floor, Money on the Shelf

Finish the story at dawn, and then decide — as a farmer and as a town planner — where everything should go.

The cold pond on the valley floor does two kinds of mischief before sunrise. If it chills below zero, everything standing in it wakes up white: the frost pocket, the bathtub turned freezer. One June night like that kills every peach blossom on the floor — a whole season's income gone before breakfast. And if the pond is moist, cooling it turns its vapour into droplets, and the valley floor disappears under a blanket of radiation fog — the white river you drive down into on winter mornings, thickest at dawn, gone by ten as the sun stirs the tub. The pond also traps smoke: every morning fire in the valley sends its smoke up into the warm lid, where it stops and spreads, which is why valley towns wear grey blankets in July.

Now place things like someone who understands the valley. The fruit trees and the vines — the money — go on the SHELF: the thermal belt, above the frost bath, below the cold heights. That is precisely where you see them in the Hex River valley: orchards and vineyards striping the mid-slope like a belt worn exactly where it fits. Sun-hungry grapes choose the braai side of the valley; forests and pasture take the fridge side. The floor gets the tough stuff — pasture, veggies, lucerne — plus the road and the railway, because flat is flat; just expect the fog there, and drive accordingly.

Houses want the shelf too: old Cape farmhouses sit a little way up the slope, above the frost and the floods, snug on the warm belt — Gran's electric blanket laid across the middle of the mountainside. The planning tragedy, and the exam's favourite evaluation question, is that low-cost housing so often ends up on the cheap land at the bottom: the coldest, foggiest, smokiest, most flood-prone spot in the whole valley, chosen for people who can least afford heating and hospital visits. The fix reads straight off the physics: homes on the belt, smoke and industry out of the tub, and the floor left to fields, parks and the river.

And if frost threatens the blossoms anyway? Farmers cheat the inversion: giant fans that pull the warm shelf air down into the orchard, heaters between the rows, even a helicopter hovering over the blocks on a critical night — stirring the bathtub so the freezer never sets.

Here come the final questions of the lesson, right now: the two dawn hazards and their recipes, what belongs on the floor, the shelf and each wall, and the planning mistake with its fix. Picture the tub, the shelf and the two walls, and this topic is banked.
