**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: I always mix up which slope is warmer. Someone told me south-facing because south is closer to the sea. Please settle this!

**Tutor:** Settle it with the sun's address, not the sea's. In South Africa the midday sun never stands overhead — we are south of the Tropic of Capricorn, so the sun at noon always hangs in the NORTHERN half of the sky. A slope that faces north is therefore tilted toward the sun: the rays hit it nearly square-on, concentrated onto a small area, and they hit it for more hours of the day. A slope facing south leans away, so the same energy skims across it thinly. North-facing warm, south-facing cool — in our hemisphere, always. The sea has nothing to do with it; that classmate was improvising. Now guard against the two exam traps. Trap one: a Northern Hemisphere question — Europe, North America — flips the answer completely, because there the midday sun stands in the southern sky, so south-facing slopes are the warm ones. Always name the hemisphere in your first sentence. Trap two: assuming every valley shows a strong contrast. The effect is strongest in east-west valleys, where one wall faces due north, and strongest in winter, when the sun is low. If the given valley runs north-south, say the contrast is weak and explain why — that observation alone is often a mark.

**Mandy:** Next: in the diagram question, why is the valley floor the coldest place at night? Cold air should be up on the freezing mountain tops, surely?

**Tutor:** Your instinct is right by day and wrong by night, and the difference is the whole topic. By day, yes — temperature generally falls with altitude, and the summit is colder than the floor. But on a clear, calm night a different machine takes over. The slopes radiate their heat away to the open sky, the air lying against them chills, and cold air is dense air — it cannot stay put on a slope any more than water can. It flows downhill as katabatic drainage, off both walls, all night, and it collects at the lowest point available: the valley floor. So by the early hours the floor holds a deep pond of the densest, coldest air in the landscape, while the air that was gently mixed and never touched the radiating ground floats above it, warmer. Temperature now INCREASES as you climb away from the floor — the inversion — until you pass the warm mid-slope thermal belt and normal cooling resumes toward the ridge. The proof you can quote: frost whitens the valley bottom while the mid-slope orchards stay green, and the fog lies in the floor like milk in a saucer. On exam diagrams, draw the temperature curve with its kink — cold floor, warm belt, cooling ridge — and label all three zones; that labelled kink is where the marks sit.

**Mandy:** Here is one from the group: what is the actual difference between radiation fog and the frost pocket? They both happen in the same place on the same night, so are they the same thing?

**Tutor:** Same place, same night, same cause — but two different physical events, and the exam separates them. The shared cause is the ponded cold air: katabatic drainage fills the valley floor and radiative cooling keeps chilling that trapped pond. What happens next depends on two thresholds. If the pond's temperature falls to its DEW POINT while the air still holds decent moisture, the water vapour condenses into liquid droplets suspended in the air — that is radiation fog, a cloud lying on the ground, thickest at dawn and burning off from the top down once the sun begins to warm and stir the pond. If the pond's temperature falls below ZERO degrees, moisture is deposited as ice crystals on cold surfaces — grass, windscreens, blossom — and that is frost, with any hollow that traps drainage air acting as a frost pocket. Notice the two can occur together, separately, or not at all: a moist pond above zero gives fog without frost; a dry pond below zero gives frost without fog. In an answer, name the shared recipe first — clear calm winter night, katabatic drainage, inversion — then state which threshold was crossed. Farmers care about the difference intensely: fog delays the school taxi, but a frost pocket in blossom season takes the whole harvest.

**Mandy:** Last one: for the settlement question, is it enough to write that farmers plant on the slopes because the valley floor is cold?

**Tutor:** That is the one-mark version of a six-mark answer, so build it into a location argument with reasons and consequences. Start with the zones, because the valley offers three addresses. The floor: coldest at night, frost pockets, radiation fog, flood risk when the river rises — but flat land and deep alluvial soil. The mid-slope thermal belt: frost-free on inversion nights, above the fog, above the floods — the prime address. The upper slopes and ridge: short growing season, exposed to wind. Then allocate with reasons: frost-sensitive, high-value crops — deciduous fruit, vines, citrus — onto the thermal belt, because one frost night on the floor can destroy a season's blossom; hardy crops and pasture onto the floor, where they tolerate the cold and use the good soil; and choose aspect within the slope — sun-hungry table grapes on the north-facing wall, plantations and dairy pasture on the cooler southern aspect. Then do the same for people: historic farmhouses and towns sit low on the slopes — on or near the belt, above flood and frost — while roads take the flat floor and accept the fog. Finish with the evaluation sentence examiners reward: pushing low-cost housing onto cheap valley-floor land parks the poorest residents in the coldest, foggiest, smokiest, most flood-prone zone, so equitable planning puts housing on the belt and leaves the floor to fields and parks. Zones, allocation, reasons, evaluation — that is the full-mark shape.
