**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: I always scramble anabatic and katabatic in the moment. Beyond the two-As trick, is there a way to rebuild them from scratch if I blank completely?

**Tutor:** There is, and it is sturdier than any mnemonic: rebuild from the energy source, because each wind has exactly one. Ask first — is the sun up? If the sun is up, the slopes are being heated, heated surfaces warm the air touching them, warm air is buoyant, and buoyant air can only go one way along a slope: up. Daytime wind, upslope, driven by heating — that is the anabatic. If the sun is down, the slopes are radiating heat away to a clear sky, the air against them is chilling, chilled air is dense, and dense air does the only thing gravity permits: it slides downhill. Night wind, downslope, driven by cooling and gravity — the katabatic. Notice you never memorised a direction; you derived it from whether energy was arriving or leaving. Then confirm with the consequences you already know: afternoon cloud on the peaks means air has been carried up all day, so the day wind climbs; frost and fog on the floor at dawn means cold air was delivered downhill all night, so the night wind sinks. Two energy states, two directions, cross-checked by cloud above and frost below. Blank all you like in the exam — thirty seconds of that reasoning writes both definitions for you, with mechanisms attached, which is exactly what the marks want anyway.

***

**Mandy:** Next: how can the middle of the slope be WARMER than the bottom at night? Heat rises — surely the top should be warmest and the bottom should just be normal?

**Tutor:** The catch is that heat rises applies to air being heated, and at night nothing is heating the valley — everything is cooling, and the question becomes who cools fastest and where the coldest product collects. Two cooling processes run at once. The ground radiates its warmth to the clear sky, chilling the air in contact with it — that happens on every surface from floor to ridge. But the chilled air does not stay where it was made: on a slope it immediately slides downhill, so the walls continuously manufacture cold air and export it to the floor. The floor therefore suffers twice — it radiates like everywhere else AND receives every other surface's cold air as a delivery. By the small hours the deepest, stillest, coldest pool in the valley lies on the floor. Now look at the mid-slope: it stands above the surface of that cold pool, so it escapes the pooled cold, and the cold air it makes itself drains away downhill instead of accumulating. Its temperature stays closer to that of the free atmosphere at its height — several degrees above the floor. That band is the thermal belt. Higher still, the upper slopes and ridge lose heat into the open sky and cool again. So the profile is cold floor, warm shelf, cool heights — not because heat rose, but because cold sank and the shelf sits above the flood line of sinking cold. Say it that way and the marker knows you own the mechanism.

**Mandy:** Third question, from the farming kids: wind machines in orchards look like giant fans just blowing on the trees. How does blowing air stop frost? Moving air usually feels colder.

**Tutor:** It feels colder to YOU because you are warmer than the air and moving air strips your heat faster — but the orchard's problem on a frost night is the opposite, and the fan is not there to cool anything. Remember what the inversion has built by two in the morning: a shallow pool of freezing air on the floor, and floating just above it, the thermal belt — air that can be five or more degrees warmer, sitting metres over the trees like an unused blanket. The wind machine is a mixing tool. Its blades are tilted to pull that warm upper air downward and stir it into the freezing layer among the branches, breaking the neat cold-under-warm layering. Mix five-degree air into minus-one-degree air and the orchard rides out the night above zero — the blossom lives. That is also exactly why a helicopter hovering over a high-value block works: its downwash is the same mixing on a violent scale, warm shelf air beaten down into the trees. And it explains the fine print: the machines only help when the frost is a radiation frost under a calm, clear inversion night, because only then is there a warm layer above to harvest. In a windborne cold snap with no inversion, there is nothing warm overhead to mix down, and the fans stand idle. In an exam answer, always justify the machine by naming what it attacks: not the frost itself, but the inversion structure that manufactures it.

**Mandy:** Last one: why do the fog patches on a winter morning drive sit exactly in the low spots? The fog seems to know where the dips are.

**Tutor:** It does know, because the fog is simply cold pooled air made visible — a map of the drainage, painted white. Rewind the night. Katabatic air trickles downhill and gathers in every hollow the landscape offers: the main valley floor, yes, but also each side kloof, each dip where the road crosses a stream, each basin behind an embankment. Wherever cold air ponds deeply enough, two things can happen: if it chills below zero you get a frost pocket, and if it chills to its dew point while holding moisture, the vapour condenses into droplets — radiation fog, filling the hollow to the brim of its cold pool and stopping there, which is why fog banks have those weirdly sharp edges. You drive along a ridge in sunshine, descend into a dip, and plunge into white precisely at the height where the cold pool's surface lies; climb the far side and you burst back into clear air at the matching height. The fog is thickest at dawn, after the longest cooling, and it burns off from the top down as the sun warms and stirs the pool. So the practical rules write themselves: on winter mornings, expect fog at every low crossing, slow down BEFORE the descent, and do not be fooled by a sunny ridge. And the exam rule writes itself too: wherever a question shows fog or frost in patches, your answer is drainage — cold air flowed downhill and pooled, and the white patches are the pools.
