# Part 1 — Expert

Cyclones make the headlines, but the three subtropical anticyclones make South Africa's everyday weather — and its deserts, its inversion layer, its berg-wind fire days and its summer thunderstorms. This session works through the three high-pressure cells by name and location, the mechanisms by which they control the country's climate through the seasons, the travelling disturbances that break their grip — the moisture front with its line thunderstorms, the coastal low and the berg wind — and finally the synoptic-chart signatures of all of it. Anchor everything to one mechanical fact: an anticyclone is a cell of sinking, diverging air, and sinking air is dry, stable and cloudless. The rest of the lesson is that fact colliding with South African geography.

## Subtopic: Three Highs by Name, Place and Character

South Africa sits at roughly thirty degrees south — squarely inside the subtropical high-pressure belt, where air that rose at the equator comes back down. Three semi-permanent cells of that belt run the country's weather, and the exam wants each one by name, position and character.

First, settle the rotation rule, because it is the classic slip: in the Southern Hemisphere, air circulates ANTICLOCKWISE around a high and clockwise around a low — the reverse of the Northern Hemisphere diagrams. Now the first cell: the South Atlantic High, sometimes called the South Atlantic Anticyclone, sitting over the ocean west of the country. Its anticlockwise, outward-spiralling circulation feeds cool air northward along the west coast, driving the cold Benguela current system and the upwelling that keeps the west coast cold, foggy and nearly rainless — the engine behind the Namib's aridity and the west coast fishing grounds.

Second, the South Indian High, over the ocean east of the country. Its anticlockwise circulation pushes warm, moist air from the tropical Indian Ocean onto the east coast — the moisture source for KwaZulu-Natal's humid summers and, when conditions allow, for rain across the interior.

Third, the Kalahari High, the continental member, sitting over the interior plateau. Unlike its ocean partners it is seasonal in strength and height: in winter it dominates at the surface; in summer it weakens and lifts, and that seasonal breathing is the master switch of interior rainfall, as the next section shows.

All three share the anticyclone's character: subsiding air that warms adiabatically as it descends, dissolving cloud; diverging surface flow; stability; clear skies. High pressure is the reason South Africa is a sunny, dry country — over sixty percent of it semi-arid.

Take this section's questions now: the three cells named and placed, the anticlockwise rule around Southern Hemisphere highs, and the character that sinking air stamps on the weather.

## Subtopic: How the Highs Run South Africa's Seasons

Mechanism one: the inversion layer. Air sinking over the plateau warms as it descends, so a layer forms where temperature increases with height — an upper-air temperature inversion, the lid on the country's weather. Rising thermals hit the lid and stop; cloud cannot build through it; moisture stays trapped below or shut out entirely. The height of that lid decides the weather. In winter the Kalahari High is strong and the inversion sits low — below the level of the escarpment — so moist ocean air cannot climb onto the plateau, and the interior is dry, cloudless and frosty at night. In summer the interior bakes, the Kalahari High weakens and lifts, the inversion rises above the escarpment, and moist air from the South Indian High floods in — afternoon thunderstorms follow. One lid, moving up and down, explains why Johannesburg has dry winters and stormy summers.

Mechanism two: the seasonal shift. The whole pressure belt migrates with the overhead sun — north in winter, south in summer. In winter the highs sit further north, blocking moist air from the interior while opening the door for mid-latitude cold fronts to swing under the continent and rain on the Cape. In summer the highs sit further south, shielding the Cape from fronts — hence its dry summers — while the lifted inversion lets the interior storm. Two rainfall regions, one migrating belt.

Mechanism three: the ocean currents. The South Atlantic High drives the cold Benguela current up the west coast: cold water, little evaporation, fog rather than rain, desert shore. The South Indian High drives the warm Agulhas current down the east coast: warm water, vigorous evaporation, humid air, green coastline. Same latitude, opposite coasts, opposite worlds — because of which high sits offshore.

Mechanism four: ridging. The South Atlantic High regularly extends a tongue of high pressure — a ridge — around the southern tip of the country behind each passing cold front. The ridge drives cool, moist onshore air against the south and east coasts, bringing the south coast much of its rain and giving KwaZulu-Natal cool, drizzly changes. Ridging is why the southern Cape gets rain in all seasons.

Attend to this section's questions before moving on: the inversion lid in winter versus summer, the seasonal migration and the two rainfall regions, the two currents matched to their highs, and what a ridging high does.

## Subtopic: The Travelling Disturbances — Moisture Front, Coastal Low, Berg Wind

Between the great stationary cells, smaller travelling systems produce some of the country's sharpest weather. The DBE names three.

First, the moisture front and its line thunderstorms. In summer, two air masses meet over the interior: moist tropical air fed in from the north and east under the South Indian High's influence, and dry, hot air over the west. The boundary between them — visible on satellite as a north-west to south-east line of cloud — is the moisture front, sometimes called the dry line. Along it, moist air is forced to rise, and thunderstorms break out in a line hundreds of kilometres long: the line thunderstorms that hammer the Highveld on summer afternoons with downpours, lightning, hail and flash floods. Management is warning-based: SAWS alerts, lightning safety, hail nets on orchards, storm-water maintenance in cities.

Second, the coastal low. A shallow low-pressure cell, trapped below the escarpment against the coast, that travels around the coastline — typically from the west coast toward KwaZulu-Natal, moving ahead of an approaching cold front or steered by the coast-parallel flow. Ahead of the coastal low, air is pulled down off the plateau — offshore, hot and dry. Behind it, cool moist ocean air pushes onshore, dropping temperatures within an hour and often bringing low cloud and drizzle. Durban's famous sudden change from a stifling berg-wind morning to a cool misty evening is the coastal low sweeping through.

Third, the berg wind — an examiner's favourite because it links straight to the anticyclone above. In winter, a strong interior high combined with a coastal low or approaching front sets up flow from the plateau toward the coast. Air pours off the escarpment and descends more than a kilometre, warming adiabatically at about one degree per hundred metres, and arrives at the coast as a hot, bone-dry, gusty offshore wind. Berg-wind days deliver the strange winter records — thirty-plus degree days on the KwaZulu-Natal or Eastern Cape coast in July — and they are the country's peak fire-danger days: dry fuel, low humidity, strong wind. The Knysna fires of June twenty seventeen exploded under berg-wind conditions. Impact reduction: fire bans and firebreaks on high-danger days, SAWS fire-danger warnings, no open flames, and rapid-response firefighting on standby.

The questions on this section are with you now: the moisture front and where its line storms strike, the coastal low's before-and-after weather, and the berg wind's mechanism with its fire link.

## Subtopic: Reading It All on the Synoptic Chart

The examiner hands you a South African synoptic chart; here is the identification drill in words.

The three highs: look for closed isobars around cells labelled H. West of the country over the Atlantic — the South Atlantic High. East over the Indian Ocean — the South Indian High. Over the interior plateau, mainly on winter charts — the Kalahari High. Typical central pressures run one thousand and twenty hectopascals and higher; a winter Kalahari High can exceed one thousand and thirty.

Season from a single glance, a bankable trick: if a high sits strongly over the interior with fronts brushing the far south — winter chart. If the interior shows a heat low with moist flow and scattered thunderstorm reports, and the highs sit well south — summer chart. Confirm with the dew-point temperatures at interior stations: moist teens in summer, bone-dry single digits or below zero in winter.

Ridging: after a cold front clears the country, watch the South Atlantic High's isobars bulge eastward around the south coast — a tongue of high pressure hooking under the continent. That signature means onshore south-westerly to south-easterly winds, cooler temperatures, and drizzle or showers along the south and east coasts.

The coastal low: a small, tight low squeezed against the coastline, often just ahead of a frontal system, with wind reports turning offshore ahead of it and onshore behind it. Where station models along the coast show hot, dry, offshore winds — plateau air descending — you are looking at berg-wind conditions, and the exam expects you to predict the abrupt cool change as the coastal low or front passes.

The moisture front: on the chart, a trough line over the interior with moist north-easterly flow on its eastern side and dry westerly-side air; on the satellite image, that tell-tale diagonal line of cumulonimbus cluster development across the Highveld on a summer afternoon.

Prediction discipline to close: systems in our latitudes track broadly west to east, coastal lows run along the coast ahead of fronts, and ridging follows frontal passage — so read today's chart, then say what tomorrow holds and give the mechanism. Marks always attach to the mechanism.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: identifying the three highs and the season, the ridging signature, the coastal low and berg wind on station reports, and the moisture front's chart and satellite fingerprints.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole topic again with three security guards, a pot lid, and the hairdryer wind — the version that survives exam-room nerves.

## Subtopic: Three Guards Around the Country

Picture South Africa as a house with three security guards posted around it. One stands in the Atlantic on the west side, one in the Indian Ocean on the east side, and one sits inside the house, in the middle of the country. These guards are the three high-pressure cells: the South Atlantic High, the South Indian High and the Kalahari High. A high-pressure cell is just a place where air is sinking — and sinking air is the great destroyer of weather. As air sinks it gets squashed and warms up, and warm sinking air eats clouds the way morning sun eats mist. That is why high pressure means blue sky, and why a country sitting under three guards is sunny and dry most of the year.

Each guard has a personality. The west-coast guard pushes cold ocean water and cold air up the west coast — that is why the sea at Blouberg makes you gasp, why the west coast gets fog instead of rain, and why the Namib is a desert. The east-coast guard is friendlier: it scoops warm, wet air off the tropical Indian Ocean and delivers it to the east coast — that is why Durban's sea is warm and its air feels like a wet towel in January. The inside guard, the Kalahari High, is the moody one: in winter it sits heavily on the whole interior; in summer it gets lazy, floats higher, and lets the rain in — the next section is his story.

One rule before we move on, because it is the number-one lost mark: in the Southern Hemisphere, air moves anticlockwise around a high and clockwise around a low. Highs go against the clock down here. Say it three times.

Your questions for this section are with you now: the three guards named and placed, why sinking air means sunshine, and which way the air turns around a high. The guards will hold the facts for you.

## Subtopic: The Pot Lid Over Johannesburg

Why is a Joburg winter bone-dry and frosty while a Joburg summer explodes with afternoon thunder? One picture answers it: a pot with a lid.

The pot is the interior plateau. The lid is the inversion layer — a sheet of warm air created by all that sinking, sitting over the country and stopping anything from rising through it. Steam cannot escape a closed pot; rising, cloud-building air cannot climb through the inversion.

In winter, the inside guard is strong and presses the lid down low — lower than the escarpment, the great step at the edge of the plateau. Moist sea air comes rolling in from the coast, hits the step, and cannot get over it because the lid is in the way. So the plateau stays cloudless: sunny cold days, frosty nights, dust and smoke trapped under the lid — that brown winter haze over the townships in July is the lid made visible.

In summer, the ground bakes, the inside guard weakens, and the lid lifts high above the escarpment. Now the moist air from the east-coast guard's ocean can pour onto the plateau. Wet air over hot ground with no lid means afternoon thunderstorms nearly every day — the four-o'clock Highveld storm you can set your watch by. Where the incoming wet air meets the dry desert air from the west, the storms line up along the boundary like soldiers on parade: the moisture front and its line thunderstorms, the ones that flood Joburg's streets in an hour.

And the seasons themselves? The whole guard system slides with the sun: north in winter, south in summer. When the guards step north in winter, the Cape's back door opens and the cold fronts sneak in to rain on Cape Town. When they step south in summer, they block the fronts and the Cape dries out while the interior storms. One sliding lid, one sliding belt — two rainfall regions explained.

Take the questions for this section now: the lid in winter against the lid in summer, why the moist air cannot climb the step in July, and how the sliding guards give the Cape winter rain. If you can tell the pot-lid story, you own this topic.

## Subtopic: The Hairdryer Wind and the Cool Change

Finish with the two travelling troublemakers every coastal family knows, even if they never knew the names.

First, the berg wind — the hairdryer. In winter, the heavy inside guard piles air up on the plateau, a kilometre and a half above the sea. When a passing weather system tugs at the coast, that plateau air spills over the edge of the escarpment and falls toward the sea. Falling air gets squashed, and squashed air heats up — about one degree warmer for every hundred metres it drops. By the time it reaches Durban or Port Elizabeth it has fallen well over a kilometre and warmed by more than ten degrees: a hot, utterly dry, gusty wind blowing from the land out to sea. That is the berg wind — mountain wind — and it is why the east coast records its strangest heat in the middle of winter: thirty-four degrees in July while Joburg shivers. It is a hairdryer pointed at the coast. And like a hairdryer aimed at dry grass, it is the fire wind: the worst wildfires — Knysna, twenty seventeen — explode on berg-wind days, when everything is dry, the humidity is on the floor and the wind fans every spark. Berg-wind day rules: fire bans, no open flames, firefighters on standby.

Second, the coastal low — the cool change. A small low-pressure cell slides along the coastline like a bead on a string, usually running ahead of a cold front. In front of the bead, air is dragged down off the plateau — that is often your berg-wind morning. Behind the bead, cool damp sea air sweeps in — temperature drops ten degrees in an hour, cloud rolls over, drizzle starts. Durban's whiplash day — sweating at breakfast, jersey by supper — is the bead passing. So the two troublemakers are partners: the hairdryer blows ahead of the bead, the cool change follows it.

Why does the exam love these two? Because they tie the whole topic together: the guards build the setup, the lid stores the hot dry air, and the travelling systems release it. Tell it as one chain — guards, lid, hairdryer, bead, cool change — and every mark in the section connects.

Here come the final questions of the lesson, right now: the falling-air arithmetic that heats the hairdryer, the fire-danger link and its rules, and the before-and-after of the sliding bead. Take them and close out the topic strong.
