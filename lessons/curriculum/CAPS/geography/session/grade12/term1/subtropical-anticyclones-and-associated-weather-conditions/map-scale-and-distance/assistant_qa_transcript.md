**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and it is the one that haunts me: I always mess up the conversion. Sometimes I divide by 1 000 to get metres and my whole answer is out by ten times. How do I stop doing that?

**Tutor:** Fix it with a chant and a catcher, not with more checking. The chant names the two rungs in order: centimetres to metres, divide by one hundred; metres to kilometres, divide by one thousand. One hundred first, one thousand second, always in that order, because a hundred centimetres make a metre and a thousand metres make a kilometre. Say the chain out loud before you touch the arithmetic — centimetres, then metres, then kilometres — and name each unit again as you land on it, so a skipped rung announces itself instead of slipping past. Then let the catcher do its work: 430 000 centimetres must land on 4 300 metres and then on 4,3 kilometres. If your answer lands on 43 kilometres you divided once instead of twice; if it lands on 0,43 kilometres you divided by a thousand on the first rung. The chant prevents the slip, and the two-stage landing catches whatever survives it.

***

**Mandy:** Next: there is a bar printed along the bottom edge of the sheet with numbers under it. Nobody has ever explained to me how to actually use the thing — and is it not just the ratio scale again?

**Tutor:** It is the same information with the arithmetic already done for you, and it is worth knowing properly. Picture the bar as a short printed ruler with a zero mark part-way along it. To the right of that zero the bar is divided into whole-kilometre blocks, numbered one, two, three and so on. To the left of the zero sits a single block cut into much finer divisions — usually steps of a hundred or two hundred metres — and that fine block is there to read the leftover part of a distance. To use it, open a pair of dividers until one leg sits on each of your two points, then lift the dividers onto the bar and set the right-hand leg exactly on one of the numbered kilometre marks. Read where the left-hand leg falls among the fine divisions to the left of the zero. Whole kilometres from the right, the remainder from the left, added together. A strip of paper works the same way if you hold each end position under a fingernail. It also survives something the ratio cannot: if a sheet is enlarged or reduced on a photocopier, the printed 1:50 000 becomes a lie, but the bar stretches with the paper and stays honest.

**Mandy:** For the winding road question, my string measurement came out at 12,1 centimetres and my friend got 12,6. Whose is right? That is a 250-metre difference on the ground!

**Tutor:** Both of you may well be right, and the memorandum expects exactly that. Curved-distance questions are marked to a range — typically a few millimetres of map measurement either side of the official figure, which at this scale forgives a couple of hundred metres — precisely because string and paper strips cannot be identical between two hands. What you can control is technique, and technique is what pulls you inside the range. Press the string down into every bend instead of letting it cut across curves, because corner-cutting always under-measures, and your 12,1 has the smell of a cut corner about it. Work in short sections, pinning the string with a fingertip at the end of each straight bit before you turn into the next bend. Mark the finishing point on the string with your thumbnail before you lift it. Then straighten it against the ruler twice and take the reading you get both times. Do that and your answer sits comfortably inside the accepted band, which is what "right" means for a measured question.

***

**Mandy:** Last one: if the paper says centimetres and the question says kilometres, why does the rest of the mapwork paper care so much about this one skill? It feels like the smallest question on the paper.

**Tutor:** It is small the way a foundation is small — invisible and holding up everything above it. Take the gradient calculation, which is the next calculation you will meet. Gradient is the vertical interval divided by the horizontal equivalent: the vertical interval is the height difference between two points, and the horizontal equivalent is the real ground distance between them. That second ingredient is nothing but this calculation, wearing a different name — measure the paper distance, multiply by 50 000, step down the ladder, and convert to metres so it can be divided by a height in metres. The heights themselves come off the sheet too: a spot height is a small dot with a number printed beside it giving that point's exact height in metres, and a trig beacon is a small triangle carrying a station number and a surveyed height. Between them run the contour lines, joining places of equal height at a fixed twenty-metre step, so that crowded lines mean steep ground and widely spaced lines mean gentle ground. Cross-sections need real distances along the section line; area calculations multiply two of these distances together. Get this one conversion automatic and every calculation downstream starts with a number you can trust.
