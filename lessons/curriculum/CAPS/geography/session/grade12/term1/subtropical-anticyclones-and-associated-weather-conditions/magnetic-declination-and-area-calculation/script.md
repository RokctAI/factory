# Part 1 — Expert

Two calculations sit beside gradient and scale in the mapwork question, and both are pure method: magnetic declination, which corrects the compass against the map, and area, which turns two measured distances into square units. Each carries three to four marks, each follows a fixed recipe, and each punishes one specific careless step. This session builds both recipes on worked examples in the exact form the examiners print: a topographic sheet whose declination information is eleven years old and must be updated to this year, and a demarcated block on the orthophoto whose area is wanted in square metres.

## Subtopic: Two Norths and the Angle Between Them

Every topographic sheet deals in two norths. True north is the direction to the geographic North Pole — the fixed point where the lines of longitude meet, the north every map is drawn on. Magnetic north is the direction a compass needle actually points: the magnetic pole, which sits away from the geographic pole and, worse, drifts from year to year as the Earth's molten core churns.

Magnetic declination is the angle between those two norths — the correction between the map's north and the needle's north. In South Africa the needle points to the west of true north, so our declination is always stated as so many degrees and minutes west of true north. The margin of every topographic sheet carries a declination note with three facts: the declination as it was measured in a stated year, the mean annual change, and the direction of that change — for us, westward, meaning the angle grows a little every year.

Why does the examiner care? Because a bearing taken from the map — a true bearing, measured with a protractor from true north — cannot be walked with a compass until it is corrected. The relationship to memorise: magnetic bearing equals true bearing plus magnetic declination, when the declination is west. Surveyors, pilots, hikers and soldiers all live by that correction, and the exam tests whether you can update it to the current year.

Angles here are measured in degrees and minutes, and minutes are not time: sixty minutes make one degree, exactly as sixty seconds make one minute of arc. All the arithmetic of this topic runs on that conversion.

The questions for this section are with you now: the two norths defined, the declination as the angle between them, the three facts in the margin note, and how many minutes make a degree.

## Subtopic: The Magnetic Declination Calculation

Now the calculation itself, in the layout that collects every mark. The sheet says: mean magnetic declination 18 degrees 19 minutes west of true north in 2014, mean annual change 5 minutes westward. The question says: calculate the magnetic declination for 2025.

Step one: the difference in years. From 2014 to 2025 is eleven years. Write the line: 2025 minus 2014 gives 11 years. The examiners often supply this difference; verify it anyway, because a miscounted year poisons everything downstream.

Step two: the total change. Eleven years at 5 minutes per year: 11 × 5 = 55 minutes of change. Attach the direction immediately — 55 minutes westward — because the direction decides step three.

Step three: apply the change. The declination is west of true north and the change is westward, so the angle is growing: add. 18 degrees 19 minutes plus 55 minutes gives 18 degrees 74 minutes. And here is the step that separates the prepared from the hopeful: 74 minutes is more than a degree. Carry sixty of those minutes up: sixty minutes become one degree, leaving fourteen minutes. The declination for 2025 is 19 degrees 14 minutes west of true north.

Write the answer in full — number, minutes, and the phrase west of true north. The direction is part of the answer, not decoration; memoranda reserve a mark for it.

Had the annual change been eastward, the angle would be shrinking and step three would subtract. Read the margin note's direction every time rather than assuming; the method is add when the change carries the needle further from true north on the western side, subtract when it swings back east.

Work this section's questions before moving on: the year difference, the multiplication, the add-or-subtract decision, and the carry that turns 74 minutes into one degree and fourteen minutes.

## Subtopic: Area — Two Distances Multiplied

The area question hands you a demarcated feature — a plantation block, a dam, a stadium precinct — and asks for its area in square metres or square kilometres. The formula line is given on the paper: Area equals Length times Breadth. The marks are not for the formula; they are for feeding it correctly.

The iron rule: convert each distance to real ground units first, and only then multiply. Never multiply the map centimetres and try to convert the square centimetres afterwards — linear scale factors do not apply to areas, and that shortcut is the single most common zero in this question.

Worked example on the orthophoto, scale one to ten thousand. Measured length: 3,5 centimetres. Measured breadth: 1,8 centimetres. Convert the length: at one to ten thousand, 3,5 centimetres represents 35 000 centimetres of ground, which is 350 metres. Convert the breadth the same way: 1,8 centimetres represents 18 000 centimetres, which is 180 metres. Only now multiply: 350 × 180 = 63 000. The area is 63 000 square metres. Because your ruler and the examiner's ruler can differ by a millimetre, memoranda accept a band of answers around the official figure — another reason to measure to the nearest millimetre and convert cleanly.

On the topographic sheet the scale is one to fifty thousand, where every centimetre is half a kilometre. A block measuring 4 centimetres by 3 centimetres converts to 2 kilometres by 1,5 kilometres, and 2 × 1,5 = 3, so the area is 3 square kilometres. Same recipe, different multiplier: convert, convert, multiply.

Match the unit to the question. Square metres suit small features on the orthophoto; square kilometres suit big country on the topographic sheet. If the question names the unit, land on that unit exactly — converting between the two mid-calculation is where answers go to die.

Take the questions for this section now: the iron rule of convert-first, the two worked conversions, the multiplication, and the choice between square metres and square kilometres.

## Subtopic: Marks, Traps and the Answer Line

Both calculations are marked line by line, so set out both the same way: the given values, the operation, the answer with its unit — and for declination, its direction. Three lines of working is the difference between full marks and a lonely method mark.

Now the traps, named so they cannot ambush you. Declination trap one: subtracting the change when the note says westward. In South Africa the standard case is a westward creep on a westerly declination — the angle grows; add. Trap two: leaving 74 minutes standing in the answer. Any minute count of sixty or more must carry into degrees; an answer of 18 degrees 74 minutes scores the arithmetic and forfeits the answer mark. Trap three: dropping the phrase west of true north. Trap four: using the wrong year difference — count from the year printed in the margin note, not from the year the map was printed or the year you sit the exam paper says nothing about.

Area trap one: multiplying centimetres by centimetres and then converting — the square-centimetre dead end. Convert each side first. Trap two: mixing scales — measuring one side on the topographic sheet and the other on the orthophoto; both distances must come off the same sheet. Trap three: writing metres when the question demanded square metres, or square metres when it demanded square kilometres. The exponent is the answer; say square metres out loud as you write the line.

Finally, place both skills in the bigger machine. The declination correction feeds every compass-bearing question and every direction instruction in the field. The area calculation feeds land-use, farming and settlement questions — how much irrigable land, how large the informal settlement has grown. And both stand on the conversion built in the scale-and-distance work: a measured map distance turned into ground truth. Master that conversion once and gradient, area and declination all become the same discipline wearing three uniforms.

The closing questions of this part are ready: the marking layout, the four declination traps, the three area traps, and the downstream questions both skills feed.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now both calculations again, from the kitchen table: a compass that tells a small lie, minutes that behave like a clock's minutes, and a floor measured for tiles.

## Subtopic: The Compass That Tells a Small Lie

Hold an imaginary compass flat in your hand. The map on the table points at true north — the top of the world, the fixed pole all maps agree on. But the needle in your hand ignores the map and swings to its own north, the magnetic one, which in South Africa sits off to the west. Two arrows, two norths, and a wedge of angle between them.

That wedge is magnetic declination: the size of the compass's lie. And the lie is not even loyal — the magnetic pole wanders as the deep Earth churns, so the wedge widens by a few minutes every year. Our wedge, measured in degrees and minutes west of true north, is a slow-growing thing, which is why the sheet's margin gives its size in some past year plus the yearly growth, and why the exam asks what the lie measures this year.

Why put up with the lying needle at all? Because in the veld, the needle is what you have. The map speaks true north; the compass speaks magnetic north; declination is the translation between the two languages. To walk a direction taken off the map, you widen it by the wedge: magnetic bearing is true bearing plus the declination, while the declination sits to the west. No translation, and every hundred metres walked drifts you further from the farmhouse you were aiming at.

So the whole topic is one honest sentence: the needle lies west by a known, growing angle, and your job is to know today's size of the lie.

The questions for this section are up: the two arrows, the wedge between them, why the wedge grows, and the translation sentence for bearings.

## Subtopic: Minutes That Are Not Time

The arithmetic of declination frightens people until they notice they already do it every day — on a clock. Angle minutes work exactly like clock minutes: sixty of them make one whole. Sixty minutes make an hour on your phone; sixty minutes make a degree on the map. Everything else is ordinary adding.

Run the example like a clock problem. The lie measured 18 degrees 19 minutes back in 2014, and it grows 5 minutes a year. From 2014 to 2025 is eleven years — count the gap, 2025 minus 2014 gives 11. Growth: eleven fives, 11 × 5 = 55 minutes. Add the growth to the old lie: 19 minutes plus 55 minutes is 74 minutes. And just as you would never say the time is six o'clock seventy-four, you never leave 74 minutes standing in an angle. Seventy-four minutes past six is fourteen minutes past seven: sixty minutes roll over into one whole. So 18 degrees and 74 minutes rolls over into 19 degrees and 14 minutes. The lie in 2025: 19 degrees 14 minutes, west of true north — say the west; it is worth a mark.

Adding or subtracting? The margin note tells you which way the needle is drifting. Drifting further west while the lie is already west: the lie grows, so add — that is the South African standard. If a note ever says the drift is eastward, the needle is swinging back towards the truth and the lie shrinks: subtract. Read the note; never guess.

That is the entire calculation: count the years, multiply by the yearly change, add it on, roll over the minutes like a clock. Four small moves, three or four marks, every single year.

Your questions for this section are ready: the clock rule for sixty, the eleven fives, the roll-over from 74 minutes, and the add-or-subtract decision.

## Subtopic: The Rugby Field in Your Answer Book

Area is the tiling question. When a floor is tiled, nobody measures the floor in photograph centimetres — they measure the real length and the real breadth, then multiply. The map's area question is the same job with one extra step: the map is the photograph, so each side must be un-shrunk to real size before the multiplying starts.

Do it with the exam's own numbers, on the orthophoto where every measurement grows ten thousand times. The block's length on paper: 3,5 centimetres. Un-shrink: 35 000 centimetres, which is 350 metres. The breadth on paper: 1,8 centimetres. Un-shrink: 18 000 centimetres, which is 180 metres. Now the tiling step: 350 × 180 = 63 000. The block covers 63 000 square metres.

Feel that size instead of just writing it. A rugby field is roughly 100 metres by 70 metres: 100 × 70 = 7 000 square metres. Our block is nine rugby fields, side by side. If your answer had come out at 630 square metres — a classroom or two — or at six million, the rugby-field test would smell it instantly. Big features on maps come out in thousands of square metres; whole farms and towns come out in square kilometres.

And the one mistake the markers wait for every year: multiplying the paper centimetres first — 3,5 times 1,8 — and then trying to convert the squashed little answer afterwards. It cannot work. Shrinking happened to each side separately, so the un-shrinking must happen to each side separately, before they meet in the multiplication. Convert the length. Convert the breadth. Then multiply. Three moves, in that order, every time.

The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the tiling order of operations, the two un-shrinkings, the rugby-field sense check, and the paper-centimetre trap. Answer them in the order they teach, and both of these calculations become the steadiest marks on your mapwork paper.
