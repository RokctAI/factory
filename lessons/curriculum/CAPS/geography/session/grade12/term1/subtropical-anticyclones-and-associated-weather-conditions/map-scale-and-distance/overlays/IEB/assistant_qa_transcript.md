**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: why do we have to write out four whole lines of working for a sum I can do on my calculator in three seconds? It feels like being punished for being fast.

**Tutor:** It feels that way until you understand what is actually being bought with those lines. Mapwork calculations are marked per step, not per final number — a mark for the correct measurement or formula, a mark for the multiplication, a mark for the conversion, a mark for the answer in its unit. Write only the final number and you are gambling everything on one keystroke: if a decimal slips, you score zero. Write the four lines and the same slip costs one mark while the other three stand — the working is insurance you buy with thirty seconds of ink. There is a second, sneakier benefit: the act of writing the ladder — divide by one hundred to metres, divide by one thousand to kilometres — is precisely what stops the ladder mistakes, because errors live in steps done invisibly in your head. And a third: when a question later says show all calculations, the habit is already installed and costs you nothing under pressure. So reframe it: you are not being slowed down, you are being paid per line. Fast people who show working collect full marks fast. Fast people who do not, donate marks to the silence.

***

**Mandy:** Next: I get confused about which way the scale works. Sometimes I multiply when I should divide. Is there a way to know the direction without memorising it?

**Tutor:** Anchor it to shrinking, and the direction chooses itself. The map is the real world shrunk fifty thousand times — everything on the paper is tiny compared to reality. So ask one question before touching the calculator: am I going from the tiny world to the big world, or from the big world to the tiny one? Map to ground is tiny to big: the number must grow, so you multiply by 50 000. Ground to map is big to tiny: the number must shrink, so you divide by 50 000. Then confirm with a plausibility glance at your result. If you convert 7,4 centimetres and get an answer smaller than what you started with, you divided when the world was supposed to inflate — impossible, since real ground distances dwarf paper ones. Going the other way, a map distance of several kilometres is equally impossible, since no sheet of paper is kilometres wide. That pair of impossibilities is your compass: paper distances are always small, ground distances are always big, and the operation must move the number in the right direction. Grow toward the ground, shrink toward the paper — no memorising required, just a question and a glance.

**Mandy:** Third one: on the trial run we did in class, my string measurement of the river was different from my friend's — mine said 12,1 centimetres and hers said 11,6. Whose answer is wrong?

**Tutor:** Possibly neither, and understanding why makes you better at both measuring and answering. A winding feature measured with string carries honest, unavoidable measurement variation: how tightly you press the string into each bend, where exactly you judge the feature to start and end, even how much the string stretches when you straighten it. Two careful people can legitimately land half a centimetre apart, which at one to fifty thousand is two hundred and fifty metres of ground — and that is exactly why answers are accepted within a band around the official figure rather than as one perfect number. Your job is to be inside the band, and three habits put you there. One: use the paper-strip method rather than stretchy string where you can — rotate the strip's edge along the route segment by segment, ticking off each straight piece; paper does not stretch. Two: define your endpoints before you measure — centre of the bridge symbol, centre of the confluence — so you are not deciding while measuring. Three: measure twice and take the agreement seriously: if your two runs differ by more than a couple of millimetres, run it a third time. Precision is not a talent; it is a procedure. Follow the procedure and your number lands in the band every time.

**Mandy:** Last one: honestly, when will any of us use this after school? My phone tells me the distance to anywhere.

**Tutor:** Your phone tells you distance along roads it knows — and the moment you step off those roads, the skill you learned today takes over. Hikers planning a route through the Drakensberg work from printed topographic sheets, because batteries die and mist eats signal; they measure tomorrow's path, convert with exactly this method, and judge whether the party can cover it before dark. Farmers do it when fencing a new camp — measure the boundary on the sheet, convert, order the wire. Town planners do it across orthophotos when laying out a new water line. Conservation teams do it estimating how far a fire line must be cut. And there is a deeper transfer: scale thinking itself. An architect's floor plan at one to one hundred, a model aeroplane at one to seventy-two, a laboratory map of a cell — every one runs on the same logic of a ratio that compares without counting, and you now own that logic. The phone is a convenience built on this skill; the person who also owns the skill can check the phone, work where the phone cannot, and reason about any shrunken picture of the world. That is not old-fashioned — that is being the one person on the hike everyone else follows.
