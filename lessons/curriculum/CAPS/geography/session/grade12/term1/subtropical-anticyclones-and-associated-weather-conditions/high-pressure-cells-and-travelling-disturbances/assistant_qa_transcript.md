**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: I keep writing that highs spin clockwise because lows spin clockwise, and losing the easiest mark on the paper. Help!

**Tutor:** Fix it with one anchor pair and one check. The anchor: in the Southern Hemisphere, lows go WITH the clock, highs go AGAINST it. Clockwise into a low, anticlockwise out of a high — they must be opposite, because one pulls air in and the other pushes air out, and the Coriolis deflection bends both flows to the left. Now the check, which rescues you even mid-exam: use the weather you already know. The South Indian High delivers moist ocean air onto the KwaZulu-Natal coast — that means air on its western side must be moving from the ocean toward the land, from the north-east. Trace that flow around the cell and it can only be circling anticlockwise. Or use the South Atlantic High: it drives cool air up the west coast toward Namibia, which again only works if the circulation runs anticlockwise. So if you blank, do not guess — reconstruct from a wind you know: Durban's humid north-easter or the Cape's summer south-easter, both of them the outer edge of an anticlockwise high. Reconstructing beats memorising, and it also earns the explanation marks when the question says justify your answer.

**Mandy:** Next: what exactly is the inversion layer? I wrote that it is a layer that stops rain and got half marks.

**Tutor:** Half marks because you gave the effect without the mechanism, and the mechanism is one sentence away. Normally temperature falls as you go up. An inversion is a layer where it does the opposite — temperature rises with height — and over South Africa that warm layer is manufactured by the anticyclones: air subsiding over the plateau compresses and warms, laying a sheet of warm air over the cooler air below. Why does that stop rain? Because rising air keeps rising only while it is warmer than its surroundings. A thermal climbing from the hot ground rises, cools, and then meets the inversion — air warmer than itself — and immediately loses its buoyancy and stops dead. No ascent, no cooling to condensation, no cloud, no rain. That is the full chain the memo pays for. Then add the seasonal sentence that turns half marks into full marks: in winter the strong Kalahari High presses the inversion below the escarpment, locking moist coastal air off the plateau entirely; in summer surface heating weakens the high, the inversion lifts above the escarpment, and moist Indian Ocean air pours in underneath it to feed the afternoon thunderstorms. Mechanism, then chain, then season — three sentences, full marks.

**Mandy:** Here is one from the group chat: how can Durban be thirty-four degrees in July and then freezing by supper time on the same day? Someone said the textbook must be wrong.

**Tutor:** The textbook is right, and that whiplash day is actually two of our travelling disturbances working in sequence — it is the single best exam story in this topic. The morning heat is the berg wind. In winter the Kalahari High piles cool dense air onto the plateau, some fifteen hundred metres above sea level. When a coastal low or an approaching front lowers the pressure at the coast, that plateau air drains toward the sea, descending the escarpment and compressing as it falls — warming at roughly one degree for every hundred metres of descent. Falling well over a kilometre, it lands in Durban more than ten degrees warmer than it started: hot, extremely dry, gusty, blowing offshore. Thirty-four degrees in July, and the fire-danger flags go up. Then the afternoon reversal is the coastal low itself passing through. Ahead of it, the offshore berg wind; behind it, cool moist marine air floods onshore — the temperature can drop ten degrees within the hour, low cloud rolls in, and drizzle starts. Sweating at breakfast, jersey by supper — no contradiction, just the bead sliding along the coast with the hairdryer ahead of it and the cool change behind it. Describe that sequence with the adiabatic arithmetic and you have a full-mark answer.

**Mandy:** Last one: on the exam chart, how do I tell whether it is a summer or winter synoptic map? I always just guess from the date, and once there was no date.

**Tutor:** Never guess — run the three-clue drill, because the chart itself announces the season. Clue one, the interior: find what sits over the plateau. A strong closed high — one thousand and twenty-four hectopascals or more — parked over the interior is the winter Kalahari High at full strength; a heat low over the north-western interior with moist air flowing in from the north-east is the summer pattern. Clue two, the fronts: cold fronts brushing or crossing the south-western Cape point to winter, when the shifted pressure belt lets the westerlies reach the continent; fronts trailing far south of the country, with the highs ridging strongly south of forty degrees, point to summer. Clue three, the station data: read the dew-point temperatures at interior stations. Summer interior air is moist — dew points in the teens feeding thunderstorms; winter interior air is desert-dry — dew points in single digits or below zero, with clear skies and frost likely overnight. Two of the three clues will always agree, and that agreement is your answer plus your justification in one move. Then, if the question asks for prediction, remember the standing rules: systems track west to east, coastal lows run along the coast ahead of fronts, and ridging follows every frontal passage with cool onshore weather for the south coast. Read, justify, predict — in that order, every time.
