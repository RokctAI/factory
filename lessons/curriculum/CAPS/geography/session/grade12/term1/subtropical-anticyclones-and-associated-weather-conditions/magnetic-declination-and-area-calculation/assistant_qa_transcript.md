**Mandy:** Before you move on — a few questions that come up a lot with this topic. First: why do we add the change some years and subtract it in other questions? People just memorise "add" and hope.

**Tutor:** Hoping is unnecessary, because the direction words decide it for you, every time. Ask one question: is the needle drifting further from true north, or swinging back towards it? In South Africa the declination lies to the west of true north, and the margin note almost always says the mean annual change is westward too — further west means the angle between the two norths is opening wider, so the change is added. That is why "add" feels like the rule: on our sheets it usually is. But the method is the sentence, not the habit. If a note ever declares the change eastward while the declination is westerly, the needle is swinging back towards the truth, the wedge is closing, and you subtract. So read the two direction words in the note — the declination's side and the change's direction — and say the sentence: same direction, angle grows, add; opposite directions, angle shrinks, subtract. Memorise the sentence and you never need luck.

***

**Mandy:** Next: the minutes keep breaking people. Nineteen plus fifty-five is seventy-four, and then half the class writes eighteen degrees seventy-four minutes and moves on. What is the reliable fix?

**Tutor:** Borrow the clock in your head, because it already does this perfectly. Nobody in this class would ever say the time is six seventy-four — the moment a minute count passes fifty-nine, you roll sixty minutes into the next hour without even thinking, and six seventy-four becomes fourteen past seven. Angle minutes obey the identical law: sixty make one degree. So finish every declination calculation with one deliberate glance at the minutes: is the number sixty or more? If it is, subtract sixty from the minutes and add one to the degrees — seventy-four minutes becomes one degree and fourteen minutes, and eighteen degrees becomes nineteen. Do the glance even when you feel certain, because the carry only appears in questions where the addition pushes past sixty, which is exactly when candidates are busiest congratulating themselves. The examiners choose their numbers to force that carry; treat any minute total over fifty-nine as the question's real test.

***

**Mandy:** Third: for area, why is multiplying the centimetres first so wrong? It feels like it should work — the scale is the scale.

**Tutor:** It feels right because the scale honestly does convert every distance — but an area is not a distance, it is two distances multiplied, and the shrinking happened to both of them. Picture the block on the ground: the mapmaker shrank its length ten thousand times, and separately shrank its breadth ten thousand times. So the little paper rectangle has been shrunk twice over — once in each direction. Multiply the paper centimetres together and your square centimetres are wrong by ten thousand times ten thousand, and no single multiplication by the scale can rescue them. The linear factor un-shrinks lines; it cannot un-shrink squares. That is why the iron rule exists: un-shrink the length on its own, un-shrink the breadth on its own, and only then let them meet in the multiplication. Convert, convert, multiply. Three moves in that order, and the trap has nothing left to catch.

***

**Mandy:** Last one: magnetic declination and magnetic bearing keep getting confused in answers. How do you keep the two straight when the question mixes them?

**Tutor:** Keep them straight by what kind of thing each one is. Magnetic declination is an angle between two norths — a property of the place and the year, belonging to the map sheet itself. It exists before you choose any destination; it is simply how far the needle lies west of true north here, this year. A bearing, true or magnetic, is a direction to somewhere — from the school to the windmill, measured clockwise from a chosen north. The declination is the exchange rate; the bearing is the price you are converting. So when a question says calculate the magnetic declination for this year, you are updating the exchange rate: years times annual change, applied to the old angle. When it says calculate the magnetic bearing, you are converting a direction: first read the true bearing off the map with your protractor, then add today's declination because ours lies west. One updates an angle between norths; the other corrects a direction to a destination using that angle. Name which job the question is asking before you write a single number, and the two never blur again.
