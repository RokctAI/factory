**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First up: two people want to know how the same Kalahari High can be called strong in winter but weak in summer. What is actually changing — does the cell move away?

**Tutor:** It does not go anywhere; it changes weight and altitude, and the driver is the ground beneath it. An anticyclone over land is at its healthiest when the surface below it is cold. In winter the plateau loses heat fast under clear skies, the air above it chills, chilled air is dense, and dense air piles up into a deep, heavy, surface-hugging high — that is the Kalahari High at full strength, with central pressures that can top one thousand and thirty hectopascals. In summer the same plateau becomes a hotplate. Intense heating makes the surface air buoyant, and buoyant air undermines a surface high from below; the cell weakens, and what remains of the anticyclonic circulation lifts off the ground and operates higher up — which is why some charts show a summer heat low at the surface with the high-pressure influence persisting aloft. So write it as a seesaw driven by surface temperature: cold plateau, strong low-sitting high, low inversion, locked-out moisture; hot plateau, weakened lifted high, high inversion, moisture pouring in. One landmass, two thermal states, two seasons of weather.

***

**Mandy:** Next question: I understand the inversion stops rain, but why does the exam keep going on about the ESCARPMENT? What does the step itself actually do?

**Tutor:** The escarpment is the doorframe, and the inversion is the door — the two only make sense together. Moist maritime air lives at sea level, and to rain on the interior it must first climb roughly fifteen hundred metres up the escarpment onto the plateau. Whether it can depends entirely on where the inversion sits relative to the top of that step. In winter the inversion lies below the escarpment crest, so the moist air climbs, hits the warm ceiling before it reaches the top, and slides back — the door is shut inside the doorframe, and the plateau stays dry no matter how wet the coastal air is. In summer the inversion rises above the crest, so the same moist air clears the step with room overhead to keep rising — the door stands open, and convection over the hot plateau does the rest. That is why a single sentence about inversion height is never full marks: the marks come from the comparison — inversion below the escarpment in winter, above it in summer — because the step is the fixed measuring stick against which the moving ceiling matters. Draw it once as a staircase with a sliding shelf over it and you will never write it vaguely again.

***

**Mandy:** Third one, from the coastal contingent: when a berg wind is blowing, is the sea also hot? Someone said you can surf in July because of berg winds.

**Tutor:** Half right, and the half matters. The berg wind heats the air, not the ocean. The wind arriving at the coast has descended from the plateau — it is hot because it has been compressed, and it is blowing offshore, from land to sea. So a berg-wind day gives you a beach where the air might be thirty-four degrees while the sea keeps whatever temperature its current dictates — still cool on the Eastern Cape coast in July. Surfers do love those mornings, but for a different reason: an offshore wind grooms the incoming swell, holding the wave faces up cleanly, so conditions look glassy and well-shaped. There is also a subtler ocean effect worth a mark: a sustained offshore or coast-parallel wind can push surface water away from the beach and draw deeper, colder water up in its place, so after a long berg-wind spell the sea can actually turn colder. And remember the safety sentence: that same beautiful offshore morning is a peak fire-danger day on land, and it usually ends abruptly — the coastal low or front behind it swings the wind onshore and drops the temperature within the hour. Warm air, cool sea, clean waves, high fire danger, short life: that is the berg-wind beach day in five phrases.

**Mandy:** Last one: in data questions we sometimes get a station reporting drizzle and onshore wind at the exact time another station inland reports clear frosty conditions. How can both be true on one winter chart?

**Tutor:** Both are true because two different mechanisms are running on opposite sides of the escarpment, and that contrast is a favourite data question. Take the inland station first: it sits on the plateau under the winter Kalahari High — subsiding air, low inversion, cloudless sky. Clear nights let the ground radiate its heat away unhindered, so by morning the station reports frost, calm air and perfect visibility. Now the coastal station: if a front has just passed, the South Atlantic High is ridging around the south coast, driving cool, moist marine air onshore. That shallow moist layer condenses into low stratus and drizzle against the coast — but it is far too shallow and too capped to climb the escarpment, so none of it reaches the plateau. One chart, two honest reports: drizzle and onshore south-westerlies below the step, frost and sunshine above it. In your answer, name both mechanisms — ridging high at the coast, Kalahari High and low inversion inland — and state the barrier between them. The moment you treat the escarpment as the divider between two weather worlds, every mixed-signal winter chart starts making sense.
