Welcome, student. Today we secure the most fundamental of the mapwork calculations — the one every other calculation quietly depends on: map scale and distance. Our exam problem is the standard form it always takes: the distance between two points on a 1:50 000 topographic map is 8,6 centimetres — calculate the real distance in kilometres. Four lines of working, two minutes, full marks. Let us build those lines properly.

## Subtopic: What 1:50 000 Actually Means

The scale of a map is the relationship between distance on the map and distance on the real ground. Our topographic sheets use a ratio scale: 1:50 000, spoken as "one to fifty thousand".

Read it literally: ONE unit of any length on the map represents FIFTY THOUSAND of those same units on the ground. One centimetre on paper is 50 000 centimetres of real South Africa. One millimetre is 50 000 millimetres. The ratio has no units of its own — that is its power. Whatever unit you measure with, multiply by 50 000 and you have reality in that same unit.

Get a feel for the size: 50 000 centimetres is 500 metres, half a kilometre. So on a 1:50 000 sheet, every centimetre of paper is half a kilometre of ground. Two centimetres, one kilometre. A ruler laid across the map is literally measuring the landscape.

Know the other ways scale appears, because questions test the conversions between them. The word scale says it in words: "one centimetre represents half a kilometre". The linear scale is the divided bar drawn at the bottom of the sheet — useful for stepping off distances with dividers, no arithmetic needed. All three say the same thing in different dialects; the ratio form is the one we calculate with.

One more idea examiners probe: large scale versus small scale. A 1:10 000 map is a LARGER scale than 1:50 000 — counterintuitive until you see why: one over ten thousand is a bigger fraction than one over fifty thousand. Larger scale, smaller area shown, more detail. Orthophoto maps at 1:10 000, which you meet throughout Grade 12 mapwork, are the large-scale partners of our topographic sheet.

## Subtopic: The Straight-Line Calculation

Now the exam problem, in the layout that collects every memo mark.

Step one: measure — or in our case, receive — the map distance. The question gives it: 8,6 centimetres between the two points. When you measure yourself, place the ruler edge precisely point-centre to point-centre, and read to the nearest millimetre. Sloppy measuring is not a small sin: at 1:50 000, every millimetre of error is 50 metres of ground.

Step two: state the conversion the scale licenses. Real distance equals map distance multiplied by the scale factor. Write it as a formula line: real distance = map distance × 50 000.

Step three: substitute and multiply, staying in centimetres for now: 8,6 centimetres times 50 000 gives 430 000 centimetres. Do the multiplication confidently: 8 times 50 000 is 400 000; 0,6 times 50 000 is 30 000; together, 430 000.

Step four: convert to the unit the question demands — kilometres. March through the metric ladder deliberately. Centimetres to metres: divide by 100 — 430 000 centimetres is 4 300 metres. Metres to kilometres: divide by 1 000 — 4 300 metres is 4,3 kilometres.

Answer: the real distance is 4,3 kilometres. Say it with units — a naked "4,3" forfeits the unit mark on most memos.

There is a shortcut worth knowing once the long method is secure: on a 1:50 000 map, centimetres times 0,5 gives kilometres directly, because each centimetre is half a kilometre. Check: 8,6 times 0,5 is 4,3. Same answer, one step. Use the shortcut to verify, but show the full ladder in your working — the memo awards the steps, and the shortcut only works on this one scale while the method works on every scale.

## Subtopic: Curved Distances and Other Scales

Exam setters have two favourite variations, and both fall to the same method.

Variation one: the distance is not straight. "Measure the distance along the R44 from the bridge to the station" — a winding road. A rigid ruler cannot follow bends, so bring the string technique: lay a piece of string or the edge of a strip of paper along the road, marking each straight segment in turn, then straighten it against your ruler. Read the total map distance — suppose it comes to 12,4 centimetres — and from there the calculation is identical: multiply by 50 000, walk the unit ladder, 6,2 kilometres. The measuring changes; the mathematics never does.

Variation two: a different scale. Orthophoto maps come at 1:10 000. Same question there: 8,6 centimetres times 10 000 is 86 000 centimetres — 860 metres, or 0,86 kilometres. Notice the same map measurement means far less ground on the larger-scale map — that is what "more detail, less area" looks like in numbers. Never assume the scale; read it off the sheet every time. Mixed-scale questions — "the same two points appear on both maps" — are testing exactly whether you reached for the right factor on each.

And the reverse question appears too: "the real distance is 4,3 kilometres; how far apart are the points on the 1:50 000 map?" Run the machine backwards: 4,3 kilometres is 430 000 centimetres; divide by 50 000 — 8,6 centimetres. Multiplication takes you map-to-ground; division brings you ground-to-map. One relationship, both directions.

## Subtopic: Verification, Error Traps and Where This Skill Feeds

Verification first — the sense check that saves matrics every year. On a 1:50 000 sheet, a hand-span of about 10 centimetres is 5 kilometres. Our 8,6 centimetres giving 4,3 kilometres sits exactly where it should: a bit under a hand-span, a bit under 5 kilometres. If your answer had come out as 43 kilometres or 0,43 kilometres, the sense check catches the slipped decimal instantly. Build the reflex: convert, then ask "could I walk this in an afternoon?"

Now the traps. Trap one, the deadliest: unit-ladder stumbles — dividing by 1 000 instead of 100 on the centimetre-to-metre rung, or stopping at metres when kilometres were demanded. Recite the ladder: ÷100 to metres, ÷1 000 to kilometres. Trap two: multiplying by the wrong scale because the question quietly switched to the orthophoto. Read the sheet. Trap three: rounding the map measurement before multiplying — 8,6 rounded to 9 centimetres balloons into a 200-metre error. Carry the full measurement through; round only at the end if asked. Trap four: answering in the wrong unit — the question said kilometres; a correct 4 300 with "metres" attached still drops the final mark if kilometres were demanded.

Where does this skill travel? Everywhere in Question 3. The gradient calculation needs the horizontal distance between two points — this calculation provides it. Cross-sections need real distances along the section line. Area calculations multiply two of these distances together. Development and settlement questions ask "how far is the school from the informal settlement?" — scale and distance again, wearing context. Ten of the thirty mapwork marks are calculations, and nearly all of them open with the move you now own: measure, multiply by the scale, walk the ladder, state the unit, sense-check.

Practise on a real sheet tonight: pick two farms, a stretch of railway, a dam wall. Measure, convert, check against the linear scale bar. When your string, your ruler and your arithmetic all agree, this question becomes the safest two minutes of your matric paper.

Class is dismissed.
