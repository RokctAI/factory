# Part 1 — Expert

Every calculation in the mapwork question rests on a single conversion: a distance measured on paper turned into a distance on the ground. This session builds that conversion from the meaning of the ratio scale itself, through the layout that collects every mark the memorandum offers, out to winding routes, orthophoto sheets and the reverse calculation, and finishes with the verification habits that expose a slipped decimal before a marker ever sees it. The worked problem carried throughout is the standard form the examiners use: the distance between two points on a 1:50 000 topographic map is 8,6 centimetres — calculate the real distance in kilometres.

## Subtopic: What 1:50 000 Actually Means

The scale of a map is the relationship between distance on the map and distance on the real ground. Our topographic sheets use a ratio scale: 1:50 000, spoken as "one to fifty thousand".

Read it literally: ONE unit of any length on the map represents FIFTY THOUSAND of those same units on the ground. One centimetre on paper is 50 000 centimetres of real South Africa. One millimetre is 50 000 millimetres. The ratio has no units of its own — that is its power. Whatever unit you measure with, multiply by 50 000 and you have reality in that same unit.

Get a feel for the size: 50 000 centimetres is 500 metres, half a kilometre. So on a 1:50 000 sheet, every centimetre of paper is half a kilometre of ground. Two centimetres, one kilometre. A ruler laid across the map is literally measuring the landscape.

Know the other ways scale appears, because questions test the conversions between them. The word scale says it in words: "one centimetre represents half a kilometre". The linear scale is the divided bar drawn at the bottom of the sheet — useful for stepping off distances with dividers, no arithmetic needed. All three say the same thing in different dialects; the ratio form is the one we calculate with.

One more idea examiners probe: large scale versus small scale. A 1:10 000 map is a LARGER scale than 1:50 000 — counterintuitive until you see why: one over ten thousand is a bigger fraction than one over fifty thousand. Larger scale, smaller area shown, more detail. Orthophoto maps at 1:10 000, which you meet throughout Grade 12 mapwork, are the large-scale partners of our topographic sheet.

Pause here, because this section's questions are with you now: the literal reading of the ratio, the three dialects in which scale appears on a sheet, and which of two ratios names the larger scale.

## Subtopic: The Straight-Line Calculation

Now the exam problem, in the layout that collects every memo mark.

Step one: measure — or in our case, receive — the map distance. The question gives it: 8,6 centimetres between the two points. When you measure yourself, place the ruler edge precisely point-centre to point-centre, and read to the nearest millimetre. Sloppy measuring is not a small sin: at 1:50 000, every millimetre of error is 50 metres of ground.

Step two: state the conversion the scale licenses. Real distance equals map distance multiplied by the scale factor. Write it as a formula line: real distance = map distance × 50 000.

Step three: substitute and multiply, staying in centimetres for now: 8,6 centimetres times 50 000 gives 430 000 centimetres. Do the multiplication confidently: 8 times 50 000 is 400 000; 0,6 times 50 000 is 30 000; together, 430 000.

Step four: convert to the unit the question demands — kilometres. March through the metric ladder deliberately. Centimetres to metres: divide by 100 — 430 000 centimetres is 4 300 metres. Metres to kilometres: divide by 1 000 — 4 300 metres is 4,3 kilometres.

Answer: the real distance is 4,3 kilometres. Say it with units — a naked "4,3" forfeits the unit mark on most memos.

There is a shortcut worth knowing once the long method is secure: on a 1:50 000 map, centimetres times 0,5 gives kilometres directly, because each centimetre is half a kilometre. Check: 8,6 times 0,5 is 4,3. Same answer, one step. Use the shortcut to verify, but show the full ladder in your working — the memo awards the steps, and the shortcut only works on this one scale while the method works on every scale.

Work through this section's questions before you move on: the four steps in their correct order, the value each line carries in the memorandum, and the ground error that one careless millimetre produces.

## Subtopic: Curved Distances and Other Scales

Exam setters have two favourite variations, and both fall to the same method.

Variation one: the distance is not straight. "Measure the distance along the R44 from the bridge to the station" — a winding road. A rigid ruler cannot follow bends, so bring the string technique: lay a piece of string or the edge of a strip of paper along the road, marking each straight segment in turn, then straighten it against your ruler. Read the total map distance — suppose it comes to 12,4 centimetres — and from there the calculation is identical: multiply by 50 000, walk the unit ladder, 6,2 kilometres. The measuring changes; the mathematics never does.

Variation two: a different scale. Orthophoto maps come at 1:10 000. Same question there: 8,6 centimetres times 10 000 is 86 000 centimetres — 860 metres, or 0,86 kilometres. Notice the same map measurement means far less ground on the larger-scale map — that is what "more detail, less area" looks like in numbers. Never assume the scale; read it off the sheet every time. Mixed-scale questions — "the same two points appear on both maps" — are testing exactly whether you reached for the right factor on each.

And the reverse question appears too: "the real distance is 4,3 kilometres; how far apart are the points on the 1:50 000 map?" Run the machine backwards: 4,3 kilometres is 430 000 centimetres; divide by 50 000 — 8,6 centimetres. Multiplication takes you map-to-ground; division brings you ground-to-map. One relationship, both directions.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: the string technique applied to a curved route, the factor that changes when the sheet changes, and the division that drives the conversion backwards.

## Subtopic: Verification, Error Traps and Where This Skill Feeds

Verification first — the sense check that saves matrics every year. On a 1:50 000 sheet, a hand-span of about 10 centimetres is 5 kilometres. Our 8,6 centimetres giving 4,3 kilometres sits exactly where it should: a bit under a hand-span, a bit under 5 kilometres. If your answer had come out as 43 kilometres or 0,43 kilometres, the sense check catches the slipped decimal instantly. Build the reflex: convert, then ask "could I walk this in an afternoon?"

Now the traps. Trap one, the deadliest: unit-ladder stumbles — dividing by 1 000 instead of 100 on the centimetre-to-metre rung, or stopping at metres when kilometres were demanded. Recite the ladder: ÷100 to metres, ÷1 000 to kilometres. Trap two: multiplying by the wrong scale because the question quietly switched to the orthophoto. Read the sheet. Trap three: rounding the map measurement before multiplying — 8,6 rounded to 9 centimetres balloons into a 200-metre error. Carry the full measurement through; round only at the end if asked. Trap four: answering in the wrong unit — the question said kilometres; a correct 4 300 with "metres" attached still drops the final mark if kilometres were demanded.

Where does this skill travel? Everywhere in Question 3. The gradient calculation needs the horizontal distance between two points — this calculation provides it. Cross-sections need real distances along the section line. Area calculations multiply two of these distances together. Development and settlement questions ask "how far is the school from the informal settlement?" — scale and distance again, wearing context. Ten of the thirty mapwork marks are calculations, and nearly all of them open with the move you now own: measure, multiply by the scale, walk the ladder, state the unit, sense-check.

Practise on a real sheet tonight: pick two farms, a stretch of railway, a dam wall. Measure, convert, check against the linear scale bar. When your string, your ruler and your arithmetic all agree, this becomes the safest set of marks on your matric paper.

The closing questions of this part are with you now — the sense check applied to a suspect answer, the four traps named and separated, and the downstream calculations that take this distance as their raw material.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same conversion from the shop shelf and the shoelace — a map as a photocopy that shrank a whole district, and the answer counted out like change from a hundred-rand note.

## Subtopic: The District, Shrunk Fifty Thousand Times

Forget the ratio for a moment and picture the ground itself: the hills, the tar road, the river, the school. Somebody squashed all of that down evenly until it fitted on one sheet of paper. That is all a map is — the real place, shrunk. And there is really only one thing you need to know about any map: HOW MUCH it was shrunk. Know that, and anything you measure on the paper can be un-shrunk back into real life.

The number printed on the sheet is that shrink factor. One to fifty thousand means the place was made fifty thousand times smaller to fit. So going the other way — paper back to reality — you multiply by 50 000. You are simply undoing the squashing.

Here is the part that surprises people: the shrink factor does not care what you measure with. A bottle of cordial says one part concentrate to five parts water. Mix it in a cup, mix it in a bucket for a funeral catering job — the recipe still works, because it is a comparison, not an amount. Map scale behaves the same way. Measure in centimetres and your answer comes back in centimetres; measure in millimetres and it comes back in millimetres. The ratio never argues with your ruler.

Now feel the real size. Fifty thousand centimetres is 500 metres — half a kilometre. So one centimetre of paper is half a kilometre of ground, and two centimetres is a full kilometre. Your ordinary 30-centimetre school ruler, laid flat across the sheet, is measuring 15 kilometres of South Africa.

The sheet states its shrink factor in three ways, and they all agree. In words, it may say one centimetre represents half a kilometre. As a ratio, it says 1:50 000. And along the bottom edge there is a printed bar — a little ruler divided into blocks with kilometre numbers written under them. That bar is the price-per-kilogram sticker of the map world: somebody already did the sum, so you can step a distance off with the edge of a paper strip, hold it against the bar, and read kilometres with no arithmetic at all.

Last idea, and it feels backwards until it clicks. Think of the camera on your phone. Zoom in and one face fills the screen — huge detail, but you lose the crowd. Zoom out and the whole crowd fits, but nobody has a face any more. A 1:10 000 orthophoto is the zoomed-in map: a LARGER scale, small area, big detail. Our 1:50 000 sheet is zoomed out — smaller scale, more ground, less detail.

Take the questions for this section now: the shrink factor, the cordial rule that any unit works, that printed bar along the bottom, and which sheet counts as the zoomed-in one. Go slowly; you know more of this than you think.

## Subtopic: Multiply Once, Then Step Down Twice

Here comes the actual sum, and it is friendlier than it looks, because you already do this arithmetic with money.

We measured 8,6 centimetres between our two points. Un-shrink it: 8,6 times 50 000, taken in two easy bites — 8 times 50 000 is 400 000, and 0,6 times 50 000 is 30 000, so together 430 000. The ground distance is 430 000 centimetres. Perfectly true, and completely useless to a human being. Nobody says the clinic is four hundred and thirty thousand centimetres away.

So we step down. A hundred cents make one rand; a hundred centimetres make one metre. Same move: 430 000 centimetres becomes 4 300 metres, the way 430 000 cents becomes four thousand three hundred rand. Then one more step: a thousand metres make one kilometre, the way a thousand rand makes a neat bundle, so 4 300 metres becomes 4,3 kilometres. There is the answer — 4,3 kilometres.

Write that unit down and say it out loud. "4,3" on its own is not an answer to a geographer — 4,3 what? Kilometres. That single word is often worth a mark all by itself, and it is the cheapest mark in the paper.

A lovely shortcut hides in this particular sheet, too. Since one centimetre is half a kilometre, just halve your centimetres: 8,6 halved is 4,3. Same answer, one move. Use it to check yourself, but write out the full working in the exam, because the marker pays for each step, and halving only works on this one scale while the long way works on every map ever printed.

That is the whole method, and now you can hear its name: multiply by the shrink factor, then step down the ladder from centimetres to metres to kilometres. Get it right once and you own it — it does not change again all year.

## Subtopic: Shoelaces, Zoomed-in Sheets and the Hand-Span Test

Three real-life complications, and you can beat all three with what you already have.

First, roads bend, and a stiff ruler cannot follow them. Use a shoelace, a piece of string, or the edge of a paper strip: press it into every curve of the road, pinch where it ends, then straighten it against the ruler. Say it comes to 12,4 centimetres. Nothing else changes — 12,4 times 50 000 is 620 000 centimetres, which steps down to 6 200 metres, and then to 6,2 kilometres. New measuring tool, same old sum.

Second, the sheet can change under you. On a 1:10 000 orthophoto, that same 8,6 centimetres un-shrinks by only 10 000: 86 000 centimetres, which is 860 metres, or 0,86 kilometres. Same paper distance, a fifth of the ground — because the orthophoto is the zoomed-in map. So glance at the scale printed on whichever sheet is in your hand before you reach for a multiplier.

Third, they sometimes ask it backwards: here is the real distance, how far apart is it on the map? Run the machine in reverse. 4,3 kilometres is 430 000 centimetres; divide by 50 000 and you get 8,6 centimetres. Forwards you multiply, backwards you divide — and running it backwards is a beautiful way to check a forward answer.

Now the safety net, and it costs nothing. Spread your hand on the sheet: that span is roughly 10 centimetres, which on this map is about 5 kilometres — call it an hour's walk. Our 8,6 centimetres came out at 4,3 kilometres: a bit less than a hand-span, a bit less than an hour's walk. It fits. Had the answer said 43 kilometres, you would know instantly that a decimal fell off a rung, because nobody walks that in an hour.

And that is genuinely the whole topic — you have it. Measure to the nearest millimetre, never round before you multiply, use the scale actually printed on the sheet in front of you, finish in the unit the question asked for, and give every answer the hand-span test.

Here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the shoelace on a bending road, the zoomed-in orthophoto, the sum run backwards, and the hand-span test that catches a runaway decimal. Land these and every other mapwork calculation — gradient, cross-section, area — is standing on ground you have already made solid.
