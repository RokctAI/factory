# Part 1 — Expert

The mid-latitude cyclone is the great winter rain-maker of the Western Cape and the most heavily examined weather system in the matric paper. Other names on the paper mean the same thing: frontal depression, extra-tropical cyclone, temperate cyclone. This session builds the system from the ground up: where and why these storms are born, the three-stage life cycle every examiner asks about, the exact weather sequence a cold front drags across a city, and the synoptic-chart skills and management strategies that finish the question. Hold one picture throughout: two enormous bodies of air, one warm and one cold, fighting along a boundary south of the country, and a wave on that boundary growing into a spinning storm.

## Subtopic: Where and Why Mid-Latitude Cyclones Form

Mid-latitude cyclones form between thirty and sixty degrees of latitude, over the oceans, in both hemispheres. Ours are born over the cold Southern Ocean, south and south-west of South Africa, and they travel from west to east, steered by the westerly wind belt. That direction of travel is the first exam fact: every frontal system that reaches Cape Town arrives from the Atlantic side and leaves over the Indian Ocean side.

Why there? Because that latitude belt is where two completely different air masses meet. From the south comes cold, dense polar air. From the north comes warm, moist subtropical air. The boundary where they meet is the polar front — not a wall, but a long, sloping surface where cold air wedges under warm air. The two air masses do not mix, for the same reason oil and water do not mix: their densities differ. The cold air hugs the surface; the warm air is forced to glide up and over it.

Three conditions must hold for a cyclone to develop on that front. First, the front itself — a sharp temperature contrast between the two air masses. Second, rotation supplied by the Coriolis force, which in the Southern Hemisphere deflects moving air to the left and spins the circulation around a low clockwise. Say that carefully, because it is the opposite of the Northern Hemisphere textbook diagrams: in our hemisphere, air spirals clockwise into a low-pressure cell and anticlockwise around a high. Third, divergence in the upper atmosphere — the fast river of air called the jet stream drags air away from the top of the developing system, which lowers the surface pressure and pulls more surface air spiralling inward.

General characteristics to state in an exam: a low-pressure cell, roughly one thousand to two thousand kilometres across, with fronts attached; it lasts several days; it travels east at forty to fifty kilometres per hour; and the systems come in families, one behind another, which is why the Cape gets three cold fronts in a week during July.

The questions on this section are with you now: the latitude belt and direction of travel, the two air masses and the polar front between them, and the three formation conditions with the Southern Hemisphere spin stated correctly.

## Subtopic: Stages of Development — Wave to Occlusion

The life cycle comes in three examinable stages. Picture the polar front as a long east-west line south of the country, cold air on the poleward side, warm air on the equatorward side, the two flowing past each other in opposite directions.

Stage one: the initial stage, also called the wave stage. Friction between the two opposing flows kinks the front. A wave forms — a shallow bend, like a flag starting to ripple. Warm air noses into the cold side; cold air hooks in behind it. Pressure at the tip of the wave begins to fall.

Stage two: the mature stage, the one the exam loves. The wave deepens into an open V lying on its side, with the low-pressure centre at the point of the V. The leading arm of the V is the warm front, where advancing warm air rises gently over retreating cold air. The trailing arm is the cold front, where advancing cold air drives under the warm air like a wedge and throws it violently upward. Between the two arms lies the warm sector, a tongue of warm, moist air. On our Southern Hemisphere charts the V opens toward the north-west, and the whole system circulates clockwise while sliding east.

Now build the cross-section in your mind, because a cross-section question is nearly guaranteed. Slice the mature cyclone from front to back through the warm sector. On the leading edge, the warm-front surface slopes gently — about one in a hundred — so warm air climbs it slowly, producing layered cloud: high cirrus first, thickening down through altostratus into deep nimbostratus, giving hours of steady, soaking rain before the front arrives at the surface. Behind it, the warm sector: mild, humid, mostly overcast. Then the cold front, sloping steeply — about one in fifty — where cold air shovels the warm air up fast, building towering cumulonimbus, short violent showers, possibly thunder and hail.

Stage three: the occlusion stage. The cold front moves faster than the warm front and eventually catches it. The warm sector is lifted clean off the ground — occluded means shut off. All the warm air now sits aloft, resting on cold air. An occluded front shows on the chart as the two fronts merged into one line. With its warm-air fuel cut off from the surface, the system weakens, the temperature contrast dies, and the cyclone dissipates.

Attend to this section's questions before moving on: the three stages in order, the anatomy of the mature stage with its warm sector, and the meaning of occlusion.

## Subtopic: Front Weather — Before, During and After

The exam's favourite question puts you in a town and asks what you experience as a cold front passes. Learn the sequence as a story in time, in three scenes: ahead of the front, during passage, behind it.

Ahead of the cold front, the town sits in the warm sector. Air temperature is mild to warm. The wind blows from the north-west, pulling warm air down from the interior. Pressure is falling steadily — the low centre is approaching. Humidity is high, cloud is building on the western horizon, and the air feels heavy.

During passage, everything changes within an hour or two. The wedge of cold air arrives and hurls the warm air upward. Cumulonimbus towers dump heavy showers; thunder is possible; on the high mountains the precipitation falls as snow. The wind swings sharply — from north-west, through west, to south-west. That wind swing is the single most reliable signature of a frontal passage, and examiners ask it directly.

Behind the front, the cold air mass owns the town. Temperature drops sharply, often by six to ten degrees Celsius. Pressure rises fast as the trailing high ridges in. The wind holds steady from the south-west, cold and gusty. The sky clears into bright, cold, showery weather — sunshine between racing cumulus, the classic crisp day after a Cape storm.

The warm front's weather is the gentle mirror: long warning, little violence. Cirrus appears a day ahead, cloud lowers and thickens for hours, then steady soft rain from nimbostratus, then the front passes and the air turns milder. South Africa mostly experiences cold fronts, because our latitude clips the northern edge of these systems — the warm front usually stays south of the country, over the ocean. Say that in an exam and you sound like a forecaster.

Now the impacts, because the case-study marks live there. Benefits first: mid-latitude cyclones deliver the Western Cape's winter rainfall, filling the dams that water Cape Town; mountain snow feeds rivers through spring. Damage second: gale-force winds strip roofs, especially in informal settlements; heavy rain floods low-lying areas like the Cape Flats; storm surf batters the coast and halts shipping around the Peninsula; snow closes mountain passes and traps livestock; cold spells threaten homeless people and spike energy demand. Management strategies: the South African Weather Service issues warnings days ahead because the systems are visible on satellite far out over the Atlantic; municipalities clear storm-water drains before winter; disaster-management teams pre-position blankets and shelter; farmers move livestock to lower ground before snow.

Take this section's questions now: the three-scene sequence with the wind swing stated, the contrast between cold-front and warm-front weather, and one benefit, one damage and one strategy.

## Subtopic: Reading the Synoptic Chart and the Satellite Image

Everything so far becomes marks only if you can find it on a synoptic weather chart, so build the chart in words. A synoptic chart is a map of surface weather at one moment. Curved lines called isobars join places of equal air pressure, labelled in hectopascals — one thousand and sixteen, one thousand and twelve, one thousand and eight. Closely packed isobars mean a strong pressure gradient and strong wind. A closed ring of low values labelled L south of the country is your cyclone. From it, a cold front is drawn as a line studded with solid triangles, the triangles pointing in the direction the front is moving — for us, pointing east or north-east. A warm front carries solid semicircles instead. An occluded front carries triangles and semicircles alternating on the same side.

To identify the stage on a chart: a small kink on a long front is the initial stage; a clear open wave with separate cold and warm fronts and a warm sector between them is the mature stage; merged fronts spiralled around the low is the occlusion stage.

Now place the two great highs, because the exam links them. The South Atlantic High sits west of the country, the South Indian High to the east. They matter twice. First, they steer and squeeze: the cold front must slide along the corridor between the highs, and in winter, when the highs shift north with the sun, that corridor lies across the Cape — which is exactly why frontal rain reaches Cape Town in winter and misses it in summer. Second, after the front passes, the South Atlantic High ridges in behind it — a tongue of high pressure curling around the south coast, bringing the clearing skies and onshore south-westerly winds of the after-front scene.

On a satellite image the mature cyclone is unmistakable: a comma-shaped band of white cloud, the head of the comma spiralling around the low, the tail streaming north-west along the cold front — often stretching from south of Cape Town far into the Atlantic. An occluding system wraps the comma head tighter into a spiral. For prediction: whatever sits west of the country on today's chart arrives tomorrow, because the westerlies conveyor everything east. See a deep low with a trailing front at longitude ten degrees east, and Cape Town should charge its phones and clear its drains.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: the front symbols and what the isobars say, the stage identification rules, the role of the two highs in winter rainfall, and the comma on the satellite image.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same storm again, told with a schoolyard fight, three photographs and one rainy Tuesday in Cape Town — the version you will actually remember in the exam room.

## Subtopic: Two Crowds at a Rope

Picture a tug-of-war rope lying across a field. On one side stands a crowd in thick winter jackets — that is the cold, heavy air from near Antarctica. On the other side, a crowd in T-shirts — warm, light air from the tropics. The rope between them is the polar front. The two crowds lean against each other all year, but they never mix, because heavy things and light things do not blend — sand settles under water every time. Cold air is the sand: it stays low and shoves along the ground. Warm air is the water: whenever the crowds collide, it is always the warm air that gets lifted.

And lifted air is the whole secret of rain. Air that rises, cools. Air that cools cannot hold its moisture, so the moisture becomes cloud, and cloud becomes rain. Every drop of Cape winter rain begins with warm air being forced upward at that rope.

Where does the spin come from? The Earth is turning under the fight. In our half of the world that turning nudges every moving thing to its left, so when air starts rushing toward a point of low pressure, it cannot run straight in — it curves, and the whole system winds up spinning clockwise, like water circling a drain. One more helper works from above: a super-fast wind high overhead, the jet stream, acts like an extractor fan sucking air off the top of the storm, so the pressure at the bottom keeps dropping and the storm keeps pulling more air in.

So: a rope where jackets meet T-shirts, warm air forced up whenever they push, a leftward nudge that sets it spinning, and a fan on the roof. That is a mid-latitude cyclone before the big words arrive.

Your questions for this section are with you now: which air rides over which and why, where the rain actually comes from, and which way the spin turns down here. Trust the rope and the drain and you have them.

## Subtopic: The Storm's Life in Three Photographs

Now watch the storm grow up in three photographs, because the exam always asks for the stages.

Photograph one: the rope gets a bend in it. The two crowds have been sliding past each other in opposite directions, and the rubbing kinks the line — a small wave, warm air bulging into the cold side. That is the initial stage, and it looks like almost nothing.

Photograph two: the bend has grown into a giant sideways V, and this is the picture to study hardest — the mature stage. The V has two arms. The front arm is the polite one: warm air sliding gently up over cold air like a car easing up a long ramp — that is the warm front, and its rain is soft and steady and lasts for hours. The back arm is the violent one: cold air ramming underneath like a spade under soil, flinging the warm air straight up — that is the cold front, and its rain is short, hard and stormy. Between the arms sits a wedge of warm muggy air called the warm sector — think of it as the filling in the sandwich, warm filling between two cold slices of bread.

Photograph three: the chase ends. The back arm moves faster than the front arm — the spade catches the ramp. The cold front catches the warm front, and the warm filling gets squeezed up and off the ground completely. That is occlusion, which simply means shut off: the storm's warm fuel is cut off from the surface, and without fuel the storm fades and dies, like a fire that has burned through its wood.

Three photographs: the bend, the V with its sandwich, the squeeze. If you can draw those three from memory, with the cold front carrying little triangles and the warm front carrying little half-moons, the stages question is banked.

Take the questions for this section now: what each photograph shows, which arm is violent and which is gentle, and what occlusion cuts off.

## Subtopic: A Rainy Tuesday in Cape Town

Finish with the question they ask most: describe what a person in Cape Town experiences as a cold front passes. Tell it as one Tuesday.

Tuesday morning, before the front. The air is oddly warm and sticky for winter. The wind is coming from the north-west — remember it as wind from the Namibia side. The clouds are stacking up out to sea, and anyone with a barometer sees the pressure sliding down, down, down, like a fuel gauge on a long trip. Gran says her knees can feel the storm coming. Gran is doing synoptic forecasting.

Tuesday afternoon, the front hits. In the space of an hour the sky turns black, the rain comes down in hard cold sheets, thunder is possible, and on the Boland mountains it is snowing. The big tell is the wind: it swings right around, from north-west to south-west — from the Namibia side to the Antarctica side. Whenever an exam answer describes a frontal passage, that wind swing must be in it. It is the storm's signature, as personal as handwriting.

Tuesday night and Wednesday, after the front. The air is suddenly cold — jersey weather, six to ten degrees colder than the morning. The pressure is climbing again because a big high-pressure cell is shouldering in behind the storm. The sky breaks into fast-moving puffy clouds with cold sunshine between showers, and the south-wester keeps blowing. Washing dries; the mountain wears fresh snow; the dams are fuller.

Why does this happen to Cape Town in winter only? Because in winter the whole weather machine shifts north with the sun, so the storm track runs over the Cape. In summer it shifts south again and the storms pass by in the ocean, waving from a distance. And why does it matter? The good: those storms fill the dams — the Western Cape drinks mid-latitude cyclones. The bad: roofs blown off, floods on the Cape Flats, passes closed by snow, freezing nights for people without shelter. The wise response: listen to the weather-service warnings, clear the drains before winter, get blankets and shelters ready before the front, not after.

Here come the final questions of the lesson, right now: morning, afternoon and night on that Tuesday, the wind swing that signs the storm's name, and why winter brings the storms to the Cape. Tell the Tuesday story and every mark is yours.
