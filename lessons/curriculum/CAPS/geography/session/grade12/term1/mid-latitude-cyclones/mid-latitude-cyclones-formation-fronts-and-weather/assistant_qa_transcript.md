**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one, and half the class got caught by it: the diagram in my textbook shows the low spinning anticlockwise, but you said clockwise. Which one goes in the exam?

**Tutor:** Both are right — for different halves of the planet, and that is exactly the trap. Most textbook diagrams are drawn for the Northern Hemisphere, where air spirals anticlockwise into a low. We live in the Southern Hemisphere, where the Coriolis deflection works to the left instead of the right, so air spirals clockwise into a low and anticlockwise around a high. The DBE examines South African charts, so on your paper the answer is always the Southern Hemisphere version: clockwise around the low. Here is a way to lock it in. Take our charts at face value — the cold front trails from the low toward the north-west, and the whole system rolls eastward like a wheel turning clockwise when viewed from above. If you ever blank in the exam, write the letters L and H in the margin and remind yourself: in South Africa, lows go with the clock, highs go against it. And when you draw the mature stage, check your arrows match that spin before you move on, because an otherwise perfect diagram with Northern Hemisphere arrows loses the interpretation marks.

***

**Mandy:** Next: I mix up which front makes the soft rain and which makes the storms. Is there a way to keep them straight without memorising?

**Tutor:** Reason it from the slopes, and you will never need to memorise again. The warm front is a gentle ramp — the warm air glides up over the retreating cold air at a slope of roughly one in a hundred. Air that rises slowly cools slowly, so it builds wide, flat, layered cloud: cirrus first, then altostratus, then nimbostratus, and nimbostratus delivers hours of steady, soaking drizzle-to-rain. No violence, because nothing is being thrown upward. The cold front is not a ramp at all — it is a wedge being driven under the warm air at about one in fifty, twice as steep, and moving faster. Warm air gets shovelled violently upward, and fast lifting builds tall cloud: cumulonimbus, the thunderstorm factory. Tall cloud means short, hard showers, possible thunder and hail, then clearing. So the chain is: slope decides lifting speed, lifting speed decides cloud shape, cloud shape decides rain type. Gentle slope, layer cloud, long soft rain. Steep wedge, tower cloud, short violent rain. Reconstruct that chain in the exam and the marks follow even if the labels momentarily desert you.

***

**Mandy:** One from the back: on the synoptic chart, how do I actually tell which stage the cyclone is in? They all just look like squiggles to me.

**Tutor:** Use a three-check routine, in order, and the squiggles resolve. Check one: how many separate fronts hang off the low? If you see one long front with only a slight kink or a shallow wave in it, the system is in the initial stage — the wave has just formed. Check two: if you see two clearly separate fronts, a cold front with triangles and a warm front with semicircles, meeting at the low with open space between them, you are looking at the mature stage — and that open space between the fronts is the warm sector, which you can confirm by the warmer temperatures and north-westerly winds reported at stations inside it. Check three: if the triangles and semicircles have merged onto a single line — alternating on the same side — and that line spirals tightly around the low, the cold front has caught the warm front and the system is occluding. Two supporting clues seal the identification: the deeper the central pressure and the more tightly the isobars wrap into circles, the older the system; and on the matching satellite image, an open comma means mature while a tightly wound spiral means occluding. Run those checks aloud in your head and write the stage with its evidence — examiners award the reason, not just the label.

***

**Mandy:** Last one: my cousin in Durban says cold fronts are a Cape thing. Do they really do anything up the east coast and the interior?

**Tutor:** They do, and the exam loves exactly this misconception. The front itself weakens as it slides east and north, but its effects sweep most of the country. Along the south and east coasts, the cold front drives powerful swells — the waves that close the harbour at times and delight surfers at Jeffreys Bay. Behind the front, that cold south-westerly air rides up the east coast and can drop Durban's temperature noticeably within a day. Over the interior plateau, winter cold fronts bring the icy snaps that Gauteng knows well — frost, sometimes snow on the Drakensberg and the high Eastern Cape, and the cold spells that spike electricity demand nationwide. There is also the pre-frontal effect: a day or so before the front arrives, air drawn down off the plateau ahead of it produces berg wind conditions — unseasonably hot, dry days on the coast, with raised fire danger, and that is a favourite exam link between this topic and the anticyclones topic. So the full answer is: the Cape gets the rain, but the whole country feels the passage — waves, cold snaps, snow on the high ground, and the hot berg wind day before the change. A matric answer that traces those knock-on effects across the map reads like a forecaster wrote it.
