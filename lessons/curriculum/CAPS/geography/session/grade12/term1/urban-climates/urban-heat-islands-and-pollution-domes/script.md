# Part 1 — Expert

A city manufactures its own climate. Johannesburg is measurably warmer than the farmland around it, rains a little harder on its downwind side, and wears a lid of dirty air on winter mornings. This session covers urban climates as the DBE frames the topic: the reasons urban and rural climates differ, element by element; the urban heat island with its definition, causes, effects and reduction strategies; and the pollution dome and plume with theirs. The examiner's favourite command words here are define, account for and suggest strategies — so every section builds toward those three moves.

## Subtopic: Why City Air Differs from Farm Air

Compare a city block with a maize field an hour away, element by element, and give the mechanism for each difference — that is the standing exam format.

Temperature: the city is warmer, especially at night — often two to six degrees, sometimes more. Mechanisms follow in the next section, because this difference gets its own name.

Surface materials: the countryside is covered in soil, grass and crops, which hold moisture and reflect a fair share of sunlight. The city is tar, concrete, brick and glass — dark, dry materials with low albedo, meaning they reflect little and absorb most of the radiation that strikes them, storing it as heat through the day and releasing it slowly at night.

Moisture and humidity: rural ground breathes. Plants transpire and soil evaporates, spending solar energy on turning water into vapour rather than on heating the air. The city is sealed: rain lands on roofs and roads, races into storm-water drains, and is gone. Little water remains to evaporate, so nearly all the sun's energy goes into heating surfaces, and the city's relative humidity runs lower.

Wind: the open field lets wind run; average speeds are higher. The city's buildings act as a rough obstacle course, slowing the general wind — yet the street grid funnels whatever wind remains between tall buildings, so a city gives gusty canyons inside an overall calmer roughness layer. Tall-building corridors can even generate their own local winds on otherwise still days.

Cloud, rain and fog: cities run slightly cloudier and rainier than their surroundings — a few percent more rainfall, often falling on or just downwind of the city. Two mechanisms: the heat island drives convection, lifting air over the warm city; and the city exhales condensation nuclei — smoke, dust, exhaust particles — around which droplets form readily. The same nuclei make urban fog thicker and more frequent, historically the smogs of coal-burning cities.

Sunshine: urban haze filters and scatters incoming radiation, so the city receives measurably less direct sunshine than the farm, even while staying warmer.

Take this section's questions now: each element — surface, moisture, wind, precipitation, sunshine — paired with its mechanism, city against countryside.

## Subtopic: The Urban Heat Island — Definition and Causes

Definition first, exam-ready: an urban heat island is the dome of warmer air over a city, where temperatures are noticeably higher than in the surrounding rural areas, with the effect strongest at night and in winter, and peaking over the dense city centre. Sketch its profile in words: temperature climbs gently from the rural edge, rises across the suburbs, plateaus with small dips over parks and open water, and peaks over the CBD — then falls away on the far side. The dips over parks matter: they prove vegetation cools, which is the hinge of every reduction strategy.

Now the causes, each one a mark, each with its mechanism.

Cause one: absorption by dark surfaces. Low-albedo tar and concrete absorb most incoming radiation, store it through the day and re-radiate it through the night — the city is a storage heater that discharges after sunset, which is exactly why the heat island peaks at night.

Cause two: anthropogenic heat — heat released by human activity. Vehicle engines, industry, air conditioners, heaters, cooking fires, millions of human bodies: a city constantly leaks waste heat into its own air, and in winter, when heating runs hardest, this source strengthens — one reason the island is strongest in winter.

Cause three: the missing evaporation. With rainwater drained away and vegetation replaced by sealed surfaces, hardly any solar energy is spent on evaporation; nearly all of it heats the surfaces and the air. The rural energy budget spends on evaporation; the urban budget spends on temperature.

Cause four: geometry — the urban canyon. Vertical walls catch radiation that would otherwise escape upward, reflecting it wall to wall and down into the street, trapping energy the way a pocket traps a coin. At night, the narrow strip of sky visible from a street floor gives stored heat only a small window through which to radiate away, so streets cool far slower than open fields.

Cause five: the pollution blanket. The haze layer over the city absorbs some of the heat radiating up from the surface at night and re-radiates part of it back down — a local greenhouse layer over the city.

Attend to this section's questions before moving on: the definition with its night and winter peak, the profile with its park dips, and the five causes each tied to its mechanism.

## Subtopic: Heat Island Effects and How Cities Cool Themselves

Effects, grouped for essay use. On comfort and health: hotter nights deny the body its recovery period during heat waves, raising heat-stress illness and mortality, with the elderly, infants and outdoor workers most exposed — and in South African cities, residents of tin-roofed informal housing carry the worst of it, since a zinc roof turns a shack into an oven. On energy and water: air-conditioning demand climbs in summer, loading Eskom's grid at peak; gardens and parks demand more irrigation in the drier urban air. On weather itself: the island's convection can strengthen afternoon thunderstorms over and downwind of the city — Gauteng's hail seasons ride partly on this — and the rising warm air helps hold the pollution dome aloft over the city. On ecology: urban trees bud earlier, some pests overwinter that frost would kill in the countryside, and the seasons themselves blur at the city core.

Reduction strategies — the suggest-strategies marks — organised by the cause each attacks. Attack the albedo: paint roofs white or light colours, use light paving, choose reflective materials — raising reflection lowers absorption. Attack the missing vegetation: plant street trees, keep and extend parks and green belts, green the roofs — vegetation cools twice, by shading surfaces and by transpiring, spending energy on evaporation instead of temperature; the park dips in the temperature profile are the proof it works. Attack the sealed surface: permeable paving, retention ponds and urban wetlands keep water in the city so evaporation can resume. Attack anthropogenic heat: efficient buildings, insulation, public transport instead of single-car commuting, cleaner energy. Attack the geometry: orient and space new buildings to ventilate streets, protect wind corridors that flush the city. Municipal versions exist in South Africa — Johannesburg's massive street-tree plantings and city greening projects, Cape Town and Durban planning documents that guard green corridors — and citing one earns the local-example mark.

The questions on this section are with you now: effects grouped into health, energy, weather and ecology, and each strategy matched to the cause it attacks.

## Subtopic: The Pollution Dome and the Pollution Plume

Definition, exam-ready: a pollution dome is the dome-shaped layer of polluted air that accumulates over a city under calm, stable conditions — the city's dirty halo. When a wind rises, the dome stretches downwind into a pollution plume, a tongue of urban pollution carried across the countryside — so dome and plume are the same pollution in calm and in windy weather, a one-mark distinction examiners love.

Causes: the sources fill it, the weather seals it. Sources — vehicle exhausts, industry and power-station stacks, domestic burning of coal and wood, especially in townships on winter mornings and evenings, dust from construction and mine dumps, and veld fires in season. The seal — a temperature inversion. On clear calm winter nights, air near the ground cools fastest; cold air lies under warm, rising smoke climbs into the warm layer, loses its buoyancy and stops, spreading sideways under the inversion as under a ceiling. South Africa's winter anticyclone deepens this seal: the subsiding air of the Kalahari High lays a persistent upper lid over the Highveld, which is why Johannesburg's winter mornings smell of smoke and the horizon turns brown — and why the Vaal Triangle and Highveld industrial zones are declared air-quality priority areas.

Effects: respiratory disease — asthma, bronchitis, infections — concentrated where domestic burning meets the inversion, in the poorest communities, an environmental-justice point worth stating plainly; smog and reduced visibility; acid deposition downwind, corroding buildings and acidifying soils and water; crop and vegetation damage under the plume; and the dome's blanket effect feeding back into the heat island at night.

Reduction strategies: cut the sources — electrification and cleaner household energy so homes need not burn coal, scrubbers and filters on industrial stacks enforced through the Air Quality Act's emission licences, vehicle emission standards, cleaner fuels and public transport, dust suppression on mine dumps; and work with the weather — no open burning on stable winter days, air-quality alerts when inversions strengthen, and industrial zoning placed downwind of, not upwind of, dense settlement. Monitoring networks in the priority areas track the dome daily; quoting the Highveld Priority Area seals the local-example mark.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: dome against plume, sources against the inversion seal, effects with the justice point, and strategies sorted into source-cutting and weather-wise.

## Subtopic: The City Is a Storage Heater

Why is town warmer than the farm? Stand barefoot on two surfaces on a summer evening and you already know. The grass is cool. The tar is still hot enough to hurt — hours after sunset. That is the whole secret: cities are built out of the hot-tar kind of material.

Think of those old storage heaters that soak up electricity all day and give off warmth all night. A city is exactly that. All day the dark tar, brick and concrete drink in sunshine; all night they slowly breathe it back out into the streets. A maize field cannot do this — it spends the day's energy differently. The field is wet: its soil and plants sweat, and sweating uses up energy. Every drop of water a plant transpires is solar energy spent on evaporation instead of on heating the air. Exactly like your own skin: you sweat, the sweat dries, you cool down. The city cannot sweat. Rain that falls on it is hustled off the roofs, down the gutters, into the drains and away — so the sun's energy has nothing to evaporate and spends itself entirely on heat.

Add three smaller burners. Every engine, aircon, stove, heater and human body in town leaks warmth — millions of small heaters running day and night. The tall buildings form canyons that bounce sunshine wall to wall and down into the streets, trapping it like a coin in a deep pocket, and at night the street can only cool through the thin ribbon of sky visible between the rooftops. And the dirty haze over the city acts like a thin blanket, catching heat on its way out and sending some of it back down.

Put it together and the city sits inside a bubble of warm air, warmest over the CBD, strongest at night and in winter. That bubble is called the URBAN HEAT ISLAND — an island of heat in a sea of cooler farmland. And here is the detail that unlocks the exam graph: over every big park, the temperature line dips. Green spots stay cool. Remember the dip; it is about to become the answer to the strategy question.

Your questions for this section are with you now: the storage heater, why the city cannot sweat, the pocket-canyons and the blanket, and the island with its park dips.

## Subtopic: Cooling the Island — Undo Each Cause

How do you cool a city? The clean way to think about it: the causes were a list of sins; the strategies are the same list, reversed. Go down the sins one by one and undo each.

Sin one was dark surfaces drinking in sunshine. Undo it: paint the town light. White and pale roofs, light-coloured paving — pale surfaces bounce sunshine away instead of storing it, the same reason nobody wears a black T-shirt to walk to town in January. Whole cities overseas now run white-roof programmes; the physics is Grade eight, the effect is real.

Sin two was the city that cannot sweat. Undo it: give the city its sweat glands back. Plant street trees, guard every park, grow gardens on rooftops. A tree cools twice — it shades the tar below it, and it transpires, spending sunshine on evaporating water instead of heating air. This is why the temperature graph dips over parks: each park is a patch of working sweat glands. When the exam asks for strategies, trees and parks are the first and best answer, and the park-dip is your evidence.

Sin three was sealing the ground. Undo it: let water stay. Paving that lets rain soak through, ponds and wetlands in the parks — water in the city means evaporation can resume, and evaporation is cooling.

Sin four was all those little heaters. Undo it: waste less heat. Insulate buildings so aircons and heaters work less; take the taxi, the bus or the train instead of one person per car; use cleaner, more efficient energy.

Sin five was the canyon pocket. Undo it: build for breeze. Space and angle new buildings so wind can flush the streets, and never build a wall of towers across the city's main wind corridor.

One sentence of why-it-matters to top the essay: hotter city nights are not a comfort issue only — in a heat wave they kill, and they kill selectively, hitting the elderly, babies and families under zinc roofs in informal settlements hardest, while pushing Eskom's grid to its summer edge. Cooling the city is a health project, a justice project and an energy project in one.

Take the questions for this section now: each sin paired with its undo, the double cooling of a tree, and who the heat hurts most.

## Subtopic: The Pot Lid of Smoke

Last piece: the city's dirty halo. Every town makes smoke — car exhausts, factory stacks, and on winter evenings the braziers and coal stoves of the townships. On a windy day the smoke is swept off and you never think about it. But on a still, cold winter night, something closes over the city like a lid over a pot.

The lid is the temperature inversion, and you have met it twice before — in the valley lesson and over the winter plateau. On a clear calm night the ground chills fast, so cold air lies at the bottom with warmer air resting on top of it. Now watch one thread of smoke rise from a chimney: it climbs while it is warmer than the air around it, reaches the warm layer, suddenly is not warmer than anything, and stops — flattening out sideways as if pressed under glass. Multiply by a hundred thousand chimneys and exhaust pipes and the whole city's smoke collects under the lid in a grey-brown dome: the POLLUTION DOME. Johannesburg in July, seen from a distance at dawn, wears it plainly — a brown rim across the horizon. When the wind returns, the dome smears out downwind into a long dirty banner: the POLLUTION PLUME. Same smoke; calm makes a dome, wind makes a plume.

Who suffers under the dome? The people inside it, and mostly the poorest of them, because the dome is thickest exactly where homes must burn coal and wood to stay warm, and the winter air there fills clinics with wheezing chests — asthma, bronchitis, children worst. Further out, the plume drops acid on crops, dams and buildings downwind.

Lifting the lid is not in our power — the winter inversion is the Kalahari High's doing. So the strategies all aim at making less smoke and timing it wisely. Bring electricity and cleaner energy to every home so nobody needs the evening coal fire. Put filters and scrubbers on the factory stacks, and let the law — the Air Quality Act and its priority areas on the Highveld — force the point. Cleaner fuels and fewer single-driver cars. No open burning on still winter days, when the lid is on. And plan the map properly: put the smoky industry downwind of the city, not upwind of the townships.

Here come the final questions of the lesson, right now: how the lid stops the smoke, dome against plume in one line, who the dome hurts first, and the strategies that shrink it. Close them out and the whole climate section of Paper 1 is behind you.
