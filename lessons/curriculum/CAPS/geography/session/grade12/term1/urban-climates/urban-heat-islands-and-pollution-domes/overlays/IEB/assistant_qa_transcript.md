**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the graph question keeps showing that temperature profile across the city. What exactly are they looking for when they say describe and explain the shape?

**Tutor:** They are looking for a guided tour, landmark by landmark, with a mechanism at every stop — so learn the profile as a route you narrate. Start at the rural fringe: temperature low, because moist, vegetated ground spends its energy on evaporation. Cross into the suburbs: the line climbs, because roofs, roads and gardens mix built surfaces with green ones. Approach the core: the line runs high and then crests over the central business district, where the tallest canyons, the densest tar and the most traffic concentrate every cause at once. Now the detail that separates good answers from full-mark answers: every time the line crosses a big park, golf course or river, it dips — and you must both point at the dip and explain it: vegetation and open water cool by shade and evaporation. Then the line falls away down the far side into the countryside. Two refinements add polish. First, say when the profile is most dramatic: on a calm, clear winter night, because that is when the storage-heater effect and anthropogenic heating dominate and no wind stirs the pot. Second, if the axes carry numbers, quote them — a difference of four degrees between CBD and farmland is evidence, not decoration. Describe the shape, explain each landmark, time it, and quote the numbers: that is the complete move.

**Mandy:** Next one: wouldn't the pollution dome actually shade the city and cancel out the heat island? It blocks sunshine, after all.

**Tutor:** Sharp question, and the answer is a beautiful piece of energy accounting. The dome does two opposite things at two different times of day, and the night effect wins. By day, yes: haze scatters and absorbs incoming sunshine, so the city centre logs fewer direct sunshine hours — a slight daytime cooling of the surface. But by night the same layer switches jobs. The city's stored heat leaves as long-wave radiation, and the particles and gases of the dome absorb part of that outgoing stream and re-radiate a share of it straight back down — a blanket, fitted only over the city. Now recall when the heat island itself peaks: at night. So the dome's night-time blanket reinforces the island exactly during its peak hours, while its daytime shading trims a little off a surplus the city barely notices. There is also a feedback running the other way: the island's rising warm air helps hold the dome in place over the city. So the two phenomena are partners, not rivals — the island props up the dome, and the dome tucks in the island. In an exam, that two-way link is a high-value sentence: it shows you see the topic as one connected system rather than two memorised lists.

**Mandy:** Third: why is the winter morning smoke so much worse in townships than in the leafy suburbs? Both are inside the same city and the same inversion.

**Tutor:** Because the inversion is shared but the sources and the shelter are not — and this is the environmental-justice core of the topic, worth stating without euphemism. Start with sources: households with electricity and insulation heat invisibly; households without affordable power burn coal, wood and paraffin, and imbawula braziers burn exactly at dawn and dusk, when the inversion lies lowest. So the largest volume of low-level smoke is released at the lowest points of the lid, in the most crowded air. Then geography compounds it: many townships were historically placed on low-lying land and, worse, downwind of industrial zones — so they receive their own heating smoke plus the factories' contribution, pressed down under the same ceiling. Then exposure: smaller dwellings, more people per room, cooking indoors over flame — the pollution is not only in the street, it is in the kitchen. The health ledger follows: asthma, bronchitis and pneumonia cluster in exactly these communities each winter, children and the elderly first. And that is why the strategy list starts with electrification and affordable clean energy rather than with lectures about lifestyle: people burn coal because it is the rational option they can afford, so the fix is to change the options. An answer that names this honestly — same sky, unequal smoke, unequal lungs — reads like a geographer who understands both the physics and the people.

**Mandy:** Last one: for the strategies question, I always just write plant more trees and hope. How do I turn that into a full-marks answer?

**Tutor:** By upgrading from a slogan to a matched pair plus a mechanism plus an example — four layers, every time. Layer one, name the cause you are attacking: the city has lost its evaporative cooling. Layer two, give the strategy precisely: plant street trees along transport corridors, protect existing parks, require green roofs on new commercial buildings. Layer three, the mechanism — this is where most answers die: a tree cools twice, first by shading the low-albedo surface so it never banks the heat, second by transpiring, which spends solar energy on evaporating water instead of warming air; then nail it to evidence — the temperature profile dips over every park, which proves the mechanism operates. Layer four, a real example: Tshwane's tree-lined avenues, greening programmes in historically bare townships, a metro spatial plan that protects green corridors. Now do the same four layers for one more cause — say albedo: light roofs, because pale surfaces reflect rather than store, as any two cars in a parking lot demonstrate by mid-afternoon. Two strategies, four layers each, beats six slogans every time, because the marks sit in the mechanism and the match, not in the list. Cause, strategy, mechanism with evidence, example — drill that chain tonight and the strategy question becomes an essay you have already written.
