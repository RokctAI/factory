**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: is the urban heat island just because cities have more people breathing and cars running? That is what I wrote last term and it only got one mark.

**Tutor:** One mark because that is one cause out of five, and the memo wants the spread. Anthropogenic heat — engines, industry, heaters, bodies — is real, but it is usually the smaller contributor. The heavyweight causes are about surfaces and water. First, the materials: tar, brick and concrete are dark, low-albedo surfaces that absorb most of the day's radiation, store it, and release it all night — the storage-heater effect, and the reason the island peaks after dark. Second, the missing evaporation: the countryside spends much of its solar energy evaporating water from soil and transpiring plants, but the sealed city drains its rain away, so nearly all its solar income is spent on heating. Third, geometry: street canyons trap radiation between walls and give the street only a ribbon of sky to cool through at night. Fourth, the haze blanket that re-radiates escaping heat back down. Fifth — and only fifth — the waste heat of human activity. For a full answer, name the island, say it peaks at night and in winter over the CBD, and then give at least three of those causes with mechanisms. One cause is one mark; the mechanism sentence attached to each cause is where the rest live.

***

**Mandy:** Next: the graph question shows the temperature line dipping in the middle of the city and I lost marks for calling it an error in the data. What is the dip?

**Tutor:** That dip is the most meaningful wiggle on the whole graph — it is a park. The urban temperature profile rises from the rural edge, climbs across the suburbs, and peaks over the CBD, but wherever the transect crosses a large park, a lake or a river, the line dips a degree or two before rising again. The reason is that green and wet surfaces behave like countryside embedded in the city: they shade the ground, they evaporate and transpire, and so they spend energy on vaporising water instead of heating air. Never call it an error — call it evidence, because that is exactly how the examiner wants it used. The dip proves that vegetation and water cool their surroundings, which is the scientific backbone of every reduction strategy you will list: street trees, green belts, rooftop gardens, wetlands and permeable paving all work by manufacturing more dips. So when the strategies question follows the graph question — and it usually does — connect them explicitly: the park dips on the profile demonstrate that greening lowers urban temperatures, therefore expanding green space is the city's most effective cooling strategy. That one linking sentence turns two separate answers into a small argument, and small arguments score.

**Mandy:** Here is one from the group chat: why is Joburg's pollution so much worse in winter? The same cars and factories run all year!

**Tutor:** Because pollution has two ingredients — the smoke and the weather — and only the weather changes with the season. Summer's atmosphere is unstable: surface heating drives strong convection, thunderstorms wash the air, and rising currents mix the smoke through a deep layer and carry it away. Winter flips every one of those switches. The Kalahari High strengthens over the plateau, and its subsiding air lays a persistent inversion over the Highveld; on top of that, long clear calm nights chill the ground, building a second, surface inversion by radiation cooling. Rising smoke climbs into that warm lid, loses its buoyancy and stops, spreading sideways — the pot lid from the lesson. Meanwhile the smoke supply actually increases in winter: homes burn coal and wood for heat, morning and evening, precisely during the hours when the lid sits lowest. More smoke, plus a sealed atmosphere, equals the brown dome you see rimming the horizon at dawn in July. That is also why the health burden lands hardest in the townships — the burning and the lid coincide over the same rooftops. In the exam, always answer this question with both ingredients: constant sources, seasonal seal. Naming the inversion mechanism is what separates the top answers.

**Mandy:** Last one: strategies questions feel like guesswork — I just write "plant trees and use public transport" for everything. How do I know I have covered enough?

**Tutor:** Stop guessing and use the mirror method: every strategy is a cause reflected backwards, so generate your list by walking the causes. For the heat island, five causes give five strategy families. Dark surfaces? Lighten them — white roofs, pale paving. No evaporation? Restore it — trees, parks, green roofs, permeable paving, wetlands. Canyon geometry? Build for ventilation and protect wind corridors. Waste heat? Efficient buildings, insulation, public transport, cleaner energy. Haze blanket? That one belongs to pollution control, which hands you the bridge to the dome question. For the pollution dome, split strategies into two baskets and label them in your answer: cutting the sources — household electrification so homes need not burn coal, scrubbers and filters on stacks under the Air Quality Act, vehicle emission standards, cleaner fuels, dust suppression — and working with the weather — no open burning on stable winter days, air-quality alerts when inversions strengthen, and zoning that places industry downwind of dense settlement. Then close with one local anchor: Johannesburg's street-tree greening for the island, or the Highveld Priority Area monitoring for the dome. Causes mirrored, two baskets labelled, one South African example — run that checklist and you have never under-answered a strategies question again.
