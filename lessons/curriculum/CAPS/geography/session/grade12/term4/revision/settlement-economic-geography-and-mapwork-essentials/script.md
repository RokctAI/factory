# Part 1 — Expert

Week two of the term four revision programme carries the Annual Teaching Plan's second load: Settlement geography, Economic geography of South Africa, and Map work skills and techniques — the whole of Paper 2 in one sweep. Section A of that paper asks two theory questions of sixty marks, one on settlement, one on the economy; Section B closes with thirty marks of mapwork split ten for calculations, twelve for interpretation and eight for GIS. This session moves in that order, compressing each topic to the concepts, the South African examples and the question shapes the DBE keeps setting.

## Subtopic: Site, Situation and the Rural World

Settlement geography opens with three definitions that earn two marks each, so say them cleanly. A settlement is any place where people live, from a single farmstead to a metropolis. Site is the actual land the settlement stands on — the soil, water, slope and shelter of the exact spot. Situation is the settlement's position relative to its surroundings — the rivers, routes, resources and other towns around it. Site explains why the town started; situation explains why it grew.

Settlements classify three ways: by size and complexity, running up the scale from isolated farmstead through hamlet, village and town to city, conurbation and megalopolis; by pattern; and by function. Rural settlements — where most people work in primary activities like farming — come in two broad patterns: nucleated, buildings clustered together for water, defence and community, and dispersed, farmsteads scattered across commercial farmland. Shape carries its own marks: round settlements enclosing a central cattle kraal, linear settlements strung along a river, road or valley, crossroad and T-shaped settlements growing where routes meet. Rural land use is the trio of farming, forestry and conservation.

Then the issues, where the paragraph marks live. Rural-urban migration is driven by push factors — drought, mechanisation of farms shedding labour, few services, land degradation — and pull factors, real or imagined: jobs, schools, hospitals, city lights. The result at the sending end is rural depopulation: schools and clinics close as numbers fall, the young and skilled leave first, an ageing population remains, farm labour dries up, and local shops lose their threshold customers. Strategies to slow it: rural development programmes, agri-processing to add jobs, better services and roads, and land reform. Land reform itself has three DBE legs — restitution, returning land to those dispossessed after 1913; redistribution, broadening ownership; and tenure reform, securing the rights of people living on land they do not formally own. The section's questions are with you now: the three definitions, patterns and shapes with reasons, and the depopulation chain from cause to strategy.

## Subtopic: Urban Settlements — Hierarchy, Structure and Issues

Cities demand a vocabulary first. Urbanisation is the rising percentage of a population living in towns; urban growth is the rise in the absolute numbers; urban expansion is the outward spread of the built-up area; urban sprawl is that spread uncontrolled; counter-urbanisation is the drift of people out of big cities to smaller towns. By function, towns classify as central places serving their surrounding farmland, trade and transport towns — break-of-bulk points where cargo changes transport mode, junction towns, gateway towns — and specialised towns like mining or resort towns.

The urban hierarchy ranks centres by the services they offer. Its concepts interlock: threshold population, the minimum customers a service needs to survive; range, the furthest distance people travel for it; sphere of influence, the area a centre serves. Low-order services — bread, taxis, spaza goods — need small thresholds and appear everywhere; high-order services — specialist hospitals, universities, head offices — need huge thresholds and cluster in a few metropoles.

Inside the city, land-use zones tile the map. The CBD at the historic core, land values peaked by accessibility, buildings tall because land is dear. Around it the transition zone, the old mixed belt of decaying industry and housing — urban blight's home address. Then residential zones sorting by income, industrial zones along transport corridors, decentralised nodes — the OBD and suburban malls pulled outward by congestion and cheap land — and at the edge, the rural-urban fringe where city and farm interleave. The models question asks you to describe multiple nuclei cities, the modern American-Western city, the Third World city, and above all the South African city: a colonial-apartheid skeleton with the CBD, formerly white suburbs near the core amenities, and townships and informal settlements pushed to the far periphery, separated by old buffer zones — a structure whose long commutes and service gaps are precisely today's urban issues. Those issues — congestion, housing shortages, service backlogs, informal settlement growth, pollution, urban blight — each want a cause, an impact and a strategy, with upgrading of informal settlements in situ, public transport investment like bus rapid transit, and inner-city renewal as the stock answers. Attend to this section's questions: the concept ladder, the hierarchy terms, and the South African city described honestly.

## Subtopic: Economic Geography — Sectors, Farms, Mines and Factories

The economy sorts into four sectors: primary activities extract — farming, fishing, mining; secondary activities make — manufacturing and construction; tertiary activities serve — trade, transport, finance, tourism; quaternary activities know — research, information services. The exam's twist is the mismatch between contribution to GDP and employment: agriculture's slice of GDP is small, near three percent, yet it anchors food security, exports and rural jobs; mining's direct share has shrunk below ten percent, yet it still earns the foreign exchange and feeds Eskom's boilers and the beneficiation chain.

Agriculture splits into large-scale commercial farming — capital-intensive, mechanised, feeding the export and home market — and small-scale and subsistence farming, labour-intensive and land-hungry. Factors favouring it: varied climates from winter-rainfall Cape to subtropical Lowveld, strong research and infrastructure. Factors hindering: drought and erratic rain over most of the interior, only a sliver of truly arable land, soil erosion, and farm insecurity. Food security — enough affordable, nutritious food for all — is examinable as definition, threats and strategies: climate-smart farming, support for small-scale farmers, school feeding schemes.

Mining built this economy: the Witwatersrand gold rush called the railways, the migrant labour system and Johannesburg itself into being. Today's marks favour coal on the Mpumalanga Highveld powering the stations, platinum on the Bushveld Complex, iron ore railed from Sishen to Saldanha. Factors hindering: depth and cost, electricity insecurity, labour unrest, falling grades.

Manufacturing concentrates in four core industrial regions — name them as a set: Gauteng, the PWV, the inland giant grown on gold; Durban-Pinetown with its port and petrochemicals; Port Elizabeth-Uitenhage, the motor-assembly coast; and the South-western Cape around Cape Town, agri-processing, textiles and tech. Post-apartheid strategy tries to spread that map: Industrial Development Zones — purpose-built export estates at ports like Coega, Richards Bay and Saldanha — and Spatial Development Initiatives, corridors like the Maputo Corridor linking Gauteng to a foreign port, plus the older story of centralisation's economies of scale versus decentralisation's relief. The informal sector closes the topic: unregistered, small-scale trading and services absorbing the unemployed — street vendors, taxis, backyard mechanics — vital as a survival net, constrained by no credit, no premises and municipal harassment; strategies are micro-finance, trading shelters and simpler regulation. This section's questions are ready: sectors with the GDP-jobs mismatch, the four regions as a set, IDZ versus SDI, and the informal sector's role.

## Subtopic: The Section B Toolkit — Calculations, Interpretation and GIS

Mapwork is thirty guaranteed marks, and it is the most practisable section of the paper. The scale facts first: the topographic map is one to fifty thousand — one centimetre represents half a kilometre — and the orthophoto map is one to ten thousand, five times larger in scale, showing photographic detail with contours overlaid.

The calculations run as recipes. Distance: measure in centimetres, multiply by the scale, state the unit. Gradient: vertical interval over horizontal equivalent, both in metres, expressed as one in something. Cross-sections: plot heights along a line to reveal the shape of the land, then vertical exaggeration — vertical scale divided by horizontal scale — telling you how stretched the drawing is, answered as so-many times. Magnetic declination: take the map's stated declination, multiply the mean annual change by the years elapsed, add or subtract, then apply to true bearing for magnetic bearing. Area: length in reality times breadth in reality, in square kilometres. Every one of these rewards the same discipline: formula, substitution, calculation, answer with unit — the memo awards each step.

Interpretation ties the maps to weeks one and two of this revision: reading site and situation from contours and rivers, explaining why the informal settlement sits on the floodplain and the golf estate on the north-facing slope, identifying land-use zones from street patterns — the tight grid of the old CBD, the curving crescents of the suburbs, the dense regular blocks of the township — and spotting economic activity from conventional signs: cultivated land, orchards, mines, factories along the railway. And GIS closes for eight marks: data layering stacks themes — cadastral, drainage, land use — over one another; buffering draws a zone of stated width around a feature to test what falls inside; querying asks the database questions; vector data stores points, lines and polygons while raster stores pixels; attribute data describes what spatial data locates. The final questions of this part are with you now: the calculation recipes, the interpretation links, and the GIS vocabulary.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same paper again, walked through one family's week — because Paper 2 is really the story of where South Africans live, work and travel, and you already know that story.

## Subtopic: The Village, the Taxi and the City

Start in a village in the former homelands. It is nucleated — houses clustered, because water comes from one borehole and community is safety. It sits where it sits because of site: the spring, the flat land, the shelter of the ridge. The tar road that eventually reached it is situation: how the village connects to everything else. Now watch cousin Sipho leave. The farm mechanised and shed its workers — push. Johannesburg's wages, real and rumoured, called — pull. That single taxi ride is rural-urban migration, and repeated ten thousand times it becomes rural depopulation: the village school loses learners until it closes, the clinic follows, the shop cannot keep its doors open because its threshold — the minimum number of customers it needs — has left for Gauteng, and the village greys as the young go first.

Sipho lands in the city and reads it like a map. The CBD: tall buildings crammed on the dearest land, because everyone can reach it. The zone around it: old factories and flats gone shabby — the transition zone, urban blight's address. Out along the highways: malls and office parks that fled the congestion — decentralisation. And far out, past the old buffer strips of open ground that apartheid planners drew, the township and the informal settlement where Sipho's shack now stands — the South African city model, drawn not by markets alone but by history. His commute is long because the plan put homes far from work; that is not an accident in the model, it is the model. Take this section's questions now: push and pull in Sipho's ride, threshold in the shop that closed, and the city read zone by zone.

## Subtopic: How the Country Earns Its Keep

Think of the economy as one household with four kinds of work. The grandmother grows and digs — primary. The uncle builds and makes — secondary. The sister sells, drives and teaches — tertiary. The cousin writes software and does research — quaternary. Exam trick number one lives here: the grandmother's work looks small in the money books — agriculture is only a few percent of GDP — but the whole family eats because of her, and much of the countryside works for her. Mining is the family's old inheritance: gold built the house — the railways, Johannesburg, the migrant labour system — and though the inheritance is thinner now, coal still keeps the lights on and platinum still pays school fees.

The factories cluster in four corners of the yard, and the exam wants all four named as a set: Gauteng — the PWV — the inland engine; Durban-Pinetown at the busiest port; Port Elizabeth-Uitenhage building cars; and the South-western Cape processing the farms' harvest. Government tries to spread the work around: an IDZ is a prepared industrial yard at a port — Coega is the flagship — waiting for export factories; an SDI is an extension cord, a corridor of investment like the Maputo Corridor, run from Gauteng to a neighbour's harbour. And when the formal household has no chair for you, you make your own: the street vendor at the taxi rank, the backyard salon, the bakkie builder — the informal sector, uncounted but indispensable, feeding families the formal economy misses. Your questions for this section are ready: the four workers, the inheritance, the four corners and the extension cord.

## Subtopic: Five Calculations That Always Pay

Section B hides ten marks of pure recipe-following, so learn the five recipes as sentences you can say in the dark. Recipe one, distance: measure the centimetres, times them by half a kilometre on the topographic map, and say the unit out loud — kilometres — because a naked number loses the mark. Recipe two, gradient: height difference over ground distance, both in metres, and the answer speaks as one in twenty — for every twenty metres you walk, you climb one. Recipe three, vertical exaggeration: the cross-section's vertical scale divided by the map's horizontal scale, and the answer is so-many times stretched. Recipe four, magnetic declination: years since the map was made, times the annual change, added on westward — then bolt it onto the true bearing for the magnetic bearing. Recipe five, area: real length times real breadth, in square kilometres.

Now the interpretation habit that turns map marks into free marks: every time you claim something, point at evidence. Do not say the settlement is rural — say it is rural because the map shows scattered farmsteads, cultivated land and windpumps. Do not say the factory site is good — say it lies on flat land beside the railway and the river. And keep the GIS eight-marker friendly: layers are transparencies stacked on a lightbox; a buffer is a five-hundred-metre moat drawn around a river to ask what stands too close; a query is a question typed at the map. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: five recipes, one evidence habit, three GIS pictures. That is Section B, banked before you even open the theory.
