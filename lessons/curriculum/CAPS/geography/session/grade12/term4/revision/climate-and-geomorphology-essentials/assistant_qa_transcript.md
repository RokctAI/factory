**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: on the synoptic chart I panic and mix up the tropical cyclone and the mid-latitude cyclone. Is there a foolproof check?

**Tutor:** There is, and it takes three looks. Look one: latitude. A mid-latitude cyclone lives south of the country, on the Southern Ocean side, usually south of thirty-five degrees; a tropical cyclone lives in the tropics, typically in or near the Mozambique Channel. Look two: fronts. The mid-latitude cyclone always drags fronts — a line of triangles for the cold front, semicircles for the warm front. A tropical cyclone has no fronts at all, because it is built from one warm air mass, not from two fighting ones. Look three: the isobar shape. The tropical cyclone's isobars are almost perfect small circles packed tightly around a very deep centre; the mid-latitude cyclone's isobars are broader and wave-shaped where the fronts kink them. Run latitude, fronts, isobars in that order and you cannot confuse them. And add the seasonal check for free: a tropical cyclone on a chart dated July would be astonishing — they are late-summer creatures, roughly November to April — while winter charts belong to the frontal systems.

**Mandy:** Next one: why does the inversion layer matter so much? It feels like a small detail but it keeps appearing in memos.

**Tutor:** Because it is the master switch for interior rainfall, which makes it a high-yield revision fact. An inversion is a layer where temperature rises with height instead of falling, and rising air hits it like a ceiling — it cannot punch through, so cloud growth stops. In winter the Kalahari High strengthens and sits low, the inversion lies below the escarpment edge, and the moist coastal air simply cannot climb onto the plateau: result, months of dry sunshine over the Highveld. In summer the high weakens and shifts, the inversion lifts above the escarpment, and moist Indian Ocean air floods inland underneath it — thunderstorm season. So one layer explains why Gauteng's rain is a summer story. It also links to the valley-climate work: the frost pocket on a valley floor sits under a small local inversion made by katabatic drainage, the same physics at toy scale. When a memo awards marks for 'stable descending air' or 'capping layer', it is this switch they want named.

**Mandy:** One from the back: do we really need all seven drainage patterns, or can we get away with the big three?

**Tutor:** Learn all seven, but learn them as one sentence each — the pattern plus its rock reason — because that pairing is exactly what the question asks. Dendritic: tree-like branching on uniform rock, no structural control. Trellis: right-angle junctions on folded alternating hard and soft layers — think Cape Fold Mountains. Rectangular: sharp corners following joints and faults. Radial: streams spilling outward from a dome or volcanic cone. Centripetal: streams converging inward into a basin or pan, common toward our dry interior pans. Parallel: streams running side by side down a steep uniform slope. Deranged: confused, directionless drainage with no organised network. Seven sentences, seven possible marks, and the identification usually comes with a topographic map extract, so practise spotting them in contour country too. If the tributaries meet the main stream at right angles, say trellis and name the folding; if the network looks like an oak tree, say dendritic and name the uniform rock. The reason earns the second mark every time.

**Mandy:** Last question: case studies terrify people. How much detail does a revision-week case study actually need?

**Tutor:** Less than people fear, but sharper than people prepare. The memo pattern for a case-study answer wants a named example, located, plus impacts sorted into categories, plus responses. So build each case study as a five-line card. Line one: the name and place — a recent tropical cyclone that struck Mozambique and our Lowveld, or the Hartbeespoort Dam catchment. Line two: one physical fact — wind and storm surge, or nutrient overload. Line three: one social impact — homes destroyed, displacement, disease from flooded sanitation; or unsafe water and lost recreation. Line four: one economic impact — flooded farmland and roads, or the cost of treating algal water and lost tourism. Line five: two management actions — early warnings and evacuation plans; or wetland rehabilitation and removing alien vegetation through Working for Water. Five lines, spoken aloud twice, and the case study is exam-ready. The examiners reward structure and specificity, not length — a located name plus categorised impacts beats a page of vague drama every time.
