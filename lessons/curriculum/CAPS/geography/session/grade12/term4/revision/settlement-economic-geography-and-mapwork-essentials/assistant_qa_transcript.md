**Mandy:** Questions from the class! First up: threshold, range and sphere of influence — three names that all sound the same. How do we keep them apart under exam pressure?

**Tutor:** Anchor each one to a picture of a single shop. Threshold is about the shop's survival: the minimum number of customers it needs to stay open. A spaza shop needs a few streets' worth of people; a specialist heart hospital needs millions — that is why the spaza is everywhere and the hospital is in two cities. Range is about the customer's legs: the maximum distance a person is willing to travel for that service. Nobody drives an hour for bread; people fly for heart surgery. Sphere of influence is about the map: the whole area whose people actually use that centre — draw a boundary around every customer's home and you have sketched it. So: threshold counts customers, range measures the journey, sphere shades the map. And they interlock into the hierarchy question: high-order services have big thresholds and long ranges, so only a few high-order centres exist, each with an enormous sphere of influence. If you can explain why there are thousands of spaza shops and only a handful of specialist hospitals using those three words correctly, that whole subquestion is yours.

**Mandy:** Next: the models. Do we have to know all four city models in detail, or does the South African one carry the marks?

**Tutor:** Know the others in outline, but make the South African city your specialty, because it carries both the description marks and the issues marks. The outline knowledge: multiple nuclei says a city grows around several separate centres, not one; the modern American-Western city adds edge cities and freeways; the Third World city has a colonial core with informal fringes. But the South African city is where the DBE goes deeper, because its structure explains our urban problems. Describe it as layers: CBD at the core; the old established suburbs close to the core with its amenities; industry along the rail and road corridors; and then — the defining feature — townships and informal settlements on the far edge, separated by buffer zones the apartheid planners left as open strips. Then make the link that earns the interpretation marks: because the model placed workers' homes furthest from work, today's city carries long, costly commutes, pressure on trains and taxis, and peripheral areas with the thinnest services. Every current issue question — housing, transport, informal settlement growth — traces back to that layout. Describe the layers, then draw the arrow from history to today, and you have answered at the level the memo calls insight.

**Mandy:** One from the front row: in the calculation questions, if the final answer is wrong, is everything lost?

**Tutor:** No — and this is the most comforting fact in Section B, so absorb it. The DBE marking guidelines award the steps, not only the destination. In a gradient calculation the marks sit separately on the vertical interval, the measured map distance, the horizontal equivalent, the substitution into the formula, and the final answer as a ratio. Get the measurement slightly off but run the method correctly, and you still collect the method marks — the memos even publish accepted ranges for measurements, because your ruler and the marker's ruler will differ by a millimetre. The discipline that protects you is the four-line ritual: write the formula, show the substitution, show the calculation, state the answer with its unit. Two warnings, though. First, a bare answer with no working earns only the answer mark — you gamble everything on one line. Second, a missing unit at the end costs the answer mark even when the number is perfect: four means nothing; four kilometres is a mark. So slow down, write the ritual, and let the memo pay you line by line.

**Mandy:** Last one: GIS feels abstract next to the rest of mapwork. What is the minimum picture we need to walk in with?

**Tutor:** Walk in with three pictures and one sentence-pair, and the eight marks open up. Picture one: the lightbox. A GIS stores the world as layers — one transparency for rivers, one for roads, one for erven, one for land use — stacked so you can switch layers on and off. Data layering questions want exactly that image, plus a sensible example: overlay the drainage layer and the settlement layer to see which houses flood. Picture two: the moat. Buffering draws a zone of stated width around a feature — five hundred metres around a river, one kilometre around a school — and asks what falls inside; planners use it for flood lines and clinic coverage. Picture three: the question typed at the map. Querying asks the database to show, for instance, every stand larger than a thousand square metres zoned residential. The sentence-pair is vector versus raster with spatial versus attribute: vector data stores the world as points, lines and polygons, raster stores it as a grid of pixels; spatial data says where a thing is, attribute data describes what the thing is like. Layers, moat, typed question, and that pair — rehearse them tonight and GIS becomes the friendliest eight marks on the paper.
