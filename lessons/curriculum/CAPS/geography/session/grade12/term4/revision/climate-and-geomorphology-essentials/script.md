# Part 1 — Expert

Term four is revision territory, and the Annual Teaching Plan is explicit about the first week: Climate and Weather, then Geomorphology — the two theory pillars of Paper 1, Section A, worth sixty marks each on examination day. This session compresses a year of work into the essentials the examiners return to every November. The method is deliberate: for each topic, the core mechanism first, then the exam vocabulary, then the question shapes the DBE actually sets. Keep a pen moving while you listen — revision that only listens is revision half done.

## Subtopic: The Three Weather Systems in One Sweep

Every Paper 1 climate question grows from three systems, so line them up and compare.

System one: the mid-latitude cyclone — also called a frontal depression or extra-tropical cyclone. Born on the polar front between thirty and sixty degrees south, where cold polar air meets warm subtropical air and refuses to mix. Three formation conditions: a sharp temperature contrast along the front, rotation from the Coriolis force — which in the Southern Hemisphere deflects moving air to the left, so air spirals clockwise into the low — and upper-air divergence from the jet stream, which keeps surface pressure falling. Three stages: the initial wave, the mature stage with its cold front, warm front and warm sector, and occlusion, when the faster cold front catches the warm front and lifts the warm sector clean off the surface. The examinable weather story is the cold front passage: warm sticky air and a north-westerly wind ahead of it, a violent hour of cumulonimbus showers as it passes, and behind it the signature — wind swung to the south-west, temperature down, pressure climbing. These systems bring the Western Cape its winter rain, because in winter the whole circulation shifts north with the sun.

System two: the tropical cyclone. Born over warm oceans warmer than about twenty-six and a half degrees Celsius, between five and thirty degrees of latitude, in late summer. It needs that warm sea as fuel, the Coriolis force for spin — which is why it cannot form on the equator — and weak upper winds so the tower of cloud is not sheared apart. Development runs through four named stages: tropical disturbance, tropical depression, tropical storm — when it receives a name — and full tropical cyclone. Structure: a calm, clear eye of descending air, ringed by the eye wall where winds are fiercest, then spiralling bands of cumulonimbus. It moves east to west in the trade winds, hammering Madagascar and Mozambique, and it dies when it crosses land or cold water and loses its fuel. Case-study marks live in a named example, its social and economic impacts, and management: early warning, evacuation of low-lying coasts, cyclone-resistant shelter.

System three: the subtropical anticyclones — the South Atlantic High, the South Indian High and the Kalahari High. Highs are the opposite creature: descending, diverging air turning anticlockwise in our hemisphere, bringing stability and clear skies. Their seasonal migration writes South Africa's rainfall calendar, and the next section takes them up close.

On a synoptic chart the three are unmistakable if you check three things: an L with fronts attached is a mid-latitude cyclone; a tight, nearly circular swirl in the tropics with no fronts is a tropical cyclone; a broad H with widely spaced isobars is an anticyclone. The questions for this section are with you now — the formation conditions, the stages, and the tell-apart test.

## Subtopic: Anticyclones, Travelling Disturbances and Local Climates

The three highs deserve their own sweep, because the DBE loves them. Winter: all three shift north, the Kalahari High settles over the plateau, and its descending air plus the plateau's height builds a strong inversion layer below the escarpment edge, capping the interior in dry, stable, cloudless cold — which is why Johannesburg winters are sunny and dusty while Cape Town drowns. Summer: the highs slide south, the inversion lifts and weakens, moist Indian Ocean air rides in behind it, and the interior gets its thunderstorm season. The South Indian High also ridges around the south coast behind cold fronts, feeding onshore rain.

Anticyclonic circulation breeds three travelling disturbances. The moisture front — the boundary between moist eastern air and dry western air over the interior — is where afternoon line thunderstorms erupt in summer, sometimes with hail that flattens Free State maize. The coastal low scuttles anticlockwise around the coast from west to east, bringing brief cloud and drizzle. Ahead of it blows the berg wind: air pouring off the plateau, sinking, warming by compression — hot, bone-dry days on the coast in winter, and the classic trigger for runaway fires along the Garden Route. Berg wind conditions before, cold front after: examiners adore that sequence.

Then the local scale. Valley climates: on winter nights, cold dense air drains down the slopes as katabatic wind and ponds on the valley floor, building a temperature inversion — cold floor, warm thermal belt partway up the slope, cooler crest. Frost pockets and radiation fog claim the floor; that is why Boland vineyards and orchards climb the thermal belt and why settlements avoid the valley bottom. Daytime brings the reverse anabatic flow up the warmed slopes. Aspect matters too: in the Southern Hemisphere the north-facing slope drinks the sun, so it carries the vineyards, the crops and the expensive houses. Urban climates: the city's concrete, tar, waste heat and low albedo build an urban heat island, strongest on calm clear nights, and a pollution dome that a passing wind stretches into a plume. Strategies — green roofs, parks, public transport, cleaner fuels — are worth easy marks.

Pause on this section's questions: the seasonal dance of the highs, the three disturbances, and the winter night in a valley told from floor to crest.

## Subtopic: Drainage Basins, Patterns and Profiles

Geomorphology begins with the drainage basin: all the land drained by a river and its tributaries, bounded by the watershed, with interfluves between tributary valleys. Get the concept vocabulary automatic: catchment, confluence, source, mouth, surface run-off, infiltration, groundwater, water table. River types by flow: permanent rivers flow all year, like the Orange; periodic rivers flow in the wet season; episodic rivers flow only after rare storms in the arid west; and an exotic river rises in a wet region and crosses a dry one — the Orange again, and the Nile, textbook cases.

Drainage patterns are a rock-structure detective story, and the exam asks you to name the culprit. Dendritic — a branching tree on uniform rock with no structural control. Trellis — right-angled tributaries on folded, alternating hard and soft layers, the Cape Fold Mountains' signature. Rectangular — joints and faults bending streams through sharp corners. Radial — streams spilling outward off a dome or volcano. Centripetal — streams draining inward to a central basin or pan. Deranged — chaotic, directionless drainage. Parallel — streams running side by side down a uniform steep slope. Drainage density — total stream length over basin area — rises with heavy rain, impermeable rock, sparse vegetation and steep slopes, and falls where infiltration wins. Stream ordering is mechanical: two first-order streams meet and make a second order; only two of equal order can raise the order.

Profiles: the longitudinal profile runs smoothly concave from steep source to gentle mouth in a graded river — one in balance, with just enough energy to carry its load. An ungraded profile is lumpy with knickpoints and waterfalls, all of it working down toward base level, the lowest level to which a river can erode: sea level permanently, a resistant rock bar or dam temporarily. Cross profiles evolve from a V-shaped gorge in the upper course, through a wider valley in the middle course, to a broad flat floodplain in the lower course. Take this section's questions now — the patterns and their rocks, the density factors, and grading explained in one clean sentence.

## Subtopic: Fluvial Landforms, Rejuvenation and Catchment Management

Landforms are identification-plus-formation marks. In the upper course, vertical erosion rules: waterfalls where hard rock overlies soft, rapids where resistant bands break the bed. In the middle and lower courses, lateral erosion and deposition take over: meanders with their fast, undercut outer bank and gentle slip-off inner bank; oxbow lakes pinched off when a meander neck is cut through in flood; braided streams splitting around deposited bars; floodplains built of flood silt; natural levees raised along the banks by repeated overspill; and at the mouth, a delta if the sea cannot sweep the sediment away.

Rejuvenation is the exam's favourite twist: give a river new energy — by uplift of the land, a drop in base level, or increased discharge — and it cuts down again as if young. The evidence is a knickpoint marked by a waterfall migrating upstream, paired terraces stepping up the valley sides like grandstands, a valley-in-a-valley, and incised or entrenched meanders — the old curving pattern slashed deep into rock. Significance: terraces give flat farmland and building sites; incised gorges give dams, hydro sites and tourism, like the Blyde River Canyon. River capture is the other drama: a strong captor stream erodes headward through a watershed, beheads its neighbour, and leaves a fossil record — the elbow of capture, a dry wind gap in the ridge, and a misfit stream too small for the valley it inherited. The captor grows; the captured river shrinks; ecosystems, farms and towns downstream lose water.

Catchment management closes Paper 1's theory. People load drainage basins with eutrophication from sewage and fertiliser — algae bloom, oxygen crashes, fish die — plus overgrazing, deforestation and settlement on floodplains, all of which speed run-off and silt. The Hartbeespoort Dam's green algal soup is the stock South African case; management means fencing and re-vegetating banks, wetland rehabilitation, controlled grazing, and programmes like Working for Water pulling thirsty alien trees out of catchments. State one cause, one consequence and one strategy and the marks follow. The final questions of this part are ready for you now.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Same mountain of work, new grip. Here the year's climate and geomorphology become three characters, one river's biography, and a revision routine you can run tonight.

## Subtopic: Three Storms, Three Characters

Meet the three characters who run South Africa's weather. Character one is the winter visitor from the west — the mid-latitude cyclone. It queues up over the cold Southern Ocean and arrives in winter because the whole weather machine shifts north, putting the Cape in its path. Its calling card never changes: sticky north-west wind before, a wild wet hour during, cold south-west wind after. If an answer about a cold front does not mention that wind swing, it is not finished.

Character two is the summer bully from the warm sea — the tropical cyclone. It only wakes where the ocean is bath-warm, it spins because the Earth turns beneath it, and it cannot even form on the equator because there is no spin to borrow there. It has an eye — a calm hole in the middle of a ring of fury — and it starves the moment it leaves the warm water that feeds it. That is why Mozambique takes the beating and the Highveld only gets the leftover rain.

Character three is the quiet giant — the anticyclone, and we keep three of them. Giants do not chase anyone; they sit down and squash. Air under a giant sinks, warms and dries, which is why a Johannesburg winter is sunshine, frost and no rain: the Kalahari giant is sitting on the plateau with a lid — the inversion — pressed down over it. When the giant's air spills off the plateau and slides downhill to the coast, it arrives hot and dry as a hair dryer: the berg wind, the fire-danger wind. Learn the three characters and every synoptic chart becomes a group photo: find the fronts, find the tropical swirl, find the seated giant. Your questions for this section are with you now.

## Subtopic: A River's Whole Life in One Walk

Now walk one river from birth to sea, because half of geomorphology is just its biography. In the mountains the river is a toddler — small but furious, all energy, cutting straight down. Its valley is a V, its bed is steps of waterfalls and rapids, and a waterfall is simply the toddler finding a hard table edge of rock and leaping off it. In the middle of its life the river calms down and starts wandering sideways — meanders — eating the outer bank of every bend and dumping sand on the inner bank. Wander too far and a flood cuts the corner, leaving the abandoned bend as an oxbow lake: the river's old scar. In old age, on the flat, the river is slow and loaded, building its own bed and banks — floodplain and levees — and finally, at the sea, dropping everything in a delta if the waves allow.

Two plot twists to keep. Twist one: rejuvenation — the pensioner drinks an energy drink. Lift the land or drop the sea and the old river cuts down again like a youngster, slicing its own lazy curves deep into rock. That is how you get a gorge that wriggles like a meander: an incised meander, with step-like terraces up the sides showing every old floor the river once lived on. Twist two: river capture — straight theft. A stronger river gnaws backward through the hill, breaks into its neighbour's valley, and steals its water. The stolen river shrinks into a misfit trickling through a valley far too big for it, and the dry notch in the ridge — the wind gap — is the crime scene the exam asks you to identify. Take this section's questions now: the three ages, the two twists, and the evidence each leaves behind.

## Subtopic: The Marks You Can Bank Tonight

Revision only counts when it leaves your hand, so here is tonight's routine, built from how Paper 1 actually pays. First: draw the three synoptic portraits from memory — a low with two fronts and its warm-sector sandwich, a tropical swirl with its eye, a fat H with wide isobars — and label the wind direction around each, clockwise into lows, anticlockwise around highs, our hemisphere's rule. Second: write the cold-front Tuesday in six lines — before, during, after — with the wind swing underlined. Third: list the seven drainage patterns with a two-word rock clue next to each — dendritic, uniform rock; trellis, folded layers; radial, dome — because the examiners never ask the pattern without the reason.

Fourth: practise one paragraph question, because every sixty-mark question hides an eight-mark paragraph scored as four ideas by two marks, written in full sentences, never bullets. Pick one tonight — the effects of an urban heat island, or the impact of river capture on people — and write four linked sentences, each carrying one clear idea. Fifth: prepare your case studies as one-line flashcards: a named tropical cyclone with one social and one economic impact; a named catchment like Hartbeespoort with one cause, one consequence, one fix. Definitions are two-mark gifts — drainage basin, base level, rejuvenation, inversion — so say each aloud in your own words until it fits in one breath. The final questions of the lesson are with you now: the portraits, the six-line Tuesday, and the paragraph recipe of four ideas by two marks. Bank them tonight and week one of revision is already paying interest.
