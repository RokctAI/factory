**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: on a synoptic map I still freeze between the tropical cyclone and the mid-latitude cyclone. Is there a check that works every time?

**Tutor:** There is, and it takes exactly three looks. Look one: latitude. The mid-latitude cyclone belongs to the Southern Ocean side of the map, usually south of about thirty-five degrees; the tropical cyclone belongs to the tropics, typically in or near the Mozambique Channel. Look two: fronts. The mid-latitude cyclone always trails fronts — triangles marking the cold front, half-moons the warm front — because it is built from two air masses in conflict. The tropical cyclone is built from a single warm air mass, so it carries no fronts whatsoever. Look three: isobar shape. The tropical cyclone's isobars pack into small, nearly perfect circles around a very deep centre; the mid-latitude cyclone's isobars are broader and kink into waves where the fronts cross them. Run latitude, fronts, isobars in that order and confusion becomes impossible. Then add the free seasonal check: tropical cyclones are late-summer creatures of roughly November to April, while a winter map full of fronts belongs to the westerly systems. A date on the map is a clue in plain sight.

**Mandy:** Next one: why does the inversion layer matter so much? It feels like one small detail, but it keeps deciding whole answers.

**Tutor:** Because it is the master switch for the interior's rainfall, which makes it one of the highest-yield facts in the whole topic. An inversion is a layer in which temperature rises with height instead of falling, and rising air strikes it like a ceiling — cloud growth stops dead. In winter the Kalahari High strengthens and sits low, the inversion lies below the escarpment edge, and moist coastal air simply cannot climb onto the plateau: months of dry Highveld sunshine follow. In summer the high weakens and shifts south, the inversion lifts above the escarpment, and moist Indian Ocean air floods inland beneath it — thunderstorm season begins. One layer, and Gauteng's entire rainfall calendar is explained. It also connects downward to the valley-climate work: the frost pocket on a valley floor sits beneath a miniature local inversion built by katabatic drainage — identical physics at toy scale. Whenever an answer needs the phrase stable descending air or capping layer, this switch is the thing to name.

**Mandy:** One from the back: do we really need all seven drainage patterns, or will the big three carry us?

**Tutor:** Learn all seven, but learn each as a single sentence — the pattern plus its rock reason — because that pairing is what questions actually reward. Dendritic: tree-like branching on uniform rock with no structural steering. Trellis: right-angle junctions across folded belts of alternating hard and soft rock — the Cape Fold Mountains' signature. Rectangular: sharp corners where streams follow joints and faults. Radial: streams spilling outward from a dome or volcanic cone. Centripetal: streams converging inward on a basin or pan, common toward the dry interior. Parallel: streams running side by side down one steep uniform slope. Deranged: a confused network with no organisation at all. Seven sentences, and each is worth its marks twice over, because pattern identification usually arrives on a topographic map extract — so practise spotting them among contours too. Right-angle junctions: say trellis and name the folding. An oak-tree network: say dendritic and name the uniform rock. The rock reason is what turns one mark into two, every time.

**Mandy:** Last question: case studies frighten people in revision week. How much detail does one actually need to carry?

**Tutor:** Less than the fear suggests, but sharper than most people prepare. A strong case-study answer wants a named, located example, impacts sorted into categories, and responses — so build each case as a five-line card. Line one: the name and the place — Cyclone Idai striking central Mozambique, or the Vaal River system's pollution crisis. Line two: one physical fact — storm surge and flooding, or nutrient overload feeding algae. Line three: one social impact — homes destroyed and families displaced, or unsafe water for riverside communities. Line four: one economic impact — flooded farmland and severed roads, or the cost of treating water and lost river tourism. Line five: two management actions — early warning and evacuation planning for the cyclone; wetland rehabilitation and clearing invasive alien trees through Working for Water for the catchment. Five lines, spoken aloud twice tonight, and the case study is ready. Structure and specificity earn the marks — a located name with categorised impacts beats a page of vague drama in any marking.
