# Part 1 — Expert

This session is a practice run through the theory sections of the two Geography papers, taken at examination pace. The questions are exam-style, built to the DBE's blueprint, and every one is walked with its mark allocation named, because the mark allocation is the instruction: two marks means one developed fact, a four-times-two paragraph means four full sentences. Answer each question in your head — or better, on paper — before the walkthrough gives it away.

## Subtopic: How the Theory Question Papers Are Built

Know the shape of the battlefield before the first shot. Paper 1, Section A: two compulsory theory questions of sixty marks each — one on Climate and Weather, one on Geomorphology. Paper 2, Section A: the same architecture — one question on Settlement, one on the Economic Geography of South Africa. Every sixty-mark question opens with fifteen marks of short, objective items: multiple choice, matching, true and false, one-word identifications. Then follow three subquestions of fifteen marks each, built from source material — a synoptic chart, a cartoon, a graph, a case-study extract — and stepped from two-mark definitions up through four- and six-mark explanations. Hidden inside each sixty is a paragraph question worth eight marks, marked as four ideas at two marks each, which may never be answered in point form. The cognitive mix is fixed: a quarter of the marks are lower order, half are middle order, a quarter are higher order — so by design, half the paper is within reach of solid revision, and the top quarter demands genuine insight. Marking rules worth exploiting: definitions score two marks for the concept in your own words; there is no negative marking, so never leave a blank; and in the objective items, an educated elimination is a legitimate strategy. That is the machine. Now feed it questions.

## Subtopic: The Objective Opener — Fifteen Marks Before You Blink

Practise the opener's rhythm with a Climate set. Question: name the pressure belt in which South Africa's three anticyclones lie — one mark. Answer: the subtropical high-pressure belt. Question: the front on a synoptic chart drawn with solid semicircles — one mark: the warm front. Question: true or false — air circulates anticlockwise around a Southern Hemisphere low — one mark: false, clockwise. Question: the local wind that flows down-slope at night — one mark: the katabatic wind. Question: choose the correct option — the eye of a tropical cyclone is a zone of, option A rising moist air, option B descending dry air, option C strong horizontal winds, option D thunderstorm cloud — one mark: option B, descending dry air, which is why the eye is calm and clear.

Now a Geomorphology set at the same pace. The area drained by a river and its tributaries — one mark: a drainage basin. The drainage pattern developed on a structural dome — one mark: radial. True or false — a first-order stream has no tributaries — one mark: true. The temporary base level formed where a resistant rock bar crosses a river — one mark: temporary base level of erosion. Choose the option: a misfit stream is evidence of, A rejuvenation, B river capture, C braiding, D grading — one mark: B, river capture.

The lesson inside the drill: the opener is pure retrieval speed. Fifteen single marks, none deeper than a definition or an identification, all of them bankable if the vocabulary is automatic. Train it like vocabulary in a language — daily, aloud, fast — and the first fifteen marks of every question are yours before thinking begins.

## Subtopic: Walking a Climate Question — Synoptic Chart in Hand

Now a full fifteen-mark subquestion, built on a described synoptic chart — picture it as it is read. The chart shows winter. A low-pressure cell with two fronts lies south-west of Cape Town. The South Atlantic High sits west of the country at one thousand and twenty-four hectopascals. Over the interior plateau, the Kalahari High. A tight gradient of isobars crowds the Cape Peninsula.

Question one: identify the weather system south-west of Cape Town — one times two, two marks. Answer: a mid-latitude cyclone, and naming the mature stage strengthens it, since two distinct fronts are visible. Question two: give evidence from the chart that the season is winter — two times two, four marks. Two developed facts: the high-pressure cells lie north of their summer positions, and a frontal system is reaching the Cape — both winter signatures; the Kalahari High over the plateau seals it. Question three: describe the weather Cape Town will experience in the next day as the cold front passes — three times two, six marks. Three developed facts, in sequence: ahead of the front, warm conditions with north-westerly wind and falling pressure; during passage, heavy showers from cumulonimbus with a wind swing from north-west to south-west; behind it, temperatures drop sharply, pressure rises and cold, clearing, showery south-westerly weather follows. Question four: why does this system not bring rain to Gauteng — one mark plus two, three marks total in this walk: the front's northern edge misses the plateau, and the Kalahari High's descending stable air and inversion block uplift over the interior.

Count what just happened: two plus four plus six plus three, fifteen marks, and every answer was either a chart observation or the cold-front story you have rehearsed since term one. Examination pace means budgeting effort by marks: a two-mark identification gets one line, never a paragraph; the six-marker gets its three sentences and moves on.

## Subtopic: Walking a Geomorphology Question — River Capture on the Landscape

Next, a fifteen-mark Geomorphology subquestion around a described sketch — build it in your mind. Two rivers flow roughly parallel toward the sea. River A is short, steep and vigorous. River B, beyond a low ridge, is longer and gentler. In the sketch's second frame, river A has eaten headward through the ridge and bent river B's upper course into its own channel. A dry notch remains in the ridge, and river B's lower course now trickles thin in a wide valley.

Question one: define river capture — one times two, two marks: the natural diversion of one river's headwaters into a neighbouring, more vigorous river through headward erosion. Question two: name the features labelled at the bend, the notch and the shrunken lower course — three times two, six marks: the elbow of capture; the wind gap; the misfit stream. Question three: explain why river A was able to capture river B — two times two, four marks: river A had the steeper gradient and therefore the greater erosive energy, and it eroded headward through the watershed until it intersected river B's channel. Question four: state the impact of the capture on each river — the captor gains discharge and cutting power; the captured river's lower course loses discharge and may fail its farms and settlements downstream — the remaining three marks.

The examiner's craft here is worth noticing: the sketch does the storytelling, and the marks reward naming and explaining, feature by feature. Whenever a diagram question appears, annotate mentally in this order — name it, place it, explain it — and remember that in this paper you may be asked to draw: a labelled sketch of a captured drainage system, or terraces stepping up a rejuvenated valley, earns its marks with clear labels, not artistry.

## Subtopic: Walking a Settlement Question — The Emptying Platteland

Paper 2's settlement question, in a fifteen-mark walk built on a case-study extract, read as description: a Karoo town's population has fallen for twenty years; the district's commercial farms have mechanised; the young leave for Cape Town and Gauteng; the last bank branch closed last year; the primary school has combined classes; the municipality is piloting an agri-processing plant and a heritage-tourism route.

Question one: define rural depopulation — one times two, two marks: the long-term decline of a rural area's population as people move away, mainly to urban areas. Question two: from the extract, identify two push factors driving the migration — two times two, four marks: mechanisation shedding farm labour, and shrinking services such as the closed bank — either with a line of explanation. Question three: explain one consequence for the people who remain, and one for the local economy — two times two, four marks: the remaining population ages while combined classes signal failing services; businesses lose their threshold populations, so closures cascade. Question four, the paragraph: in a paragraph of about eight lines, suggest how the municipality's two projects could slow depopulation — four times two, eight marks, full sentences compulsory. A model line of reasoning: the agri-processing plant adds secondary-sector jobs so school leavers can stay; processing local produce keeps value in the district; the tourism route brings outside spending into tertiary services like guesthouses and taxis; and together the projects diversify the town beyond farm labour, making it less vulnerable to further mechanisation. Four ideas, four sentences, two marks each — that is the paragraph contract: state the idea, then develop it, and never bullet it.

## Subtopic: Walking an Economic Geography Question — Corridors and Sectors

The economy question, walked at pace from a described resource: a bar graph shows the sectors' shares of GDP — primary small, secondary about a fifth, tertiary the giant — beside a map marking Gauteng and a development corridor running east to the port of Maputo.

Question one: define a Spatial Development Initiative — one times two, two marks: a government strategy concentrating coordinated investment along a corridor with economic potential, linking regions and unlocking trade. Question two: use the graph — which sector dominates GDP, and give one reason — one plus two, three marks: the tertiary sector, because finance, trade, transport and government services concentrate in an urbanised economy. Question three: explain two reasons why the corridor to Maputo benefits Gauteng's industries — two times two, four marks: it gives the landlocked industrial core its shortest route to a deep-water port, cutting export transport costs, and infrastructure investment along the corridor attracts linked industries and jobs on both sides of the border. Question four: account for the location of Gauteng as the country's leading industrial region — three times two, six marks: it grew on the Witwatersrand goldfields, which concentrated capital, labour and rail; it holds the country's largest market and skilled workforce; and its inland position at the centre of the national transport network lets it assemble inputs and distribute products nationally. Two plus three plus four plus six: fifteen marks, each answer sized to its allocation.

Notice the pattern across both papers: definitions open, source-reading follows, explanation builds, and insight closes. The resource changes; the staircase never does.

## Subtopic: The Eight-Mark Paragraph — Where Insight Becomes Marks

The paragraph question decides symbols, so close the practice run there. The contract, once more: eight marks, four ideas at two marks each, full sentences, insight and analysis expected — the DBE pitches it at middle and higher order only. The recipe that survives exam nerves has four moves. Move one: read the instruction verb — discuss, evaluate, suggest — and obey it; suggest wants solutions, evaluate wants a judgement. Move two: plan four ideas in the margin as four keywords before writing a word of prose. Move three: write one sentence per idea, and develop each — the second mark of every pair is earned by the because-clause. Move four: keep it in the topic's language; a paragraph on the urban heat island that never says albedo, waste heat or green roofs is leaving vocabulary marks unclaimed.

One final rehearsal, spoken as a model. Task: suggest strategies a South African city could use to manage its growing informal settlements — four times two, eight marks. Model paragraph: the city should upgrade settlements in situ by providing water, sanitation and electricity, because services improve health without destroying communities. Residents should receive secure tenure, since ownership encourages people to invest in their own homes. The municipality should plan ahead by releasing well-located land near transport and jobs, which prevents new settlements forming on floodplains. Partnerships with residents in projects like community-led upgrading build local skills and make improvements last. Four sentences, four developed ideas, eight marks — and the same recipe answers every paragraph the papers can set, from heat islands to food security. Run this session's questions once more against the clock inside your head, and the theory papers stop being a mystery and become a rubric you have already met.
