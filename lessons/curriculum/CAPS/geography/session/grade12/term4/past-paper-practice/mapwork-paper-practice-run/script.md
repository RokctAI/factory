# Part 1 — Expert

This practice run works through a real national examination: the DBE's Geography mapwork paper of November 2018, set on the one to fifty thousand topographic map of Pietermaritzburg with its one to ten thousand orthophoto extract. These are the actual questions the class of 2018 sat, walked at examination pace with every mark allocation named, and where the official marking guidelines are on record, marked the way the markers marked them. Since no map is in front of you, each map moment is described fully in words; build the terrain in your mind as it comes.

## Subtopic: Meeting the Pietermaritzburg Extract

First, meet the ground. Pietermaritzburg sits in a bowl in the KwaZulu-Natal Midlands, ringed by higher country. North-west of the city the land climbs to Town Hill and the forested heights called World's View, where a trigonometrical station crowns the escarpment edge at one thousand and fifty-seven comma nine metres. The Msunduzi River threads the valley floor. Suburbs climb some slopes; informal housing crowds others; plantations, cultivated lands and the N3 highway complete the scene. Hold that picture — every question that follows lives on it.

A note on the paper's shape, because formats shift while skills do not. The 2018 mapwork paper stood alone, with a multiple-choice opener, a calculations question, a map-interpretation question and a full GIS question. On the current papers the same skills appear as Section B, thirty marks — ten for map skills and calculations, twelve for map interpretation, eight for GIS — so every technique rehearsed here transfers directly, question type for question type. The maps themselves also carry standing information worth reading before any question: the sheet name and index, the scale, the contour interval of twenty metres on the topographic sheet, and the magnetic declination note in the margin. Examiners assume you have met the margin; meet it first and the paper starts on your terms.

## Subtopic: Question 1 — Fifteen Multiple-Choice Marks on the Maps

The 2018 paper opened with fifteen multiple-choice marks testing map codes, contour interpretation, true bearing and land use across both the topographic and orthophoto maps. Rehearse each skill as the paper tested it. Map codes: South African sheets are indexed by latitude and longitude — Pietermaritzburg's sheet code begins with twenty-nine thirty, naming the degree square, and the letters that follow narrow it to one sixteenth of that square; know how the grid of sixteen letters walks across the degree square. Contour interpretation: closely spaced contours read as steep slope, evenly spaced as uniform slope, widely spaced as gentle; a ring of contours with height rising inward is a hill, a V of contours pointing uphill is a river valley. True bearing: lay a protractor's centre on the starting point, zero on true north, and read the angle clockwise to the target — an answer like one hundred and twenty degrees places the target to the south-east. Land use: the conventional signs carry it — orchard rows of small green dots, plantation green with a tree symbol, cultivated lands in ploughed-field shading, the black rectangles of built-up blocks. Fifteen marks, one per item, and every item answerable in seconds if these four skills are automatic. On the current paper the same multiple-choice style can be woven through all of Section B, so the drill is never wasted.

## Subtopic: Question 2.1 — Map Codes, Grid References and the Hiking Trail

Question two point one, five marks, asked three things. First: state the map index of the Pietermaritzburg sheet — the code printed at the sheet corner, the twenty-nine thirty family — worth its single mark for exact transcription, digits and letters in order. Second: give the grid reference of Town Hill at the point labelled N. Method, spoken slowly: read the vertical grid line west of the point, then the horizontal line south of it; state latitude then longitude in degrees, minutes and seconds, and always attach the hemisphere letters — south and east — because a coordinate without them is incomplete. Third: the paper pointed at the hiking trail in block F six and asked what feature the contours reveal beneath it. The contours there bend in a long finger of higher ground running out from Town Hill with the trail riding its back: a spur — a ridge of high land projecting between valleys, the walker's natural staircase. Five marks total, and notice the examiner's pattern: alphanumeric blocks like F six find the place, coordinates fix it exactly, and the contour question checks you can see the shape of the land through the lines.

## Subtopic: Question 2.2 — The Average Gradient Calculation, Marked Like the Memo

Now the centrepiece, question two point two point two, five marks: calculate the average gradient between contour line O, height eight hundred and twenty metres, on the lower slopes, and the trigonometrical station at P on World's View — and the paper printed the instruction that decides everything: show ALL calculations, marks are awarded for calculations. The formula is given: average gradient equals vertical interval over horizontal equivalent.

Walk it as the memo walked it. Step one, vertical interval: the trig station's height is printed on the map as one thousand and fifty-seven comma nine metres; subtract the contour's eight hundred and twenty; vertical interval, two hundred and thirty-seven comma nine metres. Step two, horizontal equivalent: the ruler reads three comma nine centimetres between the points on the map; at one to fifty thousand each centimetre is five hundred metres, so three comma nine centimetres is one thousand nine hundred and fifty metres. Step three, substitute: gradient equals two hundred and thirty-seven comma nine over one thousand nine hundred and fifty. Step four, divide both sides so the top becomes one: one over eight comma two. State it as the memo demands: one in eight comma two — for every eight comma two metres travelled horizontally, the ground rises one metre. A serious slope; your calves would agree.

Now the marking, because this is where past papers teach what textbooks cannot. The guidelines awarded the five marks stepwise: the correct vertical interval, the measured map distance, the horizontal equivalent, the substitution, the final ratio. And the memo published tolerance: any measurement from three comma eight to four centimetres was accepted, and any final answer from one in seven comma nine to one in eight comma four earned the answer mark. Your ruler and the marker's ruler may disagree by a millimetre — the method marks protect you, but only if the working is on the page. The companion question two point two point three, two marks, asked you to describe the route of the Voortrekker Wagon Hiking Trail as it climbs from O to the station at World's View: the path winds along the spur in curves rather than attacking the slope head-on, following the gentler gradient and avoiding the steepest contour-crowded faces — two descriptive points, two marks.

## Subtopic: Question 2.3 — Cross-Sections and Vertical Exaggeration

Question two point three, five marks, turned to cross-sections drawn between the benchmark at Q, one thousand and sixty comma nine metres, and the spot height at R, one thousand one hundred and six metres. The paper showed candidate cross-sections and asked which vertical exaggeration matched — then why vertical exaggeration is used at all.

Build the skill verbally. A cross-section slices the land along a line and draws its profile: height on the vertical axis, distance along the horizontal. Drawn to true scale, South African hills flatten into near-nothing — fifty metres of rise across a kilometre of paper is a barely visible tilt — so the vertical scale is stretched. Vertical exaggeration equals vertical scale divided by horizontal scale, both written as fractions. Worked example in the Pietermaritzburg numbers: suppose the section's vertical scale is one centimetre to one hundred metres — that is one over ten thousand — and the horizontal scale is the map's one over fifty thousand. Divide: one ten-thousandth over one fifty-thousandth is fifty thousand over ten thousand — five. The profile is exaggerated five times, and the answer must say times: five times, not five, not one to five. The effect question completes the five marks: exaggeration makes relief visible and comparable — slopes look steeper than reality, so the reader must interpret with the exaggeration in mind. Matching profiles to exaggerations is then pure reasoning: the same two points, the same rise from Q to R, but the higher the exaggeration, the taller and more dramatic the drawn hill. The memo's habit repeats here: formula, substitution, calculation, stated answer with its unit — each line carries a mark.

## Subtopic: Question 3 — Frost, Aspect and Streams: Theory Applied to the Map

Question three was the interpretation heart of the paper, twenty-five marks, and it did what the current Section B's twelve interpretation marks still do: it reached back into the theory syllabus and demanded it on the map. Three scenes from the actual paper.

Scene one: the map marks a low-lying spot at M beside the river, and the paper asks why farmers there fear clear, calm winter nights. This is the valley-climate chapter standing on real ground: on such nights the slopes radiate their heat away, cold dense air drains katabatically to the valley floor and ponds there, a temperature inversion forms with warm air resting above the cold pool, and the floor at M becomes a frost pocket. Frost kills seedlings and vegetables; hence the fear, and hence — the follow-up mark — why orchards and settlements prefer the thermal belt partway up the slope, above the cold pool, below the exposed crest.

Scene two: aspect and settlement. Pietermaritzburg's valley trends roughly north-west to south-east, giving one side a north-east-facing warmth and the other a south-west-facing chill — and the paper asked which residential areas occupy which slope, and why. Southern Hemisphere rule: the slope that leans toward the north drinks the midday sun; it is warmer, drier, more desirable, and the map shows the established, higher-income suburbs there. The shadowed south-facing slope is colder and damper, historically cheaper, and carries lower-income and higher-density housing. The examiner wants the physical rule plus the social reading — geography's habit of finding history written on a hillside.

Scene three: drainage. The paper asked for stream order at a marked junction and for the drainage density and pattern of a marked basin. Order is mechanical: two first-order fingers join into a second-order stream; a second joined by a first stays second; only two seconds make a third. Density on the wetter, impermeable Midlands slopes reads high — many fine blue threads — and the pattern in this dissected country is dendritic, tree-like branching on uniform rock. Every one of these marks is theory you own, asked at a map location; the skill is simply hearing the chapter title behind the question.

## Subtopic: Question 4 — GIS for Twenty-Five Marks

The 2018 paper closed with a full GIS question worth twenty-five marks — on the current paper GIS carries eight, but the same four ideas, and the 2018 items remain the best rehearsal. Item one: the difference between spatial and attribute data, asked on this very map. Spatial data locates: the Msunduzi River's line, the trig station's point, an erf's polygon. Attribute data describes what is located: the station's height of one thousand and fifty-seven comma nine metres, the road's surface, the stand's owner. Two data types, one distinction, easy marks if the definition arrives with a map example attached. Item two: the relevance of scale to a geographical query — a city planner asking a street-level question needs large-scale data like the one to ten thousand orthophoto; a provincial flood study works at one to fifty thousand or smaller; choose the scale that matches the question, because detail invisible at small scale cannot be queried. Item three: data layering — the lightbox picture: separate transparent layers for drainage, roads, cadastral boundaries, land use, stacked and switched on demand; the paper asked which layers a specific decision needs, and the answer names layers and justifies each. Item four: buffering, the exam's favourite: draw a zone of stated width around a feature and test what falls inside — buffer the Msunduzi by one hundred metres to find flood-risk housing, buffer a school by five hundred metres to test walking access.

Close the run with the habit the whole paper teaches: mapwork marks are method marks. Formula before numbers, working before answers, units always, evidence for every interpretation claim. The 2018 candidates who wrote one in eight comma two with the working shown collected five out of five; the ones who wrote a bare ratio gambled five marks on one line. Run this paper again with your own hand doing the writing, and Section B becomes the most predictable marks on your certificate.
