**Mandy:** Questions from the class after that practice run! First: in the real exam, should we answer the objective openers first or start with the big subquestions?

**Tutor:** Take the openers first, and take them fast — they are designed as the on-ramp. Three reasons. First, they are pure retrieval: fifteen single marks that depend on vocabulary, not reasoning, so they cost the least energy while your nerves settle. Second, they warm up exactly the memory you need next: an opener that asks you to name the warm front primes the frontal-weather story the six-marker will demand two pages later. Third, momentum is real marks — students who bank the openers report attacking the paragraph question with confidence instead of dread. Two disciplines while you sprint, though. Never leave a blank, because there is no negative marking — an eliminated-down-to-two guess has half a mark of expected value, and a blank has none. And transfer answers carefully to the correct question numbers; the saddest losses in marking rooms are right answers standing next to wrong numbers. Sprint the fifteen, check the numbering, then climb the staircase of the subquestions with the engine already warm.

**Mandy:** Next one: when a question says 'account for' or 'evaluate', do those verbs really change what the memo pays for?

**Tutor:** Completely — the verb is the contract, and the DBE publishes exactly what each verb demands. 'Account for' means explain the cause of: the memo for accounting for Gauteng's industrial dominance pays for reasons — the goldfields origin, the market, the transport hub — not for a description of factories. 'Evaluate' and 'assess' demand a judgement: the memo reserves marks for an actual verdict — effective or not, sustainable or not — supported by evidence, so an evaluation answer with no opinion caps itself. 'Discuss' wants both sides examined before a conclusion. 'Describe' wants characteristics, no reasons required — students throw away minutes explaining in a describe question, earning nothing extra. 'Suggest' wants workable solutions, and creativity within the syllabus is welcomed. My drill for the final stretch: for every past question you practise, underline the verb first and say aloud what currency it pays in — causes, judgements, characteristics or solutions. Ten seconds of verb-reading is the cheapest insurance the exam sells.

**Mandy:** One from the middle: the case-study subquestions scare people because the source is always new. How do you answer on material you have never seen?

**Tutor:** By trusting that the source is new but the geography never is. Examiners must set unseen extracts — a Karoo town, a flooded settlement, a new corridor — but they can only ask the syllabus, so every extract is a familiar concept wearing a new outfit. The method has three steps. Step one: read the questions before rereading the extract, so you know what to hunt. Step two: mine the extract — the marks for 'identify two push factors from the extract' are lying in the text, and quoting the mechanised farms and the closed bank, with a line of explanation, collects them; an answer that ignores the extract and recites generic push factors can lose the 'from the extract' marks entirely. Step three: bolt the extract onto your framework — the Karoo town is just your rural depopulation chain with a local name, so run the chain: causes, consequences for people, consequences for the economy, strategies. New outfit, same body. Practise on two or three unseen articles this week — any news story about a town, a mine or a flood — and ask yourself which exam question it could carry. That habit turns fear of the unseen into recognition.

***

**Mandy:** Last one: any advice for dividing effort across the paper so the paragraph questions do not get squeezed at the end?

**Tutor:** Budget by marks, and let the allocations drive you — this is pacing advice, not clock-watching. The theory question is sixty marks: fifteen in the opener, forty-five across the subquestions, with the eight-mark paragraph the single richest item you will write. So the rule is proportionality: a two-mark item deserves a line, a six-mark item three developed sentences, and the paragraph deserves planning — four keywords in the margin — before a sentence is written. The squeeze happens when students spend paragraph-level effort on two-mark identifications early, then reach the real paragraph exhausted. Guard against it with a habit from the practice run: as you finish each subquestion, tally what you have banked — two plus four plus six — and confirm it matches the printed total. That tally keeps you honest and keeps you moving. And if a question stalls you, leave a numbered gap and move on; a stalled mind restarts beautifully on an objective item. The paper rewards steady bankers, not heroic strugglers — bank, tally, move.
