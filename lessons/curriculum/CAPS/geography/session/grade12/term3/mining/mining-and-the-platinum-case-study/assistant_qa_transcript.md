**Mandy:** Questions that come up a lot with this one! First: the essay says "mining is the foundation of the South African economy — discuss". Mining is less than ten per cent of GDP, so how do I argue foundation without exaggerating?

**Tutor:** Argue it historically and structurally, not arithmetically — the statement is about what mining BUILT, not about its current percentage, and the discuss verb invites you to test it honestly. Open by conceding the number: mining today contributes under ten per cent of GDP directly and employs under half a million people, so on today's scoreboard it is not the biggest player. Then make the foundation case: the railways were flung inland to serve Kimberley and the Witwatersrand; Eskom's coal-fired system grew to power the mines; the JSE was founded to finance the goldfields; manufacturing grew as mining's supplier; Johannesburg itself — no river, no harbour — exists because of gold, and the migrant labour system reshaped settlement across the subcontinent. The modern economy is a house whose foundations were poured by mining even though other floors now earn more rent. Then the present-tense pillar: mining still anchors export earnings — platinum group metals, coal, iron ore, gold — the foreign exchange that pays for imported oil and machinery, plus royalties, taxes and the multiplier through supplier industries and mining towns. Close the discussion with the caveat both ways: a foundation is not the whole building — services now dominate — but you do not call a foundation unimportant because you live on the upper floors. That concession-case-caveat structure is precisely what "discuss" is asking for.

**Mandy:** Next: for the platinum case study, I always muddle which factors are favouring and which are hindering, because some feel like both. Electricity, for example — the grid is nearby, but load-shedding kills production. How do I handle that in an answer?

**Tutor:** Handle it by naming the two faces explicitly, because factors that cut both ways are not a trap — they are the highest-scoring material in the question if you split them cleanly. The rule: a factor favours or hinders depending on which property of it you are describing, so attach the property, not just the noun. Electricity: the EXISTENCE of Eskom's grid close to the Bushveld favoured the industry's establishment — smelters are power-hungry and the infrastructure was there; the RELIABILITY and PRICE of that electricity now hinder it — load-shedding stalls smelters and rising tariffs squeeze margins. Labour: the AVAILABILITY of an experienced mining workforce favours; labour RELATIONS — the long strikes, the Marikana legacy — hinder. Location: nearness to Gauteng favours with railways and skills; the DEPTH and THINNESS of the reefs, a location fact underground, hinders. Even the market splits: autocatalyst demand favours today; the battery-electric future threatens tomorrow, while hydrogen fuel cells may favour again. In the exam, never write "electricity is a favouring and hindering factor" bare — write the pair as two sentences with the property named in each. Markers award the split; they penalise the muddle.

**Mandy:** The mapwork question: they give an extract and ask me to "provide evidence of mining activity". How many pieces of evidence, and how exactly should I write each one?

**Tutor:** Give as many pieces as there are marks, plus one spare, and write every piece in the same three-part shape: the feature, its map appearance, and its address. The mark allocation tells you the count — a three-mark question wants three distinct pieces of evidence, and distinct means different features, not the same dump quoted twice. Your menu, in rough order of reliability: the printed word itself — Mine, Myn, Colliery, Quarry — because the map naming the activity is unbeatable evidence; mine dumps and slimes dams — describe them as pale, straight-edged, geometric shapes that natural processes do not build; excavation symbols — the toothed or hachured depressions of pits and quarries; the transport signature — a railway spur or heavy road dead-ending at a building cluster in open country; converging power lines feeding a single site; and the settlement signature — a grid-planned town adjacent to the workings. Now the writing shape, one line per piece: "Mine dumps — pale, rectangular, straight-edged mounds — at grid reference such-and-such." Feature, appearance, address. If the question pairs the map with a photograph, add a cross-reference sentence: the headgear visible in the photo corresponds to the building cluster at that reference. And keep the corridor-then-stairs order sacred in every reference you quote — eastings before northings — because one reversed reference can bleed marks across the whole question.

**Mandy:** Last one: if platinum is so valuable, why does the exam keep asking about the industry's problems? What is the examiner really testing with the hindering-factors question?

**Tutor:** The examiner is testing whether you understand that resource wealth is a possibility, not a guarantee — the core idea of economic geography. A deposit only becomes prosperity if the full chain holds: affordable extraction, reliable power and transport, stable labour, a willing market, and prices above cost. The hindering-factors question checks that you can name where South Africa's chain strains. Rehearse the chain as five links. Link one, geology's fine print: the richest platinum reserves on earth, but locked in metre-thin reefs kilometres down — hot, labour-intensive, resistant to mechanisation, expensive per ounce. Link two, energy: smelting is power-hungry, and load-shedding plus rising tariffs turn a national grid problem into a mine-level cost. Link three, labour: wage disputes and the sector's hardest strikes — handle Marikana with the seriousness it deserves as a turning point in labour relations. Link four, market: prices swing with global car sales, and the electric-vehicle transition threatens autocatalyst demand — balanced by the hydrogen economy's potential platinum appetite. Link five, the social licence: communities around the mines still face housing and service backlogs, so expectation and unrest pressure operations. Answer with the chain and you show the deeper understanding: South Africa's platinum question is not whether the treasure exists — it is whether every link between the reef and the buyer can be kept strong. That is the sentence to end the essay with.
