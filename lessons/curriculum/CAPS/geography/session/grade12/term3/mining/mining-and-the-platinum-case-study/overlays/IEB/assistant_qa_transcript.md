**Mandy:** The essay statement "mining is the foundation of the South African economy — discuss" worries me, because mining is less than ten per cent of GDP. How do I argue foundation without stretching the truth?

**Tutor:** Argue it structurally and historically, never arithmetically — the claim concerns what mining BUILT, not what it currently earns, and the word discuss invites you to test it honestly. Open by conceding the figures: under ten per cent of GDP directly, under half a million jobs — on today's scoreboard mining is not the biggest player, and saying so first buys you credibility. Then build the foundation case: the railways were driven inland to reach Kimberley and the Witwatersrand; the coal-fired power system grew to feed the shafts; the JSE was created to raise capital for the goldfields; manufacturing and banking grew up as mining's suppliers; Johannesburg — a metropolis with no river and no harbour — exists because of gold; and the migrant labour system rearranged settlement across the whole subcontinent. The modern economy is a house whose foundations mining poured, even though other floors now collect more rent. Then bring the argument into the present: mining still stocks the export stall — platinum group metals, coal, iron ore, gold — earning the foreign currency that pays for imported oil and machinery, plus royalties, taxes and the multiplier through suppliers and mining towns. Close with the two-way caveat: a foundation is not the whole building — services dominate today — but nobody calls a foundation unimportant because they live upstairs. Concession, case, caveat: that is the full shape of a discuss answer.

**Mandy:** In the platinum case study I keep muddling favouring and hindering factors, because some seem to be both. Electricity is right there — the grid is close, but load-shedding wrecks production. How do I write that?

**Tutor:** Write the two faces separately and name the property each face belongs to — double-edged factors are not a trap; split cleanly, they are the best material in the answer. The rule: a factor favours or hinders depending on WHICH property of it you are describing, so attach the property to the noun. Electricity: the PRESENCE of a major grid beside the Bushveld favoured the industry's establishment, because smelters devour power and the infrastructure already existed; the RELIABILITY and PRICE of that power now hinder it, because load-shedding stalls smelters and tariffs climb. Labour: the AVAILABILITY of an experienced mining workforce favours; labour RELATIONS — the long strikes, the weight of Marikana — hinder. Geology: the SIZE and PREDICTABILITY of the reserves favour; the THINNESS and DEPTH of the reefs hinder. Even the market divides: autocatalyst demand favours now; the battery-electric future threatens later, while hydrogen fuel cells may swing demand back. Never write "electricity is both a favouring and a hindering factor" and stop there — write two sentences, each carrying its property. The split earns the marks; the muddle forfeits them.

**Mandy:** For the mapwork: when a question says "provide evidence of mining activity on the extract", how many pieces do I give, and exactly how should each one be written?

**Tutor:** Give one piece per mark plus a spare, and give every piece the same three-part shape: feature, map appearance, address. The allocation sets the count — three marks means three DIFFERENT features, not the same dump quoted from two squares. Your menu, roughly in order of strength: the printed word itself — Mine, Myn, Colliery, Quarry — because a map that names the activity is unbeatable; mine dumps and slimes dams, described as pale, straight-edged, geometric shapes that no natural process builds; the toothed or hachured symbols of pits and excavations; the transport signature — a railway spur or heavy road dead-ending at a cluster of buildings in open country; power lines converging on a single site; and the settlement signature — a grid-planned town laid out beside the workings. Then the sentence pattern, one line per piece: "Slimes dam — pale, rectangular, straight-edged — at grid reference such-and-such." Feature, appearance, address. If a photograph accompanies the extract, add one cross-reference line: the headgear in the photograph matches the building cluster at that reference. And guard the corridor-then-stairs order in every reference — eastings before northings — because one swapped pair can leak marks across the whole question.

**Mandy:** Last one: if platinum is such a treasure, why do the questions keep circling the industry's PROBLEMS? What is really being tested?

**Tutor:** What is being tested is the central idea of economic geography: resource wealth is a possibility, not a promise. A deposit only becomes prosperity if every link of a chain holds — affordable extraction, dependable power and transport, stable labour, a willing buyer, and a price above cost — and the hindering-factors question checks whether you can point to the links where South Africa's chain strains. Rehearse it as five links. Link one, geology's fine print: the richest reserves on earth, but locked in metre-thin reefs kilometres down — hot, hand-drilled, costly per ounce. Link two, energy: smelting is power-hungry, so load-shedding and rising tariffs turn a national grid problem into a shaft-level cost. Link three, labour: wage conflict and the sector's hardest strikes — treat Marikana with the gravity it deserves, as a turning point in the industry's labour history. Link four, the market: prices ride the global car cycle, and the electric-vehicle transition threatens autocatalyst demand, partly offset by the hydrogen economy's appetite for platinum catalysts. Link five, the social licence: communities beside the shafts still lack housing and services, so expectation and unrest press on operations daily. Answer with the chain and you demonstrate the deeper point, which also makes the perfect closing sentence: South Africa's platinum question is not whether the treasure exists — it is whether every link between the reef and the buyer can be kept strong.
