# Part 1 — Expert

Modern South Africa was assembled around holes in the ground: diamonds at Kimberley from 1867, gold on the Witwatersrand from 1886, and today the world's richest platinum deposits in the Bushveld. This session covers mining the way the examination does: its contribution and significance to South African development, past and present; the prescribed platinum case study — location, favouring and hindering factors, economic contribution; the balanced evaluation of mining's costs; and the mapwork skills welded to this topic — identifying mining activity on topographic maps, grid referencing, and the map codes and symbols the sheet uses to speak.

## Subtopic: How Mining Built and Still Carries South Africa

Begin with the historical claim, because the exam states it and asks you to justify it: mining is the foundation of the modern South African economy. The evidence chain runs like this. The mineral discoveries drew capital and labour to empty veld — Johannesburg went from farmland to the subcontinent's largest city inside a generation, entirely on gold. The mines demanded railways, and the rail network was flung from the ports to the interior to serve them; they demanded power, and Eskom's coal-fired system grew to feed them; they demanded machinery, explosives and finance, and South Africa's manufacturing and banking sectors — including the JSE, founded to fund the goldfields — grew up as mining's suppliers. And the migrant labour system, drawing workers from across southern Africa to the mines, reshaped the region's settlement patterns and family structures — a social imprint the settlement term already traced.

Today's contribution: mining provides under ten per cent of GDP directly, employs under half a million workers, but carries an outsized share of merchandise export earnings — platinum group metals, coal, iron ore, gold and manganese are consistently among the country's biggest exports, bringing in the foreign exchange that pays for imported oil, machinery and electronics. Add the multipliers: each mining job supports further jobs in suppliers, transport and the businesses where wages are spent, and mining royalties and taxes fund the state. Whole towns — Rustenburg, Witbank, Kathu, Kuruman — and substantial provincial economies stand on it.

The full picture demands the other ledger too: mining's costs. Environmental — acid mine drainage poisoning water from old gold workings, dust, sinkholes on the dolomite, scarred land and tailings dumps. Social — the legacy of the migrant labour system, dangerous work, silicosis, and the shocks when commodity prices fall and shafts close, stranding mining towns. An exam answer that carries both ledgers, contribution and cost, is the answer the levels grid calls critical.

Pause here and take this section's questions: the evidence chain for the foundation claim, today's contribution measured three ways, and the cost ledger.

## Subtopic: The Platinum Case Study: Location and Favouring Factors

Platinum is the prescribed case study, and South Africa is its world headquarters: the country holds most of the planet's known platinum group metal reserves and is the world's leading producer.

Location first, precisely. The platinum lies in the Bushveld Igneous Complex — an enormous, ancient body of once-molten rock that solidified in layers, saucer-shaped, across the northern interior. Its mineral-rich layers outcrop in arcs called limbs: the western limb curving through the Rustenburg and Brits districts of North West; the eastern limb through Limpopo and Mpumalanga near Burgersfort and Steelpoort; and the northern limb near Mokopane in Limpopo. The mining belt's capital is Rustenburg — remember the pairing: specialised mining town, platinum. Within the layered rock, the metal concentrates in specific thin horizons — the Merensky Reef and the UG2 chromitite layer — which mines follow underground for kilometres.

Now the favouring factors, each one a mark. Geological: exceptionally large, high-grade reserves in continuous, predictable layers — a geological endowment few countries share. Historical and technical: more than a century of deep-mining expertise, inherited from gold, with the engineers, drilling technology and refining capability already in the country. Infrastructural: the Bushveld sits close to Gauteng's core — railways, power lines, water transfer schemes and the skills of the industrial heartland reach it easily. Labour: an established mining labour force. Market: sustained global demand — platinum group metals are essential in autocatalysts, the converters that clean vehicle exhausts, plus jewellery, chemical catalysts and, increasingly, hydrogen fuel cell technology. South Africa supplies a commodity the world's car industry cannot currently do without.

Take the questions for this section now: the Bushveld Igneous Complex with its three limbs and reef names, and the five families of favouring factors.

## Subtopic: Platinum's Contribution and Its Hindering Factors

The contribution, counted properly. Platinum group metals rank among South Africa's largest export earners, regularly at or near the top of the mineral export table. The industry employs on the order of one hundred and seventy thousand workers directly — the largest employer in South African mining — with each wage supporting dependants and local businesses across the platinum belt. It anchors the economies of North West and the eastern limb districts: Rustenburg grew into one of the country's fastest-growing cities on platinum. It attracts major capital investment, pays royalties and taxes, and feeds beneficiation ambitions — smelters and refineries at Rustenburg and elsewhere process the ore locally, adding value before export, and government policy pushes for more such downstream processing, including hydrogen-economy initiatives built on platinum catalysts.

Now the hindering factors, and learn them in two baskets. Basket one, the physical and technical: the reefs are thin — often around a metre — and increasingly deep, so mining is labour-intensive, hot, and hard to mechanise; costs rise as shafts chase the dipping layers deeper; and the ore requires complex, energy-hungry smelting. Basket two, the economic and social: unreliable and expensive electricity — smelters stall when the power fails, and load-shedding has repeatedly cut production; volatile world prices, which swing with the global car market and can drop below production cost; labour unrest — the platinum belt endured the sector's longest strikes and the 2012 Marikana tragedy sits in this history; rising wage and input costs; deteriorating rail and water infrastructure; and long-term demand risk, because battery electric vehicles need no autocatalyst — though fuel-cell technology may replace some of that demand with new demand. Communities around the mines still struggle with housing, services and unemployment, so the industry operates amid high social expectation.

The exam verb here is usually discuss or evaluate: contribution on one side, hindrances on the other, and a conclusion that platinum remains a pillar — but a pillar under strain, needing reliable power, labour peace and beneficiation to keep standing.

This section's questions are ready: the contribution counted in exports, jobs and beneficiation, and the two baskets of hindering factors.

## Subtopic: Mining on the Map: Identification, Grid References and Symbols

Paper 2 welds this topic to the map sheet, and three skills carry the marks.

Skill one: identifying mining activity on a topographic map. Mines announce themselves with a cluster of signs. The word itself — Mine, Myn, Colliery, Quarry, Diggings — printed on the sheet. Mine dumps and slimes dams: those pale, geometrically regular mounds and rectangular dams that nature never builds — straight edges are the giveaway. Excavations and open pits shown by hachured or toothed depression symbols. Headgear and buildings clustered oddly in open country, with dense road and rail spurs running to them — ore is heavy, so a railway line ending at a works is strong evidence. Power lines converging, because mines devour electricity. And around an established mine, the settlement evidence: a grid-planned mining town nearby. In an exam answer, name at least two pieces of map evidence and quote their grid references.

Skill two: the referencing itself. A six-figure grid reference divides each map square: read eastings first, then northings — along the corridor, then up the stairs — estimating tenths within the square. Practise until the order is reflex, because reversing the pair kills the mark. Alphanumeric references — the letter-number blocks like B3 used on some sheets and orthophotos — ask only that you name the block. State references exactly in the format the question shows.

Skill three: codes and symbols as a system. Blue for water, green for vegetation, brown for contours and cultivated land symbols, black and red for human constructions — and the legend is printed on every sheet, so never guess when you can check. Questions favour the human symbols around mines: the thick red national road, thinner secondary roads, the black railway with station ticks, power line symbols marching on pylon dots, and built-up area shading. A typical integrated question hands you a photograph and a map of the platinum belt and asks: identify the economic activity, give map evidence with grid references, and explain why the activity locates here — and that final part is answered with the case study you now carry: the Bushveld's layered riches, the infrastructure, the labour, the world's demand for the metal in every exhaust pipe.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: the mine's signature on the map, the corridor-then-stairs referencing order, and the symbol families by colour.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same story told from ground level — a country that got rich by digging, a giant buried saucer, and a treasure map you already know how to read.

## Subtopic: The Country That Dug Itself Into Existence

Ask why South Africa's biggest city stands where it stands. No river worth the name, no harbour, no lake — Johannesburg exists because in 1886 someone tripped over the edge of the world's richest gold deposit, and within a few years the empty highveld had a city on it. Diamonds at Kimberley had already shown the pattern twenty years earlier: find the treasure, and everything else comes running.

And "everything else" is the real lesson. The mines needed to move heavy rock, so railways were built from the coast to the interior — the skeleton of today's rail map. They needed power, so power stations rose on the coalfields. They needed drills, dynamite, boots and banks, so factories and finance grew up to supply them — the JSE itself was born to raise money for the goldfields. Workers came from across southern Africa, and towns, townships and the whole migrant labour pattern followed. Follow any thread of the modern economy backwards — the railways, Eskom, the JSE, Joburg itself — and you arrive at a mine. That is what "mining is the foundation of the economy" means: not that mining is the biggest thing today, but that the house was built on it.

Today mining earns just under a tenth of the country's income directly, employs a few hundred thousand people — but here is the part to hold: it fills a huge slice of the export basket. Platinum, coal, iron ore, gold — sold abroad, they bring in the foreign money that pays for the petrol, phones and machines South Africa buys from the world. Mining is the family member working overseas whose payments keep the household's account in balance.

But dig and the land remembers. Old mines leak acid water into rivers, dumps blow dust over townships, and when a mine dies, its town — built for one purpose — is stranded. Honest answers carry both sides: the foundation AND the bill.

Take this section's questions now: the city with no river, the everything-else that mining dragged into existence, the overseas-worker picture of exports, and the bill the land presents.

## Subtopic: The Buried Saucer Full of Platinum

Now the star of the case study. Deep under the bushveld north and west of Pretoria lies a gigantic, ancient saucer of once-molten rock — the Bushveld Igneous Complex. As it cooled, long ago, its minerals settled into flat layers, the way cooling soup settles, and a few of those layers — one called the Merensky Reef, another called the UG2 — are stuffed with platinum. The saucer's rim breaks the surface in three arcs, and that is where the mines line up: the western arc through Rustenburg, the eastern arc near Burgersfort, and a northern arc near Mokopane. Rustenburg is the capital of platinum country — pair those names permanently.

Why is South Africa the world's platinum superpower? Count the lucky cards in its hand. Card one: the deposit itself — most of the world's known platinum sits in this one saucer, in neat, predictable layers. Card two: a century of deep-mining know-how inherited from gold — the engineers and drills were already here. Card three: the saucer lies near Gauteng, so railways, power lines and skilled workers did not have to be conjured from nothing. Card four: the world is desperate for the metal — almost every petrol and diesel car on earth carries a little platinum in its exhaust to clean the fumes, plus jewellery and the new hydrogen technologies. Rich deposit, ready skills, nearby infrastructure, hungry market: four cards, winning hand.

But the hand costs a lot to play. The platinum layers are thin — about a metre, roughly waist height — and deep, so imagine mining a coin locked inside a slab, kilometres down, in heat, mostly by hand-held drills because machines struggle in a metre-high tunnel. Then the surface troubles: power cuts that stall the smelters, world prices that dive when car sales dive, painful strikes — the platinum belt carries the memory of Marikana — and a future question mark, because battery electric cars need no platinum, while hydrogen cars would need plenty. Treasure in the saucer, struggle at the surface: that balance IS the platinum case study.

Your questions for this section are up: the saucer and its three arcs, the four lucky cards, and the thin deep layers that make the treasure hard to lift.

## Subtopic: Reading the Treasure Map

Last skill: finding the mine on the map sheet, which is easier than it sounds, because a mine cannot hide. It is the untidiest, most unmistakable thing on any topographic map — you just need to know its signature.

Look for shapes nature refuses to make. Nature does not do straight edges — so those pale rectangular mounds and perfectly square dams are mine dumps and slimes dams, piled and pumped by machines. Look for the ground opened up: the little toothed symbols of pits and quarries. Look for heavy transport aimed at nothing: a railway spur that leaves the main line and dead-ends at a cluster of buildings in open veld — rock is heavy, and only something worth carrying by the trainload justifies rails to nowhere. Look for power lines converging like extension cords running to one hungry appliance. And usually, the map simply confesses: the word Mine, Myn, Colliery or Quarry printed right there. In the exam, give two or three of these clues and pin each to its grid reference — evidence plus address.

The address system is the six-figure grid reference, and the chant that orders it: along the corridor, then up the stairs. Read the easting first — the numbers increasing to the right — then the northing — increasing upward — estimating tenths inside the square. Corridor, then stairs, every single time; swap them and the answer is wrong even though your eyes found the right spot.

And the map speaks in colour-coded symbols, like a school uniform code: blue is always water, green is vegetation, brown is the shape of the land and its cultivation, black and red are things people built — roads, railways, buildings, power lines. Never memorise in a panic what the legend will hand you free — the key is printed on the sheet; your job is speed, not recall.

Put the whole lesson together on one exam question: a map extract of the Rustenburg district. You spot the dumps, the rail spur, the word Mine, and give their references. Asked what is mined and why here, you answer from the saucer: platinum, in the Bushveld's layered reefs, near Gauteng's infrastructure, feeding the world's exhaust pipes. Here come the final questions of the lesson: the shapes nature refuses to make, the corridor-and-stairs chant, the uniform code of colours, and the saucer standing behind the map. Land these, and mining becomes the topic where Paper 1 theory and Paper 2 mapwork pay you twice for the same knowledge.
