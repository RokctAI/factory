**Mandy:** Questions that come up a lot with this topic! First: some jobs seem to sit in two sectors at once. A mine has engineers doing research, and a farm has a farm stall selling jam. How do I classify the tricky ones?

**Tutor:** Classify the ACTIVITY, not the company, and the tricky cases untangle themselves. A modern business is usually a bundle of activities from different sectors under one name. The mine's core activity — extracting ore — is primary. Its research office doing geological modelling is quaternary work employed by a primary company. The farm growing fruit is primary; the moment the kitchen boils that fruit into jam, a transformation has happened, so jam-making is secondary; and the stall selling it to passing tourists is tertiary. The exam knows this and sets it deliberately: a question describing Sasol will mention coal mining — primary — and fuel manufacturing — secondary — in the same paragraph, and the marks go to the learner who splits them. So when a scenario feels double, write the split explicitly: name the activity, apply the test — taken raw from nature is primary, transformed is secondary, service delivered is tertiary, knowledge handled is quaternary — and assign each activity separately. One more boundary worth pre-learning: processing counts as secondary even on the farm. Drying fruit, ginning cotton, pasteurising milk — the CAPS line is that extraction is primary and any transformation, however small, has crossed into secondary.

**Mandy:** Next: the paired pie charts — GDP share next to employment share. I can see the slices are different sizes, but my explanations keep getting half marks. What is the full-mark explanation?

**Tutor:** Half marks means you described the difference without giving its mechanism, and the mechanism has two halves — output value and labour intensity. Take agriculture's classic pair: around two to three per cent of GDP but roughly five per cent or more of employment. Full-mark reasoning names both halves. Half one: the value of raw farm output is low — unprocessed maize, fruit and wool sell cheaply compared with financial services or manufactured goods, so the money slice stays thin. Half two: farming remains labour-intensive — harvesting, pruning and packing still need many hands, and wages are low, so the jobs slice stays fat. Now reverse both halves for finance: services like banking generate enormous value per worker — a small team can move billions — and the work is capital- and skill-intensive, so the money slice is fat while the jobs slice is thin. Then close with the general rule stated as a rule: sectors with low value per worker carry more employment than GDP; sectors with high value per worker carry more GDP than employment. Description, two-part mechanism, general rule — that ladder is the difference between two marks and the full allocation. And if the question says "suggest reasons", give at least two, because the plural in the question is an instruction, not decoration.

**Mandy:** The thirty-plus per cent unemployment figure — how do I use it in answers without just quoting it as a sad fact?

**Tutor:** Use it as an analytical lens, because almost every economic geography question this term ultimately points at it. Three disciplined uses. Use one: as the WHY behind policy. When a question asks why government promotes agri-processing, beneficiation of minerals, tourism or Special Economic Zones, the deep answer is always employment — South Africa needs sectors that absorb labour, and each of those policies tries to create jobs where raw exports create few. Quote the rate there and it is doing work, not decorating. Use two: as a tension inside mechanisation. Mines and farms mechanise to stay competitive — output holds or rises while jobs fall — so the same trend that protects GDP worsens unemployment. Naming that tension is exactly the kind of evaluative sentence eight-mark paragraphs reward. Use three: as the doorway to the informal sector. With formal work scarce, millions build their own — street trade, taxis, home services — so the informal economy is not a footnote but the shock absorber of South African unemployment; any answer about employment that includes it gains credibility and usually gains marks. And one precision habit: say "the official unemployment rate exceeds thirty per cent, and is far higher among youth" — the youth clause shows the examiner you know the structure of the problem, not just its headline.

**Mandy:** Last one: in data questions, when they ask me to "comment on the reliability" or "limitations" of the statistics, what am I actually supposed to write?

**Tutor:** You are being asked to name, briefly and specifically, what the numbers cannot see — and economic statistics have a standard list of blind spots you can prepare in advance. Blind spot one: the informal sector. GDP and employment figures capture registered, taxed activity; the spaza, the backyard mechanic and the unregistered creche largely escape them, so both output and employment are undercounted — and in South Africa that undercount is large. Blind spot two: subsistence production. Food grown and eaten at home never passes through a market, so rural livelihoods look poorer on paper than on the plate. Blind spot three: averages hide distribution. A rising GDP says nothing about who received the rise — pair any GDP claim with inequality and the picture changes. Blind spot four: definitions move. The official unemployment rate counts active job-seekers; the expanded rate adds the discouraged who have stopped looking, and the gap between the two is many percentage points — so which definition a table uses changes its meaning. Blind spot five: dates and prices. Comparing rand values across decades without adjusting for inflation, or comparing two years across a recession, misleads. In the exam, two or three of these, stated in one sentence each and tied to the actual table in front of you, is a full-mark comment. The skill is not doubting the data everywhere — it is knowing precisely where its eyesight ends.
