# Part 1 — Expert

Term three turns Geography toward money: how South Africa earns its living. Everything in economic geography hangs from one frame — the four economic sectors — so this session builds that frame properly: what each sector does, with South African examples; how the sectors contribute to GDP and GNP and what those two measures actually are; how employment is distributed and why the employment picture and the money picture disagree; and how to read the statistical tables, pie charts and graphs through which every exam question on this topic is asked. The number to carry through the whole term: agriculture contributes only a small share of South Africa's GDP, yet the tertiary sector contributes well over half — and the reasons behind those numbers are the marks.

## Subtopic: The Four Sectors: What Each One Does

The primary sector extracts raw materials directly from nature. Farming, fishing, forestry and mining — the maize farmer in the Free State, the trawler out of Hout Bay, the plantation feller in Mpumalanga, the platinum miner at Rustenburg. The test for primary is simple: is the product taken from the earth, the sea or the living landscape in its raw state? Then it is primary, even when the machinery is enormous and the company is listed on the JSE. Mining is South Africa's historic signature primary activity — the industry the whole modern economy was built on.

The secondary sector takes raw materials and MAKES something from them: manufacturing, processing, construction and the supply of power. The mill that turns maize into mealie meal, the smelter that turns chrome into ferrochrome, the plant at Sasolburg turning coal into fuel, the factory assembling bakkies in Kariega, the builders raising a new clinic, Eskom generating electricity — all secondary. The keyword is transformation: raw material in, changed product out, value added in between. That phrase — value added — is the sector's whole point: a ton of raw chrome earns little; the same ton transformed earns multiples more.

The tertiary sector sells no object it made; it provides SERVICES: trade and retail, transport, finance and banking, tourism, education, health, government administration. The spaza owner, the taxi operator, the teacher, the nurse, the bank teller, the tour guide at the Big Hole — all tertiary. This is South Africa's giant: the majority of GDP and the majority of jobs.

The quaternary sector is the specialised tip of services: knowledge work — research, information technology, data services, universities' research arms, financial analysis. Some textbooks fold it into tertiary; CAPS names it separately, so know it: the sector that handles information and ideas rather than goods or routine services.

Chain them into one story and the frame locks: the farm grows the maize — primary; the mill grinds it — secondary; the supermarket sells it and the truck delivers it — tertiary; the lab that breeds a better maize cultivar — quaternary. One product, four sectors, and every job in the country slots into exactly one link.

Pause here and take this section's questions: the four definitions, the transformation keyword, and the maize chain from field to lab.

## Subtopic: GDP, GNP and What Each Sector Contributes

Two measures of the economy's size, separated by one letter and one idea. Gross Domestic Product, GDP, is the total value of all final goods and services produced INSIDE a country's borders in a year, whoever owns the producers. Domestic means inside the house: a foreign-owned car plant in Gqeberha produces inside South Africa, so its output counts in South Africa's GDP. Gross National Product, GNP, follows ownership instead of location: the total produced by a country's citizens and companies wherever in the world they operate. A South African mining house's Australian operation counts in South Africa's GNP but in Australia's GDP. Domestic follows the ground; national follows the passport.

Now the shape of South Africa's GDP, in round figures worth memorising as proportions, not decimals. Agriculture contributes only around two to three per cent. Mining, for all its history and its export earnings, contributes under ten per cent. All of manufacturing and the rest of the secondary sector, roughly a fifth. And the tertiary sector — finance, trade, transport, government, tourism and the rest of services — contributes well over sixty per cent, roughly two thirds. Say the pattern in one sentence: South Africa has the sectoral structure of a developed economy — services dominant, primary small — inside a society with developing-world unemployment and inequality. That paradox is a favourite essay seed.

The pattern has a name and a direction. As economies develop, the weight shifts from primary to secondary to tertiary — first most people farm, then factories take over, then services dominate. South Africa has travelled that road: mining built Johannesburg, manufacturing grew behind it, and services now tower over both. But hold a caution for the exam: a small GDP share does not mean a sector is unimportant. Agriculture at two to three per cent still feeds the nation, supplies the food-processing industry, and earns export income; mining still anchors export earnings and whole provincial economies. Contribution to GDP is one measure of importance, not the only one.

This section's questions are with you now: the D-versus-N separation, the approximate sector shares, and the caution about small shares.

## Subtopic: Employment: Where the Jobs Actually Are

Money and jobs tell different stories, and the exam lives in the gap between them.

Start with the jobs picture. The tertiary sector employs by far the most South Africans — retail, transport, government, domestic services, finance. Agriculture employs more people than its tiny GDP share suggests — around five per cent of formal employment, plus substantial seasonal and informal farm work — because much of farming remains labour-intensive. Mining employs a shrinking workforce, well under half a million, as mechanisation deepens and old gold mines close. Manufacturing employs roughly one in ten workers.

Now the gap. Agriculture's share of employment is roughly double its share of GDP: many workers, modest money — because raw produce sells cheap and wages are low. Finance is the mirror image: an enormous slice of GDP from relatively few, highly paid workers. State the rule the memorandum wants: labour-intensive sectors carry a larger share of employment than of GDP; capital-intensive sectors carry a larger share of GDP than of employment. When a question hands you a table where a sector's two percentages disagree, that rule IS the answer.

Two South African realities complete the picture. First, unemployment: with the official rate above thirty per cent, and far higher among the youth, the economy's central problem is not the sectoral mix but the shortage of jobs in every sector — which is why every case study this term ends by asking what each industry can do for employment. Second, the informal sector: street traders, backyard mechanics, home-based creches, unregistered spazas — work that never enters the formal statistics but sustains millions of households. Any employment analysis that ignores informal work undercounts the real economy, and saying so earns evaluation marks.

Take the questions for this section now: the employment ranking, the labour-intensive rule, and the two realities that complicate the neat percentages.

## Subtopic: Reading the Numbers: Tables, Graphs and the Questions They Carry

Every exam question on economic structure arrives wearing data — a table of sector percentages, a pie chart, a line graph over decades — and the marks go to learners who read data in a disciplined order.

Order of reading, four steps. Step one: read the title, the units and the date before anything else — whether the numbers are percentages of GDP, percentages of employment, or rand values changes every conclusion, and a 1980 pie beside a 2020 pie is a question about change, not about either year alone. Step two: find the largest and smallest categories and say them — the obvious sentence is the first mark. Step three: find the comparison or trend — which sector grew, which shrank, where do two datasets disagree. Step four: explain with a reason from the syllabus — mechanisation shrinking primary employment, the growth of financial services, mining's decline as ore grades fall.

Two classic question shapes to rehearse. Shape one: paired pie charts, GDP by sector against employment by sector, same year. The examiner wants the gap — agriculture bigger on the employment pie, finance bigger on the GDP pie — explained with the labour-intensive rule. Shape two: a line graph of sector shares across fifty years, primary falling, tertiary rising. The examiner wants the development story: economies shift from primary through secondary to tertiary as they mature, and South Africa's lines follow it.

One calculation to be ready for: percentages into rand. If GDP is roughly seven trillion rand and agriculture contributes about two and a half per cent, then agriculture's contribution is seven trillion times two and a half hundredths — around one hundred and seventy-five billion rand. Small percentage, colossal sum — say both halves, because that contrast is usually the point of the question.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: the four-step reading order, the two classic question shapes with the rules that answer them, and the percentage-to-rand calculation.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole economy again, told through one bag of mealie meal, one soccer team, and one report card.

## Subtopic: One Bag of Mealie Meal, Four Kinds of Work

Take a bag of mealie meal off the shop shelf and hold it. Every kind of work in South Africa touched that bag, and there are exactly four kinds.

Somebody GREW the maize — pulled it out of nature: soil, sun, rain. That is primary work: farming, fishing, forestry, digging things out of the ground. If the job ends with something raw in your hands — a cob, a fish, a log, a lump of ore — it is primary. Mining is just farming for rocks: taking from nature what nature made.

Somebody CHANGED the maize into meal — the mill. That is secondary work: taking raw stuff and making it into something else. Milling, smelting, sewing, building, brewing, generating electricity. The magic word is making. And notice what making does to price: a cob is cheap; a branded two-kilogram bag of super maize meal costs real money. The difference is called value added, and it is why countries fight to do their own manufacturing instead of exporting raw stuff cheap and buying it back expensive.

Somebody SOLD you the bag, and somebody TRUCKED it to the shop, and somebody's bank moved the money when you tapped your card. None of them made anything — they provided services. That is tertiary work: shops, taxis, teachers, nurses, banks, tourism, government. Look around any mall: almost every job you can see is tertiary — which is a clue about modern South Africa.

And somewhere a scientist is breeding a maize that survives drought better. Work done with pure knowledge and information — research, IT, data — is quaternary work: the brain-work tip of the services world.

Grow it, make it, move-and-sell it, think it up. Four kinds of work, and every worker in the country is doing one of them. Take this section's questions now: the four verbs, the raw-in-your-hands test, and the value-added price jump.

## Subtopic: The Soccer Team Where the Smallest Player Scores Most

Now, how big is each kind of work? Measure the economy like a soccer team's goals for the season and ask who scored.

Here is the shock: farming — the work that feeds every single one of us — scores only about two or three goals out of every hundred. Mining, the industry that built Joburg, fewer than ten. All the factories together, about twenty. And the services — the banks, shops, taxis, schools, hospitals, government offices, hotels — score roughly two thirds of all the goals. The scoreboard of the South African economy is dominated by people who never grow or make a physical thing.

Before that feels wrong, two corrections. First: a small score is not a small importance. Farming's few goals FEED THE TEAM — no maize, no country — and its produce is the raw material the food factories and supermarkets live on. Mining's goals are heavy with export money. Judge a sector only by its percentage and you will misjudge it; the exam loves learners who say so. Second: the scoreboard measures money, not people. Count JOBS instead of goals and the picture shifts — farming employs about twice as big a share of workers as its money share, because farm work uses many hands for modest pay, while a bank does the opposite: huge money, few people. The rule in one line: many-hands sectors are bigger in jobs than in money; big-machine and big-money sectors are bigger in money than in jobs. Almost every table question this term is that one line wearing numbers.

One last pair to keep straight, because one letter separates them. GDP counts everything produced inside South Africa's borders — inside the house — no matter who owns the factory. GNP counts everything South African-owned, wherever on earth it operates. The German-owned plant in Gqeberha: South Africa's GDP, Germany's GNP. Domestic is the ground; national is the passport.

Your questions for this section are up: the surprising scoreboard, the two corrections, and the ground-versus-passport pair.

## Subtopic: Reading the Report Card Without Fear

Every exam question here hands you a report card — a table, a pie chart, a graph — and asks you to read it. Read it like you would read a friend's school report: in a calm order, obvious things first.

First, the name on the report: the title, the units, the year. Is this money or jobs? Percentages or rands? 1990 or 2023? Half of all data mistakes are answered by the heading the learner never read. Second, say the loud things: biggest slice, smallest slice. "The tertiary sector contributes the largest share; agriculture the smallest." That sentence feels too easy to be worth marks — it is worth marks; write it. Third, find the story: what grew, what shrank, what disagrees. Two pies from different years — the story is the change between them. A money pie next to a jobs pie — the story is the gap: agriculture's slice fatter on the jobs pie, finance's fatter on the money pie. Fourth, explain with a reason from the lesson: machines replacing farm and mine workers, services swelling as the economy matures, many hands versus big machines.

If a calculation appears, it will be a percentage of a big number, and you can do it with the money trick: per cent means out of every hundred rand. If the economy is seven trillion rand and agriculture is two and a half per cent, then out of every hundred rand, agriculture earns two rand fifty — and two rand fifty out of every hundred, taken across seven trillion, is about one hundred and seventy-five billion rand. Tiny share, mountain of money — write down both, because that contrast is usually the whole point.

And keep two honest sentences ready for evaluation marks, because they are true of every dataset this term: the numbers miss the informal economy — the street traders and backyard workshops that sustain millions — and a sector's percentage is not its importance, or farming would be the least important thing in a country that cannot skip a single meal. Here come the final questions of the lesson: the reading order, the gap between the two pies, the money trick, and the two honest sentences. Read the report card calmly and this term's data questions become the easiest marks in Paper 1.
