**Mandy:** Questions that come up a lot with this topic! First: raw-material-orientated versus market-orientated. I understand cane and bread, but exam scenarios give weirder industries. Is there a procedure that works on anything?

**Tutor:** There is — a three-question procedure, and it never fails if you ask the questions in order. Question one: does processing make the material LIGHTER? Weigh the input against the output in your head. Cane to sugar, timber to planks, ore to metal — massive weight loss, so the factory chases the raw material, because you would rather transport the light product than the heavy input. Question two: does anything SPOIL, and which end spoils faster? If the raw material rots — cane sours within hours of cutting, milk, fish — the plant must sit at the source. If the PRODUCT spoils or stales — fresh bread, daily newspapers — the plant must sit at the market. Question three: does processing make the product HARDER to move — heavier, bulkier, more fragile? Beer is the classic: malt and hops travel light, but the bottled product is mostly water and glass, so breweries sit in the cities that drink them; furniture and car assembly lean the same way, which is one reason assembly plants sit near ports and markets. Run the three questions on any strange scenario — a fruit cannery, a cement works, a fish-meal plant — and the answer falls out: weight loss or spoiling input says raw material; weight gain, fragility or spoiling output says market. And write the reasoning, not just the label, because the memo allocates the marks to the transport-cost logic.

***

**Mandy:** Next: the eight location factors. When a question says "discuss the factors that influenced the location of industry X", must I cover all eight? That feels impossible in the time.

**Tutor:** No — and trying to cover all eight is actually the weaker answer. The skill being assessed is selection with justification: identify the three or four factors that genuinely governed that industry, develop each properly, and briefly dismiss the rest with a reason. Look at how the professionals would treat two contrasting cases. An aluminium smelter at Richards Bay: energy first — smelting aluminium devours electricity, so proximity to major supply decides everything; transport second — imported alumina arrives by ship and metal exports leave by ship, so the deep-water port is essential; then water and flat industrial land. Labour ranks low — the plant is capital-intensive — and raw materials are imported anyway, so local materials barely matter; saying so in one sentence shows command. Now a clothing factory: labour dominates — abundant, affordable, semi-skilled workers; then market access and transport; energy hardly matters — sewing machines sip electricity. Same list, opposite rankings, and THAT contrast is the understanding examiners pay for. Structure your answer as: the two or three dominant factors, each developed with the industry's specific need; one line on secondary factors; one line dismissing the irrelevant ones. Depth on the dominant few beats a shallow tour of all eight every time.

**Mandy:** The word "footloose" confuses half the class — how can an industry have no location factors at all?

**Tutor:** It cannot, and that is precisely the refinement worth writing: footloose does not mean factorless — it means freed from the OLD factors and captured by new ones. The traditional anchors were heavy: raw materials, bulk transport, big water, big power. A footloose industry — software, electronics assembly, pharmaceuticals research, financial services back-offices — has inputs and outputs that are light, high-value or purely digital, so those heavy anchors lose their grip; the firm could, in principle, operate almost anywhere. But watch where such firms ACTUALLY go: they cluster, hard, in a small number of places. Why? Because the factors that now bind them are human: skilled labour above all — the firm goes where programmers and engineers already live; connectivity — fibre, airports, reliable power for servers; lifestyle and amenities that keep scarce skills from leaving; universities feeding graduates; and the presence of other similar firms to hire from and sell to. That is why South African tech and services cluster in the metros rather than spreading evenly across the platteland. The exam-ready formulation: footloose industries are free of raw-material and transport ties, and therefore locate according to labour skills, connectivity and quality of life — which produces clustering in metropolitan areas, not an even spread. Say it that way and the apparent contradiction becomes your best paragraph.

**Mandy:** Last one: the hindering-factors question feels like writing a news complaints list. How do I keep it geographical and earn the top level?

**Tutor:** Anchor every complaint to a mechanism and a spatial consequence — that is what turns news into geography. Take each hindrance one step further than the headline. Load-shedding: not merely "the lights go off", but production lines halt mid-batch, spoiling continuous processes like smelting and food manufacture, raising unit costs and pushing investors to price South African plants as riskier than competitors' — a mechanism, then a consequence for investment geography. Rail decay: freight shifts from rail to road, which raises costs for exactly the heavy industries that located along railways in the first place, and jams the port corridors — a spatial consequence. Skills shortage: the artisan pipeline undersupplies, so firms cluster where scarce skills already are, deepening over-concentration — see how one hindrance feeds another. And give over-concentration and pollution their full CAPS treatment, because they are named in the syllabus: over-concentration means the four core regions carry most of the country's industry, so congestion, land prices and pollution climb there while former industrial towns elsewhere decay — an argument FOR the decentralisation policies the next lesson covers; pollution means the Vaal Triangle's air and Durban South's refinery fumes fall on neighbouring communities — an environmental injustice with health costs — and carbon-intensive production now meets border taxes in green markets. Close with the balance: the hindrances are real, and yet the industrial base remains the continent's most diversified — evaluation means holding both. Mechanism, spatial consequence, balance: that ladder reaches the top level.
