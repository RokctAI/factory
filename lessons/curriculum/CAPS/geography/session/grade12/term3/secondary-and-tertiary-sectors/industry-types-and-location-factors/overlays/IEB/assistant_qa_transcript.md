**Mandy:** The orientation question worries me most. Fish and bread make sense, but the scenarios always feature some industry we never practised — a cement works, a juice plant. Is there a method that handles anything they throw at us?

**Tutor:** There is, and it is three questions asked in a fixed order. Question one: does processing make the material LIGHTER? Compare the tonnage going in with the tonnage coming out. Limestone into cement, whole fish into cans, logs into planks, chrome ore into ferrochrome — huge weight loss, so the plant chases the raw material, because trucking the light product beats trucking the heavy input every day of the year. Question two: does something SPOIL, and at which end? A raw material that dies fast — fresh fish, milk at the farm, cut sugar cane — drags the plant to the source. A product that dies fast — fresh bread, hot food, daily papers — drags the plant to the market. Question three: does processing make the product HARDER to move — heavier, bulkier, more breakable? A juice plant is a lovely trap: fruit is perishable, pulling toward the orchards, but bottled juice is mostly water and glass, pulling toward the cities — so concentrate plants sit at the fruit and bottling plants sit at the market, and writing that split shows real command. Whatever the scenario, run the three questions and let the answer fall out: weight loss or a spoiling input says raw material; weight gain, fragility or a spoiling output says market. Then write the transport-cost reasoning in full, because the reasoning is what carries the marks, not the label alone.

**Mandy:** Next: when a question says "discuss the factors influencing the location of industry X", do they really expect all eight factors? There is never time for that.

**Tutor:** They expect the opposite of all eight — they expect selection with justification, which is a higher skill than coverage. Pick the three or four factors that genuinely governed that industry, develop each against the industry's specific needs, and dismiss the rest in a single knowing sentence. Watch the method on two opposite cases. A ferrochrome smelter on the Mpumalanga coalfields: energy first — smelting eats electricity, so the plant lives beside generation; raw materials second — chrome ore railed in from the Bushveld deposits; then bulk water and flat land. Labour ranks low because the plant is capital-intensive, and being far from consumer markets barely matters because ferrochrome exports in bulk — say that dismissal out loud and you demonstrate mastery. Now a clothing factory: labour towers over everything — plentiful, affordable, semi-skilled machinists; then market access and road transport for fast fashion turnaround; energy is almost irrelevant because sewing machines sip power. The same list, ranked in reverse — and showing that the ranking flips between industries is exactly the understanding being paid for. Shape the answer as: dominant factors developed in depth, secondary factors in a line, irrelevant factors dismissed with a reason. Depth beats tour every time.

**Mandy:** Half the class writes that footloose industries "have no location factors" — and apparently that loses the mark. What is the correct version?

**Tutor:** The correct version is that footloose industries swap old anchors for new ones — they are never anchorless. The heavy, historical anchors were raw materials, bulk transport, big water and big power. For a software studio, an electronics assembler or a pharmaceutical research firm, inputs and outputs are light, valuable or purely digital, so those old anchors lose their grip and the firm could, in principle, sit anywhere. But look at where such firms actually sit: crowded together in a handful of metros. The new anchors are human. Skilled labour above all — the firm moves to where the engineers and developers already live. Connectivity — fibre, an international airport, power reliable enough for servers. Lifestyle and amenities that stop scarce professionals from emigrating. Universities pouring out graduates, and rival firms nearby to recruit from and trade with. That is why South Africa's tech and design work clusters in a few metropolitan nodes instead of scattering across the countryside. The sentence to write: footloose industry is free of raw-material and transport ties and therefore locates by skills, connectivity and quality of life — which concentrates it in cities rather than spreading it evenly. State it that way and the seeming contradiction becomes your strongest line.

**Mandy:** Last one: the hindering-factors answer always reads like a list of complaints from the news. How do I make mine geographical enough for the top level?

**Tutor:** Give every complaint a mechanism and a spatial consequence — that conversion is the whole trick. Push each hindrance one step past its headline. Power cuts: not "the lights go off", but continuous processes — smelting, food processing — cannot pause without ruining the batch, so unit costs climb and investors discount South African sites against rivals elsewhere: mechanism, then a consequence for where capital goes. Rail decay: freight migrates to road, punishing precisely the heavy industries that chose rail-side locations decades ago, and clogging the port corridors — a consequence you can point to on a map. Skills shortage: firms cluster where scarce skills already live, which deepens over-concentration — notice one hindrance feeding another; connections like that read as top-level analysis. Then give the two structural problems their full treatment, because the syllabus names them. Over-concentration: four core regions carry most of the country's industry, so congestion, land prices and dirty air climb there while old industrial towns elsewhere hollow out — which is the standing argument for the zone policies the next lesson examines. Pollution: the Vaal Triangle's smoke and south Durban's refinery fumes settle on the communities beside the fences — an environmental injustice with measurable health costs — and carbon-heavy production now pays at climate-conscious borders too. Close on the balance: the hindrances are real, and the industrial base is still the continent's most diversified. Mechanism, spatial consequence, balance — that ladder reaches the top level.
