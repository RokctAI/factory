# Part 1 — Expert

Between the mine and the mall stands the factory, and the examiners devote a full question line to it: the contribution of the secondary sector, the classification of industries into types you must identify from clues, the factors that decide where industry locates, and the factors that hold industrial development back. This is the conceptual toolkit that next unlocks the four industrial regions of South Africa — so every definition here is a key you will turn again. The worked thread throughout: why a bakery, a brewery, an iron smelter and a software firm all choose differently when they choose an address.

## Subtopic: What the Secondary Sector Contributes

The secondary sector — manufacturing, construction, electricity generation — contributes roughly a fifth of South Africa's GDP, with manufacturing alone around twelve to fourteen per cent, and employs about one in ten workers. Behind the percentages stand four arguments for why manufacturing matters more than its share, and the exam asks for them.

Argument one: value added. Manufacturing multiplies the worth of primary produce — iron ore into steel, steel into vehicles; grapes into wine; maize into meal and feed. A country that exports raw materials and imports finished goods sells cheap and buys dear; manufacturing keeps the difference at home. This is why beneficiation — processing minerals locally before export — is standing government policy.

Argument two: employment quality and multipliers. Factory jobs are formal, year-round and semi-skilled to skilled — and each factory job supports others: the components suppliers, the transport firms, the canteens. Manufacturing's employment multiplier runs deeper than any raw-material sector's.

Argument three: linkages. Manufacturing buys from the primary sector — its backward linkages — and feeds the tertiary sector with goods to sell, move and finance — its forward linkages. It is the bridge in the middle of the economy: strengthen it and both banks feel the pull.

Argument four: export diversification. Manufactured exports — vehicles above all, from the plants at Rosslyn, Kariega, East London and Durban — cushion the economy against swings in any single commodity price. The vehicle industry, deliberately nurtured by industrial policy, is the flagship: South African-built bakkies and sedans ship to Europe and beyond.

## Subtopic: The Types of Industries: A Field Guide

The exam gives a clue — a product, a location habit, a raw material — and asks the industry type. Learn the field guide in contrasting pairs.

Heavy versus light. Heavy industry handles bulky, weighty materials with massive plant: iron and steel at Vanderbijlpark, oil-from-coal at Sasolburg and Secunda, cement, heavy engineering. It needs flat land, rail links, huge water and power supplies, and it settles away from housing. Light industry makes small, high-value-to-weight goods — clothing, electronics, furniture, food products — in ordinary factory buildings, often in industrial parks beside main roads, comfortably near labour and customers.

Raw-material-orientated versus market-orientated: the pair the examiners love most, and the logic is transport cost. If the raw material is heavy, bulky or perishable and LOSES weight in processing, the factory goes to the raw material — it is cheaper to move the lighter product than the heavier input. Sugar mills sit in the KwaZulu-Natal cane belt because cane is heavy, rots fast, and yields a small fraction of its weight as sugar; sawmills sit at the plantations; smelters sit near ore and power. If instead the product is heavier, bulkier, more fragile or more perishable than its inputs — or the service must be fresh — the factory goes to the customers. Bakeries and breweries cluster in cities: flour and malt travel well, but bread stales and bottled beer is mostly heavy glass and water. The one-line test: compare what is more expensive to move, input or output, and the factory sits at the expensive end.

Footloose industry is tied to neither: inputs and products are light, high-value or digital — electronics assembly, software, pharmaceuticals research. Such firms choose locations for labour skills, lifestyle and connectivity, which is why they gather in office parks and tech corridors rather than beside mines. Ubiquitous industry is found wherever people are, because its market is everywhere and its product travels poorly — bakeries again, dairies, brickworks; every sizeable town has its own.

Bridge industry — the term CAPS attaches to break-of-bulk locations: industry that settles where cargo changes transport mode, processing imports where they land or exports before they ship. Durban's port-side industry — oil refining, sugar terminals, vehicle assembly with imported components — is the standing example; the harbour is the bridge, and industry camps at its ends.

## Subtopic: What Decides Where Industry Goes

Industrial location is a weighing of factors, and the CAPS list has eight weights. Learn each with its question and its South African face.

Raw materials: how far, how heavy, how perishable? Sugar mills in the cane belt; smelters near the ore. Labour: is there enough, at the right skill, at what cost? Clothing factories historically sought abundant semi-skilled labour; high-tech firms chase scarce skills in the metros. Water: heavy industry drinks rivers — iron, steel, paper and power stations need bulk water, which is why the Vaal River system anchors Gauteng's industry and why water transfer schemes exist to feed it. Energy: smelters and refineries locate with power — the aluminium smelters at Richards Bay stand beside big Eskom supply precisely because aluminium is congealed electricity. Transport: harbours, railways, highways — break-of-bulk points and corridors collect factories; the Durban–Gauteng corridor is the country's industrial spine. Markets: market-orientated industry wants the buying power of the PWV triangle — Gauteng's dense, wealthy market pulls consumer industries to it.

Then the two humans in the room. Political intervention: governments steer industry — apartheid-era decentralisation pushed factories to homeland borders for political ends; today's Special Economic Zones, Industrial Development Zones and the vehicle industry's support programmes pull investment to chosen points with tax breaks and infrastructure. Political stability, policy certainty and functioning municipalities weigh in the same scale. Competition and trade: firms consider who already occupies the market, tariff walls, and trade agreements — the African Continental Free Trade Area opens neighbouring markets, while cheap imports, especially in clothing and textiles, have gutted whole South African industries.

The exam's favourite trick is the scenario: given a proposed factory, weigh its location. Run the list — materials, labour, water, energy, transport, market, politics, competition — and say which two or three factors dominate for THAT industry. The weighing, not the list, earns the top marks.

## Subtopic: What Holds Industry Back

Factors hindering industrial development — a list question in form, an evaluation question in substance, and South Africa's list is concrete.

Energy insecurity leads: load-shedding halts production lines, spoils batches, and makes investors price South African risk. Infrastructure decay follows: deteriorating rail freight pushes cargo onto costlier roads; port delays at Durban and Cape Town rank among the world's worst in some years; water outages strike industrial towns. Skills shortages: the education pipeline undersupplies artisans and engineers, while emigration drains experience. Labour relations: strikes and adversarial bargaining raise costs and uncertainty. Crime and corruption: theft of cable and infrastructure, extortion at construction sites, and procurement corruption all tax industry invisibly. Global competition: cheap manufactured imports undercut local plants. Distance from world markets adds freight cost to everything South Africa ships. And the syllabus names two structural problems with their own logic: over-concentration — industry crowded into four core regions, so congestion, land prices and pollution rise there while the rest of the country starves for investment — and industrial pollution itself: the Vaal Triangle's air, the Durban South basin's refineries beside residential streets, carbon intensity that trading partners increasingly tax at their borders.

Balance before you close, because evaluation cuts both ways: against the hindrances stand real strengths — the continent's most diversified industrial base, deep capital markets, strong universities, and preferential access to African and global markets. The exam question "account for the slow growth of manufacturing" wants the hindrance list; the question "evaluate South Africa as an industrial location" wants both pans of the scale.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: the hindrance list from load-shedding to pollution, the meaning of over-concentration, and the balanced evaluation.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same toolkit through three doors you already use: the tuck-shop test for where businesses sit, a game of top trumps between factory types, and an honest list of what makes running a factory here hard.

## Subtopic: Why the Factory Matters: The Sandwich Rule

Here is the secondary sector in one sandwich. A farmer sells a tomato for a rand. A factory turns tomatoes into sauce and sells the bottle for twenty rand. A cafe puts sauce on a sandwich and charges forty. Same tomato, three prices — and the jumps between the prices are where wealth actually gets made. The middle jump, raw thing into made thing, is manufacturing, and the extra money it creates is called value added.

Now see why a country cares. Sell your tomatoes raw to the world and buy back imported sauce, and you have paid another country's workers to make your money for you. Make the sauce yourself, and the factory wages, the bottle-maker's contract, the delivery jobs and the profit all stay home. That is why South Africa pushes beneficiation — a big word meaning: stop exporting raw rocks and start exporting finished metal; do the value-adding here.

And a factory is a job machine with gears behind it. One factory floor supports the plastics firm that makes its bottles, the trucking company that moves them, the spaza shops where the workers spend wages. Economists call these knock-on jobs multipliers; you can just picture the gears — turn the factory gear and a dozen others turn with it. The flagship example to name in exams: the vehicle plants — Rosslyn, Kariega, East London, Durban — building bakkies for the world, with a whole country of parts suppliers geared to them.

Take this section's questions now: the sandwich rule, the sell-raw-buy-back trap, and the gears behind one factory.

## Subtopic: The Tuck-Shop Test: Where Does a Business Sit?

Every industry type in the syllabus answers one playground question: what is more of a pain to move — what the business USES, or what it SELLS? Call it the tuck-shop test.

The school tuck-shop sits inside the school — not at the bread factory — because its customers cannot travel and its product must be fresh at break time. It is market-orientated: parked next to the buyers. Bakeries and breweries pass the same test at city scale — flour travels happily; fresh bread and crates of heavy glass bottles do not, so the ovens and breweries sit in the city with the customers. Now flip it. A sugar mill sits among the cane fields of KwaZulu-Natal, because cane is a heavy, fast-rotting monster of a raw material that boils down to a small bag of sugar — you move the light sugar, never the heavy cane. That is raw-material-orientated: parked next to the ingredients. One question — which end is heavier, smellier, bulkier or more likely to spoil — and the factory always sits at the difficult end.

Some businesses shrug at the question. A software firm's raw material and product both travel through a cable, so it parks wherever the skilled people want to live — footloose, free of both ends. Some businesses are everywhere because everyone buys them fresh daily — every town has its bakery and its brickworks — ubiquitous, like school uniforms. And some businesses camp at the harbour gates: where ship-cargo becomes train-cargo, factories process goods right where they change hands — bridge industries, with Durban's port-side refineries and assembly plants the example to quote.

Size sorts the last pair. Heavy industry is the giant — steelworks, Sasol's fuel plants — needing flat land, rivers of water, its own railway and serious electricity, kept away from houses. Light industry is the neighbour — clothing, furniture, food — living happily in the industrial park by the highway. Meet any industry in an exam and ask: heavy or light, which end does it park at, or is it footloose, everywhere, or at the bridge? Five questions, every type identified.

## Subtopic: Choosing an Address, and What Makes It Hard

Imagine you must place a new factory, and treat it like choosing where to live — a checklist you weigh, not recite. Are the ingredients nearby, or cheap to bring in? Are there workers with the right hands and heads, at wages the business survives? Is there serious water and reliable electricity — remember, a smelter is basically electricity turned solid, which is why the aluminium works stand at Richards Bay beside major power supply. Can trucks, trains and ships reach you — is there a highway, a railway, a port? Are the customers close — because if you make bread or beer, the market IS the location? And then the human factors: is the government offering tax breaks and ready-built infrastructure in a special zone, begging you to come — and is anyone already selling what you plan to sell, or dumping cheaper imports into your market? For any real factory, two or three of these questions matter far more than the rest — say which, and why, and you are doing exactly what the exam calls "evaluating location factors".

Now the honest part: why is it hard to run a factory in South Africa? You could write this list from the news. The lights go off — load-shedding stops the line mid-shift. The trains limp — so freight crawls by expensive truck, and the ports jam. Skills are scarce — too few artisans and engineers come out of the pipeline. Strikes stop work. Cables get stolen. Cheap imports undercut the clothing factory before it opens. And the two big structural ones the syllabus names: over-concentration — nearly everything crowds into four regions, which chokes those regions with traffic, land costs and dirty air while the rest of the country waits for investment — and pollution itself, the Vaal Triangle's haze and the refinery fumes of Durban South, which sicken neighbours and now cost money at green-minded borders too.

End on the balance, because fairness earns marks: against all that stands Africa's most complete industrial machine — the factories, banks, universities and trade doors no neighbour matches. The exam wants both hands weighed: what holds industry back, and what still holds it up.
