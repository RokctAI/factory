# Part 1 — Expert

Agriculture contributes barely three per cent of South Africa's GDP and yet stands at the centre of the term three examination, because it carries three of the syllabus's favourite question machines: the small-scale versus large-scale contrast, the maize case study, and food security. This session works through all four stations: agriculture's real contribution to the economy, the two farming worlds that share one country, maize from planting region to export terminal, and the food security framework with its influencing factors and improvement strategies. Only about twelve per cent of South Africa's land is arable, and rainfall is unreliable across most of it — hold that constraint in mind throughout, because it explains half of everything that follows.

## Subtopic: What Agriculture Really Contributes

Start with the paradox you must be able to argue both ways. On the GDP scoreboard, agriculture is small: around two to three per cent. But the memorandum expects you to demolish the conclusion that agriculture is therefore unimportant, and the demolition has five hammers. One: food supply — the sector feeds sixty-plus million people, and no other contribution matters if this one fails. Two: employment — agriculture employs around five per cent of the formal workforce plus a large seasonal and informal workforce, concentrated in rural areas where alternative jobs barely exist; entire districts live off farm wages. Three: linkages — agriculture supplies the agro-processing industry, which is a substantial slice of manufacturing: sugar refineries, fruit canneries, wine cellars, maize mills, abattoirs. Count the value chain rather than the farm gate and agriculture's true weight in the economy multiplies. Four: exports — citrus, wine, table grapes, maize in good years, wool, macadamias; agricultural exports earn foreign exchange and South Africa is among the world's leading citrus exporters. Five: rural stability — farming anchors rural settlement, slows depopulation and keeps small towns alive; recall the settlement term's spiral, because farm jobs are its brake.

Products split into two markets. The home market takes the staples: maize — the national staple — wheat, sugar, vegetables, poultry, beef and dairy. The export market takes the high-value produce: citrus and deciduous fruit from the Western and Eastern Cape and Limpopo, wine from the Cape winelands, wool and mohair from the Karoo, sugar from KwaZulu-Natal, macadamias and avocados from the subtropical northeast.

Pause here and take this section's questions: the five hammers against the small-percentage argument, and the home-market against export-market product split.

## Subtopic: Two Farming Worlds: Small-Scale and Large-Scale

South African agriculture runs as two systems side by side, and the exam asks the contrast directly.

Large-scale commercial farming: roughly thirty to forty thousand commercial farming units working most of the country's farmland. Character: large farms, heavy capital — tractors, centre-pivot irrigation, precision technology — hired labour, scientific inputs, production overwhelmingly for the market and for export, access to finance, insurance and marketing chains. This system produces the great bulk of the country's marketed food.

Small-scale and subsistence farming: hundreds of thousands of households, concentrated in the former homeland areas, farming small plots — often on communal land under traditional tenure. Character: family labour, limited capital and inputs, production mainly for the household with occasional surplus sold locally, vulnerability to drought, and constrained access to credit — remember that communal land cannot easily be used as collateral for loans — plus distance from markets, extension services and irrigation.

The exam's favourite verbs here are compare and evaluate. Compare on five clean lines: farm size, capital and technology, labour source, market orientation, and land tenure. Evaluate with balance: large-scale farming delivers food security at national level and export earnings, but employs fewer people per hectare as it mechanises and concentrates ownership; small-scale farming supports millions of livelihoods and holds people on the land, but low yields leave households exposed. And the policy bridge earns the final mark: support for emerging farmers — finance, extension, secure tenure through land reform, market access — aims to move households from subsistence toward commercial production, joining the two worlds rather than choosing between them.

This section's questions are with you now: the five comparison lines, the balanced evaluation, and the collateral problem that chains small-scale farming to its plot size.

## Subtopic: The Maize Case Study: From Highveld Field to Export Terminal

Maize is South Africa's staple food, its largest field crop by area, and the prescribed case study. Learn it as three layers: where, why, and what it contributes.

Where: the Maize Triangle — the summer-rainfall grain country of the interior plateau, spanning the Free State, North West and Mpumalanga highveld, with the Free State the biggest producer. Picture the triangle's corners roughly at the western Free State grainlands, the North West around Lichtenburg, and the Mpumalanga highveld around Ermelo.

Why there — the favouring factors, each a mark: summer rainfall of roughly five hundred to eight hundred millimetres arriving in the growing season; hot summers with a long frost-free growing period; deep, fertile, well-drained soils across gently rolling plateau land that suits large machinery; and a developed support web — silos, railways, mills, and futures markets that price the crop. Add the human factors: generations of accumulated expertise and strong research producing drought-tolerant cultivars.

What hinders — and every favouring list has its shadow: rainfall variability and recurring drought, because the triangle's rain is summer thunderstorm rain that some years simply fails; hail and early frost; degraded and erosion-prone soils where practices are poor; rising input costs — diesel, fertiliser, seed — squeezing margins; and climate change shifting rainfall patterns westward producers can neither predict nor follow.

Contribution: maize feeds the nation twice — white maize as mealie meal, the staple of household diets, and yellow maize as the feed base of the poultry, egg, dairy and beef industries, so every chicken portion carries maize inside its price. Maize supports milling, animal feeds and food processing, sustains the grain-belt towns and their silos, and in surplus years earns export income for the region — South Africa is normally the subcontinent's maize barn, exporting to neighbouring states.

Take the questions for this section now: the triangle's provinces, the favouring factors with their shadows, and maize's double role as human staple and animal feed.

## Subtopic: Food Security: The Framework, the Factors, the Fixes

Food security exists, in the standard definition, when all people, at all times, have physical and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food for an active and healthy life. Every clause is load-bearing: ALL people — not the national average; AT ALL times — not only after good harvests; PHYSICAL AND ECONOMIC access — the food must exist nearby AND be affordable; SUFFICIENT, SAFE, NUTRITIOUS — enough calories, safely stored, adequately varied.

That definition powers South Africa's sharpest paradox, and you must be able to state it: the country is food secure at NATIONAL level — it normally produces enough staples and can import shortfalls — yet millions of HOUSEHOLDS are food insecure, because unemployment and poverty put adequate food beyond their means. National sufficiency, household hunger: production is not the binding problem; access is.

Factors influencing food security, grouped in threes for recall. Environmental: drought and flood cycles, climate change, soil degradation, water scarcity — remember the twelve per cent arable constraint. Economic: unemployment and poverty, food price inflation, input costs, the exchange rate that prices imported wheat and fuel. Social and political: population growth, HIV/AIDS weakening farming households, land reform uncertainty affecting investment, and conflict or instability disrupting supply chains elsewhere in Africa.

Improvement strategies, matched to the failures they repair: support small-scale and emerging farmers — extension services, finance, irrigation, secure tenure — so households grow more of their own food; school feeding schemes and social grants, which attack economic access directly; food gardens in homes, schools and clinics; reducing post-harvest losses through storage and transport; research into drought-tolerant cultivars; and at national scale, maintaining the commercial sector's productivity while diversifying water sources. A case study sentence to hold ready: South Africa's National School Nutrition Programme feeds around nine million learners on school days — an access-side intervention that also keeps children in school.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: the definition clause by clause, the national-versus-household paradox, the factor triads, and strategies matched to the failures they fix.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same content again, carried by three pictures: a plate of pap, two farmers who share a fence line but not a world, and a question about why hunger exists in a country with full silos.

## Subtopic: The Country That Runs on Pap

Start at supper. Pap, chicken, some gravy, maybe cabbage. Now trace the plate backwards. The pap was mealie meal, which was white maize, which grew on the highveld. The chicken was raised on feed made mostly from yellow maize — so there is maize on your plate twice, once visible and once hiding inside the chicken. Do that trace for the whole country and you have discovered why maize matters more than any percentage suggests: it is the floor under the national diet.

Where does it all grow? Picture the map of South Africa and put your finger on the middle of the country, the high flat interior. Sweep it across the Free State, into North West, across to the Mpumalanga highveld — that sweep is the Maize Triangle, the country's grain basket. Why exactly there? Because maize is a summer-rain plant with clear demands, and that plateau meets them all: rain in the growing season — roughly five hundred to eight hundred millimetres of it; long hot frost-free summers; deep soils on land flat enough for giant machines; and, crucially, the whole support system waiting nearby — the tall silos beside the railway line, the mills, the co-ops. A crop this size needs infrastructure the way a taxi rank needs roads.

But summer rain on the highveld is thunderstorm rain, and thunderstorms are gamblers. Some years they pay out; some years they simply do not come, and the whole triangle waits under a hot empty sky. Drought is the standing threat, with hail, frost, expensive diesel and fertiliser, and a slowly shifting climate behind it. Farming maize is planting money in the ground every October and betting on the clouds.

And the stakes are national: in a good year South Africa fills its own bowls and sells maize to its neighbours; in a drought year prices climb in every shop in the country, and the poorest families feel it first, because pap is the food of tight budgets.

Take this section's questions now: the double maize on your plate, the triangle traced by your finger, the demands the highveld meets, and the gamble of the thunderstorms.

## Subtopic: Two Farmers, One Fence

Picture two farms that touch at a fence. On one side: three thousand hectares under maize, a shed of tractors and a centre-pivot machine walking in slow circles, hired workers, bank finance, a contract with the silo company, and a farmer checking crop prices on a phone. On the other side: two hectares of communal land, a family growing maize and vegetables mostly for their own pot, hand tools and maybe a borrowed tractor for one day of ploughing, no title deed, no loan — because the bank will not lend against land the family cannot legally mortgage — and a surplus, in a good season, sold at the local market.

That fence line runs through the entire South African farming economy, and the exam calls it large-scale commercial versus small-scale subsistence farming. Compare them on five lines you can count on your fingers: size — thousands of hectares against a few; capital — machines and irrigation against hand tools; labour — hired workers against the family; market — selling to the nation and the world against feeding the household; and tenure — owned title-deed land against communal land. Five fingers, five comparison marks.

Now be fair to both sides, because the exam rewards fairness. The big farm delivers: it is why supermarkets are stocked, why exports flow, why the country as a whole does not run short. But its machines mean it employs fewer and fewer hands per hectare, and ownership sits with a few. The small farm sustains: it holds millions of families, gives rural life an anchor, and keeps the village populated. But its yields are low and one failed season lands directly on the family's plate. Neither side is the villain; they are two systems with different jobs.

And the way forward is the bridge, not the fence: help small farmers become bigger farmers — training and extension officers, affordable finance, secure land rights so the plot can back a loan, irrigation, and a route to real markets. Every one of those supports removes one difference between the two sides of the fence.

Your questions for this section are up: the five-finger comparison, the fair evaluation of both sides, and the bridge that policy tries to build.

## Subtopic: Full Silos, Empty Plates

Here is the question this whole topic funnels into, and it sounds like a riddle: how can a country's silos be full while children in it go hungry? Answer the riddle and you have mastered food security.

First the definition, taken slowly, because every word earns marks: food security means ALL people, at ALL times, can physically GET and actually AFFORD enough safe, nutritious food for a healthy life. Read it like a checklist. All people — an average is not good enough; the country can be fine while a household is not. At all times — secure in drought years, not just good ones. Get and afford — the shop can be full, but if the money is not there, the family is food insecure in front of stocked shelves. Enough, safe, nutritious — filling up on cheap starch alone fails the nutritious test.

Now the riddle resolves: South Africa is food secure as a COUNTRY — the commercial farms and imports keep national supply adequate — but millions of HOUSEHOLDS are food insecure, because more than thirty per cent unemployment means the food is there and the money is not. The problem is not the farms; it is the wallets. Production security at the top, access insecurity at the bottom. Write those two sentences in any food security answer and you have its spine.

What pushes households toward hunger? Group the causes in threes, like packing three bags: the nature bag — drought, floods, degraded soil, a changing climate; the money bag — unemployment, rising food prices, expensive transport and inputs; the people bag — growing populations to feed, illness weakening working families, insecure land. Three bags, easy to unpack in an exam.

And the fixes each aim at a specific bag. School feeding — the National School Nutrition Programme reaches around nine million learners — puts food directly onto the access problem, as do social grants. Food gardens at homes, schools and clinics shorten the distance between soil and plate. Helping small farmers — seeds, water, advice, secure land — grows the household's own supply. Better storage and transport stop food dying between farm and shop. Drought-tolerant seed answers the nature bag. Match fix to bag and your strategy paragraph writes itself.

Here come the final questions of the lesson: the riddle and its resolution, the checklist definition, the three bags, and the fixes aimed at each. Answer them, and you can walk into the exam owning one of Paper 1's most reliably asked topics.
