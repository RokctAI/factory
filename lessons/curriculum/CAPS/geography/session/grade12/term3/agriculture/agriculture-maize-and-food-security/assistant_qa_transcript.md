**Mandy:** Questions that come up a lot with this topic! First: for the maize case study, how much detail do the examiners actually want? I never know whether to write the rainfall numbers or just say "enough rain".

**Tutor:** Write the numbers, because specificity is exactly what separates a case study answer from a general answer — the case study is prescribed so that examiners can demand detail. Build a fact kit of about eight items and rehearse it until it recites itself: the Maize Triangle across the Free State, North West and Mpumalanga highveld, with the Free State the largest producer; summer rainfall of roughly five hundred to eight hundred millimetres in the growing season; hot, long, frost-free summers; deep fertile soils on gently rolling plateau land suited to machinery; the silo-rail-mill support web; white maize as the human staple, yellow as animal feed; drought as the chief hindering factor, with hail, frost and rising input costs behind it; and export to neighbouring countries in surplus years. Now the deployment rule: match the fact to the question's verb. "Name the area" wants the triangle and provinces. "Account for the location" wants the favouring factors — and each factor earns its mark only when you connect it to maize's needs: not "summer rainfall" alone, but "summer rainfall coincides with the growing season, giving the crop water when it grows". "Discuss economic contribution" wants the double role, the processing chain and exports. A number like five hundred to eight hundred millimetres, placed correctly, signals to the marker in three words that you studied the prescribed case and not just the idea of maize.

**Mandy:** Next: compare questions on small-scale and large-scale farming. My comparisons come out as two separate lists and I lose marks. What is the fix?

**Tutor:** The fix is to compare inside every sentence instead of between paragraphs, and the tool is the word "whereas". A list answer says: commercial farms are large, mechanised and market-oriented, and then, small farms are small and use family labour. The marker must assemble the comparison for you, and memoranda instruct them not to. A comparison answer runs one line per criterion, both systems inside the same sentence: commercial farms cover thousands of hectares, WHEREAS small-scale plots are often under five; commercial farming deploys tractors, irrigation and scientific inputs, WHEREAS small-scale farming relies on hand tools and family labour; commercial output goes to national and export markets, WHEREAS small-scale output mostly feeds the household; commercial land is held under title deed and can secure loans, WHEREAS communal tenure blocks access to credit. Four or five whereas-sentences, each anchored to a named criterion — size, capital, labour, market, tenure — is a full-mark comparison. And when the verb upgrades from compare to evaluate, add a judgement paragraph: which system achieves which goal — national food supply and exports on one side, livelihoods and rural stability on the other — and the bridge policy that supports emerging farmers across the gap. Structure first; the content you already have.

**Mandy:** Food security: if the exam gives me a source about hunger in a shack settlement next to a supermarket, what is the analytical point they are fishing for?

**Tutor:** They are fishing for the access argument, and the source is built to make it vivid. A supermarket full of food beside hungry households is the paradox of South African food security in one image: availability without affordability. Walk the definition through the scene clause by clause. Is food physically present? Yes — metres away, shelves stocked, so PHYSICAL access exists and national production is not the problem. Can the households buy enough of it? No — unemployment and poverty mean ECONOMIC access fails, and the definition requires both. So the settlement is food insecure in the middle of food abundance, which proves the binding constraint is income, not agriculture. Then show the time clause: a grant-dependent household may eat adequately at month start and poorly at month end — failing "at ALL times". And the nutrition clause: tight budgets buy cheap calories — starch heavy, protein and vegetable light — so even fed households can fail the "nutritious" test; that is hidden hunger. Close with strategies matched to the diagnosis: because the failure is access, the first-line fixes are income-side — grants, school feeding, food gardens, jobs — rather than production-side. That clause-by-clause walk, ending in matched strategies, is precisely the analytical structure the eight-mark paragraph is printed to reward.

**Mandy:** Last one: essays ask how climate change threatens South African food security. How do I keep that answer geographical instead of just alarming?

**Tutor:** Keep it geographical by keeping every claim attached to a place, a mechanism and a consequence — three links, no loose sentences. Start with the exposure: South Africa is a dry country — only about twelve per cent of its land is arable, and most cropping depends on rain, not irrigation — so any shift in rainfall hits a system with no slack. Then run two or three mechanism chains. Chain one: rising temperatures and more variable summer rainfall over the interior plateau threaten the Maize Triangle — heat stress at pollination cuts yields, and failed thunderstorm seasons bring drought years like the mid-decade droughts; because maize is both the staple and the feed base, a poor harvest raises the price of pap AND chicken, and poor households, who spend the largest share of income on food, absorb the blow first. Chain two: the Western Cape's winter-rainfall region, home of the fruit and wine export industries, faces drying trends — remember the Cape Town water crisis — threatening export earnings and farm jobs. Chain three, if space allows: droughts shrink grazing, forcing stock reductions in the Karoo and communal rangelands, striking small farmers with no insurance hardest. Then the response paragraph: adaptation — drought-tolerant cultivars, conservation agriculture, efficient irrigation, early-warning systems, and diversified livelihoods — each named as a response to a chain you described. Place, mechanism, consequence, response: that frame turns alarm into analysis, and analysis is where the marks are printed.
