# Part 1 — Expert

South African industry crowds into four core regions, and the examination prescribes two of them for detailed study: the Gauteng region — historically the Pretoria–Witwatersrand–Vereeniging or PWV — and the Port Elizabeth–Uitenhage region on Algoa Bay, today Gqeberha and Kariega. This session maps all four regions, then works the two prescribed case studies through the standard exam grid — location factors, main activities, successes and hindrances, economic and social impacts — and closes with the policies built to spread industry beyond the four: Spatial Development Initiatives and their corridors, and Industrial Development Zones inside today's Special Economic Zone framework, with Coega the flagship example.

## Subtopic: The Four Core Regions and the Prescribed Two

Name the four with their anchors. First, Gauteng — the PWV region: the inland giant stretching from Pretoria through the Witwatersrand to Vereeniging and the Vaal, contributing the largest share of the country's manufacturing by far. Second, the Durban–Pinetown region in KwaZulu-Natal: port-based industry around the subcontinent's busiest harbour. Third, the Port Elizabeth–Uitenhage region on Algoa Bay: the motor industry's coastal home. Fourth, the South-Western Cape around Cape Town: diversified consumer industries — food, wine, clothing, printing — serving a wealthy regional market with a port and tourism behind it.

Notice the pattern before the detail: one region is inland, born of minerals; three are coastal, born of ports. That single sentence — minerals made Gauteng, harbours made the rest — organises every comparison question. Notice also what the crowding means: these four regions hold the great bulk of South African manufacturing, which is precisely the over-concentration problem the previous lesson named, and the reason the SDI and IDZ policies at the end of this session exist.

The exam grid for each prescribed region has four rows, and you should hear every case study question as one of these rows: WHY there — the location factors; WHAT happens there — the main industrial activities; HOW it fares — factors favouring success and factors hindering; and WHO feels it — economic and social impacts, positive and negative. Four rows, two regions: that matrix is the whole topic.

Pause here and take this section's questions: the four regions with their anchors, the minerals-versus-harbours sentence, and the four rows of the case study grid.

## Subtopic: Gauteng — the PWV: South Africa's Industrial Heart

Row one, why there. Gauteng's location factors read like the birth certificate of the economy: gold on the Witwatersrand drew capital, labour and railways to the highveld; coal from the nearby Mpumalanga and Vaal fields fed cheap power; water, the region's weakness, was engineered in — the Vaal Dam and later the Lesotho Highlands scheme pipe it to the plateau; the rail network built for the mines made the region the country's transport hub, now crowned by OR Tambo's air freight; the mines accumulated skilled labour and engineering expertise; and the region's own population became the country's richest market, so industry serving that market compounds itself. Note the irony examiners enjoy: the original locating factor — gold — is nearly finished, but the infrastructure, skills and market it assembled keep the region dominant. Industrial regions outlive their reasons.

Row two, what happens there. Iron and steel at Vanderbijlpark and Vereeniging with the Vaal Triangle's heavy engineering; fuel and chemicals at Sasolburg on the region's edge; vehicle assembly at Rosslyn near Pretoria; machinery, metal products, food processing, printing and the continent's densest concentration of finance and corporate head offices stacked on top — Gauteng is simultaneously the country's factory and its office.

Row three, how it fares. Favouring success: the market, the skills, the transport hub, the agglomeration economies of thousands of linked firms. Hindering: no port — everything imported or exported pays the overland leg to Durban; water must be pumped uphill from another country's mountains; load-shedding and decaying rail bite hardest where industry is thickest; congestion, land costs and the Vaal Triangle's polluted air complete the list.

Row four, who feels it. Economic impacts: the region generates a third or more of national GDP, pays the largest share of taxes, and draws migrants from the whole subcontinent. Social: jobs and opportunity beside stark inequality — Alexandra pressed against Sandton is the standard image; informal settlements grow faster than services; air quality in the industrial south harms the poorest communities most.

This section's questions are with you now: the six location factors, the activities from steel to head offices, the no-port problem, and the Sandton–Alexandra contrast.

## Subtopic: Port Elizabeth–Uitenhage: The Motor Town on Algoa Bay

Row one, why there. The natural harbour on Algoa Bay made the city a trade gateway; rail links run inland to the interior; land was cheap and flat; the local market was modest — so the region specialised in serving national and export markets through its port; wool from the Karoo hinterland fed early processing; and in the twentieth century, deliberate investment by global vehicle makers — Ford and General Motors historically in Port Elizabeth, Volkswagen at Uitenhage — planted the motor industry that defines the region. Add the modern deep-water port at Ngqura, and the location story is complete.

Row two, what happens there. Vehicle assembly and components above all: Volkswagen's Kariega plant is among the largest car factories in Africa, exporting hatchbacks to Europe; Isuzu builds bakkies; a dense belt of components firms — catalytic converters are a regional speciality, tying this region to the platinum lesson — supplies plants here and worldwide. Around the motor core: tyres, textiles, food processing, and the port logistics that move it all.

Row three, how it fares. Favouring: deep expertise in automotive manufacture, export contracts under the government's automotive support programmes, two harbours including Ngqura's deep water, and room to grow at Coega next door. Hindering: dependence on one industry — when global car demand dips or a model contract ends, the whole region shudders; distance from the Gauteng market; water insecurity — Nelson Mandela Bay's recent droughts brought the taps near to failure; load-shedding on assembly lines; and unemployment far above national averages even in good years.

Row four, who feels it. Economic: the motor cluster earns export billions and anchors the Eastern Cape's formal economy. Social: relatively well-paid unionised jobs where they exist, but the surrounding province is the country's poorest — the gap between the plant inside the fence and the township outside it is the region's social geography in one image.

Take the questions for this section now: the harbour-rail-investment location chain, the Volkswagen and Isuzu anchors with the catalytic converter link, the one-industry risk, and the plant-and-township contrast.

## Subtopic: Spreading the Map: SDIs, Corridors, IDZs and SEZs

Four regions holding most of the industry is a problem with a policy history, and CAPS asks for two instruments by name.

Spatial Development Initiatives — SDIs. The idea: pick a corridor with unused economic potential, concentrate government and private investment along it — roads, rail, ports, pipelines — and let the upgraded artery pull industry, agriculture and tourism into places apartheid planning left behind. The flagship: the Maputo Development Corridor, upgrading the road and rail route from Gauteng through Mpumalanga to Mozambique's harbour at Maputo — giving the inland core a second, shorter route to a port and firing industry along the line. Others to name: the Lubombo SDI toward northern KwaZulu-Natal and the Wild Coast SDI in the Eastern Cape, both leaning on tourism and agriculture; the West Coast investment zone with its minerals and fishing.

Industrial Development Zones — IDZs, now folded into the broader Special Economic Zones — SEZs — framework. The idea: fence a serviced industrial estate next to a port or airport, offer purpose-built infrastructure, tax incentives, duty-free import of inputs for export production, and one-stop government services, and use it to attract export manufacturers who would otherwise not come. The named examples: Coega beside the deep-water port of Ngqura — the largest, hosting vehicle plants, agro-processing and energy projects; East London — automotive-centred, serving the Mercedes-Benz plant; Richards Bay — minerals and metals beside the bulk port; Dube TradePort at King Shaka airport — high-value, air-freighted goods; and Saldanha Bay for oil, gas and marine engineering. Since the SEZ Act, zones extend inland too, but the exam's IDZ examples remain the coastal four plus Dube.

Evaluate, because the exam will ask: successes — Coega and East London have drawn real factories and thousands of jobs, and the Maputo corridor genuinely moves freight; limits — zones cost heavily per job created, some stand half-filled, and incentives cannot outweigh load-shedding, port delays and distance when investors decide. The balanced verdict: SDIs and IDZs redirect some growth and are the right shape of policy, but they redistribute industry at the margin — they have not yet broken the four-region pattern.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: the SDI corridor logic with Maputo as flagship, the IDZ recipe with its five named zones, and the balanced verdict.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole topic as a story you can retell: four kitchens, one rich house on the hill, one town that builds your family's car, and the government's plan to get the other rooms cooking.

## Subtopic: Four Kitchens Cooking for One Country

Think of South Africa as a big house where nearly all the cooking happens in just four kitchens. Kitchen one is up on the highveld: Gauteng — the old PWV, for Pretoria, Witwatersrand, Vereeniging. Kitchen two is Durban–Pinetown, wrapped around the busiest harbour in the subcontinent. Kitchen three is Port Elizabeth–Uitenhage on Algoa Bay — today's Gqeberha and Kariega — where the cars come from. Kitchen four is the South-Western Cape around Cape Town, cooking food, wine, clothes and books for its own comfortable market.

Now the one-sentence secret of the whole map: minerals made the inland kitchen; harbours made the coastal three. Gauteng exists because gold pulled everything up onto the plateau; the other three exist because ships must dock somewhere, and factories camp where the cargo lands. Say that sentence in any comparison question and you have drawn the map in words.

Why does it matter that there are only four? Because a house where four rooms do all the cooking has crowded, overheated kitchens — traffic, expensive land, dirty air — while the other rooms sit cold, with people leaving them to find work. That is over-concentration, the problem from last lesson, now wearing its map. Hold it in mind, because the end of this story is the government trying to light stoves in the cold rooms.

And keep the examiner's grid in your pocket, four questions per kitchen: why THERE? what do they COOK? what helps and what HURTS? and who EATS — who gains and who pays? Every case study question you will ever see is one of those four in formal clothes.

Take this section's questions now: the four kitchens with their anchors, the minerals-and-harbours secret, and the four pocket questions.

## Subtopic: The Kitchen With No Tap and No Door

Gauteng is the strangest industrial giant on earth, and its strangeness is the exam's favourite content. It has no port — a factory in Kempton Park sends its exports six hundred kilometres downhill to Durban before they touch a ship. It has no big river — its water is pumped up from the Vaal Dam and even from the mountains of Lesotho, a kitchen with no tap of its own, plumbed from the neighbour's house. By the textbook rules of location, nothing this size should stand there. So why is a third of the economy up on that plateau?

Because of what the gold left behind. Gold called everyone up the hill a century ago — capital, workers, railways, power stations burning nearby coal. And once all of that existed, it became its own reason. The mines faded, but the railways still meet there, the skills still live there, the biggest, richest crowd of customers in Africa still shops there, and thousands of factories feed each other parts and services. The giant outlived the gold that made it — write that sentence in an essay and the marker knows you understand.

What does the kitchen cook? The heavy stuff: steel at Vanderbijlpark, fuel and chemicals at Sasolburg, machines and metal, cars at Rosslyn — plus, stacked above the factories, the banks and head offices, because Gauteng is the factory and the office at once.

What hurts it? Exactly its missing pieces and its crowding: every export pays the long haul to the coast, the borrowed water must keep flowing, load-shedding bites hardest where the most machines stand, the roads jam, and the Vaal Triangle's air sits heavy over the poorest suburbs. Who eats? The region pays the nation's biggest tax bill and offers its most jobs — and yet Sandton's towers look across a highway at Alexandra's shacks. Wealth and want, one window apart: that image is the social-impact answer.

Your questions for this section are up: the no-tap, no-door strangeness, the outliving-the-gold explanation, the menu, and the window between Sandton and Alexandra.

## Subtopic: The Car Town, and Lighting Stoves in the Cold Rooms

Now the second prescribed kitchen, the friendly-sized one: Port Elizabeth–Uitenhage. Its story is easy to retell. A good natural bay gave it a harbour; railways ran inland to fetch wool and hides; and then, in the age of the motor car, the global car makers chose it — Volkswagen settled at Uitenhage, today's Kariega, and others followed into Gqeberha. The region became the family car town: the VW plant is among Africa's biggest, shipping hatchbacks to Europe; Isuzu builds bakkies; and around them a belt of parts factories — including the catalytic converters that carry Rustenburg's platinum into the world's exhaust pipes. Port, rail, chosen by car makers: that is the whole location story in six words.

Its weakness is the flip side of its fame: one industry. When the world stops buying cars for a year, or a model contract ends, the whole town holds its breath. Add drought scares in the taps, load-shedding on the assembly line, and a province around it that is the country's poorest — well-paid jobs inside the plant fence, deep unemployment outside it. Strength and fragility in one postcode: say both and you have evaluated.

Last chapter: the government's plan for the cold rooms. Two tools, and both are easy to picture. Tool one, the SDI — Spatial Development Initiative: choose a promising route and upgrade the whole road-and-rail artery along it so that businesses sprout along the line, the way shops sprout along a new taxi route. The flagship is the Maputo Corridor: a fast route from Gauteng through Mpumalanga to Maputo's harbour — giving the port-less giant a second door to the sea and waking up the towns along the way. Tool two, the IDZ — Industrial Development Zone, now part of the Special Economic Zones family: build a ready-made industrial estate at a port, plug in the electricity, roads and cranes, sweeten it with tax breaks, and invite export factories to move straight in — a fully furnished flat for industry. Names to know: Coega beside the deep port of Ngqura, next door to the car town; East London, serving the Mercedes plant; Richards Bay for metals; Dube TradePort at the Durban airport for goods that fly.

Has it worked? Be honest in one breath: partly. Coega and East London have real factories and real jobs, and the Maputo road carries real freight — but the zones are expensive, some stand half-empty, and no tax break can beat load-shedding and jammed ports in an investor's spreadsheet. The four kitchens still do most of the cooking; the new stoves are lit, but small. Here come the final questions of the lesson: the car town's six-word story, the one-industry risk, the two tools with their flagship names, and the honest verdict. Retell these stories once tonight — four kitchens, the giant that outlived its gold, the car town, the new stoves — and the industrial regions question becomes the easiest essay on the paper.
