**Mandy:** Questions that come up a lot with this topic! First: the case study essays on Gauteng and PE–Uitenhage feel enormous. Is there one structure that fits both, so I only have to learn the skeleton once?

**Tutor:** There is, and it is the four-row grid — learn it once, fill it twice. Row one, location factors: WHY the region is where it is. For Gauteng: gold, nearby coal, engineered water, the converging rail network, accumulated skills, and the country's richest market. For PE–Uitenhage: the natural harbour on Algoa Bay, rail to the interior, wool from the Karoo hinterland, and the decisive investments by global vehicle makers. Row two, main activities: WHAT is made. Gauteng: steel at Vanderbijlpark, chemicals at Sasolburg, vehicles at Rosslyn, machinery, plus finance stacked on top. PE–Uitenhage: vehicle assembly at Volkswagen Kariega and Isuzu, the components belt with its catalytic converters, tyres, and port logistics. Row three, success and hindrance: Gauteng thrives on market, skills and agglomeration, and strains against no port, borrowed water, load-shedding and congestion; PE–Uitenhage thrives on automotive expertise, export programmes and two harbours, and strains under one-industry dependence, drought and provincial poverty. Row four, impacts: economic contribution beside social inequality — Sandton against Alexandra; the plant fence against the township. Practise by drawing the empty grid and filling all eight cells from memory in ten minutes. In the exam, the question names one row or asks for the lot — either way, you are only ever retrieving cells you have already filled.

**Mandy:** Next: I keep writing that Gauteng is an industrial giant, but the essay asks me to EXPLAIN its dominance when it has no port and no water. How do I turn that contradiction into marks?

**Tutor:** The contradiction IS the marks — examiners set Gauteng precisely because it breaks the textbook rules, and they want you to name the mechanism that resolves the paradox. Historical initiation first: gold on the Witwatersrand overrode every disadvantage — the deposit could not be moved, so capital, labour, railways and coal-fired power all came to IT, uphill, port or no port. Then the resolving mechanism, with its proper name: agglomeration and cumulative causation. Once the mines had assembled railways, power, skills and a huge wage-earning population, each new factory located there because everything it needed was already present — suppliers, customers, workers, finance — and every arrival made the region still more attractive to the next. Growth caused growth. The disadvantages, meanwhile, were engineered away: the Vaal Dam and Lesotho Highlands scheme piped in the missing water, and the rail network — now plus OR Tambo's air freight — became a substitute for the missing port, with the Maputo Corridor lately shortening the route to the sea. Land the essay on the memorable sentence: the gold is nearly gone, but the region it assembled remains — industrial regions outlive their reasons. Then add one critical footnote for the top level: the port-less, water-borrowed position is not fully solved — it is a standing cost that load-shedding and rail decay make heavier, which is why the region's dominance is powerful but not unconditional.

**Mandy:** SDIs and IDZs blur together in my head — both are government plans for industry. What is the cleanest way to keep them separate and remember the examples?

**Tutor:** Separate them by their geometry: an SDI is a LINE; an IDZ is a POINT. The Spatial Development Initiative takes a whole corridor — hundreds of kilometres of road and rail between an inland economy and a port — and upgrades the entire artery so that farms, factories and tourism sprout all along it. Flagship: the Maputo Development Corridor, Gauteng through Mpumalanga to Maputo's harbour, with the Lubombo and Wild Coast SDIs as supporting examples leaning on tourism and agriculture. The Industrial Development Zone takes a single fenced site beside one port or airport and furnishes it completely — serviced land, bulk infrastructure, tax incentives, duty-free inputs for exporters, one-stop approvals. Points to memorise as a coastal tour, north to south then west: Richards Bay for minerals and metals; Dube TradePort at King Shaka airport for goods that fly; East London serving the Mercedes plant; Coega beside the deep-water port of Ngqura — the largest; and Saldanha Bay for oil, gas and marine engineering. Umbrella term: since the SEZ Act, IDZs sit inside the broader Special Economic Zones family. Memory anchors: line versus point, Maputo versus Coega. And keep one linking sentence ready, because integration impresses: both tools attack the same disease — over-concentration in the four core regions — by pulling investment to the cold spots on the map, the corridor doing it along a route, the zone doing it at a gate.

**Mandy:** Last one: if the exam asks whether these policies have SUCCEEDED, what does a top-level answer actually conclude?

**Tutor:** A top-level answer refuses both cheers and despair, and it measures success against the policy's own goal — spreading industry beyond the four regions — using evidence on both sides. Evidence for: Coega is no longer an empty fence — it hosts vehicle assembly, agro-processing and energy projects with thousands of jobs, anchored by Ngqura's deep-water port; East London's zone genuinely serves the Mercedes export plant; Dube TradePort has built an air-freight niche; and the Maputo Corridor moves real freight, giving Gauteng its shorter second route to the sea and stimulating towns along the N4. Evidence against: the cost per job in the zones is high; several stand partly filled years after launch; incentives at the gate cannot cancel national problems — load-shedding, port delays, rail decay — that sit at the top of every investor's spreadsheet; and the decisive test, the map itself, still shows the four core regions holding the overwhelming share of manufacturing. So conclude with a measured verdict in two sentences: the policies have created genuine islands of new industry and useful corridors, proving the approach can work at specific points; but they have redistributed growth at the margin rather than redrawn the map, because zone-level incentives cannot outweigh economy-wide constraints. If the question invites recommendations, add the logical close: the zones will only fulfil their promise when the national basics — power, ports, rail — are fixed, since no fully furnished flat attracts tenants in a building where the lifts do not work.
