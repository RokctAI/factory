**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: I wrote that rejuvenation happens when a river gets more water, and the memo marked it wrong. But surely more water means more energy?

**Tutor:** Your physics is half right and your definition is wrong, which is why the mark slipped away. More discharge does add energy, and the syllabus does allow increased discharge — after capture, for example — as a minor trigger. But rejuvenation as the DBE defines it is about the river's relationship with BASE LEVEL, the lowest level it can erode toward, ultimately the sea. The two headline causes both change that relationship. Either the sea falls — a eustatic drop, as in an ice age, when water is locked into ice sheets worldwide — or the land rises through tectonic uplift, as the southern African plateau has done repeatedly. In both cases the river suddenly stands higher above its finish line: its gradient steepens, its velocity climbs, and vertical erosion switches back on. So structure the answer this way: define base level, state that rejuvenation follows a fall in base level or uplift of the land, name the eustatic and tectonic terms for those, and only then add increased discharge as a supporting cause. Lead with the base-level story and the definition marks are safe; lead with rainfall and you have described a flood, not rejuvenation.

**Mandy:** Next: terraces and the valley-in-a-valley — are those two features or one? The diagram looks like the same thing twice.

**Tutor:** Sharp eye — they are one event seen from two angles, and understanding that saves you from double-describing. The event: a river with a broad floodplain gets rejuvenated and cuts a new, steep-sided trench down through its own old valley floor. Look at the result in cross-section, end-on, and you see the SHAPE: a young V-shaped inner valley nested inside the old broad valley — that shape is called valley-in-a-valley. Now look at the leftovers flanking the trench: the strips of the old floodplain stranded high and dry on both sides — those flat benches are the terraces. So valley-in-a-valley names the overall nested form; terraces name the abandoned floodplain surfaces that form its shoulders. In an exam diagram, draw the broad old valley, cut the sharp inner trench, and label the paired benches as terraces — one drawing earns both labels. Then add the depth question examiners like: a second rejuvenation cuts a second, lower trench, leaving a second pair of terraces, so the staircase reads as history — the higher the terrace, the older the floodplain it once was. Two labels, one story, told from two viewpoints.

**Mandy:** Here is one from the back: on the map question, how can I tell river capture happened if nobody watched it happen? It feels like guessing.

**Tutor:** No guessing — capture leaves three witnesses on the map, and you convict on their combined testimony. Witness one: the elbow of capture. Natural rivers bend in smooth curves; a sudden sharp, almost right-angle turn in an otherwise flowing course is not natural behaviour, and it marks the exact point where the stolen headwaters swing into the captor's valley. Witness two: the wind gap. Trace the victim's old direction beyond the elbow and you find a notch or col through the high ground with NO stream in it — a valley clearly carved by water, holding none. Rivers do not carve dry valleys; that gap says water once flowed through and was taken. Witness three: the misfit stream. Downstream of the wind gap, the beheaded lower course wanders in a valley far too broad and deep for its trickle — a channel built by the fuller river it used to be. Any one witness could have another explanation; two or three together are conclusive, so in the answer, name at least two features with their map evidence and then state the verdict: river capture, with the captor identified by its swollen course and its likely rejuvenation. Evidence first, verdict second — exactly like a court, and exactly how the memo allocates the marks.

**Mandy:** Last one: why do we even learn this? A river stole another river a million years ago — how is that useful to anyone today?

**Tutor:** Because water and flat land are the two scarcest gifts in South African geography, and these ancient dramas decided where both of them sit today. Follow the water first. A captured river carries double discharge — better assurance for irrigation schemes, town supply and dam sites on the captor's side — while the beheaded valley may hold a struggling stream, dropping boreholes and a farming community short of the water its grandparents planned around. Engineers read capture maps before placing infrastructure: the wind gap is a free pass through the mountains, which is why roads and railways thread through them instead of climbing ridges. Now follow the flat land. Terraces are the best building and farming sites in any incised valley — flat, fertile, above the floods — which is why old towns and roads line them. Knickpoint waterfalls concentrate tourism and hydropower potential; Augrabies does both. Incised gorges offer cheap dam walls but expensive bridges, scenery for tourism but lethal flash floods in the confined channel. So the exam's real question is never about the ancient theft — it is whether you can read a landscape's history and say where the water, the farms, the roads and the risks belong today. Answer at that level, with one named example like Augrabies or the Blyde canyon, and you are writing top-band geography.
