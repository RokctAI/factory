# Part 1 — Expert

Average gradient is the calculation that converts the appearance of a slope into a number an engineer, a farmer or an examiner can use. This session builds it from the definition and the interpretation rule that decides half the follow-up marks, through harvesting the two ingredients off a topographic sheet, into the line-by-line layout the marking memorandum rewards, and out to the verification checks and the traps that cost matrics marks every year. The worked problem carried throughout is the standard form: calculate the average gradient between trig beacon 251, standing at 1 200 metres, and a spot height of 900 metres, where the two points lie 4,5 kilometres apart on a 1:50 000 topographic map.

## Subtopic: What Gradient Means

Gradient is the measure of how steeply the land rises or falls between two points. You already read steepness qualitatively from contour spacing — close lines, steep slope. Gradient turns that impression into a precise number.

The definition: gradient is the ratio between the vertical interval — how much the land rises — and the horizontal equivalent — the horizontal distance over which that rise happens. As a formula: gradient equals vertical interval divided by horizontal equivalent. VI over HE. Rise over run.

The answer is expressed as a ratio in the form 1 : x, and its meaning is beautifully concrete: for every 1 metre you climb vertically, you travel x metres horizontally. A gradient of 1 : 5 means climbing 1 metre for every 5 metres you walk — brutally steep. A gradient of 1 : 500 means 1 metre of climb spread over half a kilometre — you would barely notice it.

That leads to the interpretation rule that catches so many matrics: the LARGER the number after the colon, the GENTLER the slope. 1 : 15 is much steeper than 1 : 150. Do not let the bigger number fool you into saying steeper — the bigger number means your metre of climbing is diluted across more walking.

Why does the exam care? Beyond the 10 calculation marks in mapwork, gradient thinking runs through fluvial geomorphology — river profiles, discharge, erosion — and through practical questions: can a road, a railway, an irrigation canal be built across this land? Engineers live on gradients; for one lesson, so will we.

Take this section's questions now: the formula in its correct order, the plain-language meaning of a ratio such as 1 : 5, and the interpretation rule that ranks 1 : 15 against 1 : 150.

## Subtopic: Extracting VI and HE from the Map

Every gradient calculation begins with harvesting the two ingredients from the map. Ingredient one: the vertical interval.

The vertical interval is simply the height difference between the two points: the higher height minus the lower height. Our two anchors are the best height sources a map offers. A trig beacon — the small triangle symbol with a station number — gives a surveyed, exact altitude: beacon 251 stands at 1 200 metres. A spot height — a dot with a number — gives an exact summit or point altitude: 900 metres.

So: VI equals 1 200 minus 900, which is 300 metres. Write that subtraction down explicitly in the exam — the memo awards a mark for it.

Ingredient two: the horizontal equivalent — the horizontal distance between the points on the ground, in the same units as the VI. In our problem the distance is given: 4,5 kilometres. But pause on how you would harvest it yourself, because most questions make you measure it. You would lay your ruler between beacon and spot height, read the map distance — on a 1:50 000 sheet, 9 centimetres — and convert: 9 centimetres times 50 000 gives 450 000 centimetres of real ground. Divide by 100 for metres: 4 500 metres. Divide by 1 000 for kilometres: 4,5 kilometres. The same answer the question handed us.

Now the step where most marks die: units. The VI is in metres; the HE is in kilometres. A ratio of metres to kilometres is meaningless — both ingredients must speak the same language before dividing. Convert the HE to metres: 4,5 kilometres is 4 500 metres. Now both numbers are in metres, and only now may we divide.

## Subtopic: The Calculation — Laid Out for Marks

Watch the full exam layout. Every line earns something in the memorandum, so write every line.

Line one, the formula: gradient equals vertical interval over horizontal equivalent. Stating the formula is usually the first mark.

Line two, substitution with units visible: gradient equals 300 metres over 4 500 metres. Substituting correctly — with both values in the same unit — is the second mark.

Line three, simplify the fraction. We want the form 1 : x, so divide top and bottom by the numerator, 300. The top becomes 1. The bottom: 4 500 divided by 300. Work it out: 300 goes into 4 500 fifteen times, because 300 times 15 is 4 500. So the fraction is 1 over 15.

Line four, the answer in ratio form: gradient equals 1 : 15. The final answer mark — but only in this form. A decimal like 0,067, or the fraction left as 300 over 4 500, loses the mark on most memos; the ratio 1 : x is the required currency.

Now interpret it, because the follow-up question usually asks: 1 : 15 means the land rises 1 metre vertically for every 15 metres travelled horizontally. Is that steep? For land, yes — noticeably steep. A loaded truck labours on it; a railway could not take it (railways want gentler than about 1 : 50); a hiker feels it in the calves. One clean sentence of interpretation like that converts a calculation into a full geographic answer.

A note on the word average. Between beacon and spot height, the real land may dip through a valley and climb again — the slope is not uniform. Our 1 : 15 is the average gradient, the overall rise smeared evenly across the overall distance — a straight-line summary of a possibly curvy reality. If the examiner asks why "average", that is the answer.

Attend to this section's questions before moving on: the four written lines and what each one earns, the simplification that turns 300 over 4 500 into 1 : 15, and the sentence of interpretation that finishes the answer.

## Subtopic: Verification, Traps and the Full Method

Verify before you move on — thirty seconds, three checks. Check one: sense. The higher point is 1 200, the lower 900; a positive rise over a positive distance — the ratio must come out positive and of walkable steepness. If your x had come out as 0,15 or 15 000, the sense check screams unit error. Check two: reverse the arithmetic — 15 times 300 is 4 500; the simplification holds. Check three: units — both ingredients were metres before dividing. Pass all three and the answer is safe.

Now the traps, in descending order of casualties. Trap one: mixed units — dividing 300 metres by 4,5 and reporting 1 : 66,7 — wrong, because the 4,5 was kilometres. Trap two: inverting the ratio — dividing HE by VI and reporting 15 : 1 or 1 : 0,067. Remember which ingredient leads: the vertical 1 metre is the "1" in the ratio. Trap three: subtracting heights in the wrong order and carrying a negative — gradient reports the size of the rise; take the absolute difference. Trap four: on measure-it-yourself questions, sloppy map measurement — a 2 millimetre error at 1:50 000 is 100 metres of ground; measure to the nearest half millimetre.

The complete method, one breath: find both heights, subtract for VI; measure or read the distance, convert to metres for HE; state formula; substitute same-unit values; simplify to 1 : x; interpret in a sentence; run the three checks.

And know where this skill travels next: river long-profiles in fluvial geomorphology are gradient sequences — steep youthful courses, gentle old-age courses; cross-section questions ask for gradients between banks; development questions ask whether a slope can carry housing. One formula, many doors. Practise until 1 : 15 falls out of you without hesitation — those are the quickest ten marks on the paper.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: the three verification checks, each trap matched to the wrong answer it produces, and the full method recited in its proper order.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same slope from the ramp at the shopping centre — one number for going up, one number for going along, and a way of saying "steep" that a taxi driver would understand.

## Subtopic: The Ramp and the Staircase

Think about getting to the top floor of a shopping centre. You can take the stairs — short, straight up, and your legs know about it — or you can walk the parking ramp that curls slowly around the building. Both of them lift you exactly the same height. The difference is how far you walk while doing it. That difference is the whole idea we are chasing today.

So ask the slope one simple question: for every one metre I go UP, how many metres do I go ALONG? On those stairs it might be one up for every two along. On the ramp it might be one up for every twenty along. And that pair of numbers is written with a colon between them — one up, twenty along, written 1 : 20.

Now the bit that trips almost everybody. Which is steeper, 1 : 15 or 1 : 150? The instinct says the big number must be the big slope. It is the opposite. Think of one spoon of sugar. Stir it into a small cup and it is sweet; stir the same spoon into a two-litre jug and you can barely taste it. Same sugar, spread thinner. It is the same with climbing: one metre of climb spread over 15 metres of walking is a hard push, while the same one metre spread over 150 metres is a stroll you would not even notice. Bigger second number, gentler slope. Always.

Only now do we put names to the pieces, because the names are the easy part once the idea is yours. The "up" number is called the vertical interval. The "along" number is called the horizontal equivalent. And the pair together, written 1 : something, is called the gradient.

Your questions for this section are with you now: stairs against ramp, what a ratio like 1 : 20 is actually saying, and which of two slopes is the steeper. Trust the sugar-in-the-jug picture and you will get these.

## Subtopic: Two Numbers Off the Map, Both Speaking Metres

To work out a gradient you need those two numbers, and the map is holding both of them.

Start with the "up" number. Maps print heights at trustworthy points. A little triangle with a number next to it is a trig beacon — a point a surveyor actually visited and measured, so its height is exact. A plain dot with a number is a spot height, also exact. Our question gives us both: beacon 251 sits at 1 200 metres, the spot height sits at 900 metres. To find how much you climb between them, take the low one away from the high one: 1 200 minus 900 is 300 metres. That is the "up" number, and writing that subtraction down earns a mark on its own.

Now the "along" number. Our question hands it over — 4,5 kilometres — but usually you must dig it out yourself with a ruler. Lay the ruler between the two points and read the paper distance: on this sheet, 9 centimetres. Un-shrink it by the map's scale: 9 times 50 000 is 450 000 centimetres. A hundred centimetres make a metre, so that is 4 500 metres; a thousand metres make a kilometre, so that is 4,5 kilometres. Exactly what the question said.

And here is the one place this topic bites people, so take it slowly. Your "up" number is in metres. Your "along" number is sitting there in kilometres. You cannot compare those two any more than you can work out a price by dividing rands by cents — the answer would be a thousand times out and mean nothing. So make them speak the same language first: 4,5 kilometres is 4 500 metres. Now you are holding 300 metres of up and 4 500 metres of along, and both of them are saying "metres". Get that habit and the biggest gradient error in the country simply cannot happen to you.

## Subtopic: Share It Out, Then Say It in One Line

The hard thinking is done. What is left is sharing, and you have shared things out before.

You have 300 metres of climbing and 4 500 metres of walking. The question "how many metres along for each single metre up?" is just asking you to share 4 500 among 300 — the same move as working out how many R300 airtime vouchers you can buy with R4 500. The answer is 15, and you can check it the easy way round: 300 times 15 is 4 500. So it is one metre up for every fifteen metres along, and you write it as 1 : 15.

Write it out the way the marker pays for it, though, because each line carries money. Line one, the formula: gradient equals vertical interval divided by horizontal equivalent. Line two, the numbers put in with their units showing: 300 metres divided by 4 500 metres. Line three, the sharing: divide top and bottom by 300 to leave 1 over 15. Line four, the answer in its proper clothes: 1 : 15. Leaving it as 0,067, or as 300 over 4 500, is like handing someone a fifty-rand note torn in half — the value is there, but nobody will take it.

Then add one sentence of meaning, because that sentence is often a mark by itself: the land climbs 1 metre for every 15 metres you travel, which is properly steep — a bakkie feels it, a loaded truck groans on it, and a railway would refuse it outright, because railways want something gentler than about 1 : 50.

One last word to understand: average. Between the beacon and the spot height the ground is not a neat ramp. It may drop into a valley, flatten across a flood plain, then climb hard to the top. Your answer ignores all that wiggling and spreads the total climb evenly over the total walk — like working out an average taxi fare for the month when some days you paid more and some days less. That is why it is called the average gradient, and that sentence alone answers the question when they ask.

Three quick guards and you are finished. Both numbers in metres before you divide, or you will get the famous wrong answer of 1 : 66,7. The "1" is always the climbing, never the walking, so never write 15 : 1. And if the heights come out negative because you subtracted the wrong way round, just take the size of the difference — a slope's steepness does not care which of you is standing at the top.

Here come the final questions of the lesson, right now: the sharing that turns 300 and 4 500 into 1 : 15, the four lines that collect the marks, the sentence that explains what 1 : 15 feels like underfoot, and the word "average". You have earned these — go and take them.
