# Part 1 — Expert

Two dramas dominate Grade twelve fluvial geomorphology: a river that gets its youth back, and a river that steals another river's water. Rejuvenation and river capture carry the theory marks in the geomorphology section, and both are stories about energy — a river's energy budget suddenly rewritten. This session works through rejuvenation's causes and its four signature features, then river capture's mechanism and its five-piece vocabulary, then the significance of both processes for people, and finally the identification skills — reading these landforms off long profiles, cross-sections and the topographic map.

## Subtopic: Rejuvenation — A River Made Young Again

Begin with the graded profile. Over time a river smooths its long profile — the curve from source to mouth — into a gentle concave sweep, steep near the source, nearly flat at the mouth. A river at that state is graded: its energy and its load are in balance, erosion roughly equalling deposition along its length. The controlling level is base level — the lowest level to which a river can erode, ultimately sea level.

Rejuvenation means the river regains erosive energy and behaves young again — cutting DOWNWARD vigorously, as it did in its youthful stage — because the relationship between the river and its base level has changed. The exam wants the causes sorted into two families.

Family one: a drop in base level, called eustatic change when it is the sea that moves. Sea level falls — during an ice age, for instance, when water is locked up in ice sheets — and suddenly the river's mouth hangs above the new sea level. The lower reach steepens, the river's velocity and energy rise, and it begins slicing into its own floodplain, working the new steepness upstream.

Family two: uplift of the land, called tectonic or isostatic change. The crust rises under the river — as the southern African plateau has risen repeatedly — and every gradient along the profile steepens. Same result: velocity up, energy up, vertical erosion switched back on. A third, local trigger is worth a sentence: increased discharge, for example after a wetter climate phase or after capture, which also boosts energy.

Hold the mechanism in one line: rejuvenation converts potential energy into renewed downcutting, and the point where the old profile meets the new, steeper profile — the knickpoint — retreats upstream like a zip opening from the mouth toward the source.

Take this section's questions now: base level and the graded profile, the two big families of causes with their names, and the one-line mechanism with its migrating knickpoint.

## Subtopic: The Four Signatures of Rejuvenation

Rejuvenation leaves four fingerprints on the landscape, and the exam asks you to name them, explain them and identify them in diagrams.

Signature one: the knickpoint itself — a sharp break of slope in the long profile, usually marked by a waterfall or rapids. Upstream of the knickpoint the river still flows on its old, gentle profile; downstream it has already cut down to the new one. The waterfall retreats upstream year by year as the river eats at its lip. Augrabies Falls on the Gariep is South Africa's textbook knickpoint, thundering into a gorge the river has carved headward through the granite.

Signature two: terraces. Before rejuvenation the river had built a floodplain. When downcutting resumes, the river slices through its own floodplain, leaving the old valley floor stranded on both sides as flat benches above the new channel — paired river terraces, matching left and right like two shelves at the same height. Rejuvenate again and the river cuts a second, lower pair: a staircase of abandoned floodplains, oldest at the top. Terraces attract farms and roads — flat, fertile, above flood level — and that link earns application marks.

Signature three: valley-in-a-valley. Seen in cross-section, the new, steep-sided valley is incised into the floor of the old, broad valley — a young V nested inside an old U. This form IS the cross-sectional face of rejuvenation, and the terraces are simply the old valley floor's remnants flanking the inner valley.

Signature four: incised meanders. If the rejuvenated river was meandering across its floodplain when the energy returned, it keeps its winding plan but cuts vertically, so the loops themselves sink into rock — meanders entrenched in gorges hundreds of metres deep. The Blyde River's winding canyon and the tight sunken loops of the Oranje in its gorge tract show the form at grand scale. Distinguish the two exam varieties in one sentence each: entrenched meanders are symmetric, cut straight down by fast vertical erosion; ingrown meanders are asymmetric, with one steep and one gentle slope, formed where slower incision let the loops keep shifting sideways as they cut down.

Attend to this section's questions before moving on: the four features named, the staircase logic of terraces, the nested cross-section, and the difference between entrenched and ingrown meanders.

## Subtopic: River Capture — The Theft of a River

River capture, also called stream piracy, is the natural theft of one river's headwaters by another. The exam wants the mechanism, the five-piece vocabulary and the after-effects.

Set the stage with two rivers flowing roughly parallel, divided by a watershed. One of them — call it the strong river — has an advantage: a steeper gradient, softer rock to work in, or more rainfall, and therefore more erosive energy. The strong river's headward erosion is the weapon: every river extends its source backward into the watershed, but the strong river does it faster, gnawing through the divide toward its neighbour's channel.

The moment of capture: the strong river's headwaters break through the watershed and intersect the weak river's channel. Water always takes the steeper, lower exit — so the weak river's upper course abruptly turns and drains into the strong river. The theft is complete.

Now name the five pieces, because the labelled diagram is a standing exam question. The captor stream: the strong river that did the stealing, now carrying its own water plus the stolen headwaters. The captured stream: the upper course of the weak river, now redirected into the captor. The elbow of capture: the sharp, unnatural right-angle bend where the captured water swings into the captor's valley — rivers do not naturally turn ninety degrees, so an elbow on a map is a confession of piracy. The misfit stream: the beheaded lower course of the weak river, still flowing in its old broad valley but shrunken — a stream far too small for the valley it occupies, because that valley was carved by the fuller river it used to be. And the wind gap: the abandoned stretch of valley between the elbow and the misfit stream, a dry notch through the high ground where the river used to flow — a valley with no river in it.

After-effects tie the topics together: the captor, with its discharge suddenly increased, gains energy and often rejuvenates — expect knickpoints and incision working up its valley. The misfit river, starved of water, deposits more, and its valley may silt and swamp.

The questions on this section are with you now: the mechanism of headward erosion, the five labels on the capture diagram, and what happens to captor and victim afterwards.

## Subtopic: Significance for People and Reading the Map

Why do examiners care so much? Because both processes redistribute the two things settlement depends on: water and flat land.

Capture's human ledger. On the captor's side: more water — better assurance for irrigation, town supply and even hydropower potential; but also a rejuvenating, incising river that can undermine bridges and steepen banks. On the victim's side: a beheaded river means less water downstream — boreholes drop, irrigation schemes shrink, a mill town or farming valley can lose the resource it was founded on. The wind gap has its own quiet usefulness: a ready-made pass through the high ground, and road and rail builders have always threaded windgaps rather than climbing ridges.

Rejuvenation's human ledger. Terraces offer flood-free, fertile, flat building and farming land, which is why old towns line up along terrace edges. Knickpoint waterfalls offer hydropower sites and tourism — Augrabies again. Incised gorges concentrate scenery and dam sites — a deep narrow gorge is a cheap dam wall — but they also lock the river away from irrigators, make bridging expensive, and turn flash floods lethal in the confined channel.

Now the identification drill, because Paper 2 will hand you a topographic map or a profile. On the long profile: a graded river sweeps smooth and concave; a sharp step in that curve is a knickpoint — rejuvenation. On the cross-section: a V nested inside a broader valley, benches stepping up the sides — valley-in-a-valley with terraces. On the contour map: terraces show as stacked flat areas beside the river, each edged by a band of closely spaced contours; an incised meander shows as looping contour patterns with the contours crowded tight along both banks of a winding course. For capture, scan the drainage plan: a sharp elbow bend in an otherwise smooth course; a col or notch in a ridge with no stream in it — the wind gap; and downstream of it, a thin stream wandering in a conspicuously oversized valley — the misfit. Assemble two or more of those clues and you may name the capture with confidence.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: one human consequence each for captor, victim, terrace and gorge, and the map clues for knickpoint, terrace, incised meander and capture.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now both dramas again, told with a pensioner who wins the lottery and a neighbour who steals a water pipe — the versions that stick.

## Subtopic: The Old River Wins the Lottery

Think of a river's life like a person's. Young rivers, up in the mountains, are energetic: they cut downward, all sharp Vs and waterfalls. Old rivers, down near the sea, have settled: wide, slow, winding across a flat floodplain, moving mud rather than boulders. Every river is slowly smoothing its path from source to sea into one lazy curve — steep at the start, flat at the end. A river that has reached that comfortable curve is called graded: energy in balance, nothing dramatic left to do.

And the river's finish line — the level it is always working down toward — is called base level. For almost every river that is the sea. A river can never cut deeper than the level of the water it empties into; the sea is the floor of its world.

Rejuvenation is what happens when that floor suddenly drops — or the land is lifted. Either way the river finds itself higher above its finish line than it was yesterday. Higher above the finish line means steeper, and steeper means faster, and faster means powerful. The old river wakes up with a young river's energy — a pensioner winning the lottery and going back to the gym. The word says it plainly: re-juvenation, made young again.

What could move the floor? Two things, and the exam wants both. The sea itself can fall — in an ice age, so much water is stacked up in ice sheets that sea level drops worldwide. Or the land can rise — the ground under southern Africa has been lifted more than once, tilting every river on the plateau into a steeper run. Sea falls or land rises: either way, the gap between river and finish line widens, and the river starts digging again.

And where does the digging start? At the bottom, where the change happened, and it works its way UP the river like a ladder in a stocking — the point where new steepness meets old profile crawls upstream year by year. Keep that crawling point in mind; it has a name and a waterfall waiting in the next section.

Your questions for this section are with you now: the lazy curve and its finish line, the two ways the gap can widen, and which direction the digging spreads. The pensioner remembers them all.

## Subtopic: Four Clues the River Got Young

A rejuvenated river cannot hide what happened — it leaves four clues in the landscape, like a lottery winner's new car in the driveway.

Clue one: the waterfall on the stairs. The crawling point where new steep profile meets old gentle profile is the knickpoint, and the river usually marks it with a waterfall or rapids. Upstream of the fall, the river still flows its old lazy self; downstream, it has already cut down to the new level. The falls chew their way upstream year after year. South Africa's showpiece is Augrabies on the Gariep — a river stepping off its old profile into the gorge it has carved.

Clue two: the old floors left as shelves. Before the change, the river had built itself a wide flat floodplain — its old living-room floor. When the digging started, the river cut a trench down through its own floor, leaving strips of it high and dry on both sides: terraces, flat shelves stepping up the valley sides, matching pair by pair like bunk beds on both walls. Win the lottery twice and you get two pairs — a staircase of abandoned floors, the oldest at the top. People love terraces: flat, fertile, and safely above the floods — look where the farms and the old roads sit in any big valley.

Clue three: the valley inside a valley. Look at the valley end-on and you see the whole story in one shape: a sharp young V-shaped trench cut into the floor of a broad old valley. Geographers call it exactly what it looks like: valley-in-a-valley. The terraces are just the edges of the old valley floor, flanking the new trench.

Clue four: the sunken snake. If the river was already winding in big lazy loops when the energy arrived, it did not straighten out — it kept its loops and cut straight down, sinking its own curves into solid rock. Those are incised meanders: a snake-shaped gorge, loops and all, hundreds of metres deep. The Blyde River canyon winds like that — an old river's shape carved with a young river's power.

Take the questions for this section now: the four clues, which clue crawls upstream, why the shelves come in matching pairs, and the snake that sank. Four clues, one story: the river got its youth back.

## Subtopic: The Neighbour Who Stole the Pipe

Now the second drama: river capture, and it is exactly what its other name says — stream piracy. One river steals another river's water. No metaphor needed; the theft is real.

Picture two neighbouring rivers flowing side by side, a ridge of high ground between them like a boundary wall. One neighbour is stronger — steeper slope, softer rock to dig in, more rain falling on its side. Every river slowly eats backwards at its source, extending uphill — that is headward erosion, the river's arm reaching back into the ridge. The strong river's arm reaches faster. Year by year it burrows through the boundary wall toward the weak river's channel — a neighbour tunnelling toward your water pipe.

Then the breakthrough: the strong river's headwaters cut into the weak river's channel. Water is loyal to nothing but gravity — offered a steeper, lower exit, the weak river's upper half immediately turns and pours into the strong river. From that day the strong river carries double water, and the weak river below the break is left with almost nothing.

Now walk the crime scene and name the five exhibits, because the exam will hand you this diagram. The thief, now swollen with stolen water: the captor stream. The stolen upper course, flowing a new way: the captured stream. The sharp unnatural corner where the stolen water turns into the thief's valley: the elbow of capture — rivers never naturally bend at right angles, so an elbow on the map is a signed confession. The victim's remaining lower course: the misfit stream — a pitiful trickle wandering in a huge valley, like one learner in a hall built for eight hundred, because the valley was carved by the river it used to be. And the stretch of valley the stolen water abandoned: the wind gap — a dry notch through the high ground where a river used to run, now holding nothing but wind, and often a road, because engineers love a ready-made pass.

The aftermath links the two dramas of this lesson: the thief, suddenly carrying double water, gains energy — and often rejuvenates, cutting down with its new strength, knickpoint and all. The victim's valley, starved, silts up and swamps. And people inherit the outcome: farms on the captor side gain water assurance, while any town that grew up on the victim's lower course watches its river — and its boreholes — shrink.

Here come the final questions of the lesson, right now: the weapon of headward erosion, the five exhibits at the crime scene, the elbow as confession, and what the theft does to both rivers and their people. Take them, and both dramas are yours for Paper 1 and the map paper alike.
