**Mandy:** Time for the class questions! First: I get that rejuvenation means the river cuts down again, but I keep muddling WHY it starts. Sea level falls, land rises, discharge grows — do I have to know all three, and how do I keep them apart?

**Tutor:** Keep them apart by seeing that all three are one idea wearing three coats, and the one idea is the widening gap. A river's energy depends on how far it stands above its base level — its finish line, normally the sea. Widen that vertical gap and you steepen the run, and a steeper run means speed, energy and renewed downcutting. Coat number one: the finish line drops — the sea itself falls, which we call eustatic change, classically during an ice age when ocean water is stacked up in continental ice sheets. Coat number two: the runner is lifted — tectonic or isostatic uplift raises the land under the river, as has happened repeatedly to the southern African interior, steepening every gradient at once. Both are answers about the gap; they differ only in which end moved. Coat number three is the odd one out because the gap never changes: a lasting increase in discharge — a wetter climate, or the stolen water after a capture — simply gives the river more muscle over the same drop. For full explanations, give the two gap-movers as the main families and add discharge as the local extra. And always finish with the mechanism sentence: the new steepness enters at the mouth and works upstream as a migrating knickpoint. Cause, gap, knickpoint — that chain is the whole theory.

**Mandy:** Next one: terraces confuse me. If the river cut DOWN, why are there flat steps going UP the valley sides? Down and up feel like opposites.

**Tutor:** They feel like opposites until you realise the terraces never moved — the river left them behind. Rewind the film. Before rejuvenation, the river spent thousands of years swinging across its valley and building one wide, flat floodplain: a single continuous floor, and the river's channel lay IN that floor. Then the energy returned and the river changed jobs, from widening to deepening. It sawed a trench straight down through its own floodplain. Everything the saw did not touch simply stayed where it always was — and what stayed was the old floor, now stranded as two flat benches facing each other across the trench, at exactly matching heights, because they are two halves of one surface. That is a terrace pair. Nothing was lifted; the river sank and the floor stayed. Rejuvenate a second time and the river, which had begun building a new, lower floodplain in its trench, saws through that one too — a second, lower pair. Hence the staircase, with the oldest floor at the top, which trips everyone: the highest terrace is the most ancient river level, not the newest. When you explain it, use the three beats — build a floor, cut through it, the remnants flank the channel — and say the word remnant. That word tells the reader you know the terrace is the ghost of a floodplain, and that is exactly what the explanation marks are listening for.

**Mandy:** Here's one from the group: in the capture diagram, I can never remember which leftover is the misfit stream and which is the wind gap. Both are just what's left of the loser, right?

**Tutor:** Both belong to the loser, yes — but one still has water and one has none, and that single distinction sorts them forever. Replay the theft. The victim river once ran a complete course: headwaters, a middle stretch crossing the high ground, and a lower valley to the sea. The capture amputates it at the elbow: everything above the elbow is rerouted into the captor — that rerouted portion is the captured stream, the thief's loot. Now look downstream of the amputation. The middle stretch, where the river used to thread through the high ground, is left with no water source at all — its supply now flows the thief's way. That dead, dry stretch is the wind gap: a valley-shaped notch through the ridge holding nothing but wind. It cannot hold a stream, because nothing feeds it. Further down, though, the old lower valley does still collect a little local drainage — springs, small tributaries below the gap — so a stream survives there. But it is a shadow of the river that carved the valley, a trickle wandering in a canyon-sized floor: the misfit stream, so called because the stream no longer fits its valley. So run the water test: dry notch through the high ground — wind gap; undersized stream in an oversized valley below it — misfit. And remember the order along the old course: elbow first, wind gap through the ridge, misfit beyond it. Sketch that line once and the two leftovers never swap again.

**Mandy:** Last one: if a question asks me to PROVE capture happened from a map, what's the strongest single piece of evidence — and is one piece ever enough?

**Tutor:** The strongest single witness is the elbow of capture, because it is the only clue that records the crime itself rather than its aftermath. Rivers adjust their courses in smooth curves; a near-right-angle turn in an otherwise flowing course is hydrologically unnatural, and it sits exactly where the victim's headwaters were wrenched into the captor's valley. But strong is not sufficient — one clue is a suspicion, not a conviction, because look-alikes exist: a fault line or a band of resistant rock can also bend a river sharply. So prosecute with corroboration, and the beauty of capture is that its clues form a predictable line you can trace on the map. Start at the suspect elbow. Project the victim's old course beyond it: does the ridge ahead carry a dry col — a wind gap — exactly on that line? Continue past the gap: does a conspicuously undersized stream wander in a broad, over-large valley — the misfit? If you can also argue the captor's superior energy — it flows at a visibly lower level, or on a steeper course — the case is complete. In a written answer, present it exactly like a case file: name each feature, give its grid position or describe its location, and state what each contributes to the proof. Two aligned clues make capture probable; three on one line make it beyond reasonable doubt — and answers written as evidence, not assertion, collect everything the question has to give.
