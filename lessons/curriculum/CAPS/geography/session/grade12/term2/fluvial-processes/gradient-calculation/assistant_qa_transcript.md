**Student:** I got 1 : 66,7 for the beacon question and the memo says 1 : 15. I checked my division three times and it's right!

**Mandy:** Your division IS right — that's the cruel part! The error happened before the division: you fed it 300 ÷ 4,5, but the 4,5 was in *kilometres* while the 300 was in *metres*. Convert first: 4,5 km = 4 500 m. Then 300 ÷ 300 = 1 and 4 500 ÷ 300 = 15, so 1 : 15. This is THE classic gradient trap, and the fix is a habit, not more checking: the moment you write down VI and HE, write their units next to them, and don't touch the divide button until both say metres. Same units, then divide — tattoo it on your pencil case.

**Student:** How do I remember which number goes on top? Sometimes I do HE over VI without noticing.

**Mandy:** Anchor it to what the answer *means* and you'll never flip it again. The final ratio 1 : 15 reads "1 metre UP for every 15 metres ALONG." The vertical is always the 1 — it leads the ratio, so it must lead the fraction: VI on top. Quick gut-check after simplifying: for ordinary land, the second number should be bigger than the first — you always walk further than you climb. If you get something like 1 : 0,067, you've inverted it: flip it and carry on. And "rise over run" — if you've done maths gradients, it's the same order.

**Student:** Why do we even convert to a "1 : x" ratio? My answer 300/4500 is the same thing mathematically.

**Mandy:** Mathematically identical, geographically useless — and the memo knows it! Tell a road engineer "the slope is 300 over 4 500" and they must do arithmetic before they can picture anything. Tell them "1 : 15" and they instantly know: one metre of climb per fifteen travelled — too steep for a railway, hard work for a truck. The 1 : x form is the *standard language* of slope, and mapwork memos specifically award the final mark for speaking it. So always finish the job: divide both sides by the top number until the left side reads 1.
