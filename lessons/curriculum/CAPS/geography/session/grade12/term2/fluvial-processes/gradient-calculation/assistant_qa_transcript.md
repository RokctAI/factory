**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First up, the one that has half of us upset: I got 1 : 66,7 for the beacon question and the memo says 1 : 15. I have checked my division three times and it is right!

**Tutor:** Your division is right — that is the cruel part. The mistake happened before the division, in what you fed into it. You divided 300 by 4,5, but the 300 was a height in metres while the 4,5 was a distance in kilometres, and a ratio between two different units means nothing at all. Convert first: 4,5 kilometres is 4 500 metres, because a thousand metres make a kilometre. Then divide both sides by the smaller number, the 300 — three hundred divided by three hundred is one, and four thousand five hundred divided by three hundred is fifteen, because three hundred times fifteen is four thousand five hundred. The answer is 1 : 15. Notice how far off you were: 66,7 is more than four times too gentle, which is exactly what a thousand-fold unit slip does once the simplification squeezes it. The fix is a habit rather than more checking. The moment you name the vertical interval and the horizontal equivalent, say their units in the same breath — three hundred metres, four thousand five hundred metres — and refuse to divide until both of them are speaking metres.

***

**Mandy:** Second question: how do I remember which number goes on top? Sometimes I divide the distance by the height without even noticing, and it looks fine to me.

**Tutor:** Anchor it to what the finished answer says out loud, and you will never flip it again. The answer 1 : 15 is a sentence: one metre UP for every fifteen metres ALONG. The climbing is the one, so the climbing must lead — which means the vertical interval, the height difference, goes on top of the fraction, and the horizontal equivalent, the ground distance, goes underneath. Rise over run, if you have met gradients in mathematics; it is the same order. Then keep a gut-check ready for after you simplify. On ordinary land you always travel much further than you climb, so the number after the colon should be comfortably bigger than one. If you end up with something like 1 : 0,067, or you find yourself saying 15 : 1, you have divided upside down — simply invert it and carry on. And never report it the other way round hoping it counts as equivalent; the memorandum wants the vertical leading, because that is the form every engineer and road builder reads.

***

**Mandy:** Here is one from the back: what happens when there is no trig beacon and no spot height at my two points? Do I just guess the heights?

**Tutor:** No guessing needed — the contour lines carry the heights, and reading them is a marked skill in its own right. Contours are the thin brown lines that join all the places standing at the same height above sea level, and on our 1:50 000 sheets they step upward at a fixed twenty metres each, with every fifth line printed heavier and labelled with its height so you can count from a known value. So you find your point, look for the nearest labelled heavier line, and count contours towards your point in twenty-metre steps, going up when the values rise and down when they fall. If the point sits between two lines, most memoranda accept the lower contour's value or a sensible estimate between them. Now a warning about words, because this is where marks quietly disappear. The twenty metres between successive contour lines is called the contour interval. The height difference between your two chosen points is called the vertical interval, and it is usually far bigger — three hundred metres in our question. They sound almost identical and they are completely different numbers. Read the question carefully enough to know which one it is asking for.

**Mandy:** Last one: the question asked why we call it the "average" gradient. Someone wrote "because we used a formula" and got nothing at all for it.

**Tutor:** I understand why that felt like an answer, but the examiner is asking for geography, not arithmetic. Between beacon 251 and that spot height the land is almost certainly not a smooth ramp. It may fall into a valley, run flat across a stretch of flood plain, and then climb hard at the end. Your calculation ignores every one of those changes: it takes the total climb of 300 metres and spreads it evenly over the total distance of 4 500 metres, as though the whole slope were one straight, unchanging ramp. That evening-out is what the word average is naming — a straight-line summary of bumpy reality. It matters directly in this term's work on rivers. A river's long profile is a whole sequence of gradients: steep in the upper course near the source, gentler through the middle course, and very gentle across the lower course where the river meets its base level. Calculate one average gradient from source to mouth and you get a single number that describes none of those three sections honestly, which is exactly why geographers calculate gradients section by section along a river rather than in one go.
