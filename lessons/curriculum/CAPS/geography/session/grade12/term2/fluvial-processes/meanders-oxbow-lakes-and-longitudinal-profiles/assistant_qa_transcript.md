**Mandy:** Before you move on — a few questions that come up a lot with this topic. First: why does the water on the outside of the bend move faster in the first place?

**Tutor:** Because it has further to travel in the same time. Think of the bend as a curved running track: the outside lane is longer than the inside lane, yet the water in both lanes is part of one connected river that must keep moving together. The outside thread of current covers the longer path, so it must move faster; the inside thread covers the shorter path and can dawdle. Speed is energy, and that is where the two jobs come from. Fast water on the outside has energy to pick material up and to slam into the bank, so the outside is eroded and undercut into a steep river cliff. Slow water on the inside cannot even keep hold of the sand it is already carrying, so the load settles and builds the gentle slip-off slope. One bend, one current, two speeds — and every meander question, from naming the banks to explaining the oxbow lake, opens with that single fact.

***

**Mandy:** Next: learners keep writing the meander answer when the question asked for the oxbow lake, and the other way round. Where exactly is the line between the two?

**Tutor:** The line is the neck. Everything up to and including the growing loop belongs to the meander answer: water moves faster on the outside, the outer bank is eroded into an undercut slope, the inner bank receives deposition as a slip-off slope, and the loop grows and migrates. The oxbow-lake answer contains all of that and then crosses the line: the erosion on both limbs narrows the neck, a flood gives the river the power to break through, the straight new channel becomes the main course, deposition seals the two ends of the abandoned loop, and the loop survives as a horseshoe of still water. So check the question's last word. Asked about the meander, stop before the neck. Asked about the oxbow lake, the meander machinery is your opening lines and the neck story is your middle and end. And if the question offers eight lines, that is the examiner telling you all eight steps are wanted, in their cause-and-effect order.

***

**Mandy:** Third one: on the profile sketches, a dam and a waterfall both interrupt the curve. Why are they only temporary base levels when the sea is permanent?

**Tutor:** Because the river can destroy them, and it is busy doing exactly that. A base level is any level that stops the river cutting downward. A band of resistant rock holds the profile up, but the waterfall retreating across it grinds the barrier back a little every year, and when the band is finally cut through, that base level vanishes and the profile smooths itself out. A dam ponds the water and halts down-cutting locally, but dams silt up, spill and are eventually removed or breached — on a geological clock they are moments. The sea is different in kind, not merely in size: a river's energy comes from flowing downhill, and at sea level there is no more downhill left. The river cannot erode below the surface it drains into. So the test is simple: can the river, given time, remove the obstacle? If yes — rock band, dam — the base level is temporary. The sea it can never remove; that is the permanent base level, and every graded profile flattens towards it.

***

**Mandy:** Last one: in a photograph, is there a quick drill for telling the undercut slope from the slip-off slope before the nerves set in?

**Tutor:** Use a two-step drill: find the cliff, then confirm with the beach. Step one, scan the bend for the bank that looks injured — steep, bare, raw earth, sometimes with slumped blocks or exposed roots where the ground has collapsed. That wounded bank is the undercut slope, the outside of the bend, the fast side. Step two, look directly across the channel. You should see its opposite in every way: a gentle, smooth apron of sand or grass easing into the water, the kind of bank you could stroll down. That is the slip-off slope, the inside, the slow side. The confirmation matters because the two always sit paired across the channel from each other — if your two identifications are side by side rather than opposite, one of them is wrong. Then attach the one-line reasons ready for the account-for question: steep because fast water erodes and undercuts; gentle because slow water deposits. Cliff, then beach across the water, then the two reasons — that is the whole drill.
