**Mandy:** Questions that come up a lot with this topic! First: site and situation — I know the definitions, but in the exam they give me a paragraph about a settlement and I can never tell which sentence is which. Is there a sorting trick?

**Tutor:** There is, and it works on every paragraph the examiners have ever written. Read each sentence and ask one question: could I check this fact without leaving the settlement? If yes, it is site. The slope, the drainage, the soil, the spring, the aspect — you could verify every one of those standing inside the village with your own eyes. If checking the fact forces you to travel — how far to the next town, which roads pass, what lies beyond the mountains — it is situation, because situation is always a relationship with something outside. Run the test on a classic exam sentence: "The town lies at the foot of a pass on the route between the coast and the interior." Could you check that without leaving? No — the pass matters because of where it leads, the route matters because of what it connects. Situation. Now: "The town is built on a river terrace above the flood level." Check it standing there? Yes — that is site. One more layer for full marks: when a question asks you to EVALUATE site or situation, pair each fact with its benefit. Terrace above flood level — safety from floodwater. Foot of the pass — commands the trade moving through it. Fact plus benefit is what turns a two-mark answer into a four-mark answer.

**Mandy:** Next one: dispersed and nucleated. If I see a photo or map extract in the exam, what exactly do I look for before I commit to one of them?

**Tutor:** Look for two things in order: spacing, then a centre. Spacing first. Measure with your eye the distance between dwellings compared to the size of the dwellings themselves. In a dispersed pattern the gaps are enormous — one farmstead, then open land many times wider than the farmstead, then the next. In a nucleated pattern the dwellings sit within a few plot-widths of each other, touching distance in village terms. Then confirm with the centre test: nucleated settlements almost always cluster AROUND something — a church, a school, a dam, a borehole, a kraal, a junction — and the fields lie outside the cluster like the white of a fried egg around the yolk. If you can name the yolk, you can name the pattern. And prepare the follow-up mark before it is asked, because it always is: the reason. Dispersed pattern — large, privately owned farms, each farmer living on the land being worked; South Africa's commercial maize and sheep districts. Nucleated pattern — communal land tenure, shared water point, community and historical defence; many villages of the former homelands. On a topographic map, one more clue: dispersed farmsteads usually appear as tiny isolated black squares each with its own dam and windpump symbol, while a nucleated village shows a tight cluster of buildings with a school or church symbol inside it. Spacing, centre, reason — three moves, full marks.

**Mandy:** The four shapes — round, linear, T-shaped, crossroad. Do examiners really test all four, and how do they usually ask it?

**Tutor:** They test all four, and they ask in three standard ways, each with its own trap. Way one: a simple sketch or aerial description — name the shape. The trap here is rushing linear and T-shaped: a linear village follows ONE routeway with buildings along it; a T-shaped village has a SECOND road ending against the first, with buildings along both arms. Count the roads before you answer. Way two: name the shape AND account for it — that word "account" means give the cause, and each shape has one cause worth learning as a single sentence. Round: dwellings encircle a central kraal or open space for protection and community life. Linear: dwellings seek frontage along a road, river or canal. T-shaped: buildings gather where a side road meets a main road, drawn by passing trade. Crossroad: the same magnet, but two roads crossing give four arms of growth. Way three, the modern favourite: a critical question — why does a linear village struggle to provide services, or why is a crossroad village a road-safety problem? Reason from the shape itself: a linear village is long, so people at the two ends live far from a clinic placed at the middle, and the routeway that created the village carries fast traffic straight through it. Shapes are never just names; every shape is a cause wearing an outline.

**Mandy:** Last one: the question said "describe the relationship between land use and settlement in the mapped area" and half of us just listed features. What did the memo actually want?

**Tutor:** It wanted the link, spoken as a link — land use on one side, settlement evidence on the other, and the word "because" or "therefore" joining them. Listing features earns almost nothing; connecting them earns everything. Build each sentence as a pair. The map shows cultivated lands and orchards along the river, THEREFORE farmsteads are spaced at regular intervals through the farmland, because crop farmers live on the land they work. The south of the extract shows plantation symbols, THEREFORE the village beside the sawmill exists to house forestry workers. The dry western portion shows boreholes and windpumps, THEREFORE homesteads cluster at these wet-point sites, because water fixes the position of settlement in dry country. Notice the machinery of each sentence: evidence from the map, the settlement fact it produces, and the reason welded between them. Two or three such sentences is a full-mark answer; ten disconnected features is not. And keep the direction honest — land use usually explains the settlement, not the other way round, because in the rural world people settle where the work is. That single habit, pairing every feature with its consequence, is what separates a description from a geographer's answer.
