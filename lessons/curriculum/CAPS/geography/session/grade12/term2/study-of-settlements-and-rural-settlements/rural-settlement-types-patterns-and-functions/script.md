# Part 1 — Expert

Every settlement question in Paper 1 begins with the same three-part toolkit: what a settlement is, where it sits, and why it took the shape it did. This session builds that toolkit from the ground up — site and situation as the two location questions, the classification ladder from a single farmstead to a metropolis, the rural patterns and village shapes the examiners describe in words and expect you to name, and the land-use functions that explain why a rural settlement exists at all. The examples stay South African throughout: the scattered homesteads of the KwaZulu-Natal hills, the lonely Karoo farmstead, the nucleated villages of the former homelands, and the plantation towns of Mpumalanga.

## Subtopic: Settlement, Site and Situation

Start with the definition the memorandum wants: a settlement is any place where people live, together with the buildings and structures they use — from one isolated farmstead to Johannesburg. Geographers split all settlements into two families. Rural settlements are small, low-density places whose people work mainly in primary activities — farming, forestry, fishing, mining. Urban settlements are larger, high-density places whose people work mainly in secondary and tertiary activities — factories, shops, offices, services. The dividing line is not size alone; it is the dominant economic activity. A mining village of two thousand people doing primary work is still classified with the rural family in function, while a small town living off shops and services has crossed into urban territory.

Now the two location questions, and keep them permanently separate, because examiners love to swap them. Site is the actual land the settlement is built on — the physical characteristics of the exact spot. When you describe site, you talk about relief, drainage, soil, water supply, aspect. A village built on a gentle, well-drained, north-facing slope beside a reliable spring has a good site, and every word in that sentence is a site word. Situation is the position of the settlement in relation to its surroundings — other settlements, rivers, mountain passes, roads, resources. A town at the junction of two national roads, halfway between two cities, commands a good situation. Memorise the contrast as near versus far: site is what is under the settlement's feet; situation is what lies around it and beyond it.

Two site terms carry marks of their own. A wet-point site is chosen because water is available there in a dry region — a Karoo farmstead clustered on a borehole or a spring is the classic case. A dry-point site is the mirror image: in a wet, flood-prone area, the settlement climbs onto the higher, drier ground — homesteads sitting above the floodplain of a KwaZulu-Natal river, keeping their feet dry while their fields use the wet valley floor.

Pause here and take this section's questions: the definition of a settlement, the rural–urban dividing line, and the clean separation of site from situation with the wet-point and dry-point special cases.

## Subtopic: Classifying Settlements: Size, Pattern and Function

Geographers classify settlements three ways, and the exam asks for all three, sometimes in one question.

First, by size and complexity. Picture a ladder. On the bottom rung stands the isolated farmstead — one family, one working unit. Above it the hamlet: a handful of homesteads, perhaps a spaza shop, no real services. Then the village, which adds services — a school, a clinic, a general dealer, a church. Then the town, the city, and at the top the metropolis, a giant like Johannesburg or eThekwini formed when cities grow into one another. Climbing the ladder, three things rise together: population, the range of services, and the complexity of functions. The rural family occupies the lower rungs — farmstead, hamlet, village — and everything above is urban.

Second, by pattern, which asks how the buildings arrange themselves across the land. Two master patterns matter in the rural world. Dispersed settlement scatters individual homesteads far apart, each on its own land — fly over commercial farming districts of the Free State and you see one farmstead, then kilometres of maize, then the next farmstead. Nucleated settlement clusters dwellings tightly together around a central point — a water source, a church, a kraal — with the farmland lying around the outside of the cluster. Many villages of the former homelands, and the traditional Basotho and Zulu villages before them, are nucleated. Each pattern is an answer to a question the land asked: dispersal follows large privately owned farms where the farmer must live on the land being worked; nucleation follows communal land tenure, defence, shared water and community life.

Third, by function — the work a settlement performs. A rural settlement is usually unifunctional: one dominant activity, almost always tied to the land. An urban settlement is multifunctional: many activities under one name. Hold onto the term unifunctional; it anchors an entire question type.

This section's questions are ready for you now: the ladder from farmstead to metropolis, dispersed against nucleated with the reasons behind each, and the unifunctional–multifunctional contrast.

## Subtopic: Rural Shapes: Round, Linear, T-Shaped and Crossroad

The examiners name four rural village shapes, and because the exam is written, they describe each in words or with a simple sketch and expect the name back. Learn each shape as a word-picture you could recognise with your eyes closed.

The round village arranges its dwellings in a circle or oval around a central open space. In the traditional Southern African version, homes ring a central cattle kraal: the animals sleep in the protected middle, the people around them, community life in the centre. Defence and social organisation built this shape. Say the pattern aloud: houses on the rim, open working space in the heart.

The linear village is a settlement stretched into a line, one or two buildings deep, along something that flows — a road, a river, a canal, the foot of a ridge. Every dwelling wants frontage on the routeway, so the village grows longer, not wider. Picture the houses like beads on a single string. Roadside settlements strung along a rural main road, or homesteads following a river's dry terrace, take this form. Its advantage is access; its weakness is that services end up far from the two ends, and the routeway can be dangerous and noisy.

The T-shaped village grows where a side road meets a main road but does not cross it — a T-junction. Buildings line both arms, so from above the settlement itself spells the letter T. The crossroad village goes one step further: two roads cross, and buildings grow along all four arms, making a cross or X of dwellings with the intersection at its heart. Both shapes exist for the same reason — a junction concentrates passing trade, so the shop, the fuel pump and the taxi stop plant themselves where the traffic slows, and homes follow.

Exam technique: when a question shows or describes a shape, name it, then immediately give the reason for it — junction trade for T and crossroad, frontage on a routeway for linear, the central kraal and community for round. The name earns one mark; the reason earns the rest.

Take the questions for this section now: the four shapes as word-pictures, and the cause that built each one.

## Subtopic: Rural Functions and Land Use: Why the Settlement Is There

A rural settlement exists to work the land around it, so its function is read straight off its land use. The CAPS list gives the big three, each with a South African face.

Farming is the master function, and it splits in two. Crop farming — the maize districts of the Free State and North West, the sugar cane belt of the KwaZulu-Natal coast, the vineyards and deciduous fruit of the Western Cape valleys. Stock farming — cattle in the bushveld and the eastern grasslands, sheep across the great dry sweep of the Karoo. The type of farming shapes the settlement: intensive farming on small, high-value plots supports closer, denser rural settlement; extensive farming, like Karoo sheep runs, spreads farmsteads enormously far apart, because each family needs thousands of hectares.

Forestry builds its own settlement type: plantation villages inside the man-made timber forests of Mpumalanga's escarpment and the KwaZulu-Natal midlands, housing the workers who plant, thin and harvest, with a sawmill town nearby.

Conservation adds the third function: settlements serving national parks and reserves — staff villages and tourist camps inside the Kruger National Park, gate settlements at its edges — where the land's function is protection and the settlement's function is to serve visitors and manage the wild land.

Now bind the whole toolkit together, because this is how the exam uses it. Given a description or a map extract of a rural settlement, you should be able to run the full commentary: classify it — rural, because the activity is primary; give its pattern — dispersed or nucleated, with the land-tenure or water reason; name its shape if it clusters — round, linear, T-shaped, crossroad, with the cause; state its function — crop farming, stock farming, forestry or conservation, from the land-use evidence; and comment on site and situation — the wet-point borehole, the dry ground above the floodplain, the position on the route between towns. Five moves, each worth marks, all built from this one session.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: the three land-use functions with their South African regions, the link between intensive or extensive farming and settlement density, and the five-move commentary that answers any rural settlement question.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same toolkit again, but built from things you walk past every week — a stand and its street, mealies scattered or cooked in one pot, and a village read like a taxi route.

## Subtopic: The Stand and the Street: Site versus Situation

Think about a house you know well — yours, or a relative's. Two completely different conversations describe where it is, and once you hear them separately, you will never confuse site and situation again.

The first conversation is about the stand itself: the actual ground the house is built on. Is the plot flat or sloping? Does rain drain away or stand in the yard? Is the soil solid or does the wall crack? Is there water — a tap, a borehole? Does the sun reach the front rooms in winter? Every one of those questions is about the exact patch of earth under the building. That whole conversation is SITE.

The second conversation never mentions the ground at all: how far is the house from the school, from the clinic, from the taxi rank, from town? What is it near? What routes pass it? That conversation — the house in relation to everything around it — is SITUATION. Site is under your feet; situation is out the window and down the road.

Now stretch the same two conversations over a whole settlement. A village in the dry Karoo huddles around the one spot where water comes out of the ground — a spring or a borehole. The water made that exact spot worth building on, so geographers call it a wet-point site: chosen FOR the water. Flip the picture to a wet region where the river floods every summer: there the homes climb the slope and sit on the higher, drier shelf above the floodplain, planting their fields down on the wet flat land but sleeping up where the flood cannot reach. That is a dry-point site: chosen to ESCAPE the water. Same logic as choosing where to stand at a leaking tap — close enough to fill your bucket, far enough to keep your shoes dry.

Take this section's questions now: the two conversations, and which one the wet-point and dry-point choices belong to. Answer them the way you would describe that house you know.

## Subtopic: Scattered Mealies or One Pot: Dispersed and Nucleated

All the classifying in Part 1 shrinks down to two everyday questions: how big is the place, and do the homes spread out or huddle together?

The size question is just a ladder, and you have travelled it in real life. One farmhouse alone with its sheds — a farmstead. A few homesteads within shouting distance and maybe a spaza — a hamlet. Add a school, a clinic, a church, a general dealer, and it has become a village. Keep adding people and services and you pass through town and city up to a metropolis like Joburg. The rule of the ladder is simple: every step up means more people AND more services. The rural world lives on the bottom three rungs.

The huddling question has only two answers. Picture a handful of dried mealie kernels. Fling them across the floor and they land far apart, each alone — that is DISPERSED settlement, one homestead here, the next one kilometres away, exactly what commercial farm country looks like, because each farmer owns a huge private farm and must live on it. Now instead drop all the kernels into one pot — that is NUCLEATED settlement, homes packed together in a cluster with the fields lying around the outside. Villages on communal land are the classic case: the land belongs to the community, so the people live together and walk out to the fields. Scattered kernels, private land; one pot, shared land. That single link — pattern to land ownership — is the reason-mark the exam is fishing for.

And the function question: a rural place usually does ONE kind of work, almost always working the land, so we call it unifunctional — one function. A city does everything at once — multifunctional. If most people in a place earn from the land itself, you are standing in a rural settlement, whatever its size.

Your questions for this section are up: the ladder, the kernels, and the one-function word.

## Subtopic: Reading a Village Like a Taxi Route

The four village shapes stop being a memory list the moment you connect each one to a picture you already own.

Round: think of the traditional homestead pattern — huts standing in a ring, and in the middle, the kraal where the cattle sleep. The circle protects what is precious at its centre. Houses on the rim, wealth and gathering space in the heart. If a question describes homes arranged in a circle around a central open space, the answer is a round village, and the reason is protection and community life.

Linear: think of any road you have travelled where, for a stretch, every single building faces the tar — shop, houses, another shop, all in one long line, nothing behind them. The village is shaped like the route it clings to, beads on a string, because everyone wants a front door on the road. Rivers do the same job as roads: homes strung along the dry bank. Long and thin means linear, and the reason is frontage — access to the thing that flows past.

T-shaped and crossroad: these two you can read straight from a taxi window. Where a side road ends against a main road, buildings gather along both arms of the junction — from the air the village writes a letter T. Where two roads cross and carry on, buildings grow along all four arms — the village writes an X. Why there? Because a junction is where traffic slows, stops and changes direction — and wherever travellers pause, somebody sells to them. The shop, the fuel, the taxi stop, the chesa nyama plant themselves at the junction, and homes grow along the arms. The junction is the magnet; the shape is just iron filings.

Last, why is the village there at all? Look at what the land around it is doing. Mealie lands and orchards — a crop-farming settlement. Kraals, sheep and rangeland — stock farming; and notice that Karoo sheep farms need so much land per family that homesteads sit enormously far apart, while small intensive plots let people live close together. Straight dark rows of planted pine and gum on the escarpment — a forestry village serving the plantations and the sawmill. A rest camp and staff village inside the Kruger — conservation, where the settlement's job is to guard land and host visitors.

Here come the final questions of the lesson: the four shapes matched to their pictures, the junction magnet, and the land-use clues that name a settlement's function. Read every one like a taxi route you have travelled before, and the rural settlement question becomes marks you collect on sight.
