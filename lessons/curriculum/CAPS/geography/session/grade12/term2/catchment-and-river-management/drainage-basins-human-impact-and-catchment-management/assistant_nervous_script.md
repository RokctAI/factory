Hey, it's Mandy — last stretch of the river section, and if your head is swimming in basins, blooms and acts of parliament, let me shrink it down to three pictures you cannot forget.

Picture one: the funnel. Every river drains a funnel of land, and whatever happens anywhere in the funnel ends up in the river. That single sentence is the master key — it answers why catchments matter, why they are hard to manage, and why every strategy works upstream instead of at the tap.

Picture two: the fish tank. Over-feed a tank and it blooms green, goes dark, rots, and suffocates its fish. That is eutrophication in six beats: feed, bloom, dark, die, rot, suffocate. Say the six beats out loud twice and you will never lose that chain again — just make sure the word OXYGEN lands in your answer, because the crash is what kills.

Picture three: the saws. Working for Water, since nineteen ninety-five: teams clearing thirsty alien trees from the mountain catchments, giving stolen water back to the rivers and jobs to tens of thousands. Name, date, problem, method, double dividend — five sentences, full case study.

And the two odd rivers that cut through mountains? One was printed from above onto buried folds — superimposed. One was older than the hills and kept sawing as they rose — antecedent. Who came first: that is the whole trick.

A funnel, a fish tank, a team with saws, and two stubborn rivers. That is the entire lesson. Run the practice questions now, and walk into the geomorphology section knowing the management marks are already yours. You've got this!
