**Mandy:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: superimposed and antecedent drainage sound identical to me — both end with a river in a gorge through mountains. How do I keep them apart in the exam?

**Tutor:** Separate them by asking one question: who was there first, the river or the structure? In superimposed drainage the STRUCTURE is older. The folded mountains already existed, but they lay buried under a young, flat cover of rock, and the river innocently developed its course on that cover — then spent millions of years eroding downward until it was lowered onto the buried folds, keeping its inherited course and sawing through ridges it never chose. Printed from above. In antecedent drainage the RIVER is older — ante means before. The river was already flowing when folding or uplift began raising land across its path, and its downcutting kept pace with the rise, like a bandsaw holding its line while the plank is pushed up into it. Older than the hills. The landform evidence is indeed the same — a discordant river crossing high ground through a gorge — which is exactly why examiners love the pair: the marks are for the mechanism, not the scenery. So write two sentences, each starting with the age relationship: superimposed — structure older, river let down from a vanished cover; antecedent — river older, cutting down while the land rose. Get the ages right and the rest cannot go wrong.

**Mandy:** Next: for eutrophication I wrote that fertiliser poisons the fish, and got almost nothing. What actually kills them?

**Tutor:** The cruel irony of eutrophication is that nothing is poisoned — the fish die of suffocation in water that is over-FED, and the memo pays for that chain, link by link. Walk it. Link one: nutrients — nitrates and phosphates from fertilised fields, failing sewage works and detergents — wash into the dam. They are food, not poison. Link two: algae respond to the feast with explosive growth — the bloom that turns Hartbeespoort pea-green. Link three: the bloom is a curtain; sunlight cannot penetrate, so the submerged plants below die in the dark. Link four: the bloom overshoots its food supply and dies in its turn. Link five: bacteria decompose the mass of dead algae and plants, and decomposition burns oxygen — the dissolved oxygen crashes. Link six: fish and invertebrates, with no oxygen to breathe, suffocate; the dam becomes a stinking, near-lifeless soup. Six links: nutrients, bloom, darkness, die-off, decomposition, oxygen crash. The single word that must appear in your answer is OXYGEN — an eutrophication answer without it scores like a story without an ending. And note the one exception worth a bracket in your mind: blue-green algal blooms can additionally be toxic, but the defining mechanism, the one the definition marks want, is the oxygen crash.

**Mandy:** Here is one from the group: why is fixing a river so hard? Surely you just clean the water at the dam before the town drinks it?

**Tutor:** Treating at the dam is treating the symptom at the most expensive possible point, and the whole logic of catchment management is to walk upstream and treat causes instead. Remember the master principle: everything that happens anywhere in the funnel ends up in the river, so the river's problems are made across thousands of square kilometres — a leaking sewage works in one town, fertilised fields in another district, overgrazed slopes bleeding silt in a third, alien wattle drinking the headwaters in a fourth. A purification plant at the dam must then fight all of it at once, forever, at rising cost — and it can do nothing about silt filling the dam or streamflow stolen before the water arrives. Upstream repair is cheaper and permanent: fix the sewage works so nutrients never enter; buffer the rivers with vegetated strips that trap fertiliser; restore wetlands, which filter and sponge for free; correct grazing so the soil stays put; clear the invasive trees so the water arrives at all. That is also why the National Water Act organises management by whole catchment areas rather than by town — the polluter and the drinker are usually in different municipalities, and only a basin-wide authority can connect them. In an essay, make that scale argument explicitly: symptom treatment at the tap, cause treatment across the funnel — examiners reward the learner who sees the whole basin.

**Mandy:** Last one: for the case study, is Working for Water really enough, or must I learn a second strategy as backup?

**Tutor:** Working for Water, told properly, is the strongest single answer available, because it hits every criterion the marking guideline lists for a catchment management strategy — named, dated, located, mechanism explained, and evaluated. Build it in five moves. Name and date: Working for Water, a national programme running since nineteen ninety-five. Problem: invasive alien plants — wattle, gum, pine, hakea — infesting mountain catchments and riverbanks, consuming far more water than the indigenous vegetation they displaced, crowding out fynbos and fuelling hotter wildfires. Method: labour-intensive clearing, catchment by catchment — cutting, herbicide and controlled burning, with follow-up visits so regrowth never re-establishes. Results: measured streamflow returned to rivers and dams — the water dividend — plus employment for tens of thousands of people from poor rural communities, with training and many women-led teams — the social dividend. Evaluation: a world-first model copied internationally, though it must run permanently, because cleared catchments reinvade without follow-up. If your teacher prefers a second string, keep Working for Wetlands — same family, rebuilding the catchment's sponges — as a one-sentence companion. But master the five moves of the first story; a case study told with name, date, problem, method and double dividend is full marks in any phrasing of the question.
