# Part 1 — Expert

South Africa is the thirtieth driest country on Earth, and almost every drop its cities drink falls first on a catchment. This session completes the fluvial section with the management half of the syllabus: the two special drainage patterns that ignore the rock beneath them — superimposed and antecedent drainage; why drainage basins and catchment areas matter and why they are so often poorly managed; the four great human impacts on our rivers, with eutrophication at the head of the list; and management strategies, closed with the required South African case study of a catchment management strategy in action.

## Subtopic: Rivers That Ignore the Rock — Superimposed and Antecedent Drainage

Normally a drainage pattern is a conversation between water and rock: dendritic patterns branch tree-like on uniform rock, trellis patterns follow alternating hard and soft bands, radial patterns spill off a dome. The exam's two special cases are rivers whose courses seem to defy the structure they cross — both explained by history rather than by the present rock.

Superimposed drainage: a river establishes its course on a young, soft cover of horizontal rock lying over an older, folded landscape buried beneath. The river knows nothing of the buried structure; it develops its pattern freely on the cover rocks. Then, over millions of years, it erodes down through the cover and lets itself down onto the old structure — and keeps its inherited course, sawing through hard ridges it would never have chosen to cross, cutting gorges straight across the grain of the country. The pattern was superimposed — printed from above — onto a structure it does not match. Southern African rivers slicing through the Cape Fold ranges in dramatic poorts — like the Gouritz cutting through the mountains rather than flowing along the valleys — carry this signature.

Antecedent drainage: here the river is OLDER than the structure. The river is happily flowing when the land begins to rise across its path — slow folding or regional uplift. If the river's downcutting keeps pace with the uplift, it holds its course, sawing through the rising barrier like a bandsaw held against a plank being pushed up into it. The result again is a gorge through high ground — but this time because the river came first, ante, before the mountains.

Distinguish them in one line each, because that is the exam question: superimposed — the river is let down from a vanished cover onto an old buried structure; antecedent — the river predates the structure and cut down as the land rose beneath it. Both leave the same field evidence — a river crossing mountains through a deep gorge instead of flowing around them — and both matter practically: those gorges are dam sites, road corridors and flood pinch-points.

Take this section's questions now: the normal water-rock conversation, the mechanism of each special pattern, and the one-line distinction between them.

## Subtopic: Why Catchments Matter — and Why They Are Poorly Managed

Vocabulary first, because the marks start there. A drainage basin is all the land drained by a river and its tributaries; the catchment area is that same basin seen as a rain-collector — the funnel that gathers precipitation toward the channel. The watershed is the high ground rim separating one basin from the next. Everything that happens anywhere in the funnel — every farm, factory, township, mine and forest — eventually reaches the river. That is the master principle of this whole topic: a river's health is a report card on its entire catchment.

Why drainage basins and catchments matter, in exam-ready points. Water supply: basins are the collection systems for the dams that feed cities — Gauteng drinks from the Vaal's basin and, through the Lesotho Highlands scheme, from the upper Orange. Flood regulation: a healthy catchment, with wetlands and vegetated slopes, absorbs storms like a sponge and releases water slowly; a damaged one sheds water instantly and floods. Ecology: rivers, wetlands and estuaries chain the basin into one living system. Economy: irrigation, industry, power generation and fisheries all draw on the basin's yield. And water security: in a country with an average rainfall of about four hundred and sixty-five millimetres against a world average near eight hundred and sixty, the margin for mismanagement is nil.

Now the causes of poor management, which the syllabus lists explicitly. Fragmented responsibility: a basin ignores political boundaries, but municipalities, provinces and departments each manage their own patch, so no one owns the whole funnel — sewage failures in one town poison a river that three other towns drink. Weak enforcement: laws exist — the National Water Act is world-renowned — but monitoring and prosecution lag. Underfunded infrastructure: broken sewage works discharge untreated waste into rivers daily; the Vaal's sewage crisis is the national example. Competing users: farmers, factories, mines and households pull against each other, and the environment's own share — the ecological reserve — is taken last. And ignorance or short-termism: draining a wetland for building, ploughing to the bank's edge, dumping where the river will fetch it.

Attend to this section's questions before moving on: basin, catchment and watershed defined, the master principle, three reasons catchments matter and three causes of poor management.

## Subtopic: Four Human Impacts — Pollution, Overgrazing, Deforestation, Settlement

Impact one: river pollution, with eutrophication as its star process — learn the chain by heart. Nutrients, chiefly nitrates and phosphates, wash into the river from fertilised fields, leaking sewage works and detergents. The enriched water feeds explosive growth of algae — an algal bloom, often a green scum, and in South African dams the toxic blue-green cyanobacteria. The bloom blocks sunlight, so submerged plants die; then the algae themselves die, and their decomposition by bacteria consumes the water's dissolved oxygen. Oxygen crashes; fish and invertebrates suffocate; the river or dam becomes a green, stinking, near-lifeless soup. That sequence — nutrients, bloom, light blocked, decomposition, oxygen crash, death — is the definition of eutrophication, and Hartbeespoort Dam, pea-green under its blooms and invasive hyacinth mats, is the standing South African example. Beyond eutrophication, add industrial toxins, mine drainage — the acid water seeping from old gold and coal workings, low in pH and loaded with metals — and litter, with plastics riding every urban river to the sea.

Impact two: overgrazing. Too many animals strip the veld; bare soil crusts and seals; infiltration drops and runoff accelerates, carrying topsoil into the channels. The rivers silt up, dams lose capacity year by year, and gully erosion — dongas — dissects the land, bleeding sediment into every storm.

Impact three: deforestation and the loss of natural vegetation. Roots bind soil and slow water; remove trees and fynbos, and slopes shed water fast and dirty. Add the South African twist: invasive alien trees — wattles, gums, pines — lining watercourses actually consume far more water than the fynbos they replaced, stealing measurable percentages of streamflow before it ever reaches a dam.

Impact four: settlement. Cities seal the ground: roofs and tar cannot absorb rain, so storm water rushes to the river in minutes, making floods flashier and droughts deeper. Informal settlements built on floodplains and riverbanks face the water's return with each storm, while inadequate sanitation drains directly to the channel. Wetlands — the basin's kidneys and sponges — are drained and built over. And every additional user tightens the competition for the same limited yield.

The questions on this section are with you now: the eutrophication chain in order, the pathway from overgrazing to silted dams, the alien-vegetation twist, and the three ways settlement injures a river.

## Subtopic: Management Strategies and the Working for Water Case Study

Strategies, organised from the catchment's rim to the river's mouth. Govern the whole basin as one unit: the National Water Act divides the country into water management areas, each meant to be run by a catchment management agency preparing a catchment management strategy — planning by basin, not by municipal boundary, with all users at the table. Protect the source areas: the mountain catchments — the so-called water factories, like the Boland mountains and the Drakensberg — deliver a huge share of national runoff from a tiny share of land; keep them vegetated, fire-managed and free of invasive aliens. Repair the sponge: protect and rehabilitate wetlands — the Working for Wetlands programme rebuilds them — because wetlands filter pollutants, store floodwater and sustain dry-season flow. Control the pollution: fix and upgrade sewage works, enforce discharge licences, treat acid mine drainage, buffer rivers with vegetated strips that trap fertiliser runoff. Manage the land: rotational grazing and correct stocking rates, contour ploughing, keeping ploughs and buildings off riverbanks and floodplains. Manage the demand: fix leaks, price water honestly, recycle, and reserve the ecological flow the river itself needs. And educate: a catchment is managed by everyone who lives in it, or by no one.

The required case study: the Working for Water programme, running since nineteen ninety-five, is South Africa's flagship catchment management strategy. The problem it attacks: invasive alien plants — wattle, gum, pine, hakea — infest catchments and riverbanks, guzzling groundwater and streamflow, crowding out fynbos, and fuelling fiercer wildfires. The strategy: clear them, catchment by catchment — teams cut, poison and burn invasives from the water factories and the riparian zones, then follow up so regrowth never wins. The double dividend that made it famous: each hectare cleared returns stolen water to the rivers — measured gains in streamflow — while the labour-intensive clearing employs tens of thousands of people from poor rural communities, training crews, including women-led teams, in a country hungry for jobs. Water security plus poverty relief in one budget line: the model answer to an exam question on sustainable catchment management, and a genuine world first that other countries copied. Name it, date it, state the problem, the method and the double dividend, and the case-study marks are complete.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: strategies from rim to mouth, the Act and its catchment agencies, and the Working for Water story told in five sentences.

## Subtopic: The Funnel Everyone Lives In

Start with the picture that runs this whole topic: a giant funnel. Every river sits at the bottom of one. The funnel is all the land that leans toward that river — every slope, farm, town and parking lot whose rainwater, given time, ends up in that channel. Geographers call the funnel a drainage basin, or, thinking of it as a rain-catcher, a catchment area. The rim of the funnel — the high ground where a drop of rain must choose which river to join — is the watershed.

One rule follows, and it is the most important sentence of the lesson: whatever happens anywhere in the funnel ends up in the river. Spill oil in a parking lot at the top of the funnel; it is in the river by Friday. Fertilise a field; the leftover feeds the river. The river is a report card for its whole funnel — you can read the behaviour of an entire region in one glass of its river water.

Why care so much? Because South Africa is a dry country — our rainfall is roughly half the world average — and the funnels are our water supply. Johannesburg does not drink from a hole in the ground; it drinks from the Vaal's funnel and, through giant tunnels, from Lesotho's mountains. A healthy funnel also works like a sponge: its wetlands and grassy slopes soak up a storm and release it slowly, so the river runs steady instead of flooding on Tuesday and drying up by month-end.

So why are our funnels in trouble? Because nobody owns a whole funnel. A basin crosses town, province and even country borders, and each authority manages only its own patch — the sewage works of one town fails, and three towns downstream drink the consequences. Add tired infrastructure, weak policing of good water laws, too many users pulling at the same tap, and the everyday carelessness of building on wetlands and dumping where the river will collect it.

And one quick nod to the map paper: some rivers cut straight through mountain ranges in deep gorges instead of going around. Two histories explain it — either the river was lowered down from a vanished upper layer of rock onto mountains it never knew were there, which is superimposed drainage, or the river was there first and kept sawing while the mountains rose underneath it, which is antecedent drainage. Cover removed, or mountains raised: printed from above, or older than the hills.

Your questions for this section are with you now: the funnel, its rim and its rule, why a dry country lives off its funnels, why nobody manages the whole thing, and the two gorge histories.

## Subtopic: The Fish Tank That Turned Green

Now the star process of the pollution story, told with a fish tank. Suppose you feed your fish too generously every day. The extra food does not vanish — it dissolves, and the tank's water becomes rich, over-fed water. Green algae cannot believe their luck: they bloom until the glass turns pea-soup green. Then the trouble compounds: the green murk blocks the light, the plants below die, the algae boom exhausts itself and dies too, and the dead material rots. Rotting uses up oxygen. The fish rise to the surface, gasping, and one morning the tank is silent.

That exact story, scaled up to a dam, is eutrophication — the word simply means over-feeding. The fish food is nutrients: nitrates and phosphates washing in from fertilised farms, from leaking and broken sewage works, from detergents. The bloom, the blocked light, the rot, the oxygen crash, the dead fish — same chain, same order. Learn it as six beats: feed, bloom, dark, die, rot, suffocate. South Africa's famous green tank is Hartbeespoort Dam, downstream of Johannesburg's overloaded rivers — pea-green blooms, floating mats of water hyacinth, fish kills. When the exam says describe eutrophication, tell the fish-tank story in the river's terms, six beats in order.

The other bullies in the pollution story take one sentence each: factories add poisons; old mines leak acid water laced with metals — acid mine drainage; and the cities send plastic down every stormwater drain.

Then three more ways we hurt the funnel. Too many cattle and goats eat the veld bare — bare soil seals like a brick floor, rain races off it, and the topsoil goes with it, silting the dams and carving dongas. Chopping out trees and natural plants does the same — roots were the soil's grip — with the nasty local twist that thirsty ALIEN trees, wattles and gums crowding our riverbanks, drink far more water than the fynbos they pushed out, literally stealing river flow. And the city itself: tar and roofs cannot drink, so storms become sudden brown floods; shacks pushed onto floodplains stand in the water's path; and the wetlands that used to sponge it all up have been drained and built over.

Take the questions for this section now: the six beats of the green tank, the bare-soil chain from overgrazing to silted dams, the thirsty alien trees, and the sealed city. Tell the stories in order and the marks queue up.

## Subtopic: Saws, Sponges and the Water Factories

Time to fix the funnel — and South Africa owns one of the world's best true stories about doing exactly that.

First, the principles, from the top of the funnel down. Guard the water factories: a small fraction of our land — the high mountain catchments of the Boland and the Drakensberg — produces a huge share of our river water; keep those slopes healthy and everything downstream benefits. Repair the sponges: wetlands filter dirt, swallow floods and dribble water out through the dry months — stop draining them, start rebuilding them. Police the pipes: fix the sewage works, make factories clean what they discharge, treat the acid leaking from old mines, and keep a strip of natural plants along every river as a living filter that traps farm chemicals before they reach the water. Farm gently: fewer animals, rested paddocks, plough along the contours, and never to the river's edge. Use less: fix the leaks, recycle, and always leave the river its own share of water — a river squeezed dry is not a supply system, it is a corpse. And run the funnel as one team: our water law divides the country into catchment areas, each meant to be managed whole — everyone in the funnel at one table, because the river does not care whose municipality the pollution came from.

Now the story for your case study: Working for Water, running since nineteen ninety-five. The villain: alien trees — wattle, gum, pine — that have invaded our mountain catchments and riverbanks. They are thirstier than anything that belongs here; a valley full of wattle is a valley drinking its own river. The plan was beautifully simple: hire people to cut them down. Teams with saws and herbicide move through a catchment, clear the invaders, then return to beat the regrowth until the fynbos holds the slope again. And the plan pays twice. Once in water: cleared catchments measurably give their stolen streamflow back to the rivers and dams. And once in people: the work is labour-hungry, so the programme employs tens of thousands from poor rural communities — many crews led by women — turning a water project into one of the country's biggest job programmes. Water plus jobs, one budget: that double win is why the world copied it, and why it is the model answer whenever the exam asks for a South African catchment management strategy. Say it in five sentences: the name, the date, the thirsty alien problem, the clearing method, and the double dividend.

Here come the final questions of the lesson, right now: the fixes from factory to tap, the one-team principle, and the Working for Water story with both of its payoffs. Take them, and the whole river section — processes, dramas and management — is complete.
