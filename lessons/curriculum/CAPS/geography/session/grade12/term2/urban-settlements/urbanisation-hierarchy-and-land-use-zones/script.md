# Part 1 — Expert

More than half of humanity now lives in urban settlements, and about two thirds of South Africans do. This session covers the urban half of settlement geography in four moves: how urban settlement began and how the world urbanised; the precise vocabulary of urban growth that examiners test as definitions; site, situation and the functional classification of towns — central places, break-of-bulk points, junction, gateway and gap towns, specialised towns; and finally the internal anatomy of a city — the urban hierarchy and the land-use zones you must be able to identify from a description, a map extract or an aerial photograph. Every zone is described here in words, as a journey you can hear, because that is exactly how the exam describes them to you.

## Subtopic: From First Villages to an Urban World

Urban settlement has a birth story, and the exam expects its logic rather than its dates. Cities became possible only when farming produced a surplus — more food than the farmers themselves needed. A surplus frees some people from growing food, and freed people become traders, craftsmen, priests, soldiers and rulers, who gather at defensible, well-watered crossing points to exchange and to govern. The first true urban settlements grew exactly there — in river valleys such as the Nile, the Tigris-Euphrates and the Indus, where irrigation farming produced reliable surpluses. The recipe is worth stating as a formula: surplus plus specialisation plus exchange equals a town.

For most of history the urban share of humanity stayed tiny. The industrial revolution changed the gradient: factories concentrated work in towns, and towns exploded. The world crossed the halfway line — more than half of all people urban — early in the twenty-first century, and the urbanising frontier now runs through Africa and Asia, where the fastest urban growth on earth is happening. Note the pattern examiners probe: developed countries urbanised early and slowly, and their levels are now high but their rates are slow; developing countries are urbanising late and fast — lower levels still, but rapid rates. South Africa sits in between: roughly two thirds urban and still climbing, with Gauteng the great magnet.

Why does it matter? Because urbanisation concentrates opportunity and strain in the same places: jobs, universities and hospitals on one hand; housing shortages, informal settlements, traffic and service backlogs on the other. That double face returns in every urban question that follows.

Pause here and take this section's questions: the surplus recipe, the river-valley cradle, and the level-versus-rate contrast between developed and developing worlds.

## Subtopic: The Vocabulary of Urban Growth

CAPS lists seven terms, and Paper 1 tests them as clean definitions and as distinctions between near-twins. Learn each with its separator.

Urbanisation is the increasing PERCENTAGE of a country's population living in urban areas. It is a proportion, not a headcount — that is the trap. Urban growth is the increase in the actual NUMBER of people living in urban areas. The two can separate: if towns and countryside grow at the same pace, cities gain people — urban growth — while the urban percentage stands still — no urbanisation. Any question opposing these two is testing percentage against number, nothing more.

Rate of urbanisation is the SPEED at which the urban percentage is rising — high in developing countries today. Level of urbanisation is the percentage itself at a moment in time — high in developed countries. Speed against snapshot.

Urban expansion is the physical spreading of the built-up area — the city taking in more land as it grows outward. Urban sprawl is expansion's ugly cousin: low-density, unplanned or poorly planned outward spread — housing estates, malls and ribbon development leapfrogging along highways, eating farmland and forcing long commutes. All sprawl is expansion; not all expansion is sprawl — planned, compact growth expands without sprawling.

Counter-urbanisation is the reverse flow: people moving out of large cities to small towns and rural areas — retirees and remote workers relocating to Western Cape coastal towns or Midlands villages — usually chasing quality of life, cheaper property and safety, enabled by cars and connectivity.

This section's questions are ready: percentage against number, speed against snapshot, expansion against sprawl, and the direction of counter-urbanisation.

## Subtopic: Site, Situation and the Functional Classification of Towns

Site and situation carry into the urban world unchanged: site is the land the town is built on; situation is its position relative to routes, resources and other places. Cape Town's site is the sheltered ground beneath Table Mountain beside a natural bay; its situation — the resupply point on the sea route around Africa — is what made it worth building. Johannesburg has a famously poor site — no harbour, no navigable river, thin highveld streams — and a situation that overruled it: the town sits on the gold of the Witwatersrand. When an exam asks why a town is where it is, answer site first, then situation, and say which one dominated.

Functional classification sorts towns by the work they do, and each type earns its own definition. A central place is a settlement whose main function is to provide goods and services to the surrounding region — its sphere of influence. Small central places offer low-order goods people buy often — bread, groceries, basic services; large central places add high-order goods bought rarely — specialist medicine, universities, corporate law. A Karoo dorp serving its farming district is a central place in miniature.

Trade and transport towns live where movement changes. A break-of-bulk point is where cargo must be transferred from one mode of transport to another — ship to rail, rail to road — and Durban, the busiest port in southern Africa, is the textbook case: goods break bulk on its docks, and industry gathers to process them where they land. A junction town grows where routes meet or cross — De Aar grew as a railway junction. A gateway town controls entry between regions — Musina beside the Beitbridge border post is South Africa's gate to Zimbabwe. A gap town commands a natural passage through a mountain barrier — Worcester and Ceres sit at passes through the Cape Fold Mountains, collecting the traffic the mountains funnel.

Specialised towns do one thing famously: mining towns like Rustenburg and Kimberley; industrial towns like Sasolburg, built around fuel-from-coal; resort and tourism towns like Margate or Clarens; education towns like Makhanda with its university and schools; administrative capitals like Pretoria. Name the function, name the town — that pairing is the mark.

Take the questions for this section now: site against situation with the Cape Town and Johannesburg contrast, the central place and its sphere of influence, and the five trade-and-transport and specialised types with a South African example each.

## Subtopic: Urban Hierarchy and Land-Use Zones: The Anatomy of a City

The urban hierarchy ranks urban settlements by size and by the order of services they offer, and it obeys a see-saw rule: many small places offering low-order services, few large places offering high-order ones. At the base, hundreds of villages and small towns with shops and primary schools; in the middle, regional towns with hospitals and branch offices; at the top, a handful of metropolises — Johannesburg, Cape Town, eThekwini — offering everything, including services found nowhere else. The higher the order of a service, the larger the sphere of influence needed to support it, so the fewer the places that can offer it. That single sentence answers every hierarchy question.

Now walk the land-use zones, from the middle of the city outwards — listen to the journey, because you must recognise each zone from words alone. You begin in the central business district, the CBD: the historical and functional heart. Land here is the most expensive in the city, so buildings grow tall to multiply floor space on costly ground; the skyline peaks here. Offices, banks, department stores and specialist shops pack together; streets are congested by day, and because few people can afford to live here, the zone empties at night. Highest land values, tallest buildings, greatest accessibility — all public transport converges here.

Step outward and you cross into the transition zone, the ring around the CBD where the city is rebuilding itself. Old houses subdivided into flats and offices, light industry, ageing buildings awaiting demolition or renewal, some decay and some gentrification side by side. It is a zone of mixed, changing land use — the city's construction site.

Beyond it, the residential zones arrange themselves by income, and here South Africa's history speaks loudly. In the classic model, lower-income housing lies nearer the factories and the older inner city, and income rises outward to leafy high-income suburbs. Apartheid planning deformed this pattern: townships — Soweto, Khayelitsha, KwaMashu — were deliberately placed on the far urban edge, separated from white suburbs by buffer strips, railways and industrial land, so many low-income South Africans live far from work and carry the longest, costliest commutes. Informal settlements attach themselves where land is available — on the edges and along transport routes.

Industry claims its own zones: heavy industry along railways, highways and harbours where bulk transport runs, on flat land away from high-value housing; light industry and modern industrial parks near main roads and airports. And out along the arterials and at suburban crossroads, decentralised nodes have grown — shopping malls and office parks like Sandton, Century City and Gateway — pulling CBD functions outward to where the high-income customers and the highways are, leaving some old CBDs to decline and forcing their renewal projects.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: the see-saw rule of the hierarchy, the CBD's signature trio of land value, height and accessibility, the transition zone's mixed change, the apartheid deformation of the residential gradient, and the pull of the decentralised nodes.

## Subtopic: Why Cities Exist and Why Everyone Is Moving to Them

Start with a question that sounds silly: why are there towns at all? Because one day, long ago, farmers grew MORE food than their families could eat. That extra — the surplus — is the whole secret. Once there is extra food, not everyone has to farm. Someone can make pots full-time, someone can trade, someone can run things. And those non-farmers gather in one convenient spot — a river crossing, a safe hill, a harbour — to do business with each other. Extra food, split-up jobs, a market: press those three together and a town appears. The first ones grew where farming was easiest to do at scale — the great river valleys like the Nile.

For thousands of years towns stayed small and rare. Then factories arrived, and factories are gathering machines: they pull thousands of workers to one gate. Towns became cities, cities became the norm, and somewhere around the early two-thousands the world quietly passed a line — more people in cities than outside them, for the first time ever. South Africa passed its own version long ago: about two out of every three of us are urban now.

One contrast to carry into the exam. Rich countries did their city-building rush long ago — so today their urban LEVEL is high but their SPEED is slow, like a car already parked at the top of the hill. Developing countries, including much of Africa, are in the rush right now — level still climbing, speed high, the car still accelerating up the slope. Level is where you are; rate is how fast you are climbing. Two different questions, two different answers.

Take this section's questions now: the surplus secret, the factory magnet, and the parked car against the climbing car.

## Subtopic: The Ladder of Shops and the Jobs Towns Do

Here is the entire urban hierarchy in one shopping trip. For bread and airtime, you walk to the corner spaza — every neighbourhood has one. For school shoes, you take a taxi to town — fewer of those. For a wedding outfit or a specialist doctor, you travel to the big city — fewer still. And for the most specialised things in the country — a heart transplant, a stock exchange — there is basically one place. Feel the pattern in your feet: the more ordinary the thing, the closer and more common the place that sells it; the more special the thing, the further you travel and the fewer places offer it. Many small, few big — that see-saw is the urban hierarchy, and every hierarchy question is that shopping trip in disguise.

Now the jobs towns do, told as their one-line job descriptions. The central place is the shop-keeper town: it exists to serve the farms and villages around it — its customer area even has a name, the sphere of influence. The break-of-bulk town is the place where cargo changes vehicles: at Durban's docks, goods step off ships and onto trains and trucks, and wherever cargo must be handled anyway, factories set up to process it on the spot. The junction town stands where routes meet, living off everything that passes — De Aar and the railways. The gateway town holds a door between countries — Musina at the Zimbabwe border. The gap town sits in a mountain doorway — where the Cape Fold ranges allow only a few passes, towns like Worcester collect all the traffic the mountains squeeze through. And specialised towns are famous for one thing: Rustenburg mines, Sasolburg makes fuel, Clarens hosts tourists, Makhanda teaches, Pretoria governs.

Site and situation come along unchanged from the rural world, with one great example pair. Cape Town: lovely site — sheltered ground, fresh water, a bay. Johannesburg: terrible site — no river worth the name, no harbour — but a situation sitting on top of the world's richest gold. Towns forgive a bad site for a good enough reason. Say that in an exam and you have understood the whole idea.

Your questions for this section are up: the shopping trip, the six job descriptions, and the city that forgave its site.

## Subtopic: A Taxi Ride Across the Zones

Last picture: board a taxi in the middle of a South African city and ride it out to the edge, and you will pass through every land-use zone in the syllabus, in order. Listen for the changes.

Downtown, where you start, the buildings are tallest and the streets are fullest — banks, head offices, big stores stacked on the most expensive ground in the city. Why tall? Think of it as a tug-of-war over land: everyone wants the most reachable spot, the place all the taxis and trains converge, so the ground there costs a fortune, and the only way to afford a fortune is to stack thirty floors of rent on top of it. That is the CBD: dearest land, tallest buildings, busiest days — and quiet nights, because almost nobody sleeps there.

Pull away from the centre and the city gets scruffy and interesting for a few blocks: old houses carved into flats and offices, small workshops, a building coming down here, a renovated block going up there. Half building site, half waiting room — the transition zone, the ring where the city is busy changing its mind.

Further out, the houses begin, and notice the sad South African truth the window shows: in the model, housing simply gets richer as you ride outward. Here, apartheid rearranged the seats — townships like Soweto and Khayelitsha were pushed right out to the far edge, with railway lines, empty buffer strips and factory belts placed between them and the old suburbs. So the people with the least money were given the longest, most expensive ride to work — a planning wound the taxi fare still measures every morning.

You will also pass the working zones: factory districts stretched along the railway lines and highways — heavy industry needs flat ground and bulk transport, and nobody wants it next to the garden suburbs — and nearer the airport, neat modern industrial parks. And before the city finally thins out, one more stop: a giant mall with office towers around it — Sandton, Gateway, Century City. These decentralised nodes are pieces of the CBD that moved out to chase the highways and the high-income suburbs, which is exactly why some old downtowns struggle and need renewal projects.

Here come the final questions of the lesson: the tug-of-war that builds towers, the waiting-room ring, the rearranged seats, the factory belts, and the mall that ate the CBD's lunch. Ride the taxi in your head one more time, and any zone the exam describes in words will announce itself before the sentence even ends.
