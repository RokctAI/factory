**Mandy:** Questions that come up a lot with this topic! First: urbanisation versus urban growth. I know one is a percentage and one is a number, but the exam examples still catch me. Can you make the difference bulletproof?

**Tutor:** Make it bulletproof with a two-column test you can run on any scenario in ten seconds. Column one: what happened to the NUMBER of people in cities? Column two: what happened to the PERCENTAGE of the whole population that is urban? Urban growth lives entirely in column one; urbanisation lives entirely in column two. Now run the three scenarios examiners rotate. Scenario A: rural people stream into Gauteng while the countryside empties. Numbers up, percentage up — both urban growth AND urbanisation. Scenario B: city families have many children, and rural families do too, at the same pace. City numbers rise — urban growth — but the urban share of the population stands still — no urbanisation. Scenario C, the sneaky one: rural areas lose population to emigration abroad while city numbers stay flat. The urban percentage rises with not one extra person in the cities — urbanisation without urban growth. If you can explain scenario C, you own the distinction, because it proves the percentage can move on its own. And attach the other pair while you are there: LEVEL of urbanisation is the percentage right now, a snapshot; RATE is how fast that percentage is changing, a speed. Developed countries: high level, low rate. Developing countries: lower level, high rate. Four terms, two columns, one snapshot, one speedometer.

**Mandy:** Next: functional classification. Break-of-bulk, junction, gateway, gap — they all sound like "town on a route". How do I keep four route-towns apart in a written exam?

**Tutor:** Give each one a different question that it answers, because they really are four different route events. Break-of-bulk asks: does the CARGO change vehicles here? The defining event is transfer — ship to rail, rail to road — and the giveaway in any source is a port, harbour, docks or container terminal; Durban is the anchor example, and the bonus mark is knowing that industry gathers there because goods being handled anyway are cheap to process on the spot. Junction asks: do ROUTES meet or cross here? No border, no mountain, just convergence — De Aar living off its railway crossing. Gateway asks: does this town control ENTRY between two regions or countries? The giveaway is a border post — Musina at Beitbridge, holding the door to Zimbabwe. Gap asks: does this town sit in a natural PASSAGE through a barrier? The giveaway is mountains with a pass — Worcester collecting the traffic the Cape Fold ranges funnel. So interrogate the source: see docks, say break-of-bulk; see crossing routes, say junction; see a border, say gateway; see a pass through mountains, say gap. And always attach the South African example — the classification mark and the example mark are usually printed separately in the memo.

**Mandy:** The land-use zone questions describe a place in words and ask me to name the zone. What are the trigger words I should listen for in each description?

**Tutor:** Every zone has a fingerprint of three or four trigger phrases, and the setters use them with surprising loyalty. CBD: highest land values, tall or multi-storey buildings, congestion, all transport routes converging, daytime crowds but empty at night — any two of those and you can commit. Transition zone: the vocabulary of change and mixture — old subdivided buildings, light industry beside housing, decay, demolition, renewal, gentrification; if the description sounds like a place that cannot decide what it is, that indecision IS the answer. Residential zones: the question is usually WHICH residential type, so listen for density and position — small plots, high density, far from the CBD, near a railway or industrial buffer says township or low-income housing, and in the South African city you should name the apartheid planning behind that position; large plots, gardens, security estates on the outer suburbs says high-income. Informal settlement: unplanned, lacking services, on the urban edge or along routes, vulnerable land. Industrial zone: flat land, railways and highways, harbours, smoke or bulk storage — and split heavy industry near rail and harbour from light industry near main roads and airports. Decentralised node: mall, office park, highway interchange, far from the old centre — Sandton is the name to drop, plus the consequence: functions leaving the CBD cause its decline and force renewal. Train your ear on those fingerprints and the zone names itself before the question finishes asking.

**Mandy:** Last one: essay questions ask me to discuss how apartheid shaped the structure of South African cities. I know the facts, but how do I organise them so it reads like geography and not history?

**Tutor:** Organise it spatially, zone by zone, so every historical fact lands as a feature on the map of today's city. Open with the model: in the classic city, land use is arranged by distance from the centre, and income rises outward in the residential gradient. Then show the deformation, moving outward exactly as the taxi ride does. The centre: apartheid kept CBD ownership and prime access racially exclusive, and the Group Areas Act cleared mixed inner-city communities — District Six is the named example that always earns its mark. The middle ring: buffer strips, industrial belts and railway lines were deliberately placed as separators between group areas — empty space used as a wall. The edge: townships — Soweto, Khayelitsha, KwaMashu — positioned far from the centre and from work, creating the long, expensive commutes that still define the daily geography of millions; dormitory settlements with too few local jobs and services. Then bring it to the present tense, because the question says shaped, and the marker wants continuity: the pattern persists — post-apartheid, low-cost housing has often still been built on cheap peripheral land, informal settlements attach to the same edges, and decentralised nodes in historically advantaged suburbs pull investment further from where most people live. Close by naming the repair attempts: urban renewal of CBDs, infill housing on buffer land, transport corridors like bus rapid transit linking township to centre. Structure it centre, ring, edge, present, repair — five paragraphs, every fact pinned to a place — and it reads as geography from the first line.
