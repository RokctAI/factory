**Mandy:** Let's take the class questions! First one: in a written answer, can I just list six push factors and six pull factors and collect the marks, or is a list not enough?

**Tutor:** A list earns the naming marks and forfeits everything above them, and this topic is mostly marked above them. Think of the difference between naming ingredients and explaining a recipe. The question behind every migration question is mechanism: two forces acting on one person at one moment, and movement happening when their sum beats the comfort of staying. So structure the answer as that mechanism. Open with the definition — movement from rural to urban settlements. Then give the push side as conditions at home that drive people out, and make each factor a small cause-and-effect sentence rather than a word: not "unemployment", but "mechanised farms shed labour, so rural work is disappearing". Then the pull side as attractions, and here plant the phrase that distinguishes a top answer: real or believed. Migrants move on the perception of city opportunity, and the city often under-delivers — many arrive to joblessness and a shack on the fringe. Finally add selectivity: it is the young, economically active who go, historically men first under the migrant labour system. Definition, mechanised push, believed pull, selectivity — four moves, each one a sentence or two. That is the same six factors you wanted to list, but arranged as an engine instead of an inventory, and the engine is what the marks are attached to.

**Mandy:** Next question: why does the lesson keep insisting that depopulation is a spiral? Isn't it enough to say lots of people left?

**Tutor:** Because "lots of people left" describes the total, while the spiral explains why the leaving cannot stop on its own — and explanation is where this topic pays. Walk the machinery. Services survive on thresholds: a school needs enough learners, a bank branch enough customers, a shop enough buyers. Out-migration pushes each service below its threshold in turn. The school merges away; now every remaining family with children has a new, powerful reason to go. Their departure drops the bank below its threshold; the branch closes; now pensioners and small businesses have their reason. Each closure manufactures the next round of push factors, so the process feeds itself — a loop, not a line. Two consequences follow for your answers. First, when asked for causes of depopulation, include the structural pair — farm mechanisation and consolidation, and service closure — because they operate even when no drought or crisis does. Second, when asked for strategies, aim them at the loop: every service installed or restored is a reason-to-leave deleted, which is why electrification or a staffed clinic is an anti-depopulation measure and not merely a nice thing. If you can draw the circle — people leave, service closes, more people leave — with three arrows in a margin sketch, do it. A drawn spiral often communicates the mechanism faster than a paragraph, and it shows the marker you grasp the difference between an amount and a process.

**Mandy:** This one's from the group chat: tenure, redistribution, restitution — I know all three definitions separately, but in scenario questions I still pick the wrong one. Is there a foolproof sorting question?

**Tutor:** There is a two-question sorter that never misses. Question one: do the people in the scenario need to STAY where they already are, or to GET land they do not have? If the scenario is about staying — a farm-dweller family threatened with eviction, households on communal land with nothing in writing — you are in tenure reform, full stop. Tenure moves nobody; it converts a fragile permission into a secure right, the farmyard promise into a signed contract. If the scenario is about getting land, ask question two: does anyone name a SPECIFIC piece of land and a SPECIFIC removal after the nineteenth of June nineteen thirteen? If yes — our families were removed from that valley, under those laws, and we can prove it — that is restitution: a named place, a proven wrong, a return or a payout, running through the restitution commission and the Land Claims Court. If no — the scenario simply wants ownership spread more fairly going forward, an emerging farmer assisted to buy a farm, no historical claim to that particular ground — that is redistribution. Notice what the sorter really tests: tenure is about the strength of existing rights, restitution is about the past, redistribution is about the future. Stay or get; named wrong or fair future. Run those two questions and the scenario sorts itself before you finish reading it.

**Mandy:** Last one: essay questions ask me to evaluate strategies against depopulation. How do I avoid just writing a wish list — build clinics, create jobs, everyone stays?

**Tutor:** By giving every proposal a target, a mechanism and a limitation — three parts that turn a wish into an evaluation. The target: name the push factor the measure attacks, because that is the lesson's central test — a strategy succeeds when it switches off a reason to leave. The mechanism: say how the switch-off happens. An agri-processing depot does not vaguely help — it moves the value-adding step, and therefore the wages, from the city to the village; a maintained road does not vaguely help — it cuts the cost and time of reaching the clinic, the market and the college, weakening three push factors with one budget. The limitation: no measure works alone or instantly. Tourism wages are seasonal and depend on scenery not every district has; public works jobs end when the project ends; a staffed clinic holds families but cannot employ the school leavers. Then close with the deepest point in the topic: the strongest long-term strategy is land reform itself, because secure rights on productive land — the tenure leg holding families where they are, the redistribution leg opening farming livelihoods — delivers the very thing migration promises, a living, without the one-way taxi. An evaluation built as target, mechanism, limitation, and crowned with that connection, reads like analysis rather than hope — and analysis is what the top levels of the rubric are reserved for.
