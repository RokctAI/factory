# Part 1 — Expert

The rural settlement question with the highest mark weighting is not about shapes or patterns — it is about people leaving. This session works through the machinery of rural–urban migration: the push and pull factors that drive it, rural depopulation with its causes, consequences and counter-strategies, the social justice issues that keep rural poverty in place, and the three-legged land reform programme — tenure, redistribution, restitution — that the examiners test every single year. The running example is the one South Africa knows best: the emptying villages of the Eastern Cape and the young workers they send to Gauteng and Cape Town.

## Subtopic: Push and Pull: The Engine of Rural–Urban Migration

Rural–urban migration is the movement of people from rural settlements to urban settlements, and the exam wants it explained as a balance of two forces acting on the same person at the same time.

Push factors are the conditions at home that drive people out. In the South African countryside the list is concrete: unemployment, because mechanised commercial farms need fewer workers every decade and communal plots cannot support growing families; poor services — under-resourced schools, distant clinics, gravel roads, unreliable water and electricity; drought and land degradation shrinking what farming can pay; small and fragmented plots that cannot be subdivided again for the next generation; and the sheer absence of choice — few jobs beyond the farm, little tertiary education, thin entertainment and opportunity for the young.

Pull factors are the real or believed attractions of the city: jobs and higher wages, better schools and universities, hospitals, electricity and piped water, the bright-lights draw of urban life, and the pull of family members who moved earlier and can offer a first bed and a first contact. Note the phrase real or believed — the exam rewards you for observing that migrants often move on the perception of opportunity, and the city does not always deliver; many arrive to unemployment and informal settlement life on the urban edge.

Who moves matters as much as why. It is overwhelmingly the young, economically active adults — school leavers and workers in their twenties and thirties — and historically in South Africa, men first, through the migrant labour system that took workers to the mines while families stayed behind. That selectivity is the key that unlocks the next subtopic, because when the young and able leave, what remains behind is a settlement out of balance.

Pause here and take this section's questions: push against pull, the perception point, and the selectivity of who actually boards the taxi.

## Subtopic: Rural Depopulation: Causes and Consequences

Rural depopulation is the decline of the population living in rural areas — the visible result of sustained out-migration outrunning natural increase. Its causes are the push factors already on the table, plus two structural ones the memorandum loves: the mechanisation and consolidation of commercial agriculture, which replaces farm workers with machines and merges small farms into large ones; and the closure of rural services — when a school, bank branch, post office or police station shuts, it pushes the next round of families out, which closes the next service, a downward spiral feeding itself.

The consequences divide neatly into what happens to the people who stay and what happens to the place. The people first: the age–sex structure collapses into a hollowed-out pyramid — children, the elderly, and in the old migrant-labour pattern, women, remain, while the working-age middle is missing. Households depend on social grants and remittances — money sent home by family members working in the cities. The dependency ratio climbs: few workers supporting many dependants. Skills drain away, because the ambitious and educated leave first — the term is brain drain — and leadership, teachers and nurses become hard to keep.

Now the place: fields go out of cultivation and grazing land is underused or, around the remaining settlement, overused; buildings stand empty and decay; schools close for lack of learners; shops lose their customers and follow; and the tax and consumer base that keeps a small town's businesses alive evaporates. Some Karoo and Eastern Cape towns show the end state — a main street of closed shopfronts serving a shrinking, aging population.

Balance the account before you move on, because evaluation questions demand it: out-migration is not purely loss. Remittances flow back and build houses; pressure on overworked communal land can ease; and returning migrants sometimes bring skills and savings home. The full picture — heavy costs, modest compensations — is what a top answer sounds like.

This section's questions are with you now: the definition, the two structural causes, the hollowed-out population structure, and the balanced evaluation.

## Subtopic: Strategies, Social Justice and Access to Resources

If depopulation is a spiral, strategies are attempts to break it, and the exam asks for them as practical measures, each aimed at a specific push factor.

Aim at unemployment: rural development programmes that create work where people live — agri-processing plants that add value to local produce instead of railing it away raw, public works programmes building and maintaining rural roads and water schemes, and support for small-scale farmers with finance, extension services and access to markets. Aim at services: building and staffing rural schools and clinics, electrification, piped water, and maintaining the roads that connect villages to towns — because every service that arrives removes a reason to leave. Aim at opportunity: tourism development — game farms, cultural tourism, hiking routes — that turns rural landscapes into rural incomes. The one-line logic that should open or close your answer: strategies succeed when they attack the push factors, because people rarely leave home when home offers a living.

Now the social justice layer, which CAPS names directly. Rural poverty in South Africa is not an accident of geography; it carries the imprint of apartheid planning. Access to natural resources — productive land and water — was racially skewed for a century: the Natives Land Act of 1913 confined black land ownership to a small fraction of the country, and the former homelands concentrated large populations on the least productive land. Access to human-made resources is skewed the same way: rural areas received, and many still receive, less investment in schools, clinics, roads, communication networks and financial services. Limited investment and lack of infrastructure are not just background facts — they are themselves push factors, and they explain why depopulation runs hardest in the former homeland districts. When an exam question asks why rural poverty persists, the strong answer connects history to geography: the map of today's poorest rural districts is, to a large degree, the map apartheid drew.

Take the questions for this section now: strategies matched to the push factors they attack, and the historical roots of unequal access to resources.

## Subtopic: Land Reform: Tenure, Redistribution, Restitution

Land reform is the state's programme to correct the racially skewed pattern of land ownership and land rights left by the 1913 Land Act and apartheid. The examiners test it as three distinct legs, and the marks are lost by learners who blur them. Learn each leg as its own sentence: what it fixes, and how.

Leg one: land tenure reform. Tenure means the terms on which you hold land — the security of your right to be there. Millions of South Africans occupy land without secure rights: labour tenants on commercial farms, families on communal land administered by traditional authorities, residents with permits rather than title. Tenure reform upgrades the security of those rights — protecting farm dwellers from arbitrary eviction, formalising rights on communal land — so that the people using land cannot simply be removed from it, and can invest in it with confidence. Tenure reform moves no one; it strengthens the rights of people already on the land.

Leg two: land redistribution. This leg changes who owns land. It aims to transfer farmland from the white commercial sector to black South Africans — emerging farmers, labour tenants, rural households — through purchase and state support, historically on the willing-buyer, willing-seller principle. Redistribution is forward-looking: it does not ask who owned a specific farm in the past; it asks who should have access to farmland now, for equity and livelihoods.

Leg three: land restitution. This leg looks backwards at specific wrongs. People or communities dispossessed of land after 19 June 1913 by racially discriminatory laws — the forced removals, the cleared districts — can claim that specific land back, or financial compensation instead. Restitution runs through the Land Claims Court and the Commission on Restitution of Land Rights. The distinguishing feature: a restitution claim names a particular piece of land and a particular historical removal.

The separator that earns full marks: tenure secures the rights of those on the land; redistribution transfers land to redress unequal ownership; restitution returns specific land taken by specific past removals — or compensates for it. And the settlement geography connection that closes the loop: successful land reform is itself a rural depopulation strategy, because secure rights and productive land give rural people what migration promises — a livelihood — without leaving home.

The closing questions of this part are with you now: the three legs kept separate, the 1913 anchor date, and the link from land reform back to depopulation.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same topic told the way it actually happens — one family, one village, one decision at the kitchen table.

## Subtopic: The Kitchen-Table Decision

Forget the terminology for a moment and sit at the table where the decision is made. A twenty-two-year-old in an Eastern Cape village has finished matric. The family plot is small — it fed the grandparents, but it cannot pay for data, transport, clothes and a future. The nearest work is seasonal. The clinic is a taxi ride away, and the taxi runs when it runs. In Gauteng there is a cousin with a bed available, stories of jobs, and better wages even for the same work.

Everything pressing on that young person from behind — no work, thin services, a plot too small to split again, drought making the mealies unreliable — geographers call push factors. They shove. Everything shining from the far end — jobs, colleges, hospitals, electricity that stays on, the cousin's open door — those are pull factors. They tug. Migration happens when the shove and the tug, added together, beat the comfort of staying home.

One honest detail makes your answers ring true: the tug is partly a story. The city promises more than it pays. Many arrive to find no job and a shack on the urban edge — but the promise, believed, is enough to move a person. Examiners reward you for saying that migrants move on perception as much as reality.

And notice WHO is at the table. Not the grandmother — she stays. Not the small children — they stay too, often with her. It is the young and strong who go, the way it has been since the mines first sent buses for workers. Hold that picture; the whole next section grows from it.

Take this section's questions now: the shove, the tug, the story the city tells, and who actually packs the bag.

## Subtopic: The Village After the Taxi Leaves

Now stand in the village a few years later and count who is left at the bus stop on a Monday morning: schoolchildren, pensioners, and mostly women running households. The working-age middle has gone. Geographers draw this as a population pyramid with its waist pinched in — the shape alone tells the story of a place the young have left.

Watch how one leaving causes the next. The school loses learners each year until the department merges it with a school two villages away. The bank branch closes — not enough customers. Then the post office. Every closed door removes another reason to stay, so the next family goes, which closes the next door. It is a spiral, and it feeds itself. That is rural depopulation: not one dramatic event but a slow leak, the way a football goes soft.

Money still arrives — sent home from Joburg and Cape Town. Those remittances, plus the grandmother's pension and the child grants, hold the household together, and you can see the Joburg money in the new brick rooms going up beside old homesteads. So be fair when you evaluate: the leak sends something back through the pipe. But a village cannot live on sent money forever — fields go unploughed, and the shopkeeper cannot bank remittances that get spent in town.

How do you patch the leak? Aim at the holes. No jobs? Bring work to the village — a plant that turns local milk or wool or fruit into products, road-building teams hired locally, real support for small farmers so the plot pays again. Thin services? Build and staff the clinic, electrify, fix the road — every service that arrives is one less reason to go. Nothing for the young? Tourism can turn scenery, heritage and hiking country into wages. The test for any strategy is one question: which push factor does this switch off?

Your questions for this section are up: the pinched pyramid, the self-feeding spiral, the honest balance sheet, and matching each fix to its hole.

## Subtopic: Three Repairs for One Old Injustice

Why is the countryside poor in the first place? Not by chance. A century ago the law itself — the 1913 Natives Land Act — squeezed black South Africans onto a small fraction of the land, and apartheid then packed people into homelands on the driest, steepest, least productive ground, and spent the roads-schools-and-clinics money elsewhere. Today's map of rural poverty still traces those old lines. So the state runs a repair programme — land reform — and it has three tools, each fixing a different kind of damage. Keep them apart with three everyday pictures.

Picture one: a family has lived and worked on a farm for three generations, but nothing is in their name — they can be told to leave next month. The repair is not to move them anywhere; it is to make their right to stay strong — paperwork, protection from eviction, secure rights on communal land too. That is TENURE reform: same people, same land, stronger grip. Think of it as changing a handshake agreement into a signed lease.

Picture two: farmland ownership in the district is almost entirely in a few hands, a pattern history built. The repair is to help land change hands — the state assists black farmers to buy farms and start farming, with training and finance. Nobody is claiming a specific old wrong on a specific farm; the point is to share the asset fairly going forward. That is REDISTRIBUTION: new owners onto the land, for fairness and livelihoods.

Picture three: a community can point at a specific valley and say, our families were removed from HERE, in the removals, by those laws — after 1913. They lodge a claim, prove it, and get that land back or money in its place. That is RESTITUTION: a named place, a proven removal, a return or a payout. It is the only leg that looks backwards at one particular wrong.

Grip, share, give back. Tenure strengthens the grip of people already on land; redistribution shares land more fairly; restitution gives back what was specifically taken. Say those three short verbs in the exam and then expand each — that is the structure of a full-mark answer.

And here is the loop that ties the whole lesson closed: land reform, done well, is itself an answer to the kitchen-table decision. A family with secure rights and productive land has what the city was promising — a living — without the one-way taxi. Here come the final questions of the lesson: the three pictures, the three verbs, and the loop back to the village bus stop. Answer them and this high-mark topic is banked.
