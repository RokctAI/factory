**Mandy:** Questions that come up a lot with this one! First: in the exam, if they give me a case study paragraph about a village losing people, how do I know whether they want push–pull factors or depopulation consequences?

**Tutor:** Read the command word and the tense of the question, because the two answers face opposite directions in time. Push and pull factors explain WHY people leave — they come before the migration. Consequences describe what the leaving DOES to the village — they come after. So a question asking why the population is declining, or what factors caused the migration, wants push factors from the paragraph — mechanised farms shedding jobs, the closed clinic, drought — paired with the pull of urban wages and family contacts. A question asking about effects, impacts or results wants the after-picture: the hollowed-out age structure, rising dependency on grants and remittances, closing schools and shops, unploughed fields. The trap is a paragraph that contains both, because case studies usually do. Sort every fact into before or after as you read: a sentence about the bank branch closing is interesting, because it can be both — a consequence of earlier leaving and a push factor for the next wave. If you spot that double role and name it — service closure as both consequence and cause, the downward spiral — you have just written the sentence that distinguishes a level-three answer from a list.

**Mandy:** Next: I mix up the three land reform legs under pressure. Tenure and redistribution especially — both seem to be about giving people land rights. What is the cleanest separator?

**Tutor:** Ask one question of the scenario: does anyone new arrive on the land? Tenure reform never brings anyone new — the family is already there, has been for years, and the reform simply hardens their right to stay: protection from eviction for farm dwellers, formalised rights on communal land. Same people, same land, stronger paperwork. Redistribution always brings someone new to ownership — the state helps black farmers acquire farmland they did not previously own, for equity and livelihoods, with no requirement to prove any historical claim to that specific farm. And restitution is redistribution's backward-looking cousin, separated by one test: can the claimant point at a specific piece of land and a specific dispossession after 19 June 1913? If yes — a community removed from a named valley under apartheid laws — that is restitution, settled by return of the land or by compensation. Run the three scenarios the examiners recycle: labour tenants fearing eviction — tenure. An emerging farmer assisted to buy a farm — redistribution. A community claiming the district their grandparents were removed from — restitution. Anchor the date 1913 to restitution specifically, because that anchor appearing in the wrong leg is the commonest lost mark in this question.

**Mandy:** This one caused a class debate: is rural–urban migration actually bad? My friend says the money sent home makes it a good thing overall.

**Tutor:** The exam does not want a verdict; it wants a weighing, and your friend has correctly found the lighter pan of the scale. Put both pans on the table. The costs: the village loses exactly the people it can least spare — the young, the skilled, the energetic — so the dependency ratio climbs, leadership thins, schools and services lose the numbers that justify them, and fields fall out of use. The receiving city pays too: pressure on housing produces informal settlements, and services strain. Now the other pan: remittances genuinely sustain rural households and build houses; pressure on overgrazed communal land can ease when people leave; migrants gain skills and some return with savings and experience; and the urban economy gains workers. A top answer holds both pans up, then adds the observation that tips the analysis: the costs and benefits land on different people. The migrant may benefit; the grandmother raising the grandchildren carries the cost; the village loses capacity while the household gains income. That distributional sentence — who wins, who carries it — is precisely the kind of insight the eight-mark paragraph question is designed to reward. Conclude with a position, but let the weighing carry you there.

**Mandy:** Last one: for strategies to address rural depopulation, I wrote "the government should create jobs" and got almost nothing. What does a full-mark strategy answer look like?

**Tutor:** It looks specific, mechanised and targeted — three qualities your sentence had none of, which is why it earned what it earned. Specific means naming an actual measure: an agri-processing plant that turns the district's milk into cheese locally instead of tankering it away; an expanded public works programme employing local people to maintain rural roads; extension officers, finance and market access for small-scale farmers; electrification and a staffed clinic; a tourism route built on the area's heritage. Mechanised means saying HOW the measure works: the processing plant creates year-round wage jobs AND gives farmers a reliable buyer, so two incomes stabilise at once. Targeted means naming the push factor it switches off: this attacks unemployment, that attacks poor services. So the pattern for each strategy is measure, mechanism, target — one sentence each. Write three strategies in that pattern and you have nine or more marks of material where "create jobs" earned one. And close with the evaluative line markers love: strategies succeed when they attack push factors, because people rarely abandon a home that offers a living. That sentence shows you understand not just the list, but the logic underneath it.
