# Part 1 — Expert

The statements of Molefe and de Waal Traders are published. Now comes the skill the whole course has been building toward: READING them. Analysis and interpretation turns statement figures into indicators — small, comparable numbers that answer the questions every partner, banker and rival quietly asks. Today: the profitability indicators revised from grade 10; the four NEW operating-cycle indicators — stock turnover rate, stock holding period, debtors' collection period and creditors' payment period; the risk-and-reward pair — debt-equity ratio and return on average partners' equity; and the discipline of writing a comment that earns full interpretation marks. All figures come from the statements built this term.

## Subtopic: Profitability Revised

Profitability asks: out of every rand of sales, how much survives? Three percentages, each spoken with its formula.

Gross profit on sales: gross profit over sales, times one hundred — R820 000 over R2 460 000 — 33,3 percent. Every rand of sales keeps thirty-three cents after paying for the goods themselves. Its twin, gross profit on COST of sales: R820 000 over R1 640 000 — 50 percent, which should equal the intended mark-up. Here it does, exactly — so the business achieved its pricing policy: no undetected theft of takings, no excessive markdowns, no recording errors large enough to show.

Operating expenses on sales: R441 000 over R2 460 000 — 17,9 percent. Operating profit on sales: R439 000 over R2 460 000 — 17,8 percent. And net profit on sales: R421 000 over R2 460 000 — 17,1 percent. Read them as a cascade: fifty cents of mark-up per sales rand becomes thirty-three cents of gross margin, expenses eat seventeen-and-nine, and seventeen-comma-one cents reaches the owners per rand of sales.

The indicators only speak when COMPARED — against last year, against the budget, against the mark-up policy, against a similar business. A gross margin of 33,3 percent is neither good nor bad in a vacuum; a gross margin that fell from 36 percent last year while the mark-up policy stayed at 50 is a question demanding an answer: markdowns, theft of stock before sale, or errors. Interpretation begins where the comparison begins. Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: each percentage computed, and its comparison named.

## Subtopic: The Operating Cycle: Four New Indicators

Grade 11 adds four indicators that clock the business's operating rhythm — how fast stock sells, how fast debtors pay, how slowly creditors are paid. Each is a speed reading on money moving through the cycle.

STOCK TURNOVER RATE: cost of sales over AVERAGE stock. Opening stock was R194 800, closing R185 200 — average R190 000. R1 640 000 over R190 000: 8,6 times a year. The shelves empty and refill roughly every six weeks. Its mirror, the STOCK HOLDING PERIOD: average stock over cost of sales, times three hundred and sixty-five — 42,3 days of sales sitting on the shelves. Same fact, two dialects: rate counts refills per year, period counts days per refill.

DEBTORS' COLLECTION PERIOD: average debtors over CREDIT sales, times three hundred and sixty-five. Debtors averaged R54 000 — opening R52 000, closing R56 000. Credit sales were 60 percent of total sales: R1 476 000. R54 000 over R1 476 000, times three hundred and sixty-five: 13,4 days. Debtors settle in under a fortnight — excellent against 30-day terms, and a sign the provision for bad debts is unlikely to be strained.

CREDITORS' PAYMENT PERIOD: average creditors over credit purchases, times three hundred and sixty-five. Creditors averaged R83 000 — opening R79 300, closing R86 700 — against credit purchases of R1 245 000: 24,3 days. The business pays its suppliers in twenty-four days on 30-day terms.

Now read the three periods TOGETHER, because that is where interpretation lives. Money is locked up in stock for about 42 days and in debtors for 13 — call it 55 days from buying stock to banking cash — while suppliers are paid after 24. The gap between fifty-five and twenty-four must be financed by the business's own money. Comment: collections are superb; the stock holds six weeks, which suits a hardware trade but is worth watching; and paying creditors at 24 days on 30-day terms is GENEROUS — six days of free credit are being given up unless an early-settlement discount justifies it. Stop for this section's questions now: all four indicators computed, and the cycle read as one story.

## Subtopic: Gearing and Return: Risk and Reward

Two indicators judge the partnership's financing choices — one measures the risk taken, the other the reward earned.

DEBT-EQUITY RATIO: non-current liabilities to owners' equity. The loan stands at R138 000; equity at R765 800. R138 000 over R765 800 — 0,2 to 1. For every rand the partners have in the business, outsiders have twenty cents of long-term money. This is LOW gearing: little dependence on borrowed money, little exposure to interest rates — a safe structure. But gearing is a dial, not a virtue: borrowing is worthwhile whenever the business earns MORE on the borrowed money than the borrowing costs. This business returns far above the loan's 12 percent, so a case exists for gearing UP — borrowing to expand — and a comment that can argue both sides of that dial earns the top marks.

RETURN ON AVERAGE PARTNERS' EQUITY: net profit over average equity, times one hundred. Equity opened the year at R702 800 — capital R700 000 plus opening current account balances of R8 200 credit less R5 400 debit, R2 800 — and closed at R765 800. Average: R734 300. R421 000 over R734 300 — 57,3 percent. Compare it with the ALTERNATIVE: the partners could earn roughly 8 to 10 percent in a fixed deposit, risk-free. Their money in Bushveld Hardware earned 57,3 percent — but remember what sits inside that figure: the partners' own LABOUR. Molefe and de Waal work in this business; their salaries are appropriations, not expenses, so the return rewards their money AND their work together. A fair comment says: the return handsomely beats the risk-free alternative even after mentally pricing the partners' labour, so the investment is sound.

And the SOLVENCY check closes the set: total assets to total liabilities — R995 550 to R229 750 — 4,3 to 1. The business could pay every debt more than four times over; no going-concern doubt. Pause for this section's questions: both indicators computed, the gearing dial argued in both directions, and the return compared against the alternative.

## Subtopic: Liquidity and the Art of the Comment

Liquidity asks the shortest question in accounting: can the business pay its bills THIS year? Two ratios from grade 10, recomputed on the new statements, then the discipline that turns figures into marks.

CURRENT RATIO: current assets to current liabilities — R375 550 to R91 750 — 4,1 to 1. ACID-TEST RATIO: current assets MINUS inventories, to current liabilities — R190 350 to R91 750 — 2,1 to 1. Even if not one more item of stock sold, the business covers its short-term debts twice over. Healthy — arguably too healthy: cash of over R130 000 sitting in the bank earns almost nothing, and a sharp comment notes the OVER-liquidity and suggests putting idle cash to work: expansion, early-settlement discounts, or reducing the loan.

Now the comment discipline, because interpretation questions carry more marks than calculations and are marked to a pattern. A full comment has four parts, in order. FIGURE: quote the indicator and its comparison — this year against last year, against the norm, against the policy. TREND or COMPARISON: state plainly whether it improved, worsened or beats the benchmark. CAUSE: name a plausible reason rooted in the business — not "sales went up" but "collections at 13,4 days against 30-day terms keep debtors low". CONSEQUENCE or ADVICE: say what it means or what the partners should do. Figure, comparison, cause, advice — four sentences, four marks, every time.

And the traps. Never judge an indicator alone: a rising current ratio can mean strengthening liquidity or stock piling up unsold — the stock holding period decides which. Never confuse the dialects: a turnover RATE rising is the same news as a holding PERIOD falling. And never present a ratio without its comparison: 4,1 to 1 means nothing until it stands next to last year, a norm, or a rival. The final questions of this part are with you now: both ratios computed, an over-liquidity comment written in four parts, and the paired-indicator traps disarmed.

## Subtopic: Reading the Stats Sheet

A coach never watches every match a player ever played; she reads the stats sheet — goals per game, passes completed, minutes played. Small numbers that stand for a big performance. That is exactly what analysis does to a business: the statements are the whole season, the indicators are the stats sheet.

Profitability is the goals-per-game line. Out of every hundred rand of sales at Bushveld Hardware, thirty-three stay after paying for the goods — that is the gross margin of 33,3 percent. Check it against the game plan: the shop prices everything at cost plus fifty percent, and fifty percent on cost gives exactly thirty-three on sales, so the plan and the result MATCH. That match matters more than the number itself: if the policy says fifty but the margin comes out at forty-four, goods are leaking — stolen before sale, marked down quietly, or misrecorded — and the stats sheet has just caught it.

Then the rand-by-rand cascade: after the goods, the running costs take about eighteen of every hundred; about seventeen reach the owners. Seventeen cents of every sales rand, kept.

But here is the coach's first rule, and it is the whole subject in one line: a stat means nothing alone. Seventeen percent — good? Against last year's fourteen, wonderful. Against a rival's twenty-five, worrying. Every number needs a NEXT TO: last year, the plan, the rival. No comparison, no meaning. Quick check before we carry on — questions on the stats sheet are coming to you right now.

## Subtopic: The Rhythm Section

Some stats measure skill; these four measure TEMPO — how fast money moves through the shop. Picture one rand doing a lap: it becomes stock on a shelf, waits, becomes a debtor's promise, waits, and finally comes home as cash.

Lap one: the shelf. Stock at the hardware shop turns over 8,6 times a year — the shelves empty and refill about every 42 days. Six weeks per refill: fine for hammers and paint, terrible if it were bread. The norm depends on the trade — tempo is always judged against the kind of music being played.

Lap two: the promise. Customers who buy on credit pay in 13,4 days on average, against 30-day terms. That is quick — these debtors are sprinting. Quick collection means less money stuck in promises, less chance of bad debts, more cash at work.

Lap three runs in the OTHER direction: how long before the shop pays its own suppliers? 24,3 days, on 30-day terms. And here is the counterintuitive one: paying FAST is not automatically a virtue. Those unused six days are free credit — someone else's money the shop could be using — so unless a prompt-payment discount is being earned, the shop is being polite at its own expense.

Now the full rhythm: money sits 42 days on the shelf plus 13 in promises — about 55 days out — but the supplier gets paid at day 24. From day 24 to day 55, the shop is financing its own lap with its own money. Shorten the shelf time, keep the fast collections, use the full 30 days of supplier credit, and the lap needs less of the shop's cash. That sentence, with those numbers, is a top-marks answer. Your questions for this part are up now — clock all three laps and read the tempo as one piece.

## Subtopic: Was It Worth It?

Two questions close every trial. Can the team survive the season? And was this the best use of everyone's talent?

Survival first — liquidity. Bills due within the year: R91 750. Resources arriving within the year: R375 550. Four times cover; even ignoring every item of stock, twice over. The shop cannot be embarrassed by a payday. If anything it is TOO safe — over R130 000 dozing in the bank earns nearly nothing, and idle money is a quiet cost. A sharp answer says both things: safe, and slightly lazy.

Then risk. The shop's long-term debt is small next to the owners' money — twenty cents of loan for every rand of equity, a debt-equity ratio of 0,2 to 1. Low risk. But listen to the numbers argue: the loan costs twelve percent, and the business earns far more than twelve on the money it uses. That is a reason to borrow MORE, not less — carefully — because cheap money earning a big return multiplies the owners' reward. Gearing is a volume knob, not a sin: low is safe, higher is louder, and the right setting depends on how steady the earnings are.

Finally, the question the partners actually care about: was it worth it? Their money in the business — averaging R734 300 across the year — earned R421 000: a return of 57,3 percent. The bank would have paid maybe nine, risk-free. Even remembering that the partners also gave the business their working days, 57,3 against 9 is not a contest. The stats sheet's verdict: a profitable, liquid, low-risk shop that collects brilliantly, could use its supplier credit better, and rewards its owners many times over the alternative.

And when you write any of this in an exam, keep the four-beat bar: the figure, the thing it is compared to, the likely cause, the advice. Four beats, full marks. And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: deliver the verdict on this business in four-beat comments, indicator by indicator.
