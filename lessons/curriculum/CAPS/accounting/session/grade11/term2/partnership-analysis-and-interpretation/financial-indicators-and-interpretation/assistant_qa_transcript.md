**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! Start with the one everyone whispers: why do some formulas use AVERAGE figures — average stock, average debtors, average equity — while others just use the year-end number?

**Tutor:** The rule is about matching the two halves of the fraction in TIME. Look at what each formula divides. Cost of sales is a FLOW — it accumulated across the whole year, every month contributing. The stock figure it is divided by should therefore represent the whole year too, and a single year-end snapshot cannot: stock might have been unusually low on 28 February after a good season, making turnover look faster than it ever really was. Averaging opening and closing — R194 800 and R185 200 giving R190 000 — is a rough but honest stand-in for the year's typical shelf. The same logic drives average debtors against the year's credit sales, average creditors against the year's purchases, and average equity under the year's net profit: flow over snapshot is a mismatch; flow over average is fair. By contrast, the current ratio and acid-test compare a snapshot with a snapshot — assets ON the last day against liabilities ON the last day — so no averaging is needed; both feet already stand at the same moment. When an exam gives you only the closing figure, use it and carry on — the principle bends to the data available — but when both opening and closing are provided, the examiner is signalling that the average is expected, and marks sit on that expectation.

***

**Bianca:** Next: my current ratio came out at 4,1 to 1 and I wrote that liquidity is excellent. The memo apparently wants criticism too. How can a strong ratio be a problem?

**Tutor:** Because a ratio measures a POSITION, and every position has a price. Liquidity is protection: 4,1 to 1 means the business cannot be embarrassed by its short-term debts, and the acid-test of 2,1 to 1 says that stands even if the stockroom never sells another item. Excellent protection — but protection is bought with idle resources. Money parked in a big bank balance earns close to nothing; stock beyond what the shelves need ties up cash and risks obsolescence; and every rand sleeping in current assets is a rand not expanding the business, not settling the twelve percent loan, not earning early-settlement discounts from suppliers. So the complete comment runs both directions: safe beyond doubt, AND over-liquid — with a recommendation attached, because advice is the fourth beat of the comment. Suggest putting the surplus to work: expand the range, reduce the loan, negotiate discounts for early payment where they exist. The general skill here transfers to every indicator: ask what the position COSTS as well as what it protects. Too little liquidity risks survival; too much wastes opportunity — and the examiner's favourite learners are the ones who can see both edges of the same number.

**Bianca:** A learner asked: the return on equity is 57,3 percent, but the partners work in the shop all day. Isn't part of that return really just their unpaid wages dressed up as investment income?

**Tutor:** Sharply seen — and yes, partly, which is exactly why the comment must mention their labour. Unpack the figure. The net profit of R421 000 was measured BEFORE any reward to the partners, because their salaries, interest and bonus are appropriations, not expenses. So the 57,3 percent bundles together the return on their MONEY and the return on their WORK. To judge the investment fairly, do the thought experiment the examiner is fishing for: imagine the partners stepped back and hired managers to replace themselves. Competent managers for the store and the books might cost, say, R270 000 a year in real salary expense. Profit would drop to roughly R151 000, and the return on average equity to about 20,6 percent — R151 000 over R734 300. THAT is the pure return on money — and the verdict survives: twenty percent still beats the eight-to-ten of a fixed deposit comfortably, with the difference compensating for the business risk carried. So the polished answer has three layers: quote the 57,3 percent, acknowledge that the partners' labour is inside it, and show that even after pricing that labour the investment outperforms the alternative. Three layers, and the interpretation marks are yours in full.

**Bianca:** Last one: in comment questions I always freeze — there is so much I COULD say about a ratio. How do I know what the marker actually wants?

**Tutor:** You stop improvising and deliver the four-beat bar, every single time. Beat one, FIGURE: quote this year's indicator next to its comparison — last year's figure, the norm, the policy, or the rival's. No naked numbers: "the collection period improved from 19 days to 13,4 days against 30-day terms" is a figure; "13,4" alone is not. Beat two, DIRECTION: say plainly whether that is an improvement, a deterioration, or better or worse than the benchmark — markers cannot award a judgement you never stated. Beat three, CAUSE: offer a plausible business reason, rooted in this business: tighter screening of credit customers, an early-settlement discount, a stronger invoicing routine. You are not psychic and the memo knows it — any sensible cause consistent with the figures earns the mark. Beat four, CONSEQUENCE or ADVICE: what it means or what should be done — cash arrives sooner, bad-debt risk falls, keep the policy. Four beats, four sentences, and the mark allocation almost always maps onto them one to one. When the question says "comment on the liquidity", pick the two or three relevant indicators, play the bar for each, and finish with one overall verdict sentence. Structure is what the marker is paid to find; give it to them on a plate.
