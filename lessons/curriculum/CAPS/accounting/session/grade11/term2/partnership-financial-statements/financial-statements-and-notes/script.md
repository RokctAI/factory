# Part 1 — Expert

Term one closed the books of Molefe and de Waal Traders and divided the profit. Term two publishes the result. The final accounts were internal machinery; the FINANCIAL STATEMENTS are the formal report a partnership presents to its partners, its bank and anyone else with the right to ask. Today: the Statement of Comprehensive Income built line by line; the Statement of Financial Position built section by section; and the NOTES — fixed assets, capital accounts and current accounts — where the detail lives. Every figure carries over from term one: Bushveld Hardware, financial year ended 28 February.

## Subtopic: The Statement of Comprehensive Income

The statement answers one question — how much richer did the year make the owners — and it answers in a fixed order, spoken here from the top.

The heading first, in full: Statement of Comprehensive Income of Molefe and de Waal Traders for the year ended 28 February. FOR THE YEAR ENDED, not "on" — this statement covers a period, like a video, not a moment.

Then the body. Sales, R2 460 000. Less cost of sales, R1 640 000. GROSS PROFIT: R820 000. Add other operating income: rent income R38 400 and commission income R21 600 — R60 000 — giving gross operating income of R880 000. Less operating expenses, each on its own line and totalling R441 000: salaries and wages R248 000; depreciation R54 400, which is the vehicles' R48 000 plus the equipment's R6 400; trading stock deficit R2 100; bank charges R9 800; insurance R21 300; telephone R18 900; provision for bad debts adjustment R400; bad debts R6 100; sundry expenses R80 000. OPERATING PROFIT: R439 000. Less interest expense — the R18 000 capitalised on the Thebe Bank loan. NET PROFIT FOR THE YEAR: R421 000.

Notice what the adjustments of term one did to these lines: rent income shows twelve honest months, insurance excludes the prepaid cover, depreciation and the provision movement appear as expenses though no cash moved, and interest expense appears though the bank was never paid a cent of it in cash. The statement reports EARNING, not receiving — accrual accounting, published. And notice what is absent: partners' salaries, interest on capital, the bonus. Those are appropriations, and they happen AFTER this statement's last line, in the notes. Pause here — this section's questions are with you now, top of the statement to the bottom.

## Subtopic: The Statement of Financial Position

The second statement is the photograph: Statement of Financial Position of Molefe and de Waal Traders ON 28 February — a date, not a period. It answers: what does the business own, and who has claims on it? Build it in two towers that must weigh the same.

Tower one: ASSETS. Non-current assets first — fixed assets at carrying value, R620 000, with the detail pushed to a note. Then current assets, in order: inventories, meaning trading stock, R185 200; trade and other receivables — debtors of R56 000 less the provision of R2 800 gives net trade debtors R53 200, plus prepaid expenses R2 400 and accrued income R1 500; then cash and cash equivalents — bank R131 050 and cash float R2 200. Current assets total R375 550. TOTAL ASSETS: R995 550.

Tower two: EQUITY AND LIABILITIES. Owners' equity: capital accounts R700 000 plus current accounts R65 800 — R765 800, both figures detailed in notes. Non-current liabilities: the Thebe Bank loan at its closing balance of R138 000 — the year's capitalised interest already inside it. Current liabilities: trade and other payables — creditors R86 700, accrued expenses R1 850 and income received in advance R3 200 — R91 750. TOTAL EQUITY AND LIABILITIES: R995 550. The towers match, and that match is not luck: it is the accounting equation, published.

Two classification disciplines earn the marks here. Time: anything turning into cash or falling due within twelve months is current; longer is non-current — and if a loan instalment is payable within the year, that portion moves to current liabilities. Netting: debtors appear NET of the provision, fixed assets NET of accumulated depreciation — the statement shows honest values, the notes show the workings. Stop for this section's questions now: both towers assembled, and every item placed and classified.

## Subtopic: The Notes: Fixed Assets

The face of each statement stays clean because the NOTES carry the detail. The fixed assets note is the biggest, and it reconciles every class of asset from opening to closing. Speak it class by class.

Land and buildings: carrying value R480 000, no depreciation — land does not wear out, and the historical cost principle keeps it at cost regardless of what property prices do.

Vehicles: cost R240 000; accumulated depreciation at the START of the year R109 600; opening carrying value R130 400. Depreciation for the year at 20 percent on cost: R48 000. Closing accumulated depreciation: R157 600. Closing carrying value: R82 400 — cost R240 000 less accumulated depreciation R157 600.

Equipment: cost R120 000; accumulated depreciation at the start R56 000; opening carrying value R64 000. Depreciation at 10 percent on the diminishing balance: R6 400. Closing carrying value: R57 600, against closing accumulated depreciation of R62 400.

Add the three closing carrying values: R480 000 plus R82 400 plus R57 600 — R620 000, exactly the one figure shown on the face of the statement. That is the anatomy of every note: the face shows the conclusion, the note shows the reconciliation, and the two must meet to the rand. Had assets been bought or sold during the year, additions and disposals would each take their own line inside this same note — the structure simply grows rows.

The note also carries the depreciation POLICY in words — vehicles at 20 percent on cost, equipment at 10 percent diminishing balance — because CONSISTENCY requires the same method year after year, and the reader is entitled to know which method the figures obey. Pause for this section's questions: the note reconstructed, and any one missing figure recovered from the others.

## Subtopic: The Notes: Capital, Current Accounts and the Principles

Two more notes complete the equity story, and both are tables the examiner loves to leave half empty.

The CAPITAL ACCOUNTS note, partner by partner: Molefe — opening balance R400 000, no contributions or withdrawals this year, closing R400 000. De Waal — opening R300 000, closing R300 000. Total: R700 000. Dull this year — and that dullness is the point: fixed capital moves only on a formal decision, so most years this note simply confirms stillness.

The CURRENT ACCOUNTS note is the busy one — it repeats, in statement form, the ledger work of term one. Two columns, one per partner, every row spoken. Opening balance: Molefe R8 200 credit; de Waal R5 400 DEBIT — shown as a negative. Interest on capital: R36 000 and R27 000. Partners' salaries: R120 000 and R144 000. Bonus: nil and R10 000. Share of remaining profit: R48 000 and R36 000. Drawings for the year: minus R176 000 and minus R182 000. Closing balances: Molefe R36 200; de Waal R29 600; together R65 800 — the exact current-accounts figure on the face. Every rand of the appropriation reappears here; the note is the appropriation account wearing its Sunday clothes.

Behind the whole package stand the IFRS-aligned GAAP principles, and exams ask them in words. Historical cost: assets at what they cost, not what they might fetch. Prudence: the provision held against debtors, stock at the counted figure. Matching: the accrual adjustments that placed each rand in its year. Business entity: partners' private affairs outside these pages. Going concern: values assume the business trades on — a business being wound up would value everything at forced-sale prices instead. Materiality: trivial items grouped, significant ones on their own line. The final questions of this part are with you now: both notes completed, and each principle matched to the line it governs.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same two statements from the stands at a school soccer final — because everyone already knows the difference between the match highlights and the team photo.

## Subtopic: The Highlights Reel and the Team Photo

A season ends. Two things get made: the highlights reel — everything that HAPPENED across the season — and the team photo — everyone lined up on the last day. Nobody confuses them. Nobody asks the photo what the score was in March.

The Statement of Comprehensive Income is the highlights reel. It covers the whole year — FOR the year ended — and it tells the story of earning: sales of R2 460 000 at the top, the cost of those goods pulled off, the running costs pulled off, until the final scene shows net profit of R421 000. The Statement of Financial Position is the team photo — ON 28 February, one frozen day — showing everything the business owns lined up on one side, and everyone with a claim on it lined up on the other. Photo rule: both lines must be the same height. R995 550 owned; R995 550 claimed. Always.

Why two documents? Because each is blind to what the other sees. The reel cannot tell you whether the business can pay next month's bills — a profitable year can still end with an empty bank account. The photo cannot tell you whether the year was good — a fat bank balance might be borrowed money. Read together, reel plus photo, the picture is complete.

One habit protects you from the classic heading mark: ask of every statement, is this a period or a moment? Period gets FOR the year ended. Moment gets ON the date. Two words, one mark, every paper. Quick check before we carry on — questions on reel against photo are coming to you right now.

## Subtopic: The Fine Print That Shows the Workings

The face of each statement is like the scoreboard: clean, final, no clutter. But every big number on the scoreboard has workings behind it, and the workings live in the NOTES — the fine print that proves the headline.

Take the R620 000 of fixed assets on the photo. The note behind it opens like a service history. Land and buildings: R480 000, never depreciated — ground does not wear out. Vehicles: bought for R240 000, worn down by R157 600 of accumulated depreciation over their life, so worth R82 400 in the books today. Equipment: cost R120 000, worn down to R57 600. Add the three: R480 000 plus R82 400 plus R57 600 — R620 000. The note climbs exactly to the number on the face. If it doesn't, something is wrong, and the marker will follow your trail to find out what — which is precisely why showing the trail earns marks.

Same with the debtors. The face doesn't show R56 000, because honesty says five percent of that is probably never coming. It shows R53 200 — the R56 000 dimmed by the R2 800 provision. The face shows the honest number; the note shows the honesty happening.

So read notes the way you read the fine print on a phone contract: that is where the real information sits. Face for the conclusion, note for the proof. Your questions for this part are up now — match each face figure to the note that backs it.

## Subtopic: The Partners' Pages

Two notes belong to the partners personally, and they tell a two-line story anyone can follow: what each partner PLANTED, and what each partner is OWED.

The capital note is the planted money: Molefe R400 000, de Waal R300 000, opening and closing, no movement. Boring on purpose — planted money is supposed to stand still, and this note is the public record that it did. If a partner had added capital during the year, this is the page where the world would see it.

The current accounts note is each partner's tab, published. Follow de Waal's column down: he starts at minus R5 400 — last year he ate ahead of his earnings, and the note says so in public; the statements hide nothing. Then the year pays him: R27 000 interest for his planted money, R144 000 salary for running the store, R10 000 bonus for hitting the target, R36 000 as his share of the leftovers. Then the year charges him: R182 000 of drawings. He closes at plus R29 600 — out of the red, and the business owes him that. Molefe's column runs the same race and closes at R36 200. The two closing figures, R65 800 together, walk straight onto the photo as the current accounts line inside equity.

And underneath it all, the quiet rules you should name when asked WHY the statements look this way: things recorded at what they cost — historical cost. Never prettier than the truth — prudence. Each rand in its own year — matching. The shop's books are not the partners' pockets — business entity. And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: complete a partner's column with one figure missing, and name the rule behind each honest choice.
