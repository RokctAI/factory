**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: we already did the final accounts in term one. Why does the same information have to be written out AGAIN as statements and notes? It feels like doing the work twice.

**Tutor:** Because the two documents serve two different audiences, and the difference changes everything about how they are written. The final accounts — trading, profit and loss, appropriation — are LEDGER machinery: internal closing accounts, written in debits and credits, readable only by someone trained in double entry. The financial statements are a REPORT: written in plain classified lines for people who will never open the ledger — the partners judging their investment, the bank deciding on a loan, a prospective new partner deciding whether to buy in. Same underlying figures, completely different presentation duties. The statements must classify — current against non-current, operating against interest; they must disclose — the notes showing how R620 000 of fixed assets was arrived at; and they must be comparable — same formats, same policies, year after year, business to business. That is also why the statement format is not negotiable in an exam: a marker is testing whether you can produce the standard report a stranger could read. The ledger answers "is the double entry complete?" The statements answer "what should a reader believe about this business?" Both questions matter; neither substitutes for the other.

***

**Bianca:** Next: why does interest on the loan get its own line AFTER operating profit, instead of just sitting inside the expenses list with telephone and insurance?

**Tutor:** Because the statement is deliberately built to separate two different skills of the business: TRADING and FINANCING. Everything above the operating profit line answers one question — how good is this business at its actual work, buying and selling hardware? Telephone, insurance, salaries, depreciation: all costs of running the shop, whoever owns or funds it. Interest expense answers a different question — how is the business FUNDED? Molefe and de Waal chose to borrow R150 000 from Thebe Bank rather than contribute more capital; the R18 000 is the price of that choice, and it would vanish tomorrow if the partners repaid the loan, without the shop selling one hammer differently. Mixing the two would blur every comparison that matters. Two identical shops, one debt-funded and one capital-funded, would show different operating abilities when their trading is in fact identical. Separated, the reader sees: operating profit R439 000 — strong trading; less interest R18 000 — a light funding cost; net profit R421 000. In term two's analysis lessons this separation becomes the foundation of the gearing and return indicators, so the format you learn now is the analysis you will do next.

**Bianca:** A learner asked this about the fixed assets note: if the vehicles are five years old and secondhand prices have gone up, why do the statements still show them at cost less depreciation? Isn't that just wrong information?

**Tutor:** It is less informative about market value, and deliberately so — because the alternative is worse. The historical cost principle keeps assets at what they verifiably cost, and depreciation then spreads that cost over the years that consume it. Cost is a FACT: an invoice exists for R240 000, any auditor can confirm it, and no two accountants will disagree about it. Market value is an OPINION: it changes monthly, differs between valuers, and — this is the danger — it is manipulable. A partnership wanting a bigger loan could nudge its vehicle values upward and no one could prove the number wrong. The statements choose verifiable-but-conservative over flattering-but-arguable, which is prudence and reliability working together. Notice the carrying value does not pretend to be a selling price; it is the unconsumed portion of a real cost, matched against the income the asset helped earn. And the system is honest about its own limits: the note discloses cost, accumulated depreciation and policy, so an informed reader can form their own view of the fleet's age and likely worth. When the vehicle is actually sold, the truth arrives anyway — the difference between the price and the carrying value lands in the income statement as a profit or loss on disposal, exactly as you learned in the fixed assets topic.

**Bianca:** Last one: with the current accounts note, I never know which figures go in as negatives. Is there a rule that stops me guessing?

**Tutor:** There is — stop thinking in plus and minus, and think in OWED-TO and OWED-BY. The current account measures one thing only: what the business owes this partner right now. Anything that increases what the business owes the partner enters positive: interest on capital, salary, bonus, share of the remaining profit. Anything that reduces it enters negative: drawings, always — the partner has already taken that value home — and an opening DEBIT balance, because the year began with the partner owing the business, which is a negative amount of "business owes partner". Then the column is pure arithmetic. De Waal: minus R5 400 to start, plus R27 000, plus R144 000, plus R10 000, plus R36 000, minus R182 000 — closing at plus R29 600, the business owes him. If a closing balance comes out negative, that is not an error to hide; it is information — that partner has drawn ahead of earnings, the balance sits as a debit, and in the statement of financial position an overdrawn current account is disclosed rather than netted away silently. Final check, every time: your two closing balances must add to the current accounts figure on the face — R36 200 plus R29 600 giving R65 800 here. If they do not, one sign is wrong somewhere in a column, and the check finds it before the marker does.
