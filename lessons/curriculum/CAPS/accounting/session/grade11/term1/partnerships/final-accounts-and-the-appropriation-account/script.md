# Part 1 — Expert

The adjustments are posted; the books of Molefe and de Waal Traders are honest. Now the year must be closed and the profit must be shared — and sharing is where a partnership differs from everything you closed off in grade 10. Today: the closing sequence through the trading account and the profit and loss account; the APPROPRIATION account, where the partnership agreement is executed rand by rand; the partners' current accounts written up in the general ledger; and the accounting-equation analysis that examiners attach to every partnership transaction. The figures continue from the last lesson: Bushveld Hardware, financial year ended 28 February.

## Subtopic: Closing Off: Trading and Profit and Loss

Final accounts are closing accounts — they exist only at year end, to gather the nominal accounts and distil them into one profit figure.

First the TRADING account, home of gross profit. Close Sales to its credit side: R2 460 000. Close Cost of sales to its debit side: R1 640 000. The difference, R820 000, is the gross profit — carried down to the credit of the profit and loss account. Check it against the business's pricing policy: Bushveld Hardware marks up 50 percent on cost, and R820 000 on a cost of R1 640 000 is exactly 50 percent, so the figure is coherent.

Then the PROFIT AND LOSS account, home of net profit. To its credit: gross profit R820 000, plus the operating incomes — rent income R38 400 and commission income R21 600, R60 000 together. To its debit: every operating expense, each closed off in turn — salaries and wages, depreciation, trading stock deficit, insurance, telephone, bank charges, bad debts, the provision for bad debts adjustment, sundry expenses — R441 000 in total, and then interest on loan of R18 000. Total the two sides in words: credits of R880 000 against debits of R459 000 leave a NET PROFIT of R421 000.

In a sole trader's books, that R421 000 would now close to the owner's capital account and the story would end. In a partnership it cannot — because two people own that profit, and a contract says exactly how it is divided. So net profit is carried instead to a third closing account, one grade 10 never needed. Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the closing sequence in order, and the two profit figures verified.

## Subtopic: The Appropriation Account

The APPROPRIATION account executes the partnership agreement. Net profit of R421 000 enters on its credit side, and the agreement is paid out from the top, in order.

First, interest on capital, the reward for money invested: Molefe, 9 percent of R400 000 — R36 000. De Waal, 9 percent of R300 000 — R27 000. Together R63 000, debited to appropriation, credited to the partners' current accounts.

Second, partners' salaries, the reward for work: Molefe R120 000, de Waal R144 000 — R264 000 together. Debit appropriation, credit each current account.

Third, the bonus: the store met its sales target, so de Waal earns R10 000. Debit appropriation, credit his current account.

Now add what has been appropriated so far: R63 000 plus R264 000 plus R10 000 — R337 000 of the R421 000 is spoken for, leaving R84 000. That REMAINING profit is shared in the agreed ratio of four to three: four sevenths of R84 000 is R48 000 to Molefe; three sevenths is R36 000 to de Waal. The appropriation account now balances to the cent — R421 000 in, R421 000 out.

Prove each partner's total earnings in words. Molefe: interest R36 000, salary R120 000, profit share R48 000 — R204 000. De Waal: interest R27 000, salary R144 000, bonus R10 000, profit share R36 000 — R217 000. And R204 000 plus R217 000 returns R421 000 — the whole profit, fully distributed, nothing lost.

One warning that saves marks every year: partners' salaries and interest on capital are APPROPRIATIONS of profit, never expenses. They do not appear in the profit and loss account, they do not reduce net profit, and a question that sneaks a partner's salary into operating expenses is testing exactly this. Wages paid to employees — expense. Salary allowed to a partner — appropriation. Stop for this section's questions now: the account built top to bottom, and the expense-or-appropriation trap.

## Subtopic: The Current Accounts in the Ledger

The appropriation account's debits all landed as credits somewhere — in the partners' current accounts. Write up each account fully, and speak the sides.

Current account of Molefe. Credit side: balance brought down from last year, R8 200 — the business owed him that. Then this year's earnings: interest on capital R36 000, salary R120 000, share of remaining profit R48 000. Debit side: drawings for the year, R176 000. Balance the account in words: credits total R212 200; debits R176 000; the closing balance is R36 200 CREDIT — the business owes Molefe R36 200.

Current account of de Waal. He starts the year in the red: balance brought down R5 400 on the DEBIT side, because last year he drew more than he earned. Credit side: interest on capital R27 000, salary R144 000, bonus R10 000, profit share R36 000 — R217 000. Debit side: the old R5 400, plus drawings of R182 000 — R187 400. Closing balance: R29 600 CREDIT. He has worked his way out of the debit and the business now owes him R29 600.

Note the discipline in each account: every line names its source — interest on capital, salary, bonus, appropriation share, drawings — because markers award the label as well as the figure. And note what never appears here: capital contributions, which belong in the fixed capital accounts, and cash movements that are not partner flows.

Together the two current accounts now hold R65 800 of undrawn earnings — R36 200 plus R29 600 — and that combined figure joins the capital of R700 000 in the equity section of the statements you will build in term two. Pause for this section's questions: both accounts written up, balances defended, and the debit-opening trap of de Waal handled.

## Subtopic: The Accounting Equation, Partnership Edition

Every partnership transaction still obeys assets equal owner's equity plus liabilities — examiners simply dress the equation in partnership clothing. Analyse each event in three columns spoken aloud: effect on assets, effect on equity, effect on liabilities.

De Waal contributes R50 000 additional capital by EFT. Assets: bank up R50 000. Equity: capital up R50 000. Liabilities: no effect.

Molefe takes trading stock of R3 000 for personal use. Assets: trading stock down R3 000. Equity: down R3 000 through drawings. Liabilities: nil. Note the account: DRAWINGS, at cost — not sales, because nothing was sold to an outsider.

Interest on capital of R36 000 is allowed to Molefe. Assets: nil — no cash moves on the allowance. Equity: appropriation shifts R36 000 from accumulated profit to Molefe's current account claim — total equity unchanged by the allowance itself. This is the subtle one: appropriations REDISTRIBUTE equity between the profit pool and individual partners; they do not create or destroy it. The profit that feeds them was created earlier, by trading.

Interest of R18 000 is capitalised on the loan. Assets: nil. Liabilities: loan up R18 000. Equity: DOWN R18 000 — the expense. The equation stays level: plus eighteen on one side's liabilities, minus eighteen on the same side's equity.

The business pays a creditor R14 000. Assets: bank down R14 000. Liabilities: creditors down R14 000. Equity: untouched — settling a debt is not an expense.

The method that never fails: name the two accounts in the double entry first, classify each as asset, equity or liability, then read the effects straight off the debits and credits. Learners who guess effects from the story make sign errors; learners who route through the double entry do not. The final questions of this part are with you now: five transactions, three columns each, every effect with its amount and direction.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same closing from the kitchen of the kota shop — because dividing profit between two friends fairly is something everyone already argues about correctly.

## Subtopic: One Pot, Three Ladles

At year end, all the shop's earnings boil down into one pot: the net profit. For Bushveld Hardware, R421 000. Now two hungry partners stand at the pot — and the agreement is the recipe for serving it. Three ladles come out, in strict order.

Ladle one pays the MONEY for waiting. Whoever planted capital gets interest on it first — nine percent. Molefe planted R400 000, so R36 000. De Waal planted R300 000, so R27 000. Fair: their money could have been earning interest at the bank instead.

Ladle two pays the HANDS for working. Molefe runs the books, salary R120 000. De Waal runs the store every day, salary R144 000. Plus his R10 000 bonus, because the store hit its target. Fair: work deserves wages even between friends — especially between friends.

Ladle three splits WHAT'S LEFT as the reward for risk. Count what the first two ladles served: R63 000 plus R264 000 plus R10 000 — R337 000. The pot held R421 000, so R84 000 remains, and the agreement splits leftovers four to three: R48 000 to Molefe, R36 000 to de Waal. Pot empty, argument over — because the recipe was written down before the cooking started.

And here is the line to underline twice: the ladles serve profit AFTER it is measured; they never shrink it. A partner's salary is not an expense of the shop — the pot is the same size whether the recipe pays big salaries or none. Quick check before we carry on: questions on the three ladles are coming to you right now, in serving order.

## Subtopic: Each Friend's Tab

The ladles don't hand out cash — no partner walks off with R204 000 in an envelope. The servings go onto each friend's TAB: the current account.

Molefe's tab: it opened with the shop owing him R8 200 from last year. Add this year's servings — R36 000 interest, R120 000 salary, R48 000 leftovers — that side reaches R212 200. But all year he was taking money out to live on: drawings of R176 000. Tab at year end: the shop owes him R36 200.

De Waal's tab opens the other way around — R5 400 in the RED, because last year he drew out more than he earned. It happens; it is not a scandal, it is a debit balance. This year he earns R217 000 in servings, draws R182 000 plus that old R5 400 against it, and ends with the shop owing HIM R29 600. He worked his way out of the red — the tab shows it plainly, which is exactly what tabs are for.

The tab teaches the difference between EARNING and TAKING. You can earn R217 000 and take R182 000 — tab grows. You can earn less than you take — tab goes red. Either way, the planted capital on the wall never moved. Your questions for this part are up now: run both tabs top to bottom and say who owes whom, and how much.

## Subtopic: The Scale Never Lies

Last tool, and it is grade 8's oldest friend wearing grade 11 clothes: the accounting equation. What the shop OWNS equals what the owners are OWED plus what outsiders are OWED. Every partnership event tips the scale somewhere — and it must always level out.

De Waal EFTs in R50 000 more capital: the bank fattens by fifty, the owners' side fattens by fifty. Level. Molefe takes stock worth R3 000 home for a family event: shelves lighter by three, owners' side lighter by three — through drawings, priced at cost, because no customer bought anything. Level. The bank quietly adds R18 000 interest to the loan: outsiders' side up eighteen, owners' side down eighteen — the shop got poorer without the bank balance moving. Level, and sneaky, and beloved by examiners.

And the one that catches everyone: interest on capital allowed to Molefe. Feels like the shop paying out — but no cash moves and the total owners' side does not change. All that happens is that R36 000 of the profit pool gets a name on it: Molefe's. Sharing the pot doesn't shrink the pot; it just writes names on the servings.

When any equation question comes, refuse to guess from the story. Write the double entry first — which account is debited, which credited — label each account asset, equity or liability, and read the plus and minus straight off. The story can mislead; the entry cannot. And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: five events on the scale, and every one must balance before you answer.
