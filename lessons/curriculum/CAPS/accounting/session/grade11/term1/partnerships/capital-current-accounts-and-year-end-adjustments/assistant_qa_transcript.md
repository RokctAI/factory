**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: why does the capital account have to stay fixed? Surely it's simpler to run everything through one account per partner?

**Tutor:** Simpler to write, impossible to read — and the agreement itself would break. Three separate jobs depend on the capital figure staying still. First, interest on capital is calculated ON the capital balance: Molefe's R36 000 is nine percent of a fixed R400 000. If salaries, drawings and profit shares churned through the same account, the base for that calculation would change every month, and the partners would argue about which balance the nine percent applies to. Second, the profit-sharing ratio of four to three IS the ratio of the fixed capitals — let the balances drift and the ratio drifts with them, silently rewriting the agreement nobody signed. Third, readers of the statements need to distinguish permanent investment from undrawn earnings: a bank deciding whether to lend looks at capital as the partners' long-term commitment, and at the current accounts as money that could be drawn out next week. One merged account hides that difference. So the fixed capital account is not bureaucracy — it is the agreement made visible, and the current account exists precisely so the capital account never has to move.

***

**Bianca:** A learner asked this and it is a good one: with the provision for bad debts, we reduce profit for debts that might never actually go bad. Isn't that just making the profit wrong on purpose?

**Tutor:** It feels that way until you see what the alternative claims. Showing debtors at the full R48 000 asserts that every single debtor will pay — and years of experience say that assertion is false; we simply cannot yet name the ones who will fail. Prudence resolves the dilemma: when uncertainty exists, never overstate assets or income. But notice how carefully the mechanism avoids dishonesty in the other direction. We do not write off anyone — every debtor stays in the ledger, every debt is still chased, and the debtors list is untouched. We only hold a measured percentage, five percent based on the business's own collection history, against the total. And the profit is not punished twice: next year we do not create a fresh provision, we adjust the existing one, so only the movement — the R400 when debtors rose to R56 000 — touches that year's profit. If collections improve and the provision falls, the adjustment is credited and profit gains it back. The provision is not pessimism; it is the most honest number available under uncertainty, revised every year as the uncertainty resolves.

**Bianca:** Next: capitalised interest confuses everyone. If no money left the bank, how can the business have an interest expense at all?

**Tutor:** Because an expense is not defined by cash leaving — it is defined by the business becoming poorer, and this business became R18 000 poorer the moment the bank added the interest to the loan. Test it with the accounting equation. Before the interest: liabilities include a loan of R150 000. After: the loan stands at R168 000. Liabilities rose by R18 000 while assets did not move a cent — so owner's equity must have fallen by R18 000, and a fall in equity from operations is exactly what an expense is. The double entry says the same thing: debit Interest on loan, credit Loan. Compare it with ordinary interest paid in cash: there the credit is Bank, assets fall, equity falls — same expense, different second leg. The exam consequence is the one to hold onto: capitalised interest appears in the income statement as interest expense like any other, but in the cash budget it appears nowhere, because no cash moved. And when you reconstruct the loan account, the interest sits on the credit side alongside the opening balance, with repayments on the debit side — opening R150 000 plus R18 000 less R30 000 gives the closing R138 000.

**Bianca:** Last one: reversals feel like extra work for nothing. If the bookkeeper is careful, can't we just skip them and post the March phone bill straight to Accrued expenses?

**Tutor:** You could — that is the alternative method, and it works right up until a human being touches it. Skipping the reversal means every arriving invoice must be split by memory: the March telephone payment must be posted partly against last year's accrual and, if the bill differs from the estimate, partly to this year's expense. The bookkeeper must remember, for every one of dozens of accrued and prepaid items, that a year-end adjustment exists and which portion of each receipt or payment belongs to it. One forgotten split and an expense is doubled or an income vanishes. The reversal replaces memory with mechanism: flip every accrual and deferral on the first morning, and from then on the bookkeeper posts every bill and receipt the ordinary way, to the ordinary account, with no special knowledge required — the opening credit of R1 850 on the telephone account absorbs the payment automatically. That is what makes it good internal control rather than ceremony: a system that depends on nobody's memory cannot be defeated by anybody's forgetfulness. The double-counting is prevented by structure, not by carefulness — and structure is cheaper than carefulness, every single year.
