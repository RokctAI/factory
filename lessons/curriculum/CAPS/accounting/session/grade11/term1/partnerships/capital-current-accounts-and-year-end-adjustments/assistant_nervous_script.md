Hey, it's Bianca — I know partnerships felt like the topic where accounting suddenly doubled: two owners, two capital accounts, two current accounts, and a pile of adjustments on top. Breathe. Underneath, it is one small idea wearing several jackets.

The idea: keep what a partner PLANTED apart from what a partner is OWED. Planted money is the capital account, and it stands still. Everything else — interest earned, salary earned, bonus, profit share, drawings taken — flows through the current account, the running scorecard. If a question ever confuses you, ask of each amount: is this planting, or is this flowing? Planting touches capital. Flowing touches current. That one sort settles most marks in this topic.

The adjustments are the same six you did in grade 10, plus two new ones — and the new ones each have a one-line handle. Provision for bad debts: a dimmer switch on debtors, set at a percentage, and after the first year you only record the turn of the dial, never a whole new switch. Capitalised interest: the loan grows instead of the bank shrinking — debit the interest expense, credit the loan, and chase the balance with one sentence: opening, plus interest, minus repayments, equals closing.

Reversals scare people, but the rule is one question long: did the adjustment shuffle an amount across the year-end line? Shuffles — accrued and prepaid expenses, income in advance, accrued income — get flipped on day one. Real losses — depreciation, stock deficit, bad debts, the provision — stay exactly where they are.

And in the exam, write your double entries in full words: debit this account, credit that account, with the amount. Markers pay for the entry even when a later total slips. You've got this!
