# Part 1 — Expert

Until now the businesses in this course had one owner. From today, Bushveld Hardware has two. Lerato Molefe and Riaan de Waal have signed a partnership agreement, and the books of Molefe and de Waal Traders must now answer a question a sole trader never faces: whose money is whose? Today: why a partnership splits each owner's interest into a capital account and a current account; what the partnership agreement promises each partner; the year-end adjustments revised from grade 10; the two adjustments new to grade 11 — provision for bad debts and capitalised interest on a loan; and the reversals that open the next financial year. The financial year ends on 28 February.

## Subtopic: Capital Accounts, Current Accounts and the Agreement

A partnership exists because two or more people pool money, skill and risk under a written agreement, and the agreement is the constitution of the books. Molefe contributed capital of R400 000; de Waal contributed R300 000. Those amounts sit in two separate CAPITAL accounts, one per partner, and by agreement they stay FIXED — the capital account moves only when a partner formally increases or withdraws capital, never for day-to-day earnings.

Everything that flows between the business and a partner during the year runs instead through that partner's CURRENT account. Interest on capital earned, a partner's salary earned, a bonus earned, a share of the remaining profit — all credited to the current account. Drawings taken in cash or stock — debited to it. So the current account is a running scorecard: a credit balance means the business owes the partner; a debit balance means the partner has drawn more than they have earned and owes the business.

Why the split? Because the two accounts answer different questions. The capital account answers: how much has this partner permanently invested? The current account answers: how much of this partner's earnings is still sitting in the business right now? Collapse them into one account and both answers blur — and interest on capital, which the agreement calculates on the FIXED capital, loses its base.

The agreement of Molefe and de Waal promises three things before any profit is shared. Interest on capital at 9 percent per year — R36 000 to Molefe and R27 000 to de Waal. Partners' salaries — Molefe R120 000 per year for managing the finances, de Waal R144 000 per year for running the store. And a bonus of R10 000 to de Waal if the store meets its sales target. Only after those come off is the remainder shared between the partners in the ratio of their capitals, four to three. Hold those promises — the appropriation account in the next lesson pays them out. And note the GAAP frame around all of it: the BUSINESS ENTITY rule says the partnership's records are kept apart from the partners' private affairs, which is exactly why partners earn interest and salaries from the business like outsiders would. Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: fixed capital against moving current, and what the agreement promises whom.

## Subtopic: Year-End Adjustments Revised

At 28 February the trial balance of Molefe and de Waal Traders is provisional, because the MATCHING principle demands that this year's statements carry this year's income and expenses — no more, no less. Six adjustments from grade 10 return, each with its double entry spoken in full.

Accrued expenses: the February telephone account of R1 850 arrives in March. It belongs to this year — debit Telephone, credit Accrued expenses, a current liability. Prepaid expenses: the insurance total includes R2 400 paid for March, April and May at R800 a month — next year's cover. Debit Prepaid expenses, a current asset, credit Insurance. Income received in advance: a tenant has paid rent of R3 200 for March already, so the rent income of R41 600 in the books contains thirteen months. Debit Rent income, credit Income received in advance, bringing rent income to R38 400 — twelve months at R3 200. Accrued income: commission of R1 500 earned in February has not yet been received — debit Accrued income, credit Commission income.

Depreciation, twice. Vehicles cost R240 000, depreciated at 20 percent per year on cost — R48 000: debit Depreciation, credit Accumulated depreciation on vehicles. Equipment carries a value of R64 000 at the start of the year, depreciated at 10 percent on the diminishing balance — R6 400: debit Depreciation, credit Accumulated depreciation on equipment.

And trading stock deficit: the records say stock of R187 300 should be on the shelves; the physical count finds R185 200. The missing R2 100 is an expense — debit Trading stock deficit, credit Trading stock — and an internal control alarm, because stock that the books cannot find has been lost, broken or taken.

Each adjustment obeys matching; the prepaid and accrued entries also obey PRUDENCE — never overstate income or assets, never understate expenses or liabilities. Stop for this section's questions now: each adjustment named, its double entry given, and its effect on profit stated.

## Subtopic: The Two New Adjustments

Grade 11 adds two adjustments, and both are tested every year.

First, the PROVISION FOR BAD DEBTS. Debtors owe Molefe and de Waal Traders R48 000. Experience says some will never pay — but we do not yet know which ones. Prudence will not let us show R48 000 as fully collectible, so the agreement of the partners is to provide 5 percent: R2 400. Creating it: debit Provision for bad debts adjustment, an expense-like nominal account, credit Provision for bad debts, a negative asset that stands against debtors control. In the statements, debtors appear at R48 000 less R2 400 — R45 600 — the amount we prudently expect to collect. Keep the provision apart from BAD DEBTS itself: bad debts writes off a NAMED debtor we have given up on; the provision is a percentage against the UNKNOWN.

The next year the provision is not created again — it is ADJUSTED. Suppose debtors rise to R56 000; five percent is R2 800; the provision must rise by R400. Debit Provision for bad debts adjustment R400, credit Provision for bad debts R400. Had debtors fallen, the provision would fall, and the adjustment account would be credited — income, not expense. Only the MOVEMENT hits the profit each year.

Second, CAPITALISED INTEREST ON A LOAN. The partnership owes Thebe Bank a loan of R150 000 at 12 percent per year, and the bank does not send a separate interest bill — it ADDS the interest to the loan itself. Interest for the year: R18 000. The entry: debit Interest on loan, credit LOAN — not credit Bank, because no money left the business; the debt simply grew. At year end the partnership repaid R30 000. Follow the loan balance in words: opening R150 000, plus capitalised interest R18 000 gives R168 000, less the repayment of R30 000 — closing balance R138 000. The examiner's favourite trick is to give you three of those four figures and ask for the fourth; the relationship opening plus interest less repayments equals closing answers every version. Pause for this section's questions: the provision created, the provision adjusted, and the loan balance chased through the year.

## Subtopic: Reversals on the First of March

The adjustments truthfully closed 28 February; left alone, they would falsify March. So on the first day of the new financial year, the accrual and deferral adjustments are REVERSED — flipped exactly — so that the new year starts clean.

Watch the telephone. At year end we debited Telephone R1 850 and credited Accrued expenses. In March the bill arrives and is paid — and the bookkeeper will naturally debit Telephone again. Without a reversal, R1 850 of expense would be counted twice, once in each year. So on 1 March: debit Accrued expenses, credit Telephone — the telephone account opens the year with a CREDIT of R1 850, and when the March payment of R1 850 is posted, the account nets to nil for that bill. The expense lands in exactly one year: the old one, where it belongs.

The same flip serves all four accrual adjustments. Prepaid expenses: debit Insurance, credit Prepaid expenses R2 400, so this year's insurance account already carries the three months paid last year. Income received in advance: debit Income received in advance, credit Rent income R3 200, releasing March's rent into March. Accrued income: debit Commission income, credit Accrued income R1 500, so that when the debtor pays the commission, the account nets correctly.

What is NEVER reversed: depreciation, trading stock deficit, bad debts and the provision for bad debts. Those were not timing shifts between two years; they recorded value genuinely consumed or lost, and reversing them would resurrect losses that really happened. The test is one question: did this adjustment move an amount ACROSS the year-end boundary? If yes, reverse it on day one. If it recorded a permanent loss or consumption, leave it. The final questions of this part are with you now: reverse the right four, defend leaving the others, and show the telephone account netting to nil.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same lesson from the kota shop on the corner — because two friends running one till is the oldest partnership there is.

## Subtopic: Two Friends, One Till, Two Scorecards

Two friends open a kota shop. Thabo puts in R40 000; Sipho puts in R30 000. Write those two numbers on the wall and do not touch them — that is each friend's CAPITAL, the money planted in the business like a tree. It only changes if someone plants more or digs some out, with both agreeing.

But every week, things flow. Sipho works the counter full time, so the agreement pays him a bigger salary. Thabo's money planted was bigger, so he earns more interest on capital. Sipho takes airtime and bread from the shop — drawings. You cannot write all of that on the wall over the planted money; you would lose track of what was planted and what was earned. So each friend also gets a SCORECARD — the current account. Earnings go on one side, takings go on the other, and the balance says who is owed what. Scorecard in credit: the shop owes the friend. Scorecard in debit: the friend has eaten ahead of his earnings.

That is the whole first idea of partnerships: the wall shows what each partner planted, the scorecard shows what each partner is owed right now, and the written agreement decides the salaries, the interest and how leftover profit splits. In Bushveld Hardware's case: Molefe planted R400 000 and de Waal R300 000, the agreement pays interest at 9 percent, salaries of R120 000 and R144 000, a R10 000 bonus if the store hits target, and the leftovers split four to three. Quick check before we carry on — questions on wall against scorecard are coming to you right now.

## Subtopic: Making the Photo Honest

Think of year end as taking a photo of the business on 28 February. The adjustments are just honesty edits before the shutter clicks — nothing sneaky, only truth.

The phone bill for February hasn't arrived? February still used the phone. Write the R1 850 in as an expense and as a debt. Paid insurance for three months of NEXT year? That R2 400 is not this year's cost — set it aside as something the business is owed. The tenant paid March's rent early? That R3 200 is not this year's income yet — it is money held for next year. Somebody owes you February commission of R1 500? It was earned in February, so it counts in February.

Then the two new edits for grade 11. First: customers owe the shop R48 000, but be honest — a slice of that will never come. You don't know WHO will fail to pay, only that someone will. So you dim the picture by 5 percent, R2 400, and show debtors at R45 600. That dimmer switch is the provision for bad debts, and next year you don't build a new one — you just turn the dial. Debtors up to R56 000 means the dial moves to R2 800, and only the R400 turn touches this year's profit.

Second: the bank loan that charges interest by making the loan BIGGER. No money leaves the shop — the debt just grows quietly. Loan R150 000, interest 12 percent is R18 000, so the debt becomes R168 000; pay back R30 000 and it ends at R138 000. Say it as a sentence: what I owed, plus what it grew, minus what I paid, is what I owe now. Your questions for this part are up now — make each edit, and say whether it makes profit look bigger or smaller.

## Subtopic: Flipping the Switches on Day One

Here is the morning-after problem. On 28 February you wrote in the phone bill that hadn't arrived. Fine — the photo was honest. But in March the bill actually arrives and gets paid, and whoever pays it will write it down AGAIN. Same bill, written twice, in two different years. The fix is simple and mechanical: on the first morning of the new year, flip the switch back. Every adjustment that moved money across the year-end line gets reversed, exactly, on 1 March.

Flip the phone: the telephone account starts the new year R1 850 in credit, the payment lands, and the two cancel to nothing. Flip the insurance, so the three prepaid months now count in the year they cover. Flip the tenant's early rent, so March's rent counts in March. Flip the owed commission, so the money arriving doesn't get counted as new income.

But do NOT flip everything. Depreciation stays — the bakkie really did wear out, and no new year un-wears it. The missing stock stays missing. The bad debts written off stay written off, and the provision dial stays where you set it. One question sorts them: did this adjustment shuffle an amount between two years, or did it record something truly lost? Shuffles get flipped. Losses stay.

And keep the why in your pocket for exam day: all of this serves matching — every rand of income and expense lands in the year it belongs to — and prudence — never paint the picture prettier than it is. And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: flip the right four switches, leave the rest, and prove the phone bill gets counted exactly once.
