# Part 1 — Expert

The bank reconciliation compared our record of cash with an outsider's. This lesson repeats the discipline on a different account and a different outsider: the supplier. Every month a creditor posts a STATEMENT showing what its books say we owe; our own creditors ledger holds an account for that same supplier showing what our books say we owe. The two will differ. Today: why, how to work through both records, how to build the Creditors Reconciliation Statement, and why this small monthly job protects real money. Bushveld Hardware — Molefe and de Waal Traders of Polokwane — carries the example, and the supplier is Limpopo Timber & Board Suppliers.

## Subtopic: Two Records of One Debt

First fix what each record is. The supplier's STATEMENT is an extract from the supplier's own DEBTORS ledger: to Limpopo Timber, Bushveld Hardware is a debtor, and the statement lists every invoice raised, every credit note issued, every receipt banked, and the balance the supplier believes is owing. Our CREDITORS LEDGER account for Limpopo Timber is the mirror image: every purchase on credit posted from the Creditors Journal, every return posted from the Creditors Allowances Journal, every payment and discount posted from the Cash Payments Journal, and the balance we believe we owe. One debt, two sets of books, kept by two different businesses who never see each other's entries.

The causes of disagreement fall into the same three families as the bank reconciliation. TIMING: an invoice dated before month end but delivered to us after it; a payment we released on the twenty-seventh that the supplier only banks in the new month. Both records are correct; the post is simply slower than the calendar. OMISSIONS: interest the supplier charged on an overdue account, or a settlement discount the supplier allowed, neither of which we knew about until the statement arrived. And ERRORS, on either side — and this is the difference from the bank reconciliation, where only the bank could be wrong about a fee. Here the supplier is another ordinary business with an ordinary bookkeeper, so the statement itself may contain mistakes: an invoice charged to the wrong customer, a return not processed, a figure transposed.

That third family sets the discipline for the whole lesson. Nothing on a supplier's statement is automatically true. Read it as a claim to be tested, not an instruction to be obeyed. Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the two records identified, and the three families of difference named with an example of each.

## Subtopic: Reading the Statement Against the Ledger Account

At 28 February 2026 the statement from Limpopo Timber shows R59 250 owing. Our creditors ledger account for Limpopo Timber shows R40 440. Comparison, item by item, produces five differences.

One: invoice 2214 for R6 400, for boards delivered on 26 February. The supplier has raised it; the invoice reached our office only on 3 March, so the Creditors Journal never saw it. This is our omission — the goods were received before year end. Record it: debit Trading stock, credit Creditors control, and post to the supplier's account in the creditors ledger. Our balance rises by R6 400.

Two: credit note 118 for R3 600, issued for warped boards we returned, was entered in the Creditors Allowances Journal as R2 340. Our error, and the R1 260 difference divides by nine — a transposition. We reduced the debt by too little, so our balance must come down a further R1 260: debit Creditors control, credit Trading stock.

Three: interest of R620 charged by the supplier on the overdue portion of the account. Our omission — nobody told us. Debit Interest expense, credit Creditors control. Our balance rises by R620.

Four: a settlement discount of R800 allowed by the supplier on our early January payment, never recorded by us. Our omission again, and it works the other way: debit Creditors control, credit Discount received. Our balance falls by R800.

Five: R1 850 of ironmongery charged on the statement was never delivered to us — the supplier invoiced another customer's order to our account. Their error, and the correction belongs in their books, not ours. Nothing is journalised; the item goes on the reconciliation statement, and we telephone Limpopo Timber the same day.

Six: our electronic transfer of R12 000, released on 27 February, does not appear on the statement. A timing difference: we recorded it correctly, the supplier had not yet processed it. Again nothing is journalised.

Work our balance: R40 440 plus 6 400, less 1 260, plus 620, less 800 — a corrected creditors ledger balance of R45 400. Stop for this section's questions now: each difference classified, and each entry that belongs in our books stated as a debit and a credit.

## Subtopic: Building the Creditors Reconciliation Statement

The corrected ledger balance of R45 400 still does not equal the statement's R59 250, because two items remain that only the supplier can resolve. The Creditors Reconciliation Statement reconciles them, and it moves in one direction: from the balance per the statement received to the balance per our corrected creditors ledger account.

"Creditors Reconciliation Statement of Limpopo Timber & Board Suppliers on 28 February 2026." Balance per statement received: R59 250. Less the payment of R12 000 in transit, because the supplier will still deduct it when it processes our transfer. Less R1 850 invoiced to us in error, because the supplier will still reverse it. Total: R45 400 — agreeing with our corrected account to the rand.

The direction test is the same one the bank reconciliation taught, aimed at a different outsider: what will the SUPPLIER still do to this balance? Still going to deduct it — deduct it. Still going to add it — add it. An invoice we have not yet received but the supplier has correctly raised does NOT appear here at all, because we fixed that in our own books; the reconciliation statement carries only what remains for the supplier to do.

One structural point marks are given for: this statement reconciles ONE supplier's account, not the whole Creditors Control account. Grade 10 reconciled the control account against the total of the creditors list — an internal check. This reconciliation is external, one supplier at a time, and the two exercises answer different questions. The questions for this section are in front of you now: build the statement with its full heading, and defend why each remaining item sits where it does.

## Subtopic: Errors, Traps and Control Value

Four traps recur. First, adjusting our books for the supplier's error — a learner who journalises the R1 850 quietly pays for goods another business received. Test every statement line against our own delivery notes and invoices before accepting it. Second, the direction of a credit note. A credit note REDUCES what we owe; understating one, as we did by R1 260, leaves our balance too high, so the correction reduces it. Learners reverse this constantly, and reversing it puts the answer out by twice the error. Third, treating interest charged as an expense we may ignore because we did not agree to it: we did agree to it, in the credit terms, and it must be recorded — debit Interest expense, credit Creditors control — and then queried if it looks wrong. Fourth, confusing the two reconciliations: control-against-list is internal; statement-against-ledger-account is external.

Method marks follow the same architecture as the bank topic. Show the corrected creditors ledger account with the opening balance, each correction on its own line, and the corrected balance. Show the reconciliation statement with a full heading, every item named and bracketed where deducted, and one total. A marker rewards the classification of each item and the direction of each adjustment separately from the final figure.

Then the control value, examined in words. This reconciliation is the only routine check that catches a supplier overcharging, double-invoicing, omitting a credit note, or charging interest that the credit terms do not permit — because nobody inside the business is in a position to notice. It also catches OUR failures: unrecorded invoices, understated returns, payments allocated to the wrong supplier's account. Sound practice: reconcile every supplier's statement in the month it arrives; require the reconciliation to be done by someone who does not authorise payments; pay from our own corrected ledger account, never from the statement; investigate old items rather than carrying them; and query differences in writing so a record exists. Paying a statement without reconciling is the simplest way a small business loses money quietly for years. The final questions of this part are with you now: traps named, method defended, and the control argument written out.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same job from behind a spaza counter — because anyone who has ever bought stock on account from a wholesaler has done this reconciliation without knowing its name.

## Subtopic: The Spaza Owner and the Wholesaler's Bill

You run a spaza. Every week you fetch stock from the wholesaler and sign for it, and you write in your own exercise book what you took and what you paid. At month end the wholesaler posts you a bill: "You owe us R59 250." Your exercise book says R40 440.

Now — do you just pay their number? Of course not. That is somebody else's book about your money. But do you refuse to pay and insist on yours? Also no, because your book has holes in it too. What you actually do is sit down with both and go line by line until you understand every single difference.

And once you sit down, you will find the differences are of only three kinds, and you already know them from the bank lesson. Things that are late — an invoice posted to you that has not arrived yet, a payment you made that they have not banked yet. Things you never knew about — they charged you interest for paying late, or gave you a discount for paying early, and nobody phoned you either time. And things that are simply WRONG — and here is the twist that makes this different from the bank: the wholesaler can be wrong. A bank charge is a bank charge; but the wholesaler's clerk is a person, and people put one customer's delivery on another customer's account.

So the golden rule for the whole lesson: a supplier's bill is a claim, not a command. Quick check before we carry on — questions on the three kinds of difference are coming to you right now, and especially on who is allowed to be wrong.

## Subtopic: Whose Book Is Wrong?

Take the five differences one at a time and just ask: whose book must change?

The R6 400 of boards delivered on the twenty-sixth, with the invoice arriving in March. The goods are in the yard — you have them. So YOUR book is short. Add it. The credit note for the warped boards: they credited you R3 600, you wrote R2 340. Yours is wrong by R1 260 — and again, when a difference divides neatly by nine, you swapped two digits. You wrote off too little, so you owe less than your book says: bring your balance DOWN by R1 260. The interest of R620 for paying late: annoying, but you signed the terms. Your book is short. Add it. The R800 discount they gave you for paying early in January: lovely, and you never wrote it down, so your book is too high. Take it off. Count: R40 440, plus 6 400, minus 1 260, plus 620, minus 800 — R45 400. That is what you honestly owe.

Now the two that are NOT your problem. The R1 850 of ironmongery you never received — that is their clerk's mistake, and you fix nothing in your book; you phone them today. And the R12 000 you transferred on the twenty-seventh that has not landed on their statement — you did nothing wrong, they are just behind.

Your questions for this part are up now: for every difference, say out loud whose book must change before you move a single rand.

## Subtopic: Why This Small Job Protects Real Money

Last piece, and it is the part that matters most once you are running a real business. Why bother? Because R45 400 and R59 250 are almost fourteen thousand rand apart, and if you had simply paid the bill you would have handed a stranger money you did not owe.

The bridge that proves you are right is short. Start where they are: R59 250. They still have to knock off the R12 000 you already sent — knock it off. They still have to take back the R1 850 that was never yours — take it off. R45 400. Their number and your number now agree, and you can pay with a clear conscience. The same question steers every line, exactly as it did with the bank: what does the OTHER side still have to do to this balance?

And the control point, in plain words. Nobody inside a small business can catch a supplier overcharging, because nobody inside the business sees the supplier's books — except through this statement. Do the reconciliation the month the statement arrives. Let the person who checks it not be the person who authorises payments, so no one can cover their own tracks. Pay from YOUR corrected account, never from their statement. Chase old items instead of carrying them forward year after year. And put every query in writing, so there is a record when the argument comes. Businesses do not usually collapse from one big theft; they bleed slowly from bills nobody checked. And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: cross the bridge yourself, and say why paying a statement without reconciling it is the most expensive shortcut in the book.
