**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! The big one first: why can we not simply take the bank's balance as the correct one? The bank has computers and auditors — surely their number wins?

**Tutor:** The bank's number is accurate about what the BANK has processed; it says nothing about what the business owns at that date. Keep the two facts apart. Amatola Building Supplies banked R19 800 on the twenty-seventh of February: that money is legally the business's, it is sitting in the clearing system, and the shop's own record rightly includes it. The bank simply had not processed it by midnight on the twenty-eighth. Equally, the shop released two transfers totalling R21 300 to suppliers; those payments have left the business's control, and the shop's record rightly shows the money gone, even though the bank had not yet debited it. Adopt the bank's balance and you would be claiming money you have already spent while denying money you already hold. So we treat the bank's record as an independent CHECK, not as the truth — we borrow the items the bank knew and we did not, we fix our own mistakes, and then we prove the remaining gap is nothing but timing. The figure that reaches the Statement of Financial Position is the corrected Bank account balance of R68 990, not the statement's R67 540.

**Bianca:** A learner sent this in and I think it is a fair one: a customer's electronic transfer of R5 400 bounced. Why is that not simply written off as a bad debt?

**Tutor:** Because nothing has been proven about the customer's ability to pay — only about the state of their account on a single day. The reversal restores the position to what it always truly was: debit Debtors control, credit Bank. The receipt we recorded was, in substance, never received; the debt was never settled; the customer is back on the debtors list owing R5 400, and the business will phone them tomorrow and very often be paid in full. Writing it off immediately would do three wrong things at once. It would remove a debt that is still collectible, understating both the Debtors control account and the debtors list. It would charge an expense that has not been incurred, understating profit — a breach of the matching principle, not an act of prudence. And it would kill the follow-up, because nobody chases a debt that has been written off. A bad debt entry belongs at the end of a proper process — reminders sent, terms exhausted, the account judged irrecoverable, and a partner authorising the write-off in the General Journal. Bounced today, chased tomorrow, written off only when genuinely lost.

**Bianca:** Next one: what happens when the account is in OVERDRAFT? Learners say everything they memorised suddenly flips over and they freeze.

**Tutor:** Nothing flips if you reason instead of memorise. An overdraft simply means the Bank account carries a CREDIT balance in our ledger — the bank has become a creditor rather than an asset — and it is shown in brackets among current liabilities in the Statement of Financial Position. The corrections behave identically: fees still reduce the balance, interest charged on the overdraft still reduces it, a dishonoured receipt still reduces it, and each entry still names its contra account. On the reconciliation statement the single test survives untouched: what will the bank still do to this balance? An uncleared deposit will still be added by the bank, so it is still added, and it pulls an overdraft towards zero. Outstanding transfers will still be deducted, so they are still deducted, and they push an overdraft deeper. The only genuine discipline is care with negatives — write the overdraft in brackets, keep it in brackets through every add and subtract, and state plainly at the end whether the final figure is favourable or unfavourable. Overdraft questions exist precisely to separate learners who reason from learners who recite.

**Bianca:** Last one: the reconciliation is called an internal control, but the person doing it already works in the office. How does it actually stop anyone stealing?

**Tutor:** It works because of who the third participant is. Almost every internal check in a small business is one employee watching another, and either of them can be tired, fooled or complicit. The bank cannot. It keeps a complete, dated, independent record of every rand that entered or left, it has no stake in the business's story, and it cannot be talked into altering an entry. The moment a cashier pockets a receipt, the business's records and the bank's records stop agreeing, and the disagreement is a fixed amount that refuses to disappear. The control only holds under three conditions, though. The person who reconciles must not be the person who handles cash or writes up the cash journals — otherwise they are checking their own work and can bury the gap. It must happen monthly, because a difference found four weeks old can be investigated, while one found a year old is archaeology. And a partner must review and sign it, with every long-outstanding item explained rather than rolled forward — because the oldest trick in cash theft is a permanent "outstanding deposit" that never quite clears, covering a hole dug months before.
