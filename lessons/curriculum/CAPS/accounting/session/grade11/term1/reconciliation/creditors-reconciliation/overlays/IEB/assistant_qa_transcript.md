**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! Start with this one: we already did a creditors reconciliation last year. Why are we doing it again, and what actually changed?

**Tutor:** Last year's exercise was a different job wearing a similar name. There you reconciled the Creditors Control account in the general ledger against the total of the creditors list drawn from the creditors ledger — both records kept inside the business, by the same staff, from the same journals. That check proves the posting was done consistently; it cannot prove the underlying amounts are right, because if an invoice was captured wrongly in the Creditors Journal, the control account and the list are wrong together and still agree perfectly. This year introduces an OUTSIDE record: the supplier's own statement, kept in another town by another business with no interest in flattering us. Comparing our account for that supplier with the supplier's account for us tests the substance, not just the arithmetic. The two exercises answer different questions and neither replaces the other: control-against-list asks "did we post consistently?", statement-against-ledger asks "do we and our supplier agree on what is actually owed?" Questions are set on both, and muddling them is a reliable way to lose marks.

***

**Bianca:** Here is the one that started an argument in class: an invoice for R7 200 is on their statement and not in our books, but the invoice only reached our office in March. Some learners say it belongs on the reconciliation statement because it is a timing difference. Who is right?

**Tutor:** It belongs in our books, and the reason is the date of the transaction, not the date of the paperwork. The roof sheeting was delivered on the twenty-fifth of February. From that moment Amatola Building Supplies owned stock it had not paid for, which is precisely the definition of a liability, and the period in which that liability arose is the one ending on the twenty-eighth. Leaving it out would understate both trading stock and creditors at month end, which the accrual basis forbids. The postal delay explains why we had not recorded it; it does not excuse leaving it unrecorded once we know. So we enter it now — debit Trading stock, credit Creditors control — and it vanishes from the reconciliation entirely, because there is nothing left for the supplier to do about it. The test that settles every case of this kind is simple: has the supplier already done everything correctly on its side? If yes, the difference is ours to record. If the supplier still has an entry to make or reverse, it goes on the reconciliation statement.

**Bianca:** Next: the supplier charged us R540 of interest for paying late. It feels wrong to just accept that. Should we not query it before recording it?

**Tutor:** Record it first, query it second — and the order matters. Recording is not agreement; it is recognition that a claim exists and that, on the face of the credit terms we signed, it is a real obligation at month end. Debit Interest expense, credit Creditors control. Leaving it out because we resent it would understate both our expenses and our liabilities, which is a plain breach of prudence — a business may never improve its own result by ignoring a bill it has been sent. Once it is recorded, the commercial work begins: check the terms, check the dates we actually paid, and if the charge is wrong, query it in writing and reverse it when the supplier issues a credit. Notice, too, what that interest is telling management. Interest charged by a supplier is more than an expense; it is evidence that the business is paying outside its credit terms, which is a signal about liquidity, about how slowly debtors are being collected, and about settlement discounts being thrown away. A strong answer names both the entry and the warning.

**Bianca:** Last one: how do I actually decide the direction — add or subtract — on the reconciliation statement? I keep guessing and I keep getting it backwards.

**Tutor:** Stop hunting for rules about item types and ask one question about each surviving item: what does the SUPPLIER still have to do to this balance? Take the payment of R10 800 released on the twenty-sixth. The supplier has not processed it yet, but it will, and when it does the supplier's balance for us falls by R10 800 — so on the statement we deduct it. Take the R2 380 of guttering charged in error. The supplier will reverse that charge, and its balance for us falls again — so we deduct that too. If instead the difference were an invoice the supplier had genuinely raised and simply not yet shown on the statement, its balance would rise, and we would add it. The reason this works where memorised rules fail is that the statement is somebody else's account, and we are predicting their future entries, not describing our own. Two more disciplines around it: write the heading in full, naming the supplier and the date; and put brackets on every deduction so the marker can see the direction you intended — that is where a large share of the marks actually sits.
