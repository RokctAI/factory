# Part 1 — Expert

Grade 10 posted the cash journals to the Bank account and trusted the result. Grade 11 tests that trust. Two independent records exist of the same money — the business's own Cash Receipts and Cash Payments Journals, and the monthly statement the bank issues — and they will not agree. Today: why they differ, which differences are our omissions and must be journalised, which are timing differences and must be reconciled, how the corrected Bank account is arrived at, and how the Bank Reconciliation Statement proves the two records tell one story. The running example for this whole grade is Molefe and de Waal Traders, trading as Bushveld Hardware in Polokwane — partners Lerato Molefe and Riaan de Waal — financial year ending 28 February.

## Subtopic: Why Two Records of One Bank Account Differ

Start with the mechanics that create the gap. The business records a receipt the day it banks the money; the bank records it the day the deposit clears. The business records a payment the day the EFT is released; the bank records it the day the recipient's account is credited. Those lags create TIMING differences — both records are correct, and time will close the gap by itself.

Then a second, different cause: the bank knows things the business does not. Service fees, cash deposit fees and card machine charges are levied by the bank without asking. Interest on a favourable balance is credited without notice. Stop orders and debit orders are executed by the bank on standing instruction. A debtor's electronic transfer may be dishonoured — reversed for want of funds — days after we recorded it. None of these appear in our journals until the statement arrives, because we did not initiate them and were not told. These are OMISSIONS on our side, and they must be entered in the journals.

And a third cause, the least comfortable: ERRORS. Ours — a transposed figure in the Cash Payments Journal, a receipt cast twice, an amount posted to the wrong column. Or the bank's — an amount debited to our account that belongs to another client. The rule that governs the whole topic follows from that classification: OUR omissions and OUR errors are corrected in OUR books, through the cash journals; TIMING differences and the BANK's errors are never entered in our books at all — they belong on the Bank Reconciliation Statement, waiting for time or the bank to resolve them.

Hold that three-way sort — timing, our omissions, errors — because every item you meet in an examination question must be placed in one of those boxes before you touch a figure. Pause here: this section's questions are with you now, and they ask exactly that sorting.

## Subtopic: Working Through the Statement

The February statement of Bushveld Hardware arrives. The provisional balance in the Bank account in the general ledger, from the two cash journals, stands at R64 310 favourable — a debit balance, an asset. Comparing statement with journals, line by line, produces seven findings.

One: bank charges of R1 860 — service fees, cash deposit fees and card machine commission — on the statement, absent from the Cash Payments Journal. Enter in the CPJ: debit Bank charges, credit Bank.

Two: interest of R400 credited by the bank on the favourable balance. Enter in the Cash Receipts Journal: debit Bank, credit Interest income.

Three: a stop order of R2 400 for the monthly insurance premium. Debit Insurance, credit Bank.

Four: a debit order of R1 250 to the security company that guards the yard. Debit Sundry expenses, credit Bank.

Five: an electronic transfer of R6 800 received from a debtor and recorded in the CRJ in January has been DISHONOURED — the debtor's account lacked funds. The receipt must be reversed and the debt reinstated: debit Debtors control, credit Bank. Note what this means in substance — the debtor still owes us; nothing was collected.

Six: our own error. A payment to a creditor of R4 380 was entered in the CPJ as R3 480. The bank paid R4 380. We understated the payment by R900 — and note the fingerprint: a difference divisible by nine signals a transposition. Correct it: debit Creditors control, credit Bank with R900.

Seven: cheque number 1046 for R9 300, issued on 20 August 2025 to a creditor, has still not been presented. A cheque more than six months old is STALE and the bank will not honour it. Cancel it: debit Bank, credit Creditors control — the money is ours again and the creditor is owed again.

Work the balance: R64 310 less 1 860, plus 400, less 2 400, less 1 250, less 6 800, less 900, plus 9 300 — a corrected Bank account balance of R60 800 favourable. That is the figure the Statement of Financial Position will carry, and every later lesson in this grade uses it. Stop for this section's questions now: each finding classified, each double entry stated in full, and the corrected balance defended.

## Subtopic: The Reconciliation Statement

The corrected R60 800 will still not equal the statement balance, because the timing differences remain open. The Bank Reconciliation Statement bridges them.

At 28 February the bank statement closes at R55 650 favourable. Three bridging items exist. A deposit of R28 400 was banked late on 27 February and had not cleared by month end — an OUTSTANDING DEPOSIT, in our books but not yet in the bank's. Two electronic transfers released to suppliers on 26 and 27 February had not yet been debited — EFT 0412 for R14 250 and EFT 0417 for R12 700, R26 950 together, in our books but not yet in the bank's. And R3 700 was debited to our account in error by the bank; it has been notified and will reverse the entry.

Now build the bridge, and mind the direction of each adjustment. Begin with the balance per bank statement, R55 650. ADD the outstanding deposit R28 400, because the bank will add it. ADD the R3 700 wrongly debited, because the bank will give it back. LESS the outstanding EFTs, R26 950, because the bank still has to take them off. Total: R60 800 — the corrected Bank account balance, to the rand.

Two disciplines earn marks here. First, the heading in full: "Bank Reconciliation Statement of Molefe and de Waal Traders on 28 February 2026" — a statement drawn at a date. Second, the direction test, applied item by item, asked as one question: what will the BANK still do to this balance? If the bank will still add it, add it; if the bank will still subtract it, subtract it. Learners who memorise "deposits are added" fail the moment a question opens with an overdraft or asks the reconciliation to run the other way. Reason the direction; never recite it. The questions for this section are in front of you now — build the bridge, and justify every sign.

## Subtopic: Traps, Method Marks and Internal Control

Four traps recur, and each has a defence. First, entering timing differences in the journals — a learner who "corrects" the outstanding deposit destroys both records at once; outstanding items are already in our books, which is precisely why they are outstanding. Second, direction errors on an OVERDRAFT: an unfavourable bank balance is a credit balance in our books and appears in brackets, and every add and subtract still follows the same question — what will the bank still do? Third, the dishonoured payment recorded as an expense: it is not a loss, it is a reversal — debit Debtors control, credit Bank — and only if the debt is finally written off does Bad debts appear. Fourth, forgetting the stale cheque, which quietly leaves R9 300 sitting in an outstanding list forever.

Method marks are earned by showing structure even when the arithmetic slips. Write the corrected Bank account as an account, with the provisional balance brought down, each correction on its own line with its contra account named, and the balance carried down. Write the reconciliation statement with a full heading, every item labelled, brackets on every subtraction, and one final total. Markers award the classification and the direction independently of the final figure — so a wrong total with correct workings still collects most of the marks, while a bare correct number collects only its own.

Then the control purpose, which the syllabus examines in words. The reconciliation is a DIVISION OF DUTIES made arithmetic: an outside party — the bank — independently records every movement of cash, and comparing its record with ours catches theft, omission and error that no internal check would find. Sound practice follows: reconcile monthly, without fail; have the reconciliation performed by someone who does not handle cash or write up the journals; have it reviewed and signed by a partner; investigate every long-outstanding item rather than carrying it forward; and question deposits that repeatedly appear only after month end, which is the classic signature of lapping — using a later receipt to cover an earlier theft. The final questions of this part are with you now: traps named, method marks defended, and the control argument set out in full sentences.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same reconciliation from the taxi rank — because two people counting the same money and getting different answers is not an accounting problem, it is an everyday one.

## Subtopic: Two People Counting the Same Money

Picture a stokvel. You keep the book at home; the treasurer keeps a book at the bank. At the end of the month you compare and the numbers differ. Nobody stole anything. Nobody is lying. The books differ because two people wrote at two different moments.

That is exactly the hardware shop and its bank. Bushveld Hardware writes down a deposit the afternoon it drops the bag at the branch. The bank writes it down when the money clears — maybe the next working day. The shop writes down a payment the second it releases the transfer; the bank writes it when the supplier's account lights up. Same money, two clocks. Give it a few days and the two books say the same thing on their own.

But there is a second reason the books differ, and this one is not about time. The bank does things to your money without phoning you. It takes its fees. It pays you a little interest. It carries out the standing instructions you signed months ago — the insurance premium, the security company. And if a customer's transfer bounces because their account was empty, the bank quietly takes that money back out.

So when the statement arrives you are really reading a letter from the bank listing everything it did that you did not know about. Those things are not the bank's mistakes — they are your missing entries. Quick check before we carry on: questions on the two reasons the books differ are coming to you right now — time on one side, news on the other.

## Subtopic: The Bank's News and Your Own Mistakes

Sort everything on that statement into three piles, and the whole topic becomes easy.

Pile one — NEWS. Things the bank did that you never wrote down: fees of R1 860, interest of R400, the R2 400 insurance stop order, the R1 250 security debit order, and the customer's R6 800 transfer that bounced. All of these go into your books now, through the cash journals. Money out goes into the payments journal; money in goes into the receipts journal. The bounced R6 800 is the one that stings — you thought you had been paid, you had not, so the customer goes straight back onto the debtors list.

Pile two — YOUR SLIPS. The bookkeeper wrote a R4 380 payment as R3 480. The bank paid the real amount. Nine hundred rand short in your book, so fix your book. Little tip that has saved many learners: when a difference divides neatly by nine, you have almost certainly swapped two digits around. And the old cheque number 1046 for R9 300, written back in August and never cashed — a cheque goes off after six months, like milk. Cancel it, put the money back, and put the supplier back on the list of people you owe.

Pile three — THINGS YOU LEAVE ALONE. The deposit of R28 400 that has not cleared. The two transfers of R14 250 and R12 700 that have not gone out yet. And the R3 700 the bank took off by mistake. You do not touch these in your books. Your book is right; the bank is simply behind, or wrong. Your questions for this part are up now: take each item, and say out loud which of the three piles it lands in before you do anything with it.

## Subtopic: The Bridge Between the Two Numbers

After you write in all the news and fix your slips, your own book says R60 800. The bank's letter says R55 650. Two honest numbers, still apart — and the reconciliation statement is simply the bridge you build between them.

Walk it. Start where the bank is: R55 650. That deposit of R28 400 is still coming — the bank will add it, so add it. The R3 700 the bank took by mistake is coming back — the bank will add that too, so add it. The two transfers of R26 950 have not left yet — the bank will take them off, so take them off. Count: 55 650 plus 28 400 plus 3 700 less 26 950 — sixty thousand eight hundred. The same R60 800 your own book shows. The bridge holds.

And here is the only question you ever need when you are not sure whether to add or subtract: what is the bank still going to do to this? Not "is it a deposit?" — that rule breaks the moment a question hands you an overdraft. Ask what the bank still has to do, and follow it.

Last thought, and it is the part examiners love in words. Why bother at all? Because the bank is an outsider with its own record of every cent that moved. If the person who handles the cash also writes the books and also does the reconciliation, nobody is watching. Split those jobs, do the reconciliation every single month, let a partner sign it, and chase anything that has been sitting on the outstanding list too long — that is how a small business catches a hand in the till before it becomes a habit. And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: cross the bridge in both directions, and say in your own words why the bank makes such a good watchdog.
