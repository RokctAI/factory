Hey, it's Bianca — I know that bank statement looked like a wall of numbers designed by somebody who wanted you to give up. Take a breath. There are only three things you can ever do with a line on that statement, and you already know all three.

Thing one: write it into your books, because you genuinely did not know about it. Fees, interest, stop orders, debit orders, a customer's payment that bounced. Thing two: fix your own slip, because you wrote the wrong number down. Thing three: leave it completely alone, because your book is right and the bank is just running late — the deposit that has not cleared, the transfers that have not gone out, and anything the bank did by mistake. Three piles. That's the whole topic.

The habit that protects your marks is small and boring and works every time: before you write a single figure, put a letter next to each item on the question paper. B for "goes in my books", R for "goes on the reconciliation statement". Do all the sorting first, all the arithmetic second. Learners lose marks by mixing the two jobs, not by being bad at adding.

And when you get to the bridge, do not memorise which things get added. Ask the question instead: what is the bank still going to do to this? Still going to add it — add it. Still going to take it off — take it off. That one question survives overdrafts, reversed formats and every trick a paper can play on you.

One more thing worth knowing: markers pay for structure. A properly headed reconciliation statement, every item named, brackets on every subtraction — that collects marks even when your final total is out. So write it out fully, even when you are unsure. You've got this!
