**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! Start here: we already did a creditors reconciliation in Grade 10. Why are we doing it again, and what actually changed?

**Tutor:** Grade 10 did a different job with a similar name. There you reconciled the Creditors Control account in the general ledger against the total of the creditors list drawn from the creditors ledger — both records kept inside the business, by the same staff, from the same journals. That check proves the posting was done consistently; it cannot prove the underlying amounts are right, because if an invoice was captured wrongly in the Creditors Journal, the control and the list are wrong together and still agree beautifully. Grade 11 introduces an OUTSIDE record: the supplier's own statement, kept in another town by another business with no interest in flattering us. Comparing our account for that supplier with the supplier's account for us tests the substance, not just the arithmetic. So the two exercises answer different questions and neither replaces the other: control-against-list asks "did we post consistently?", statement-against-ledger asks "do we and our supplier agree on what is actually owed?" Examiners deliberately set both, and mixing them up is a reliable way to lose marks.

***

**Bianca:** Here is the one that caused a fight in class: an invoice for R6 400 is on their statement and not in our books, but the invoice only reached our office in March. Some learners say it belongs on the reconciliation statement because it is a timing difference. Who is right?

**Tutor:** It belongs in our books, and the reason is the date of the transaction, not the date of the paperwork. The boards were delivered on the twenty-sixth of February. From that moment Bushveld Hardware owned stock it had not paid for, which is exactly the definition of a liability, and the accounting period in which that liability arose is the one ending on the twenty-eighth. Leaving it out would understate both trading stock and creditors at year end, which the matching principle and the accrual basis both forbid. The postal delay explains why we had not recorded it; it does not excuse leaving it unrecorded once we know. So we enter it now — debit Trading stock, credit Creditors control — and it disappears from the reconciliation entirely, because there is nothing left for the supplier to do about it. The test that settles every case of this kind is simple: has the supplier already done everything correctly on its side? If yes, the difference is ours to record. If the supplier still has an entry to make or reverse, it goes on the reconciliation statement.

**Bianca:** Next: the supplier charged us R620 of interest for paying late. It feels wrong to just accept that. Should we not query it before recording it?

**Tutor:** Record it first, query it second — and the order matters. Recording it is not agreement; it is recognition that a claim exists and that, on the face of the credit terms we signed, it is a real obligation at year end. Debit Interest expense, credit Creditors control. Leaving it out because we dislike it would understate both our expenses and our liabilities, which is a straightforward breach of prudence — a business may never improve its own profit by ignoring a bill it has been sent. Having recorded it, we then do the commercial work: check the terms, check the dates on which we actually paid, and if the charge is wrong, query it in writing and reverse it when the supplier issues a credit. Notice, too, what the interest is telling management. Interest charged by a supplier is not just an expense; it is evidence that the business is paying outside its credit terms, which is a signal about liquidity, about the debtors' collection period, and about whether the discount for early settlement is being thrown away. A good answer in an examination names both the entry and the warning.

**Bianca:** Last one: how do I actually decide the direction — add or subtract — on the reconciliation statement? I keep guessing and I keep getting it backwards.

**Tutor:** Stop trying to remember rules for item types and ask one question about each surviving item: what does the SUPPLIER still have to do to this balance? Take the payment of R12 000 released on the twenty-seventh. The supplier has not processed it yet, but it will, and when it does the supplier's balance for us will fall by R12 000 — so on the statement we deduct it. Take the R1 850 of ironmongery charged in error. The supplier will reverse that charge, and its balance for us will fall again — so we deduct it too. If, instead, the difference were an invoice the supplier had genuinely raised and simply not yet added to the statement, its balance would rise, and we would add it. The reason this works and memorised rules fail is that the statement is somebody else's account, and we are predicting their future entries, not describing our own. Two more disciplines around that: write the heading in full, naming the supplier and the date; and put brackets on every deduction so the marker can see the direction you intended, which is where a large share of the marks actually sits.
