# Part 1 — Expert

Assets do not stay forever. They are sold, traded in, scrapped or taken by the owner — and every one of those exits must be recorded, because an asset leaving carries three things with it: its cost, the depreciation accumulated against it, and a profit or loss on the transaction. Today: the ASSET DISPOSAL account and why it exists; the four-step procedure that never varies; disposal at the start, during and at the end of a financial year; the four kinds of disposal — cash, credit, trade-in and drawings; and the internal control that governs an asset leaving the premises. This is one of the most reliably examined procedures in grade 11 accounting.

## Subtopic: Why an Asset Disposal Account Exists

When an asset leaves, three ledger accounts hold pieces of it. The asset account — Vehicles, Equipment — holds its COST, anchored there since purchase. The Accumulated Depreciation account holds every cent of wear written off against it. And whatever the buyer pays holds the third piece. Those three must be brought together in one place, compared, and the difference recognised — and that place is the ASSET DISPOSAL account, opened for the transaction and closed the moment it is done.

Why not simply credit the asset account with the selling price? Because the selling price and the cost are different amounts, and the difference is not a mystery to be absorbed quietly — it is a PROFIT or LOSS that belongs on the income statement. Post the sale directly against cost and both the asset account and the profit figure are corrupted.

So the logic of the account, stated once: the disposal account receives the asset's cost on its debit side, receives the accumulated depreciation and the proceeds on its credit side, and the balancing figure is the result. If the credits exceed the debits, the asset sold for more than its carrying value — a PROFIT on disposal, closed off to the income statement as other operating income. If the debits exceed the credits, it sold for less — a LOSS on disposal, an operating expense.

And understand what that profit or loss actually MEANS, because examiners ask: it is not a trading profit. It is a correction — the accumulated verdict on every depreciation estimate made across the asset's life. A profit on disposal says depreciation was charged too heavily; a loss says the asset wore out faster, or was sold in poorer condition, than the rate assumed. Depreciation is an estimate, and disposal is the day the estimate is settled against reality.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the three pieces the disposal account gathers, the meaning of the balancing figure, and why proceeds are never posted straight against cost.

## Subtopic: The Four Steps That Never Vary

Every disposal question, however dressed, is answered by the same four steps in the same order. Learn the order and the arithmetic follows.

STEP ONE: bring the depreciation up to date. An asset sold in the eighth month of the year has worn for eight months of that year, and that wear must be charged BEFORE the disposal is computed — debit Depreciation, credit Accumulated Depreciation, for the part-year. Skip this step and every figure after it is wrong; it is the single most common error in the topic, and it is worth the most marks.

STEP TWO: transfer the COST to the disposal account — debit Asset Disposal, credit the asset account (Vehicles or Equipment) with the full original cost. The asset account is now clear of that item.

STEP THREE: transfer the ACCUMULATED DEPRECIATION — debit Accumulated Depreciation, credit Asset Disposal with the total written off against that asset, including the part-year just charged in step one. The accumulated account is now clear of it too.

STEP FOUR: record the PROCEEDS — whatever is received, credited to Asset Disposal against the appropriate debit: Bank for a cash sale, a Debtor for a credit sale, the new asset account for a trade-in, or Drawings if the owner took it.

Then balance the account. Work it with figures. A vehicle cost R180 000 on 1 March two years ago; depreciation is 20 percent per annum on the diminishing balance; the financial year ends 28 February; the vehicle is sold for R95 000 cash on 31 August, six months into the third year.

Depreciation year one: 20 percent of 180 000 — R36 000; carrying value R144 000. Year two: 20 percent of 144 000 — R28 800; carrying value R115 200. Now step one, the part-year: six months of 20 percent of 115 200 — 23 040 times six over twelve — R11 520. Accumulated depreciation total: 36 000 plus 28 800 plus 11 520 — R76 320. Carrying value at disposal: 180 000 less 76 320 — R103 680.

The disposal account: debit cost R180 000; credit accumulated depreciation R76 320; credit bank R95 000. Credits total R171 320 against debits of R180 000 — the debits exceed by R8 680: a LOSS on disposal of R8 680. Confirm it the quick way, which is also the exam's check: proceeds R95 000 against carrying value R103 680 — sold R8 680 below what the books carried. The two methods must agree, always.

Stop for this section's questions now — the four steps in order, the part-year depreciation computed first, and the loss confirmed both ways.

## Subtopic: Timing, and the Four Kinds of Disposal

TIMING first, because it decides step one. An asset sold on the FIRST DAY of the financial year has worn for none of that year — no additional depreciation is charged, and the accumulated figure is simply the balance brought forward. An asset sold DURING the year needs the part-year charge, counted in months from the start of the financial year to the date of sale. An asset sold on the LAST DAY has worn a full year, so a full year's depreciation is charged first. Read the date against the financial year end and count the months on your fingers — the fraction over twelve is a mark.

Now the four kinds of disposal, each differing only in step four.

CASH sale: debit Bank, credit Asset Disposal with the amount received. The plainest case.

CREDIT sale: the buyer owes the business. Debit a DEBTOR — and note the distinction examiners test: this debtor is not a trade debtor, because the business does not trade in vehicles; it is a sundry or other debtor, and it belongs under other receivables rather than in Debtors Control.

TRADE-IN: the old asset is exchanged as part-payment for a new one. The trade-in value takes the place of proceeds: debit the NEW asset account with the trade-in allowance, credit Asset Disposal. The new asset is then recorded at its full cost — the trade-in allowance plus whatever cash or credit settles the balance. Watch the trap: learners record the new asset at the cash paid only, understating it by the trade-in value and understating every future depreciation charge with it.

DRAWINGS: the owner takes the asset for private use. Debit Drawings, credit Asset Disposal with the agreed value. And the ethical point the syllabus attaches: that value must be a fair one. An owner who takes a vehicle worth R95 000 and records drawings of R20 000 has understated their withdrawals and overstated the business's equity — a misstatement, not a favour to themselves, and one an auditor traces by comparing the recorded value against the asset's carrying value and market evidence.

DONATED or SCRAPPED assets are the limiting case: no proceeds at all, so the entire remaining carrying value becomes the loss on disposal.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — timing decided from the dates, and each kind of disposal recorded correctly at step four.

## Subtopic: The Register, the Controls and the Ethics

Behind every disposal stands the ASSET REGISTER, and disposal is where its discipline pays. The asset's page carries its description and identifying number, purchase date and cost, the method and rate, and the year-by-year depreciation, accumulated total and carrying value — so the disposal's figures are read from the page rather than reconstructed. When the asset leaves, the page is closed off with the disposal date, the proceeds and the profit or loss, and the register's totals must still agree with the ledger's Vehicles and Accumulated Depreciation balances afterwards. An asset removed from the ledger but left open on the register — or the reverse — is a control failure that shows up immediately in that reconciliation.

The CONTROLS over disposal, which examiners ask for by name. AUTHORISATION: no asset leaves without written approval from a person senior enough to be accountable, because an asset walking out on a verbal instruction cannot be distinguished from theft. PRICING: obtain quotes or a valuation, so the business can show the price was reasonable rather than a favour to a friend or relative — this is where nepotism enters an accounting question. DOCUMENTATION: a numbered invoice or receipt for the sale, a signed gate release for the physical asset, and the register updated. SEGREGATION of duties: the person who authorises the disposal should not be the person who receives the money or who updates the records. And PHYSICAL verification: periodic counts of assets against the register, so a disposal that was never recorded — or an asset recorded as sold that is actually sitting in a director's garage — is caught.

The frauds these controls answer are specific: selling a business asset and pocketing the proceeds, with the asset written off as scrapped; selling to a related party at far below value; and recording a disposal that never happened, to explain an asset that has simply disappeared. Each is defeated by the same pairing — independent authorisation and a register reconciled to the ledger.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the register's role at disposal, the controls named with the fraud each prevents, and the fair-value duty when an owner takes an asset.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same procedure from the second-hand car deal — because everyone has watched a bakkie change hands, and the accounting is exactly that transaction, written down.

## Subtopic: What the Books Still Think It's Worth

Your uncle bought a bakkie three years ago for R180 000. Ask him what it's worth today and he'll say what any owner says: "about R100 000, maybe." Now ask his books the same question, and they answer with a number they've been calculating quietly all along — the cost, minus everything written off for wear. That's the carrying value, and today it stands at R103 680.

Then he sells it. And here's the whole topic in one moment: he gets R95 000, and his books were expecting R103 680. He's R8 680 short. That gap has a name — a loss on disposal — and it must go on the record.

Now, why can't he just write "sold, R95 000" and move on? Because three separate facts about that bakkie are sitting in three different places in his books: what he PAID for it, how much WEAR he's written off against it over three years, and now what he GOT for it. Leave any of them behind and the books tell a lie — the bakkie's cost still sitting in the vehicles account for a bakkie that's gone, or the wear still stacked up against a vehicle that isn't there.

So the books open a temporary desk for this one transaction — the asset disposal account. All three facts land on that desk, they're weighed against each other, and the difference is the profit or loss. Then the desk is cleared away. It exists for one deal and closes the same day.

And one honest thing about that R8 680 loss, because it's not a business failure: it's the books admitting their guess was slightly off. Depreciation is always an estimate — a rule about how fast things wear. The day you sell is the day reality checks your guess. Sell for more than the books expected and you show a profit on disposal; less, and a loss. Either way it's a correction, not a trading result.

Questions on this piece are with you now: the three facts that must be gathered, and what a profit or loss on disposal really tells you.

## Subtopic: Four Moves, Always the Same Order

Here's the recipe, and it never changes — only the last step varies.

Move one, and this is the one everybody forgets: **catch up the wear**. He sold on 31 August, six months into his financial year. That bakkie was driven for those six months, so six months of wear must be written off BEFORE anything else happens. Twenty percent a year on R115 200 is R23 040 for a full year, so six months is R11 520. Miss this move and every number after it is wrong — and it's the step worth the most marks in the whole question.

Move two: **put the price he paid on the desk**. The full R180 000 he paid three years ago — not what it's worth now, the original price. That clears the bakkie out of the vehicles account.

Move three: **put all the wear on the desk**. Everything ever written off against it: R36 000, then R28 800, then the R11 520 you just caught up — R76 320 altogether. That clears the wear account.

Move four: **put what he got on the desk**. R95 000 in cash.

Now weigh the desk. R180 000 went on one side; R76 320 plus R95 000 — R171 320 — on the other. Short by R8 680. Loss.

And here's the quick check that takes five seconds and catches nearly every mistake: what did the books say it was worth (R103 680), and what did he actually get (R95 000)? The difference must be the same R8 680. If your long method and your quick check disagree, one of them has a mistake — usually the forgotten part-year wear from move one.

Your questions for this part are up now: run the four moves in order, then prove the answer with the quick check.

## Subtopic: Cash, Credit, Trade-In, or Uncle Takes It Home

Only the last move changes, depending on how the bakkie left.

**Cash** — money into the bank. Simplest.

**On credit** — the buyer will pay next month. So somebody owes the business. But careful: this person is not a normal customer. Your uncle's business sells building materials, not bakkies, so the buyer isn't a trade debtor — he's a once-off debtor, filed separately from the customers who buy stock. Examiners like that distinction.

**Trade-in** — he drives the old bakkie onto the dealer's floor and drives a new one off. The dealer allows him R95 000 for the old one against a new bakkie priced at R250 000, so he pays R155 000 in cash. Now the trap that eats marks: the new bakkie goes into the books at **R250 000**, not at the R155 000 he handed over. The old bakkie was part of the payment — real value, just not in cash. Record it at R155 000 and the new asset is understated by ninety-five thousand rand, and every depreciation charge for the next five years is wrong too.

**Uncle takes it home** — he keeps the bakkie for the family. Nothing comes into the business, so this is drawings: the owner helping himself to R95 000 of business property. And the value written down must be the honest one. Writing "drawings R20 000" for a bakkie worth R95 000 isn't clever tax planning; it's understating what he took and overstating what the business is worth — and the first person to notice will be anyone comparing that figure to the carrying value.

**Scrapped or given away** — nothing received at all, so the whole remaining book value becomes the loss.

Last thing, and it's the reason the paperwork matters: an asset leaving a business is the easiest thing in the world to steal. Written approval before anything moves, a quote or valuation so nobody can claim a mate's price was fair, a numbered receipt, and different people authorising, receiving the money and updating the records. Plus a walk around now and then, checking that what the register lists is actually there — because the classic fraud is simple: sell the thing, pocket the cash, and write it up as "scrapped".

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: four ways out, the trade-in value recorded properly, and the controls matched to the fraud each one stops. Get this and you can handle any disposal question a paper throws at you.
