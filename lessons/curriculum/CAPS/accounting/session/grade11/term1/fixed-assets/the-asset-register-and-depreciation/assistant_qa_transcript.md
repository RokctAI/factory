**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: why does delivery and installation get added to the machine's cost? Those feel like expenses — we paid a truck driver and an electrician, not the machine supplier.

**Tutor:** Ask what the business was actually buying, and the answer settles it: not a machine sitting on a supplier's floor in another town, but a working machine, bolted down and wired in on its own premises. Until the delivery and installation were paid, the business owned something that could not produce a cent — so those costs were not optional extras alongside the purchase; they were part of getting the asset into the condition the business needed. That is the principle: cost includes everything necessary to bring the asset into use. Now look at what it protects. Depreciation spreads an asset's cost over the years it serves, so if delivery and installation were expensed immediately, this year would carry costs that will be earning income for the next ten years — matching broken — while every future depreciation charge would be computed on too small a figure. One caution on the boundary, because examiners test it: costs that keep the machine running AFTER it is in use — servicing, repairs, insurance, operator wages — are revenue expenditure. Getting it working is capital; keeping it working is revenue.

***

**Bianca:** Next: the part-year rule counts from the month of purchase. But what if the asset was bought on the twentieth of the month — do we count that month or not?

**Tutor:** Count it, and count it whole — the convention in school accounting is months, not days. An asset bought on the twentieth of June is treated as owned from June, so a February year end gives nine months, exactly as if it had arrived on the first. The reasoning is proportionality of effort: depreciation is an estimate already, spread by a rate someone chose, so computing it to the day adds false precision to an approximation and costs time the examination does not reward. What you must NOT do is round the other way and drop the month of purchase, which learners do when the date falls late in the month — that quietly understates the charge. Two practical rules follow. First, count inclusively from the month of purchase to the month of year end, on your fingers, and write the fraction into your working. Second, be alert that some questions state a policy explicitly — 'depreciation is calculated from the date of purchase' or 'no depreciation in the year of acquisition' — and where the question sets a policy, the question's policy wins over the convention every time. Read the note above the figures before you calculate.

***

**Bianca:** This one confused a few of us: if an asset is fully depreciated but still working, hasn't the business been depreciating it wrongly all along? The estimate was clearly too fast.

**Tutor:** The estimate was conservative rather than wrong, and the distinction matters. Depreciation rates are set in advance from a judgement about useful life, and no judgement made ten years early is going to land exactly. If the asset outlives its written-down life, the business charged its cost over a shorter period than reality delivered — meaning earlier years carried more expense and later years carry none, so profits were understated then and are flattered now. That is a known limitation of the method, not a misstatement, because the TOTAL charged over the asset's life can never exceed what it cost: you may not depreciate below zero. What a business should do is review its rates periodically — if machines routinely outlast their assumed lives, the rate is too aggressive and future assets should be depreciated over a longer period. Two practical points for the exam. A fully depreciated asset still in use is usually left at a nominal R1 carrying value so it does not disappear from the records. And it remains on the register, in its location, with a responsible person — because its book value has nothing to do with its resale value or its attractiveness to a thief.

***

**Bianca:** Last one, and it's the one people argue about at home: the boss lets staff use the company bakkie on weekends. Is that actually wrong, or is everyone being dramatic?

**Tutor:** It is only wrong when it is undisclosed — the problem is never the use itself but the absence of a decision and a record. Take the two honest paths. The business can adopt a policy permitting limited private use as part of remuneration, in which case it is a benefit, it is disclosed, its cost is understood, and everyone from the owner to the auditor can see what the business is providing and what it costs in fuel, wear and insurance risk. Or the private use is treated as what it is when the owner does it — drawings at fair value, reducing equity. Both are defensible. What is not defensible is the third path, which is what usually happens: nobody decides anything, the bakkie is used at weekends, the fuel is paid by the business, the extra wear shortens the asset's life, and the accounts quietly report all of it as business expense. Now the statements overstate business costs, understate drawings or remuneration, and the depreciation policy is being consumed by kilometres nobody authorised. The control that resolves it is unglamorous and effective: a vehicle logbook recording who took it, when, and for what purpose — which turns an argument about principle into a document anyone can check.
