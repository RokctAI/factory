**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: why do we bother bringing depreciation up to date on an asset we're about to get rid of? It's leaving anyway.

**Tutor:** Because it was working right up until the day it left, and the months it worked belong to this year's expenses. Think about what depreciation actually records: the consuming of an asset's usefulness over the time it serves. A vehicle sold at the end of August served the business for six months of that financial year — it carried stock, it burned diesel, it wore out — and matching insists this year's income statement carries the cost of that service. Skip the charge and two figures go wrong at once: this year's profit is overstated because six months of wear was never expensed, and the disposal itself is computed against a carrying value that is too high, which manufactures a fictitious loss or hides a real profit. There is also a clean logical point: the profit or loss on disposal is meant to be the difference between what you received and what the asset was genuinely worth in the books ON THE DAY IT LEFT — and that value does not exist until the final months of wear have been charged. The step is not bookkeeping ceremony; it is what makes the final figure mean anything.

***

**Bianca:** Next: on a trade-in, why do we record the new asset at the full price? We only paid cash for part of it.

**Tutor:** Because you paid for all of it — just not all in cash. Historical cost records what a business gave up to acquire an asset, and here it gave up two things: money, and a vehicle it owned. The old bakkie was worth something real, which is precisely why the dealer reduced the price by that amount; had you sold it privately for R95 000 and then paid the dealer R250 000 in cash, nobody would hesitate to record the new asset at R250 000. Doing both in one transaction changes the paperwork, not the economics. Now see what recording only the cash would do downstream, because this is where the marks and the real damage sit: the asset enters the books R95 000 too low, so every depreciation charge computed on it for the next five years is too low, so profit is overstated year after year, and the eventual disposal of the NEW vehicle is wrong too. One understatement quietly corrupts half a decade of statements. The rule to carry: trade-in allowance plus cash paid equals cost — always.

***

**Bianca:** This one is uncomfortable but someone asked it honestly: if the owner takes a business vehicle home, who is actually harmed by writing down a low value? It's his business.

**Tutor:** In a sole trader it is his business, and that is exactly why the honest number matters more than people expect. Start with who can be harmed. The bank reading his statements to decide on a loan sees equity that includes R75 000 of value the owner has actually removed — it is lending against something that is not there. A future buyer of the business values it on those statements. SARS assesses on them. And if there is a partner, as there will be in your next topic, the understated drawings are quite literally that partner's money — the profit distributed among partners is calculated after drawings, so understating what one partner took overstates what remains for everyone. Even alone, the owner deceives himself: he believes the business is worth more than it is, and reinvests or spends on that belief. The accounting principle underneath is the business entity rule — the business is treated as separate from its owner, so anything the owner removes is recorded at what it was actually worth. The auditor's test is exactly the comparison you would make yourself: the recorded drawings against the asset's carrying value and any market evidence. A large unexplained gap between them is not an accounting preference; it is a misstatement.

**Bianca:** Last one: our textbook shows both a "profit on disposal" and sometimes a "profit on sale of asset" — and one exam memo put it under other income while another treated it differently. Which is right?

**Tutor:** They are the same thing under different labels, and the variation is in presentation rather than principle — but knowing where it belongs protects your statement marks. The amount is the difference between proceeds and carrying value, and it arises from a transaction outside the business's trading operations: a shop earns its living selling stock, not selling its own vehicles. So it is not turnover and it never touches gross profit. In the statement of comprehensive income it is presented among OTHER OPERATING INCOME when it is a profit, and as an operating expense — usually shown as loss on disposal of asset — when it is a loss. Whichever label a particular textbook uses, apply two tests and you will place it correctly: is it operating income or expense rather than trading revenue, and is it presented separately rather than buried, so a reader can see that this year's profit includes a once-off item? That second point is the substance behind the presentation rule — a business whose profit looks healthy only because it sold a building has told the truth about the total and hidden the truth about the trading, which is precisely what separate disclosure prevents.
