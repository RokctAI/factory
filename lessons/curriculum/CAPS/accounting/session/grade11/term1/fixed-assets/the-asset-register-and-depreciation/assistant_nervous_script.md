Hey, it's Bianca — fixed assets look like a lot because there are three different things happening at once: a notebook to keep, a cost to work out, and depreciation with a new twist. Take them one at a time; none of them is hard on its own.

The notebook — the asset register — is just the debtors list wearing a different hat. One page per expensive thing, and all the pages added up must equal what the ledger says. You've reconciled a list to a control account before; this is the same move on different stuff.

The cost is where quiet marks live. Everything needed to get the thing WORKING counts: the price, the delivery, the installation. And then the sorting question for later spending — does it make the thing better or longer-lived (add it to the asset), or does it just keep it going (expense it)? New engine: asset. Service and tyres: expense.

The new twist in depreciation is the part-year. Something bought in June, with a February year end, was only owned nine months — so work out a full year first, then take nine twelfths. Count the months on your fingers and WRITE THE FRACTION in your working; markers give marks for that fraction even if the arithmetic wobbles afterwards. And slicing only happens in the year a thing arrives or leaves — every year in between is a full year.

One oddity to be ready for: a fully written-down machine still working. No more depreciation, often one rand left on the page, and it stays in the notebook because it's still worth stealing.

Notebook, true cost, part-year slice. You've got this!
