# Part 1 — Expert

Every till slip in South Africa carries a line most people never read: the VAT. Today we make that line intelligible — the need for and purpose of Value Added Tax, the principles on which it works, which supplies are standard-rated, zero-rated and exempt, the four calculations the examination demands, the treatment of bad debts, discounts and returns, and the ethics and control that surround a tax a business collects on the state's behalf. VAT appears in the grade 11 examination and again in grade 12; the calculations are short and the classification is where marks are lost.

## Subtopic: Why VAT Exists and How It Works

VALUE ADDED TAX is an INDIRECT tax on consumption: levied on goods and services rather than on income, and paid by the final CONSUMER, though collected and paid over by businesses. Its purpose is revenue — VAT is one of the largest single contributors to the national budget, funding the schools, clinics and infrastructure of the topics you studied in Economics — and its design has a practical virtue: it collects at every stage of production rather than relying on one final point, which makes evasion harder and collection steadier.

The mechanism is the phrase in its name: tax on the VALUE ADDED at each stage. Follow a loaf's ingredients through three businesses. The miller sells flour to the bakery for R100 plus VAT. The bakery sells bread to the shop for R160 plus VAT. The shop sells to you for R200 plus VAT. Each business charges VAT on what it SELLS — that is OUTPUT tax — and each claims back the VAT it PAID on what it bought — INPUT tax. Each pays over only the difference. So the bakery, charging output tax on R160 and claiming input tax on R100, hands SARS the tax on just R60 — the value IT added. Add the three businesses' payments together and SARS has collected exactly the VAT on the final R200 the consumer paid. Businesses are collectors, not payers: the tax rests where consumption happens.

Two principles govern the timing. Most VENDORS — businesses registered for VAT, compulsory above the prescribed turnover threshold and optional below it — account on the INVOICE BASIS: VAT is recorded when the invoice is issued, whether or not payment has happened. Smaller qualifying vendors may use the PAYMENTS (receipts) BASIS: VAT is recorded when money actually moves. The distinction matters for credit transactions, and the examination expects the difference stated.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the nature of VAT, output against input tax through the three-business chain, and the two accounting bases distinguished.

## Subtopic: Standard-Rated, Zero-Rated and Exempt

Three categories, and confusing them is this topic's most reliable lost mark — because two of them look similar and behave completely differently.

STANDARD-RATED supplies carry VAT at the current standard rate — most goods and services: clothing, appliances, restaurant meals, professional services, fuel-powered deliveries. The vendor charges output tax and claims input tax normally.

ZERO-RATED supplies are TAXABLE, but at a rate of zero. The list exists to protect poor households from tax on essentials: brown bread, maize meal, rice, samp, dried beans and lentils, milk, eggs, fresh fruit and vegetables, vegetable oil, paraffin — and, in a different category of reasoning, exported goods, which are taxed in the country of consumption instead. Because zero-rated supplies remain TAXABLE supplies, the vendor selling them still claims INPUT tax on its purchases: a vendor selling only zero-rated goods charges no output tax but reclaims its inputs, and receives a refund from SARS.

EXEMPT supplies fall outside the VAT system altogether: no VAT is charged, and — the crucial difference — NO input tax may be claimed on the expenses of making them. The list includes financial services on which interest is charged, residential rent, public road and rail transport, and educational services provided by recognised institutions.

Hold the distinction with the input-tax test, because it is the whole difference: zero-rated means taxable at nought percent, so inputs ARE recoverable; exempt means outside the system, so inputs are NOT recoverable. Ask "may the supplier claim its input tax?" and the two separate instantly.

Stop for this section's questions now — the three categories with examples, and the input-tax test applied to tell zero-rated from exempt.

## Subtopic: The Four Calculations

VAT calculations are arithmetic; the marks live in choosing the right one. Work at a standard rate of fifteen percent — that is, VAT is fifteen hundredths of the price EXCLUDING VAT, so the inclusive price is 1,15 times the exclusive price.

Calculation one: ADDING VAT to an exclusive amount. Goods priced at R2 000 excluding VAT: VAT is 2 000 times 15 over 100 — R300; the inclusive price is R2 300. Or in one step: 2 000 times 1,15.

Calculation two: EXTRACTING VAT from an inclusive amount — the one examiners favour, because learners reach for fifteen percent of the inclusive figure and are wrong. The inclusive amount is 115 percent of the exclusive amount, so the VAT portion is 15 over 115 of the inclusive figure. A till slip totalling R2 300 inclusive carries VAT of 2 300 times 15 over 115 — R300, with R2 000 excluding. Confirm it against calculation one: the two must agree, and that check costs five seconds.

Calculation three: FINDING THE EXCLUSIVE AMOUNT from an inclusive one: divide by 1,15. R2 300 over 1,15 is R2 000.

Calculation four: COST PLUS MARK-UP PLUS VAT — the pricing chain a business actually runs, and the favourite integrated question. Goods cost R1 200; the mark-up policy is 50 percent on cost; the selling price excluding VAT is 1 200 plus 600 — R1 800; add VAT: 1 800 times 1,15 — R2 070 payable by the customer. Note the order rigorously: MARK-UP FIRST, on the cost excluding VAT, THEN VAT on the marked-up price. Reverse them and both figures are wrong — and note too that the R270 of VAT never belonged to the business: its profit is the R600 mark-up.

Then the VAT RETURN itself: total output tax on sales, less total input tax on purchases and expenses, equals the amount PAYABLE to SARS — or, where inputs exceed outputs, REFUNDABLE by SARS.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — all four calculations, computed and cross-checked, with the mark-up order respected.

## Subtopic: Adjustments, Ethics and Control

Three transactions disturb VAT already recorded, and each has its own treatment.

GOODS RETURNED: a customer returns goods, so the sale reverses — and so does its output tax. The vendor issues a CREDIT NOTE showing the VAT portion, and reduces output tax accordingly. Goods returned to a supplier reverse input tax in the same way, on a debit note.

DISCOUNT: a discount reduces the amount actually charged, so it reduces the VAT with it. Trade discount is deducted before VAT is calculated at all. A settlement (cash) discount granted after invoicing reduces the consideration, and the output tax is adjusted by a credit note for the discount and its VAT.

BAD DEBTS: a debtor's account is written off as irrecoverable. On the invoice basis the vendor already paid over output tax on that sale — tax on money it will never receive. The Act permits recovery: the VAT portion of the written-off debt is claimed back as input tax. And should the debtor later pay after all, the VAT must be declared again. Note the symmetry examiners test: relief when the income proves illusory, restoration when it materialises.

Now the ethics, which this topic carries seriously. VAT is TRUST money — the vendor collects it from customers on SARS's behalf and holds it until the return is due. Using it to pay salaries or suppliers is not a cash-flow decision; it is spending money that belongs to the state, and it is exactly how vendors fall into unrecoverable arrears. The frauds the syllabus names: claiming input tax on private purchases put through the business; fictitious invoices manufactured to inflate input claims; charging VAT while unregistered — pocketing a "tax" the customer believes goes to SARS; understating sales by selling for cash off the books; and the smuggling and under-invoicing of imports.

The internal controls that answer them: register properly and display the VAT number on every tax invoice; issue valid tax invoices containing every required detail; keep source documents for the prescribed retention period; separate the duties of recording, banking and payment; reconcile the VAT control account to the returns monthly; and submit and pay on time, since penalties and interest compound quickly. The internal auditor's test is simple and searching: does every input tax claim rest on a valid tax invoice for a business purpose?

The final questions of this part are with you now: returns, discounts and bad debts treated correctly, and the frauds matched to the controls that catch them.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same tax from the till slip in your hand — because you have been paying VAT since you were old enough to buy sweets, and today it stops being invisible.

## Subtopic: The Line at the Bottom of the Slip

Buy anything at a supermarket and look at the bottom of the slip. There is a line marked VAT. You paid it. You did not choose to, and nobody asked you — and yet that line is one of the biggest sources of money the government has, paying for clinics, roads and schools.

Here is the part that surprises people: the shop did not keep that money. The shop is a COLLECTOR, not an owner. It takes the VAT from you at the till, holds it, and hands it to SARS. Businesses collect; consumers pay. That single sentence explains everything else in this lesson.

And the collecting happens in a clever chain. Think of bread. The miller sells flour to the bakery and adds VAT. The bakery sells bread to the shop and adds VAT — but it first claims BACK the VAT it paid the miller, so it only hands over the tax on the bit of value IT added by baking. The shop does the same when it sells to you. Every business in the chain pays tax on its own slice, nobody pays twice, and when you add up all the slices, SARS ends up with exactly the tax on what YOU finally paid. That is why they call it value added tax: each business is taxed only on the value it added.

So when you hear a business owner complain about "paying VAT", listen carefully — a registered business does not really pay it, it passes it on. What it pays is the difference between what it collected and what it was charged.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the slip are coming to you right now: who collects, who really pays, and what "value added" means in the chain.

## Subtopic: The Three Baskets at the Till

Not everything on the slip is taxed the same way, and the differences are deliberate — they are about fairness.

Basket one: MOST THINGS. Cooldrinks, airtime, jeans, a plate at a restaurant, a phone. Standard rate, VAT added, nothing special.

Basket two: THE ESSENTIALS BASKET. Brown bread, maize meal, rice, samp, dried beans, milk, eggs, fresh fruit and vegetables, cooking oil, paraffin. These carry ZERO VAT — deliberately, because a tax on food hits a poor household far harder than a rich one; if bread carried the full tax, the family spending most of its income on food would be paying most of its income into tax. So the state zero-rates the basics. Notice which bread, though — brown bread is zero-rated, and a fancy seed loaf is not; the list protects staples, not shopping preferences. Exports sit in this basket too, for a different reason: the goods will be consumed in another country, so that country taxes them, not us.

Basket three: OUTSIDE THE SHOP ENTIRELY. Some things are EXEMPT — no VAT at all, because they are not in the system: the interest a bank charges you, the rent on a home you live in, a bus or train fare, school and university fees.

Now the difference that trips everyone, told simply. Zero-rated means "in the tax system, charged at nothing" — so the shop selling maize meal still gets back the VAT it paid on its own costs. Exempt means "not in the system at all" — so a landlord renting out flats charges no VAT and gets NOTHING back on what he paid for paint and repairs; he simply absorbs it. Same zero on your slip, opposite consequence behind the counter. Ask "does the seller get its own VAT back?" and you will never mix them up again.

Your questions for this part are up now: sort items into the three baskets — and use the get-it-back question to separate the last two.

## Subtopic: The Fifteen-on-One-Fifteen Trick, and Whose Money It Is

Two things left: the sum everyone gets wrong, and the honesty that matters more than the sum.

The sum. Adding VAT is easy — a price of R2 000 becomes R2 300, because you add fifteen percent. But now go backwards: a slip says R2 300 INCLUDING VAT — how much VAT is in it? Almost everyone takes fifteen percent of R2 300 and gets R345. Wrong. Why? Because the fifteen percent was calculated on the SMALLER number — the price before tax. The R2 300 is already the whole thing: 100 parts of price plus 15 parts of tax — 115 parts altogether. So the tax is fifteen parts out of a hundred and fifteen: multiply R2 300 by fifteen over one-fifteen, and there is your R300. Remember it as a slogan: going up, fifteen on a hundred; coming down, fifteen on a hundred and fifteen.

And one order-of-operations rule for shopkeepers: work out your PROFIT first, then the tax. Stock cost R1 200; you want fifty percent on cost, so your price is R1 800 — that R600 is yours. Then add VAT on the R1 800, making R2 070 at the till. The extra R270 is not profit and never was; it is SARS's money sitting in your till until the return is due.

Which brings the real lesson. That VAT you collect is TRUST MONEY — someone else's money, held by you. Every month, businesses in trouble borrow from it: "I'll pay SARS next month, I need to cover wages now." It feels like cash flow. It is spending money that was never yours, and it compounds with penalties until the business drowns. The frauds are cousins of the same thinking: claiming back VAT on the family's groceries by pushing them through the business; inventing invoices to claim tax that was never paid; charging customers "VAT" while not even registered — that last one is simply theft from the customer, who believes the money is going to SARS.

The controls are ordinary discipline: proper registration and a VAT number on every invoice, real documents kept for every claim, the person who records not being the person who banks, and the VAT account reconciled every single month. One question catches almost everything: for every rand claimed back, is there a real invoice, for a real business purchase? If not, someone is stealing — from all of us, since it is the clinics and schools that go short.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the fifteen-on-one-fifteen calculation both ways, the mark-up-then-VAT order, and the trust-money test applied to a scenario. Pay attention to the bottom of the next slip you get — you will finally know exactly what that line means.
