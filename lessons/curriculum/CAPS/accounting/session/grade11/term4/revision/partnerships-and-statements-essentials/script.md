# Part 1 — Expert

Grade 11's centrepiece was the PARTNERSHIP — two or more owners, one business, and the bookkeeping that keeps their claims separate and fair. This revision session rebuilds that centrepiece end to end: the accounts each partner owns, the appropriation account that divides the profit, the financial statements and notes that report the whole — including the fixed assets that live inside them — and the indicators that judge the result. One partnership carries every figure: Zola and Sipho, trading as ZS Traders, year ended the last day of February, and every division will balance to the rand.

## Subtopic: Two Owners, Four Accounts

A sole trader needed one capital account; a partnership needs a PAIR of accounts PER PARTNER, and the split is the whole design. The CAPITAL account holds the partner's long-term investment — Zola contributed R200 000, Sipho R100 000 — and it barely moves: only a deliberate increase or decrease of the investment touches it. The CURRENT account is the living account: every year it receives the partner's share of profit and gives up the partner's drawings, and its balance says where the partner STANDS against the business — a credit balance, the business owes the partner; a debit balance, the partner has drawn more than earned.

Everything that flows through those accounts is governed by the PARTNERSHIP AGREEMENT: the salaries partners earn for working, the interest their capitals earn for waiting, any bonus, and the ratio that divides what remains. The agreement is the constitution — the bookkeeping merely obeys it. And keep the sharpest distinction in the topic: partners' salaries and interest on capital are NOT expenses of the business. They are APPROPRIATIONS — divisions of a profit already made. The income statement ends at net profit; only after that line does the dividing begin. Confuse the two and both statements break.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: capital against current, agreement items named, and appropriations held apart from expenses.

## Subtopic: Dividing the Profit — the Appropriation Account

Net profit for the year: R240 000. The appropriation account divides it in the agreement's order, and the discipline is that the account must EMPTY exactly.

First, salaries: Zola R96 000, Sipho R72 000 — R168 000 appropriated. Second, interest on capital at ten percent: Zola R20 000 on R200 000; Sipho R10 000 on R100 000 — R30 000 more. Third, the agreement grants Sipho a bonus of R12 000. Now the remainder: 240 000 less 168 000, less 30 000, less 12 000 — R30 000, shared equally as the agreement says: R15 000 each. Check by partner: Zola 96 000 plus 20 000 plus 15 000 — R131 000. Sipho 72 000 plus 10 000 plus 12 000 plus 15 000 — R109 000. Together: R240 000. The account empties; the division is proved.

Each share lands in its partner's current account, and the year completes there. Zola: opening credit 4 000, plus 131 000, less drawings 110 000 — closing credit R25 000. Sipho: opening DEBIT 6 000 — last year he overdrew — plus 109 000, less drawings 95 000 — closing credit R8 000. Read them like a bank would: the business owes Zola 25 000 and Sipho 8 000; nobody is living beyond their earnings this year.

Stop for this section's questions now — the appropriation run in order, the empty-account check, and both current accounts closed.

## Subtopic: The Statements, the Notes and the Asset That Left

The partnership's statements wear the same architecture as grade 10's, with the ownership rewritten. The STATEMENT OF COMPREHENSIVE INCOME: sales R900 000, cost of sales R600 000 — gross profit R300 000, fifty percent on cost. Other income includes a profit on the disposal of an asset — R5 000, built in a moment — and operating expenses total R65 000; net profit R240 000, which flows to the appropriation, not to one capital account.

The disposal deserves its four steps, because an asset left during the year: a vehicle costing R80 000, accumulated depreciation R50 000, sold for R35 000. Step one: transfer the COST out — credit Vehicles, debit Asset Disposal, 80 000. Step two: transfer the accumulated depreciation — debit Accumulated Depreciation, credit Asset Disposal, 50 000; the disposal account now holds the carrying value, 30 000. Step three: record the proceeds — 35 000 against the disposal account. Step four: the difference is the profit — R5 000, income. Four steps, never varying, whatever the payment method.

The STATEMENT OF FINANCIAL POSITION: non-current assets at carrying value R335 000; current assets R168 000 — trading stock 98 000, debtors 60 000, bank 10 000. Total assets R503 000. Financed by: partners' equity — capitals 300 000 plus current accounts 33 000 — R333 000; a non-current loan of R100 000; current liabilities R70 000. Total R503 000. And the NOTES carry the workings: the fixed-asset note reconciling opening carrying value through additions, disposals and depreciation to closing; the capital and current account notes showing each partner's column — the transparency that lets each partner verify their own treatment.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the disposal's four steps, the statement totals, and what each note discloses.

## Subtopic: The Indicators — Reading the Partnership

Now judge the year. Profitability: gross profit on sales — 300 000 over 900 000 — thirty-three comma three percent; on cost, fifty percent, matching the mark-up policy. Net profit on sales: 240 000 over 900 000 — twenty-six comma seven percent.

The operating rhythm. STOCK TURNOVER: cost of sales 600 000 over average stock 100 000 — six times a year; the shelves empty and refill every two months. DEBTORS COLLECTION: debtors 60 000 over credit sales 360 000, times twelve — two months. Against thirty-day terms, that is slow: money resting in customers' pockets that could be working here.

Liquidity: current ratio 168 000 over 70 000 — two comma four to one; acid test — strip the stock — 70 000 over 70 000, one to one: immediate debts exactly covered without selling an item. Sound.

Then the gearing pair. DEBT-EQUITY: the loan of 100 000 against equity of 333 000 — nought comma three to one: lightly geared, low risk; the business could borrow more if expansion called. RETURN ON AVERAGE EQUITY: 240 000 over average equity of 320 000 — seventy-five percent. Before celebrating, read it honestly: partners' salaries sit INSIDE that return — it rewards their labour as well as their capital. Even so, against any bank rate the partnership is emphatically worth its owners' while. The comment technique never changes: figure, comparison, judgement — and for partnerships, always ask whose question the indicator answers: the creditor asks liquidity, the lender asks gearing, the partner asks return.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the rhythm indicators computed, the gearing pair read, and each indicator matched to the eye that watches it.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole topic again, from the pavement — because a partnership is just two friends and one till, and every rule exists to keep the friendship alive.

## Subtopic: Two Friends, One Till, and the Paper That Keeps the Peace

Two friends open a shop together. One puts in more money; the other works longer hours. Within a month, the dangerous sentences start: "I put in more, so more is mine." "I work harder, so more is mine." Every partnership fight in history is one of those two sentences — and the partnership agreement exists to answer both BEFORE the shouting: a salary for the one who works, interest for the money that waits, a ratio for whatever remains. Written, signed, obeyed.

The books then keep two piles per person, and the two piles are the peace. The BIG pile — capital — is what each friend planted in the business: Zola two hundred thousand, Sipho one hundred. It stays planted. The LIVING pile — the current account — moves every year: in comes each friend's share of the profit, out goes what each friend took home. If your living pile shows the business owing you, you have lived within your earnings; if it shows you owing the business, you have eaten more than your share grew. One line per friend, and nobody argues from memory — the paper remembers.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the two piles are coming to you right now: which pile moves, and which two sentences the agreement answers.

## Subtopic: Slicing the Profit Pot

The year ends and the pot holds R240 000. Watch it emptied fairly, ladle by ladle, in the agreement's order.

Ladle one — wages for work: Zola runs the shop full-time, R96 000; Sipho, part-time, R72 000. Ladle two — thanks to the money that waited: ten percent on each friend's planted pile — twenty thousand to Zola, ten to Sipho. Ladle three — the promised extra: Sipho grew the delivery side, and the agreement pays that bonus, R12 000. Now the pot holds thirty thousand, and the last ladle splits it as agreed — fifteen each.

Count each friend's plate: Zola one hundred and thirty-one thousand; Sipho one hundred and nine. Together, two hundred and forty — the pot is empty, to the rand. That emptiness is the whole discipline: appropriation never creates or loses a cent; it only divides. And catch the trap that fails learners every year: those salaries are not wages the business paid to staff — they are slices of profit. The business first works out what it MADE; only then does the family sit down to share. Expenses come before the profit line; sharing comes after. Two different rooms, and the exam always checks which room you are standing in.

Your questions for this part are up now: the ladles in order, both plates counted, and the two rooms kept apart.

## Subtopic: Reading the Scoreboard

Season over — is the team any good? The statements are the match record; the indicators are the scoreboard, and each number answers a different supporter.

The shopper's question: are they trading well? Bought for six hundred, sold for nine hundred — a third of every sale is gross profit, exactly the intended mark-up: nothing leaking. The stock moves six times a year — shelves alive, not a museum. But customers pay in two months against thirty-day terms — the friends are quietly lending their customers money, and tightening that alone would fatten the bank account.

The creditor's question: can they pay me soon? Two rand forty of near money for every rand of near debt; and even without selling a shirt, one rand for every rand owed. Sleep easy.

The bank's question: how deep are they in debt? One rand borrowed for every three the friends own — light gearing; room to borrow for growth if they choose.

And the friends' own question: was it worth it? The money they keep in the business returned seventy-five out of every hundred rand — mountains above any savings account — though remember the honest footnote: part of that return is simply their own labour coming home as profit. Read all of it the scoreboard way: never a number alone — always against last year, against the terms, against the bank — and the story tells itself.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: each supporter matched to their number, the slow-payers spotted, and the worth-it verdict argued with figures. Finish them, and the partnership year is revised — fairly divided, fully reported, and honestly judged.
