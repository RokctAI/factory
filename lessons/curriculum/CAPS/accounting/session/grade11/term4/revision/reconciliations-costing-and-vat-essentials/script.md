# Part 1 — Expert

The partnership session revised the year's centrepiece; this one revises the year's toolkit — the four skills that surround any set of books. Reconciliation: forcing two records of one truth to agree. Costing: knowing what a unit costs and when production starts paying. Inventory and forecasting: valuing what the shelf holds and writing the months ahead. And VAT: the tax a business collects without ever owning it. Each station carries a worked example, and every figure will meet its counterpart exactly.

## Subtopic: Two Records, One Truth — the Reconciliations

The bank keeps a record of your account; you keep your own. Same account, two bookkeepers, and month-end forces them to face each other. The method never changes: first UPDATE YOUR OWN BOOKS with everything the statement knows and you did not — bank charges R150, a debit order for insurance R450, interest earned R60. The bank account in the ledger stood at R10 940; less 150, less 450, plus 60 — the TRUE book balance, R10 400. Then the BANK RECONCILIATION STATEMENT explains what you know and the bank does not yet: statement balance R9 800, plus the outstanding deposit of R3 200 still on its way, less payments of R2 600 not yet through. That lands on R10 400 — the same figure from the other direction, and the agreement is the proof. Timing differences reconcile; errors get corrected in whoever's record is wrong.

The creditors reconciliation is the same discipline aimed at a supplier. Our creditors ledger says we owe R6 600; the supplier's statement says R8 750. Work both sides: the statement carries an invoice of R950 for goods we received but had not yet recorded — our balance corrects to R7 550. Our payment of R1 200 left too late to appear — the statement's balance adjusts to R7 550. Both records meet, and the debt is KNOWN, not assumed. What the habit buys: errors surface monthly instead of festering, disputes are settled with workings instead of tempers, and nobody pays a statement blindly.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: books updated, both statements built, and both meetings landing on the same figure.

## Subtopic: Costing — Contribution and the Break-Even Line

The manufacturer's ledger splits the trader's one stock account into three — raw materials, work-in-process, finished goods — and costs flow through them as the product takes shape: materials and labour directly into the product, overheads gathered and absorbed, finished cost arriving in finished goods. That structure was term three's work; revision needs its sharpest instrument — BREAK-EVEN.

Sort every cost by its behaviour. VARIABLE costs travel with each unit made: materials, piece-rate labour. FIXED costs stand still whatever the volume: rent, salaries, insurance. Now the numbers: a product sells at R25; its variable cost is R10. Each unit therefore CONTRIBUTES R15 — first toward the fixed costs, and after they are covered, straight to profit. Fixed costs run R7 500 a month. Break-even: 7 500 over 15 — five hundred units. At 499 the month makes a loss; at 500 it stands level; every unit beyond carries its full R15 home. Sell 620 and profit is 120 units times 15 — R1 800, confirmable the long way: sales 15 500, variable costs 6 200, contribution 9 300, less fixed 7 500 — R1 800.

The instrument answers management's real questions: what if rent rises — break-even climbs; what if the price drops to R22 — contribution falls to R12 and break-even jumps to 625; is a big order at a low price worth taking — only if it still contributes. Cost behaviour first, contribution second, decisions third.

Stop for this section's questions now — costs sorted by behaviour, break-even computed, and the profit at 620 units proved both ways.

## Subtopic: Stock Systems and the Forward Documents

Two ways to know cost of sales. The PERPETUAL system records it with every sale — the stock account always current, theft visible as the gap between the account and the count. The PERIODIC system waits: purchases pile into a Purchases account, and cost of sales emerges only by calculation — opening stock R12 000, plus purchases R40 000, plus carriage on purchases R3 000, less closing stock counted at R15 000 — cost of sales R40 000. Cheaper to run, blinder between counts: the calculation cannot tell sold from stolen. Choose by the trade: high-value items suit perpetual's constant eye; high-volume small goods often accept periodic's economy.

Then the forward documents, which plan rather than record. The PROJECTED INCOME STATEMENT forecasts profit on the accrual calendar; the CASH BUDGET forecasts survival on the cash calendar — and the pair can disagree profoundly, because profit is not cash. The bridge between them is the DEBTORS COLLECTION SCHEDULE, which converts planned credit sales into planned receipts. The pattern: forty percent of a month's credit sales pay in the month of sale, fifty-five percent the following month, five percent never — written off. September's collections, with August credit sales of R28 000 and September's R30 000: forty percent of 30 000 — R12 000; fifty-five percent of 28 000 — R15 400. Total collected: R27 400. Note what the five percent teaches: budgeted receipts are always less than budgeted sales, and a business that plans on the full hundred percent plans on money that will never arrive.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — cost of sales computed backwards, the two systems chosen for the right shops, and the September collections built.

## Subtopic: VAT — the Collector's Arithmetic

Value Added Tax, revised to its skeleton. The vendor CHARGES output tax on sales, RECLAIMS input tax on purchases, and pays over the difference — the tax itself belongs to the final consumer and travels through the business untouched. Three categories of supply: STANDARD-RATED at fifteen percent; ZERO-RATED — taxable at nought, the staples and exports — where the vendor still reclaims input tax; EXEMPT — outside the system, no output charged, no input reclaimed. One question separates the confusable two: may the seller reclaim its own VAT? Zero-rated, yes; exempt, no.

The arithmetic, both directions. Going up from an exclusive price: multiply by 1,15. Coming down from an inclusive figure — the examiners' favourite — the VAT inside is fifteen over one hundred and fifteen: an invoice totalling R4 600 inclusive carries VAT of 4 600 times 15 over 115 — R600 — on an exclusive R4 000. Never fifteen percent of the inclusive figure. The return: output tax R9 000 for the period, input tax R6 300 — payable to SARS, R2 700. Inputs above outputs would mean a refund.

And the ethics that carry the marks: the R2 700 is TRUST MONEY — collected from customers on the state's behalf, never the vendor's to spend. The named sins: spending VAT to ease cash flow, claiming inputs on private purchases, fictitious invoices, charging VAT while unregistered. The named controls: valid tax invoices behind every claim, the VAT control account reconciled to the return monthly, returns submitted and paid on time. The auditor's one question: does every input claim rest on a real invoice for a real business purpose?

The final questions of this part are with you now: the categories separated by the reclaim question, both calculations run, and the return computed with its ethics stated.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the toolkit from the street — a spaza counter, a vetkoek pan, and a till slip — because all four skills are things careful households already do.

## Subtopic: When Two Notebooks Disagree

You keep a notebook of your bank money; the bank keeps its own. Month-end, the two never agree at first glance — and that disagreement is NORMAL. The bank knows things you missed: it quietly took its fee of a hundred and fifty, the insurance debit order of four-fifty went, a little interest arrived. Write those in first — your notebook now tells the truth: ten thousand four hundred. Then flip it: you know things the bank has not seen yet — the money you deposited that is still travelling, the payments you made that have not landed. List those against the bank's number and it arrives at the same ten thousand four hundred. Two notebooks, corrected toward each other, meeting in the middle — nothing mystical, just both sides admitting what they did not know.

Same trick with the wholesaler who sends a statement saying you owe eight seven five-oh when your book says six six hundred. Do not fight; reconcile. Their statement knows about the goods that arrived before you wrote them up — nine-fifty, add it to your book. Your book knows about the payment still travelling to them — twelve hundred, subtract it from their statement. Both land on seven five five-oh, and now you pay what is TRUE, not what is shouted. The habit in one line: never argue balances; correct both records toward each other and pay the meeting point.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the meeting notebooks are coming to you right now: who updates what, and where the two sides land.

## Subtopic: The Vetkoek Line That Pays the Rent

A vetkoek stand. Each vetkoek sells for twenty-five rand; the flour, oil and filling inside it cost ten. So every vetkoek sold walks fifteen rand toward the stand's fixed life — the rent, the gas bottle contract, the fixed things that cost seven and a half thousand a month whether one vetkoek fries or a thousand.

Ask the only question that matters at month-start: how many vetkoek until the rent is paid? Seven and a half thousand divided by fifteen — five hundred. Vetkoek number one to four ninety-nine are working for the landlord. Number five hundred squares the month. And number five-oh-one is the first one working for YOU — from there, every single vetkoek drops its whole fifteen rand in your pocket. Sell six hundred and twenty this month? A hundred and twenty vetkoek past the line, times fifteen — eighteen hundred rand of profit, and you can feel exactly where it came from.

Now every business decision becomes visible. Landlord raises the rent — the line moves further away; count the new distance before agreeing to stay. Cut the price to twenty-two — each vetkoek now carries only twelve, and the line jumps to six hundred and twenty-five; cheaper is not automatically smarter. A school wants three hundred at a discount — fine, IF each discounted vetkoek still carries something toward the line. The break-even line is not exam decoration; it is the difference between working for the landlord and working for yourself.

Your questions for this part are up now: find the line, move the line, and count the vetkoek that work for you.

## Subtopic: The Slip, the Shelf and the Month Ahead

Three last street skills, quickly and cleanly.

The slip: that VAT line at the till is money passing THROUGH the shop, never resting there. The shopkeeper collected it for SARS; spending it on stock or wages is spending someone else's money, and the penalties compound like a debt to a loan shark. The one calculation to own: the slip says four thousand six hundred INCLUDING VAT; the tax inside is fifteen parts of one hundred and fifteen — six hundred rand — not fifteen percent of the total. Going up, fifteen on a hundred; coming down, fifteen on one-fifteen.

The shelf: how does the shop know what selling cost it? Either the notebook way — write the cost out with every sale, always current, theft visible the moment the count disagrees with the book — or the counting way: what did the shelf start with, what came in, what remains? Twelve in, forty bought, three of transport, fifteen still standing — forty thousand went out. The counting way is cheaper and blinder; it cannot tell sold from stolen. Pick the eye your stock deserves.

And the month ahead: the household that plans knows the difference between EARNING and RECEIVING. Customers on account pay some now, some next month, and some never — plan on forty percent now and fifty-five later, and let the five percent go before it lies to you. Write the month's money before living it, and the shop meets its problems while they are still small.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: the VAT extracted from a slip, the shelf counted backwards, and next month's collections written down honestly. Finish them, and the grade 11 toolkit — reconciling, costing, counting, collecting, planning — is revised, balanced, and ready for the examination.
