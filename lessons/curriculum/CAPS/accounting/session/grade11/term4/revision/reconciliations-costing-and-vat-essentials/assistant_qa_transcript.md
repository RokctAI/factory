**Bianca:** Revision questions! First: in the bank reconciliation, why do some items go INTO our books while others only go on the reconciliation statement? Learners mix those two destinations up constantly.

**Tutor:** One question sorts every item: has this ALREADY HAPPENED, with only our books ignorant of it — or has it not yet reached the bank at all? Bank charges, debit orders, interest: the bank has already taken or added that money; the event is complete, our record is simply behind, so our books must be updated — cash journals, then the bank account. Outstanding deposits and payments not yet through: we did our part, but the banking system has not finished its side — nothing about our books is wrong, the STATEMENT is behind, and statements belonging to the bank cannot be corrected by us. So those wait on the reconciliation statement as timing differences that next month's statement will absorb. The destinations follow from ownership of the lag: their record behind, adjust ours is impossible — we list and wait; our record behind — we update ours. Errors obey the same logic: our error, correct our books; the bank's error, list it on the statement and instruct the bank. Ask "whose record is behind?" for every item and the two destinations never blur again.

**Bianca:** Next: break-even treats fixed costs as one solid block — but real rent rises, and salaries change. Doesn't that make the whole calculation a fantasy?

**Tutor:** It makes the calculation a SNAPSHOT, and snapshots are exactly what decisions need. Fixed costs are fixed only within a range and a period — economists call it the relevant range: for this month, at this scale, in this building, the rent and salaries genuinely do not move with output, so the break-even of five hundred units is true for the month being decided. When the landlord raises the rent, you do not abandon the tool — you rerun it, and the rerun IS the tool's value: new fixed costs over unchanged contribution gives the new line, and the owner sees the rent increase translated into vetkoek before signing anything. That translation is the discipline: every change arrives as a question the model answers in units. And notice the deeper honesty — a business that outgrows its range, needing a second stall or a second fryer, discovers that some "fixed" costs step upward in blocks; the model handles that too, one range at a time. The exam wants both hands: calculate the line from the given figures, and comment knowing the figures hold only within their range and month. A snapshot is not a fantasy; pretending the photo covers the whole year would be.

**Bianca:** This one from the shop kids: the periodic system can't see theft — so why would any sane shop choose the blind system on purpose?

**Tutor:** Because sight has a price, and for some shelves the price of watching exceeds the losses being watched for. The perpetual system demands a cost entry with every single sale — for a spaza moving hundreds of small items daily, that is either constant clerical work or a point-of-sale system with scanners and coded stock; real money, every month, forever. The periodic system replaces all of it with one disciplined stocktake and a four-line calculation. So the choice is a comparison of two costs: the cost of the eye against the value of what the eye protects. A jeweller with ten items worth thousands each buys the eye without hesitating — one missing ring outweighs a year of admin. A vendor selling sweets and airtime accepts periodic blindness, then narrows it cheaply: count MORE OFTEN — a month of blindness is smaller than a year's — watch the gross profit percentage, because a mark-up that should yield fifty percent yielding forty-one announces leakage arithmetically, and lock away the few high-value lines even inside a periodic shop. Blindness between counts is not helplessness; it is a known gap, managed by shortening it. The exam answer names the trade: control against cost, chosen per shelf.

**Bianca:** Last one, and it's the uncomfortable classic: the shop is short of cash this month, and the VAT money is just sitting there until the return is due. Borrowing it quietly for stock — how bad, really?

**Tutor:** Call it what the arithmetic calls it: spending money that belongs to someone else, with a repayment date that does not move. The R2 700 collected above inputs was never the shop's income — it passed through the till as the state's money, held in trust until the return; the shop's true position is whatever the books show WITHOUT it. Spending it feels painless precisely because no invoice arrives — but the return date arrives instead, and now the shop must find the original amount PLUS the stock money already spent, in a month no richer than the last. Miss the payment and penalties and interest attach — compounding, not negotiable, and SARS collects with powers no supplier has. That is the mechanical answer; the structural one is worse: a business that needs the VAT money to buy stock is signalling that its real cash flow cannot support its operations, and the borrowing hides that signal from the owner until the hole is too deep to climb from. The discipline that prevents all of it costs nothing: a separate record — or account — for VAT collected, reconciled monthly against the control account, treated as already gone. And the ethical line for the accountant is the same one as always: trust money is not working capital, and being asked to treat it as working capital is the moment to say no, in writing.
