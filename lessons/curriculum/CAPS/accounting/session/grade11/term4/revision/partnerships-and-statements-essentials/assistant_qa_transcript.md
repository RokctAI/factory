**Bianca:** Revision questions from the class! First: why does a partner get BOTH a salary and a share of profit? At my cousin's work you get a salary or you own the place — not both.

**Tutor:** Because a partner plays two different roles at once, and fairness requires paying each role separately. Role one: worker. Zola stands in the shop every day doing work the partnership would otherwise hire a manager for — the salary appropriation prices that labour, and it protects the harder-working partner from subsidising the other. Role two: investor. Zola also locked R200 000 into the business — money that could have earned interest elsewhere — and the interest on capital pays for that waiting, in proportion to how much each partner planted. Only after both roles are paid does the remainder reward the third thing: shared ownership of the risk. Collapse the three into one split and unfairness is guaranteed — equal shares would rob the harder worker; capital-ratio shares would rob the smaller investor of wages. And note the legal texture your cousin's example misses: a partner cannot be an employee of their own partnership — the "salary" is not a wage expense with UIF and PAYE machinery inside the business's costs; it is a first, priority slice of profit, agreed in the deed. That is exactly why it lives in the appropriation account, below the net profit line.

***

**Bianca:** Next: if the partnership makes a LOSS, or a profit too small for the salaries and interest — what happens then? The pot can't pay out more than it holds, surely?

**Tutor:** The appropriation machinery runs exactly the same — it just runs into negative territory, and the current accounts absorb the truth. The agreement's promises of salaries and interest do not vanish when profit is thin: they are appropriated in full, and the remainder becomes a NEGATIVE remainder — a shortfall shared in the profit-sharing ratio, debited to the partners' current accounts. Suppose the salaries and interest total 198 000 but the year's profit is only 150 000: the appropriation account is short by 48 000, so each partner's account is charged 24 000 on an equal ratio — and a partner's current account can swing to a debit balance because their share of the shortfall and their drawings exceeded their salary and interest. That debit balance is the books saying: you have taken out more than the business made for you. Nothing about it breaks the system — it disciplines it: next year's drawings should shrink, or the agreement should be renegotiated. The exam version usually hides one twist: read whether the ratio for sharing remainders applies to losses too — most agreements say it does, and the mark scheme expects the shortfall shared in that same ratio.

***

**Bianca:** This one trips everyone: the vehicle sold for thirty-five thousand and we recorded a PROFIT of five thousand. But the vehicle cost eighty! How is selling an eighty-thousand-rand vehicle for thirty-five a profit?

**Tutor:** Because the profit is measured against what the books still claimed, not against what the vehicle once cost — and that distinction is the entire logic of depreciation coming home. Over its years of service, R50 000 of the vehicle's cost was already charged against profits as depreciation, year by year, as the business consumed its working life. So by the day of the sale the books carried it at R30 000 — cost 80 000 less accumulated depreciation 50 000 — and that carrying value is the only honest yardstick left: it is the portion of the cost never yet charged anywhere. Selling for 35 000 recovers all of that remaining 30 000 plus 5 000 more, which means the earlier years depreciated slightly too fast — the business charged a little more wear than the market says actually happened. The profit on disposal quietly corrects that over-charge. Had the vehicle fetched 25 000, the 5 000 loss would confess the opposite: depreciation ran too slow. Either way, the four-step disposal account is just the reconciliation between the books' opinion and the market's verdict — and the exam mark is for comparing proceeds to CARRYING value, never to cost.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: seventy-five percent return on equity sounds impossible — the bank pays single digits. Should the partners believe their own number?

**Tutor:** They should believe it and then read it with both eyes open, because the number is true and still flattering. It is true arithmetically: 240 000 of profit on an average 320 000 of equity is seventy-five percent — the partnership genuinely multiplied its owners' money at a rate no savings account approaches. It flatters because the comparison to a bank rate is not like-for-like on three counts. First, labour: the 168 000 of partners' salaries sits inside that profit — a bank deposit pays interest while you sleep, but much of this return was earned standing behind the counter; strip the salaries out and the return on pure capital is closer to 22,5 percent — still excellent, honestly framed. Second, risk: the bank's rate is near-guaranteed; this return rode on stock that might not sell and debtors who pay in two months — a higher return is partly the PRICE of that risk, not a free gift. Third, liquidity: bank money is reachable tomorrow; equity is locked in shelves and vehicles. So the exam-grade comment runs: the return of 75% far exceeds alternative investments, even allowing for the partners' labour inside it, and adequately compensates the risk — the partnership is emphatically worth continuing. Figure, comparison, judgement — with the flattery named out loud.
