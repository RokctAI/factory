**Bianca:** Revision questions! First: in the bank reconciliation, why do some items get written INTO our books while others only appear on the reconciliation statement? The class mixes those destinations up every single time.

**Tutor:** One question sorts every item instantly: has this ALREADY HAPPENED, with only our books lagging behind — or has it not yet reached the bank at all? Bank charges, debit orders, interest: the bank has already moved that money; the event is finished, our record is merely late, so our books must be updated — cash journals first, then the bank account. Outstanding deposits and payments not yet presented: we did our part, but the banking system has not completed its side — nothing in our books is wrong, the STATEMENT is the one lagging, and we cannot write corrections into a document the bank owns. Those items wait on the reconciliation statement as timing differences that next month's statement will swallow. The destinations follow from who owns the lag: their record behind — we list and wait; our record behind — we update ours. Errors obey the same law: our mistake, correct our books; the bank's mistake, list it on the statement and notify the bank. Ask "whose record is behind?" for every single item and the two destinations never blur again.

***

**Bianca:** Next: break-even treats fixed costs like a block of concrete — but real pitch fees rise and equipment instalments end. Doesn't that make the whole calculation make-believe?

**Tutor:** It makes the calculation a SNAPSHOT, and a snapshot is precisely what a decision needs. Fixed costs are fixed only inside a range and a period — the relevant range: for this month, at this scale, at this taxi rank, the pitch fee and the instalment genuinely do not move with output, so the break-even of eight hundred units is true for the month being decided. When the fee rises, you do not throw the tool away — you rerun it, and the rerun IS the tool's worth: new fixed costs over an unchanged contribution gives the new line, and the owner sees the increase translated into koeksisters before agreeing to anything. That translation is the discipline: every change arrives as a question the model answers in units. And notice the deeper honesty — a stand that outgrows its range, needing a second fryer or a second table, finds that some "fixed" costs climb in steps; the model copes with that too, one range at a time. A strong answer shows both hands: calculate the line from the given figures, then comment knowing the figures hold only within their range and month. A snapshot is not make-believe; pretending one photo covers the whole year would be.

**Bianca:** This one from the shop kids: if the periodic system cannot see theft, why would any sensible shop pick the blind system on purpose?

**Tutor:** Because sight has a price, and for some shelves the price of the watching exceeds the losses being watched for. Perpetual demands a cost entry with every sale — for a spaza moving hundreds of small items a day, that means either endless clerical work or a scanner till with coded stock: real money, every month, forever. Periodic replaces the whole apparatus with one disciplined stocktake and a four-line calculation. So the choice is a contest between two costs: the cost of the eye against the value of what the eye guards. A jeweller with a dozen items worth thousands each buys the eye without blinking — one missing chain outweighs a year of admin. A trader selling sweets and airtime accepts periodic blindness, then shrinks it cheaply: count MORE OFTEN — a month of blindness is smaller than a year's — watch the gross profit percentage, because a mark-up meant to yield fifty percent that yields forty announces leakage in pure arithmetic, and lock the few high-value lines away even inside a periodic shop. Blindness between counts is not helplessness; it is a known gap, managed by shortening it. The polished answer names the trade: control against cost, decided shelf by shelf.

**Bianca:** Last one, the uncomfortable classic: the shop is squeezed this month, and the VAT money is just sitting there until the return is due. Quietly borrowing it for stock — how bad, honestly?

**Tutor:** Call it what the arithmetic calls it: spending someone else's money against a repayment date that will not move. The R3 500 collected above inputs was never the shop's income — it passed through the till as the state's money, held in trust until the return; the shop's true position is whatever the books show WITHOUT it. Spending it feels painless precisely because no invoice follows — but the return date follows instead, and now the shop must produce the original amount PLUS replace the stock money already spent, in a month no fatter than the last. Miss the payment and penalties and interest attach — compounding, non-negotiable, and collected by SARS with powers no supplier possesses. That is the mechanical answer; the structural one is uglier: a business that needs the VAT money to buy stock is broadcasting that its real cash flow cannot carry its operations, and the borrowing muffles that broadcast until the hole is too deep to escape. The prevention costs nothing: a separate record — better, a separate account — for VAT collected, reconciled monthly against the control account, treated as money already gone. And the accountant's line is the same as ever: trust money is not working capital, and being asked to treat it as working capital is the moment to refuse, in writing.
