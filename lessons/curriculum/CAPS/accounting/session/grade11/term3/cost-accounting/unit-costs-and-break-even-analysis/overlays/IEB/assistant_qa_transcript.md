**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: why does break-even divide by CONTRIBUTION per unit and not PROFIT per unit? Profit feels like the obvious thing to measure against fixed costs.

**Tutor:** Because profit per unit does not exist until you already know the answer you are hunting for. Follow the circle. To find profit per unit you must first spread the fixed costs over the units — R560 000 across some number of bags. But WHICH number? At 8 000 bags the fixed load is R70 each and so-called profit per unit is negative; at 20 000 it is R28 each and profit per unit is R28. Profit per unit changes with volume — it is an output-dependent number, so using it to FIND an output is chasing your own tail. Contribution breaks the circle because it is volume-independent: every single bag, from the first to the thirty-thousandth, sells for R120 and carries away R64 of variable cost, leaving R56 — the same R56 no matter how many bags exist. That constancy is what legitimises the division: a fixed wall of R560 000, chipped at by a steady R56 per unit, is a well-posed question with exactly one answer — 10 000. The habit underneath: whenever volume is the unknown, work only with quantities that do not move with volume — fixed totals and per-unit contributions — and keep anything that blends the two, like full unit cost or profit per unit, out of the formula entirely.

***

**Bianca:** Next: my friend got a break-even of 9 333 bags by dividing the fixed costs by R60. Where do wrong contribution figures usually come from?

**Tutor:** From three habits, and naming each one lets you patrol for them. First and most common: leaving out a variable cost that does not feel like production — the selling and distribution R8 of packaging and commission per bag. Break-even is a WHOLE-business calculation: a bag is not truly sold until it is packed, delivered and commissioned, so every cost that varies with a unit sold belongs in the R64, factory or not. Drop the R8 and the contribution looks like R64, giving a flattering, false line at 8 750. Second: the mirror error — dragging a fixed cost INTO the per-unit figure, usually rent or depreciation divided by planned output and added to variable cost; that smuggles back the circular reasoning we just buried, because the fixed wall must stay on top of the fraction as a total and never leak into the per-unit bottom. Third: dividing by the selling price instead of the contribution — R560 000 over R120 gives 4 667, a number that pretends the bags cost nothing to make. The patrol routine takes twenty seconds: list every cost in the question; stamp each one FIXED-TOTAL or VARIABLE-PER-UNIT; check that the stamps cover everything exactly once; only then divide. Every wreck in this topic is a cost with the wrong stamp, two stamps, or none.

**Bianca:** A learner asked: if the factory plans 20 000 bags and break-even is 10 000, why should anyone still care about the number? The business is miles clear of it.

**Tutor:** Because the distance itself is the information — and the distance moves. The 10 000-bag gap is the margin of safety, half the plan, and it is the business's shock absorber: demand can halve — a competitor arriving, a weak back-to-school season, a supplier failure stopping production for months — before a single rand of loss appears. A manager who knows the cushion is fifty percent decides differently from one whose cushion is eight percent: bolder pricing experiments, more confidence signing fixed commitments, less panic in a slow month. And every proposal that lands on the desk drags the line, so the number is consulted constantly, never filed. The outfitting contract at R112 lifts break-even to 11 667 — the cushion shrinks toward forty percent. The new lease lifts it to 11 000 exactly. Canvas at R4 more per bag, to 10 770. Each decision is priced in cushion before it is signed. There is a darker use too: when a downturn does arrive, break-even tells management precisely how far output can fall before the factory burns cash — which drives the decisions about shifts, short time and stock levels. A business far from its break-even is not a business that can ignore it; it is a business enjoying the distance, and the wise ones glance at the gauge every time the road bends.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: the model assumes the price and the unit costs never change with volume. Real life isn't like that — so why learn a tool built on assumptions that are false?

**Tutor:** Because every useful model rests on assumptions that are false somewhere, and the skill being taught is using the tool INSIDE the range where they hold. Near the planned output — say between 8 000 and 25 000 bags for Mokoena — the assumptions are approximately true: the price list is stable, canvas is bought at the same rate per bag, the factory fits inside its current workshop, and what is sewn is sold within the season. Inside that band the model's answers are reliable enough to price real decisions, and vastly cheaper than the alternative — discovering break-even empirically by losing money. Far outside the band, each assumption snaps in a predictable way, and a top answer names the snapping points: push volume high enough and fixed costs STEP — a second shift, more machines, a bigger workshop; chase that volume in the market and the price may have to fall, cutting contribution; buy triple the canvas and the supplier offers bulk discounts, so variable cost per unit bends; and if production outruns sales, unsold stock breaks the made-equals-sold assumption. So the mature statement for an exam: break-even analysis gives quick, decision-ready answers within the relevant range of output, and its limits — stepped fixed costs, price and cost changes at extreme volumes, and unsold production — must be acknowledged whenever a decision pushes outside that range. A tool with known limits is not a weak tool; it is an honest one.
