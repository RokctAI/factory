# Part 1 — Expert

Every business so far in this course BOUGHT the goods it sold. Today's business MAKES them, and that single difference rebuilds the ledger. Meet Kagiso Footwear Manufacturers of Pietermaritzburg, maker of school shoes: leather comes in one door, finished pairs leave by another, and the accounting must trace every rand of the journey in between. Today: the cost classifications the syllabus demands — direct and indirect, variable and fixed; the three stock accounts a factory keeps where a trader kept one; the cost accounts — direct materials, direct labour and factory overheads — and the flow of costs through work-in-progress to finished goods; and the ethics and internal controls of a manufacturing floor. The financial year ends on 28 February.

## Subtopic: The Language of Cost

Before a factory can be recorded, its costs must be sorted twice — by TRACEABILITY and by BEHAVIOUR.

Traceability first. A DIRECT cost can be traced to the product itself. The leather and soles in a school shoe: direct materials. The wages of the machinists who cut and stitch: direct labour. Together, direct materials plus direct labour make PRIME COST — the costs that touch the shoe. An INDIRECT cost keeps the factory running but cannot be pinned to any one pair: the factory foreman's salary, factory rent, electricity for the machines, oil and needles, depreciation on the stitching machines. These are FACTORY OVERHEADS. Add factory overheads to prime cost and you have the TOTAL COST OF PRODUCTION.

Two more cost families never enter production cost at all. ADMINISTRATION costs — the office, the bookkeeper's salary, office telephone — and SELLING AND DISTRIBUTION costs — advertising, the delivery bakkie's diesel, sales commission. They are period costs: expenses of the income statement, not part of what a shoe costs to make. The classification test for any cost: does it happen in the FACTORY, for MAKING? Production cost. Office? Administration. Getting sold goods to customers? Selling and distribution.

Behaviour second. A VARIABLE cost moves in step with output: make more shoes, buy more leather — direct materials, direct labour, and selling costs like commission are typically variable. A FIXED cost ignores output within a range: the factory rent is the same whether the month makes five thousand pairs or nine thousand — rent, the foreman's salary, depreciation, insurance are typically fixed. Hold both sorts in your head at once: factory overheads are mostly FIXED but still PRODUCTION costs; sales commission is VARIABLE but never a production cost. The two classifications cut across each other, and the exam tests exactly that crossing. Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: costs sorted both ways.

## Subtopic: Three Stock Accounts Where the Trader Kept One

A trader's trading stock account held goods ready for sale, full stop. A factory's stock exists in three different states of becoming, so the ledger keeps three stock accounts — four, counting the consumables.

RAW MATERIALS STOCK: leather, soles, thread, buckles — bought but not yet touched. Kagiso Footwear opened the year with R84 000 of raw materials, purchased R516 000 more during the year, and paid R14 000 carriage on those purchases — carriage on raw materials joins the cost of the materials, exactly as carriage on purchases did for a trader. Closing stock, counted: R76 000.

WORK-IN-PROGRESS STOCK: shoes half-made — cut uppers, unsoled lasts — value trapped mid-process. Opening balance R46 000; closing R66 000. Work-in-progress is the account where ALL production costs will meet.

FINISHED GOODS STOCK: completed pairs awaiting sale — the closest cousin to the trader's trading stock. Opening R90 000; closing R110 000.

And CONSUMABLE STORES: factory supplies consumed in production but not part of the product — machine oil, needles, cleaning materials. Consumables used during the year flow into factory overheads as indirect materials.

Each stock account obeys the same grammar you have used since grade 10: opening balance plus additions, less what moved on, equals closing balance. Say it for raw materials, because it produces the year's first key figure: opening R84 000, plus purchases R516 000, plus carriage R14 000, gives R614 000 available; less closing stock R76 000 — so raw materials ISSUED to production cost R538 000. That R538 000 is the direct materials cost, and it now begins its journey. Stop for this section's questions now: the three states of stock, and the issue figure computed from the account.

## Subtopic: The Cost Accounts and the Flow to Finished Goods

Now the machinery of the topic: three COST accounts gather the year's production costs, then empty themselves into work-in-progress. Speak the flow.

DIRECT MATERIALS COST account: debited with the R538 000 issued from raw materials stock. DIRECT LABOUR COST account: debited with the machinists' wages of R376 000 plus the employer's contributions of R26 000 that ride on those wages — R402 000. Those two accounts close off to work-in-progress, carrying prime cost: R538 000 plus R402 000 — R940 000.

FACTORY OVERHEAD COST account gathers the indirect production costs, each named: indirect materials — the consumables used — R38 000; the foreman's salary R132 000; factory rent R96 000; factory electricity and water R54 000; depreciation on factory machinery R60 000; factory insurance R20 000. Total factory overheads: R400 000. This account too closes to work-in-progress.

The WORK-IN-PROGRESS account now tells the year's whole production story. Opening balance R46 000. Add prime cost R940 000 and overheads R400 000 — total manufacturing costs of R1 340 000 for the year. Available in process: R1 386 000. Less the closing half-made stock of R66 000: the COST OF PRODUCTION OF FINISHED GOODS — R1 320 000 — transferred to finished goods stock.

Kagiso completed 24 000 pairs this year, so the UNIT COST of production is R1 320 000 over 24 000: R55 per pair — the figure every pricing and break-even decision will stand on.

Finished goods completes the chain in trading-stock grammar: opening R90 000, plus production R1 320 000, gives R1 410 000 available; less closing R110 000 — COST OF SALES R1 300 000. Sales for the year were R1 950 000, and R1 950 000 less R1 300 000 leaves a gross profit of R650 000 — exactly 50 percent on cost of sales, confirming the mark-up policy. One ledger, one unbroken chain: materials to prime cost, prime cost plus overheads to work-in-progress, work-in-progress to finished goods, finished goods to cost of sales. Pause for this section's questions: the flow traced end to end, and any missing figure recovered from the chain.

## Subtopic: Ethics and Control on the Factory Floor

A factory concentrates temptation: valuable materials in bulk, hours that can be inflated, scrap that can vanish. The syllabus asks for the controls in words, and they follow the flow you just learned.

Over MATERIALS: order from approved suppliers at quoted prices; count and inspect deliveries against the delivery note and order; store under lock with a storeman responsible; issue to production only against signed requisitions; and compare the materials the job SHOULD have used with what the storeroom issued — a gap is waste, damage or theft, and each has a different remedy. Watch scrap and offcuts too: leather offcuts have value, and unbooked scrap is the classic leak.

Over LABOUR: clock cards or biometric registers so that paid hours are worked hours; a foreman approving overtime BEFORE it happens; wages calculated by someone who does not also pay them out; and idle time recorded with reasons — machines waiting for materials point at planning, not workers.

Over OVERHEADS: budgets per overhead line with monthly comparison; meters read, not estimated; maintenance scheduled so breakdowns do not become emergencies bought at any price.

The ETHICS run deeper than theft. Quality: substituting cheaper glue on a school shoe that a child wears daily is a decision about safety, not only cost. Fair labour: paying legal wages, safe machines, guards on cutters. Honest costing: a factory that dumps waste into the river has not lowered its costs — it has shifted them onto the community, and a costing that ignores that is a lie told in figures. And environmental duty is enforceable: fines and closures turn moral costs into financial ones quickly. The final questions of this part are with you now: a control for each risk, and the ethical line defended in words.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same factory from a kitchen — because everyone who has ever cooked for a school fete already understands manufacturing.

## Subtopic: The Vetkoek Stand

You are frying vetkoek for the school fete, five hundred of them. What does one vetkoek COST? Start sorting, and you are doing cost accounting.

Flour, oil, mince for the filling — you can point at them inside the vetkoek. Those are DIRECT MATERIALS. Your cousin who fries all day for a fee — her pay is DIRECT LABOUR: hands on the product. Add those two and you have PRIME COST, the costs that literally touch the vetkoek.

But the stand also burned gas, and you hired the gas bottle and the gazebo, and someone lent you a fryer that is slowly wearing out. None of that sits INSIDE any one vetkoek, yet without it there is no frying at all. That basket is FACTORY OVERHEADS — the costs of keeping the making possible. Prime cost plus overheads: the TOTAL COST OF MAKING.

Two costs stay out of the vetkoek entirely. The flyers you printed to advertise — that is selling cost. The notebook and airtime for taking orders — that is admin. They are costs of the DAY, not costs of the VETKOEK, and keeping them out of the making cost is what keeps the unit cost honest.

Then the second sorting: which costs grow when you fry more? Flour, oil, mince — double the vetkoek, double the flour: VARIABLE. The gazebo hire is the same whether you fry fifty or five hundred: FIXED. Feel the two sorts pulling differently: the fixed gazebo gets cheaper PER vetkoek the more you fry, and that one sentence is the seed of the whole next lesson. Quick check before we carry on — questions on sorting the stand's costs are coming to you right now.

## Subtopic: Dough, Half-Fried, and Ready to Sell

A trader has one box of stock: things ready to sell. Your stand has three, and so does every factory. Look at your tables.

Table one: the SACKS — flour, oil, mince, bought but untouched. Raw materials. Table two: the HALF-DONE — dough resting, vetkoek in the oil, filled but not wrapped. Work-in-progress: money trapped mid-becoming. Table three: the WARMER — wrapped vetkoek ready for the customer. Finished goods, the only table a customer may buy from.

Each table keeps the same simple arithmetic you have used since grade 10: what was there, plus what arrived, minus what is still there, equals what moved on. The factory's leather works exactly like your flour: Kagiso Footwear started with R84 000 of leather and soles, bought R516 000 more, paid R14 000 to truck it in, and ended with R76 000 still in the storeroom. So what got USED in the making: R538 000. Not the purchases — the USED. The storeroom keeps back its closing stock, and only the issued materials become part of the shoes.

The oil, the needles, the machine grease — used up in the making but never inside the product — get their own small box called consumable stores, and from there they slip into overheads as indirect materials. Everything has a table, every table has the grammar, and the exam's favourite trick — giving you three of the four figures and hiding the fourth — is beaten by the grammar alone. Your questions for this part are up now: place every item on its table, and find the missing figure.

## Subtopic: One Pair of Shoes, Followed All the Way

Now walk one year of the shoe factory end to end, the way a rand walks it.

Materials issued to the floor: R538 000. Machinists' wages riding with them: R402 000. Together — prime cost, R940 000: everything that touched the shoes. Then the factory's basket tips in: foreman, rent, electricity, machine depreciation, insurance, oil and needles — R400 000 of overheads. Total poured into the making this year: R1 340 000.

But the floor was not empty on day one — R46 000 of half-made shoes waited from last year — and it is not empty at year end either: R66 000 of half-made pairs still sit on the lasts. So what came OUT as finished shoes: R46 000 plus R1 340 000 minus R66 000 — R1 320 000 of completed pairs, marched into the finished goods store.

Count the pairs: 24 000 finished this year. So one pair cost R1 320 000 over 24 000 — R55 a pair. That number is the factory's heartbeat. Price the pair at R80, and you know before selling a single shoe that R25 per pair is there to cover the office, the deliveries and the profit. Watch R55 drift up over the months, and you know something on the floor is leaking — more leather per pair, slower stitching, a fatter electricity bill — before any statement is drawn.

Last leg: the store. Shoes worth R90 000 waited at the start, R1 320 000 marched in, R110 000 remain at year end — so shoes costing R1 300 000 went out to customers. Sales of R1 950 000 against R1 300 000: gross profit R650 000, and the chain closes clean. One rand, one path: sacks, floor, store, customer. Learn the path and no manufacturing question can hide from you. And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: walk the path, name each hand-off, and find the unit cost yourself.
