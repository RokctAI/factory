**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the supervisor stands on the factory floor from morning to evening. Why is her salary an OVERHEAD when the machinists' wages count as direct labour? They are all making backpacks.

**Tutor:** The test is never where a person stands — it is whether their effort can be traced into a UNIT of the product. A machinist cuts and sews actual bags: her hours attach to backpacks, and if the floor produced nothing this month, her production work would not exist. The supervisor watches the WHOLE floor: she schedules runs, checks quality, unblocks problems across every bag at once, and nobody can honestly say which minutes of her day belong to which backpack. Her salary is a genuine production cost — it belongs in the factory, never in administration — but it enters through the overheads gate, where all the untraceable factory costs gather: rent, electricity, machine depreciation, insurance, oil and needles. Then notice the second axis: behaviour. Machinists' wages tend to be VARIABLE — more bags, more sewing hours — while the supervisor earns the same salary at four thousand bags or eight thousand, so her cost is FIXED. The two classifications are independent, and questions live at the crossings: an indirect cost that is fixed, like her salary; a direct cost that is variable, like canvas; and the trap pairing — sales commission, variable but never production, because it lives outside the factory altogether.

***

**Bianca:** Next: why is the closing stock of raw materials SUBTRACTED when we compute direct materials cost? The business paid for all of it — surely all of it is a cost?

**Tutor:** Paid for, yes; consumed, no — and cost accounting charges consumption, never payment. The R552 000 available this year — opening R72 000, purchases R468 000, carriage R12 000 — splits into two different fates. R488 000 was issued to the floor and is now sewn into bags: that is this year's direct materials COST, because that canvas has been transformed and can never be canvas again. The remaining R64 000 lies untouched on the storeroom shelves, ready for next year — it is not a cost at all; it is an ASSET, and it will stand in the statement of financial position as raw materials stock. Charge the whole R552 000 and you would be billing this year's production for next year's canvas — overstating this year's cost, understating this year's profit, and then double-charging next year when the canvas is finally used. It is the matching principle in overalls: the cost travels with the material into the product, in the year the transformation happens. The same grammar guards every station: work-in-progress subtracts the half-made closing stock, finished goods subtracts the unsold closing bags, and at each gate the subtraction is simply the asset staying behind while the cost moves on.

***

**Bianca:** A learner asked: what is the work-in-progress account really FOR? It just sits between the cost accounts and finished goods — couldn't we send the costs straight through?

**Tutor:** You could skip it only if every bag started and finished inside the same year — and no real factory is that tidy. Pick any night you like: Mokoena's floor holds cut panels, pinned zips, half-sewn bags — R48 000 of value at this year end that is neither raw material nor sellable product. Some account must hold that value, or it evaporates from the books and the statements mislead. That is the balance-sheet job. The costing job is larger: work-in-progress is the meeting pot where the three cost streams — direct materials, direct labour, overheads — combine, and its closing arithmetic yields the most important figure in the topic: the cost of production of FINISHED goods. Opening R38 000, plus the year's R1 210 000 of manufacturing costs, less the R48 000 still on the floor: R1 200 000 completed. Without the account you would know what the year SPENT but not what the year FINISHED — and those two differ whenever the floor's holdings change. Divide the finished cost by the 20 000 completed bags and the unit cost of R60 appears; skip the work-in-progress step and half-sewn bags would contaminate that figure. The account is not a corridor; it is the measuring chamber.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: my figures balance and my unit cost looks right. Why does this topic insist on all the internal control and ethics content — isn't that the manager's job, not the accountant's?

**Tutor:** The accountant is the one person positioned to SEE the problems, which makes them the accountant's job by definition. Every control in this topic is a comparison between two records, and the cost records are always one side of the pair. Materials: the storeman's issues against what the completed runs should have consumed — a widening gap is waste, damage or theft, and the costing exposes it before any physical count would. Labour: clock-card hours against wages paid, overtime against prior approvals. Overheads: metered electricity against budget, month after month. A bookkeeper who computes the unit cost monthly is holding the factory's early-warning system: R60 creeping toward R64 with no supplier price rise means the floor is leaking, and the line-by-line variances name the suspects. Then the ethics, which is costing with its eyes open. Cheaper stitching that fails on a child's school bag, wages under the legal minimum, guards taken off the cutting tables to speed the line, dye waste tipped into the drain — every one of those arrives in the books dressed as a COST SAVING. The figures will applaud the decision unless the person behind them refuses. That is why the topic binds the two together: the ledger measures what a choice costs the business; ethics asks what it costs everyone else — and a factory run on the first question alone eventually answers with fines, closures and a community that does not forget.
