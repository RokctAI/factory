Hey, it's Bianca — manufacturing looks like a brand-new subject: new accounts, new names, costs flying everywhere. It isn't. It is one path, walked by one rand, and you already know every step's arithmetic from trading stock.

Draw the path before you touch any numbers: sacks, floor, store, customer. Raw materials, work-in-progress, finished goods, cost of sales. Every figure in a manufacturing question lives at one of those four stations, and every station runs the same grammar you have used since grade 10: what was there, plus what arrived, minus what remains, equals what moved on. Four stations, one grammar. That is the whole ledger.

Sorting costs is two quick questions, asked separately. Question one: can I point at this cost inside the product? Yes — direct: leather, machinists' wages. No, but it keeps the factory running — overhead: foreman, rent, machine depreciation, oil and needles. Not factory at all — office is admin, getting goods to customers is selling, and neither ever enters production cost. Question two: does it grow when output grows? That is variable or fixed — and it is a separate question, so don't let "fixed" trick you into "not production".

When a figure is missing — and one always is — don't panic and don't guess. Write the station's grammar with the three figures you have, and the fourth falls out. Examiners hide figures precisely because the grammar finds them; that is the skill being marked.

And keep the unit cost close: cost of finished production divided by units completed. It is the factory's heartbeat, and half the interpretation marks come from noticing it drift and naming a suspect. You've got this!
