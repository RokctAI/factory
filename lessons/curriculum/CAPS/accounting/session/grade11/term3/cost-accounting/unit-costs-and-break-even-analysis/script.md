# Part 1 — Expert

The last lesson measured what a shoe COSTS. This one answers the question every owner actually loses sleep over: how many must we SELL before the factory stops losing money? That number is the BREAK-EVEN POINT, found through contribution per unit, and it is the sharpest decision tool in this course. Today: cost behaviour formalised — fixed and variable seen per unit and in total; contribution per unit and what it pays for; the break-even calculation itself, and why the answer always rounds UP; and the working uses of break-even — margin of safety, comparing it with planned production, and testing what happens when prices or costs move. The example continues: Kagiso Footwear Manufacturers, budgeting the coming year.

## Subtopic: How Costs Behave When Output Moves

The budget for the coming year, spoken in full. Planned production and sales: 24 000 pairs. Selling price: R95 per pair. VARIABLE costs per pair: direct materials R23, direct labour R17, and selling and distribution — packaging and commission per pair sold — R8. Total variable cost: R48 per pair. FIXED costs for the year: factory overheads of R400 000 and administration of R258 000 — R658 000 in total, payable whether the factory makes one pair or thirty thousand.

Now watch each family from two angles, because this is where learners slip. Variable costs are constant PER UNIT — every pair consumes R48 — but grow IN TOTAL as output grows: at 10 000 pairs, R480 000; at 24 000 pairs, R1 152 000. Fixed costs are the mirror image: constant IN TOTAL at R658 000, but shrinking PER UNIT as output spreads them — R65,80 per pair at 10 000 pairs, R27,42 at 24 000, rounded to the cent. That shrinking is why factories chase volume, and why a unit cost that includes fixed costs is only true AT the output it was computed for.

The examiner's classification traps live here. Rent is fixed — until the question adds a second building for extra output, when it steps. Commission is variable but a SELLING cost, not production. Electricity often splits: a fixed basic charge plus a variable usage charge — name both parts if a question shows them. The discipline: classify each cost by its behaviour in THIS question's facts, not by memory of the last question. Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: costs classified, and both angles — per unit and in total — computed.

## Subtopic: Contribution Per Unit

Sell one pair for R95. The variable costs of that pair — R48 — leave with it: leather, labour, packaging, commission. What remains is R47, and that R47 has a name: the CONTRIBUTION PER UNIT. Selling price per unit, less variable cost per unit.

Contribution is the most misread word in the topic, so fix its meaning now: it is NOT profit. It is what each pair CONTRIBUTES toward the pile of fixed costs — the R658 000 that stands there regardless. Every pair sold tosses R47 onto that pile. Until the pile is covered, the business is still losing money overall; after the pile is covered, each further pair's R47 has nothing left to pay off, and only then does it fall through as pure profit.

Prove the year with contribution, in words. Planned sales, 24 000 pairs at R95: revenue R2 280 000. Total variable costs, 24 000 at R48: R1 152 000. TOTAL CONTRIBUTION: R1 128 000 — check it as 24 000 pairs times R47. Less fixed costs of R658 000: budgeted PROFIT of R470 000. The whole income statement of a factory, four lines: sales, less variable costs, contribution, less fixed costs, profit. Every break-even question is that little statement rearranged.

And feel what the two cost families do inside it: the variable R48 scales with every decision; the fixed R658 000 sits still and waits to be buried. The entire logic of the next subtopic is one question — how many R47 coins does it take to bury R658 000? Stop for this section's questions now: contribution computed, distinguished from profit, and the four-line statement built.

## Subtopic: The Break-Even Point

The BREAK-EVEN POINT is the output at which total contribution exactly equals total fixed costs — no profit, no loss. The formula, in words: total fixed costs, divided by contribution per unit.

Compute it for Kagiso Footwear: R658 000 divided by R47 — 14 000 pairs. At 14 000 pairs, check it in full: revenue is 14 000 times R95 — R1 330 000. Variable costs: 14 000 times R48 — R672 000. Contribution: R658 000 — precisely the fixed costs. Profit: zero. The R1 330 000 is the break-even VALUE — break-even units times selling price — and questions ask for it in rand as often as in units.

Interpret the number against the plan, because a bare 14 000 earns half marks. Planned production is 24 000 pairs, so the business breaks even at just over half its planned output — 58,3 percent of it. Every pair from number 14 001 onward drops R47 of pure profit; 10 000 such pairs produce the budgeted R470 000. State it in the exam's language: the break-even point of 14 000 pairs is well below planned production of 24 000, so the budget carries a healthy cushion.

Two disciplines guard the calculation. First: only FIXED costs go on top, only CONTRIBUTION PER UNIT below — sneaking a variable cost into the numerator, or using selling price instead of contribution, are the two classic wrecks. Second: when the division is not exact, round UP, always. If fixed costs were R658 100, the division gives 14 002 comma 1, and 14 002 pairs would still leave a sliver of loss — the business breaks even only at 14 003. Break-even is a threshold, not an approximation: you have either buried the pile or you have not. Pause for this section's questions: the point computed in units and in rand, interpreted against the plan, and rounded the right way.

## Subtopic: Working With Break-Even

Break-even earns its keep when things CHANGE. Three standard uses, worked with our figures.

MARGIN OF SAFETY: how far sales can fall before the loss line. Planned 24 000 less break-even 14 000: 10 000 pairs — as a percentage of plan, 41,7 percent. Sales can drop by two fifths before Kagiso loses money. A thin margin — five, ten percent — reads as fragile: one bad season away from loss.

WHAT-IF: PRICE. A school-uniform chain offers a big contract if the price drops to R89. New contribution: R89 less R48 — R41. New break-even: R658 000 over R41 — 16 048 comma 8, so 16 049 pairs. A six-rand price cut raised the survival line by more than two thousand pairs. That asymmetry is the lesson: price cuts attack contribution directly, and small cuts move break-even violently. The decision question: will the contract's extra volume out-earn the contribution lost on every existing pair?

WHAT-IF: COSTS. Leather rises by R3 per pair: variable cost R51, contribution R44, break-even R658 000 over R44 — 14 954 comma 5, so 14 955 pairs. Or the landlord raises fixed costs by R47 000: new pile R705 000 over the old R47 — exactly 15 000 pairs. Notice the two routes differ: variable increases shrink every pair's contribution; fixed increases grow the pile the contributions must bury. Either way the cushion thins, and management sees it BEFORE signing the lease or the supply contract — that is the point of the tool: it prices decisions before they are made.

The limits, for full marks in a discussion question: the model assumes the selling price and unit variable cost stay constant at any volume, that fixed costs never step, and that everything made is sold — assumptions that hold near the planned range and weaken far from it. Used inside its range, break-even analysis is the cheapest management consultant a small factory will ever hire. The final questions of this part are with you now: margin of safety, both what-ifs, and the assumptions named.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same idea from a Saturday at the taxi rank — because everyone who has ever hustled anything already knows break-even in their bones.

## Subtopic: The Pile of Stones

You want to sell boerewors rolls at the taxi rank on Saturday. Before you sell roll number one, Saturday has already cost you: R400 to hire the gas braai and the stand, R258 for the gazebo and the cooler boxes. R658 gone — sold nothing yet. That R658 is your pile of stones. It does not care how your day goes. Sell zero rolls, the pile is R658. Sell a thousand, still R658.

Then every roll has its own little costs that travel WITH the roll: the wors, the bread, the sauce, the serviette — say R48 a roll. Sell a roll for R95, let R48 of it leave with the ingredients, and what stays in your pocket from that one roll is R47.

That R47 is the magic number of the whole lesson: the CONTRIBUTION. It is not profit — not yet. It is one stone lifted off the pile. Roll after roll, R47 at a time, the pile shrinks. All morning you are not earning; you are digging yourself out of the R658 hole that Saturday dug before you arrived.

Notice the two kinds of cost behaving differently as the day heats up. Ingredients scale: every extra roll costs another R48, forever. The braai hire doesn't: whether you sell one roll or a mountain, it is the same R658 — so the busier the day, the cheaper the braai becomes per roll. Big sellers win twice: more contributions, and thinner fixed cost per unit. Quick check before we carry on — questions on the pile and the travelling costs are coming to you right now.

## Subtopic: Roll Number Fourteen

So the only question that matters at six in the morning: how many rolls until the pile is gone? Each roll lifts R47. The pile weighs R658. R658 divided by R47: FOURTEEN. Roll number fourteen pays off the last stone. Rolls one to fourteen: you are working for the braai man, the gazebo man, the cooler boxes. Roll FIFTEEN is the first roll you sell for yourself — from there, every roll drops R47 of pure profit into your pocket.

That is the break-even point, and the factory version only adds zeros: Kagiso Footwear's pile is R658 000, each pair contributes R47, so the factory's roll number fourteen is pair number 14 000. Selling 24 000 pairs means 10 000 pairs sold entirely for profit — R470 000 of it.

Two habits to carry from the rank into the exam. First, if the division comes out ugly — say the pile divided by the contribution gives fourteen comma two — you cannot sell comma two of a roll: you break even on roll FIFTEEN. Always round the survival line UP; at fourteen you are still one stone short. Second, ask where the survival line sits inside your plan. Expecting to sell twenty-four rolls with break-even at fourteen: comfortable — ten rolls of cushion, forty-two percent of the plan. Expecting fifteen with break-even at fourteen: one bad hour from a loss, and you should be nervous. The line means nothing until you compare it with what you expect to sell. Your questions for this part are up now: find roll number fourteen in three different setups, and say how much cushion each one has.

## Subtopic: When the Price of Wors Goes Up

The real power move is asking what-if BEFORE Saturday comes — because every change in your setup moves the survival line, and you want to see where it lands while you can still say no.

The wors supplier raises prices by R3 a roll. Your travelling cost becomes R51, your contribution drops to R44, and the pile of R658 now needs R658 over R44 — fifteen rolls, not fourteen. One price rise, one extra roll just to survive.

A taxi boss offers to buy for his whole rank — but at R89 a roll. Contribution falls to R41; the survival line jumps to just over sixteen rolls. See the trap in slow motion: a R6 discount FEELS small against a R95 price, but it comes straight out of your R47 pocket-money — that is nearly thirteen percent of your contribution, gone. Price cuts always bite harder than they look, because they bite the contribution, not the price. Take the deal only if the extra volume more than repays what every single roll now gives up.

And if the braai man raises his hire by R47? The pile grows to R705, and at R47 a roll the line moves to exactly fifteen. Different route — bigger pile instead of smaller stones — same effect: more rolls just to reach zero.

This is why the shoe factory runs the same sums before signing anything: leather up R3 a pair pushes break-even to 14 955 pairs; a contract at R89 pushes it to 16 049; rent up R47 000 pushes it to 15 000 exactly. None of these are surprises if you calculate BEFORE you commit — and all of them are disasters discovered in February if you don't. The survival line is not a report card at year end; it is a headlight. And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: move the line all three ways — dearer wors, cheaper selling price, pricier braai — and decide, with numbers, which deals deserve a yes.
