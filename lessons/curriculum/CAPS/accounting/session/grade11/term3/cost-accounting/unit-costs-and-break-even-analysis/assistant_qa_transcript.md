**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: why does break-even use CONTRIBUTION per unit and not PROFIT per unit? Profit feels like the natural thing to divide into fixed costs.

**Tutor:** Because profit per unit does not exist until you already know the answer to the question you are asking. Follow the circle. To compute profit per unit you must first spread the fixed costs across the units — R658 000 over some number of pairs. But WHICH number? At 10 000 pairs the fixed load is R65,80 each and the so-called profit per unit is negative; at 24 000 it is R27,42 each and profit per unit is R19,58. Profit per unit changes with volume — it is an output-dependent figure, so using it to FIND an output chases your own tail. Contribution escapes the circle because it is volume-independent: every single pair, from the first to the thirty-thousandth, sells for R95 and carries away R48 of variable cost, leaving R47 — the same R47 regardless of how many pairs exist. That stability is what makes the division legitimate: a constant pile of R658 000, chipped at by a constant R47 per unit, is a well-posed question with one answer — 14 000. The deeper habit: whenever volume is the unknown, work only with quantities that do not move with volume — fixed totals and per-unit contributions — and leave anything that mixes the two, like full unit cost or profit per unit, out of the formula entirely.

***

**Bianca:** Next: my friend calculated break-even as 13 854 pairs by dividing the fixed costs by the R47,50 she got somewhere. Where do wrong contribution figures usually come from?

**Tutor:** From three habits, and it is worth naming each so you can patrol for them. First and most common: leaving a variable cost out because it does not feel like production — selling and distribution costs, our R8 of packaging and commission per pair. Break-even is a WHOLE-business calculation: the pair is not truly sold until it is packed, delivered and commissioned, so every cost that varies with a unit sold belongs in the R48, factory or not. Leaving out the R8 gives a contribution of R55 and a flattering, false break-even near 11 964. Second: the mirror error — dragging a fixed cost INTO the per-unit figure, usually depreciation or rent divided by planned output and added to variable cost. That imports the circular reasoning we just buried: the fixed pile must stay on top of the formula as a total, never leak into the per-unit bottom. Third: using the selling price instead of the contribution — R658 000 over R95 gives 6 926, a number that assumes the pairs cost nothing to make. The patrol routine, twenty seconds long: list every cost in the question; stamp each one FIXED-TOTAL or VARIABLE-PER-UNIT; check the two stamps cover everything exactly once; only then divide. Every wreck in this topic is a cost that got the wrong stamp or two stamps or none.

**Bianca:** A learner asked: if the factory plans 24 000 pairs and break-even is 14 000, why should anyone still care about the break-even number? The business is nowhere near it.

**Tutor:** Because the distance itself is the information — and because the distance moves. The 10 000-pair gap is the margin of safety, 41,7 percent of the plan, and it is the business's shock absorber: it says demand can fall by two fifths — a competitor opening, a bad uniform season, a supplier failure halting three months of production — before a single rand of loss appears. A manager who knows the cushion is 41,7 percent makes different decisions from one whose cushion is 8 percent: bolder pricing experiments, more willingness to sign fixed commitments, less panic in a slow month. And every proposal that crosses the desk moves the line, so the number is consulted constantly, not filed. The uniform-chain contract at R89 moves break-even to 16 049 — the cushion thins from 41,7 to a third. The new lease moves it to 15 000 exactly. Leather at R3 more per pair, to 14 955. Each decision is priced in cushion before it is signed. There is also a darker use: when a downturn does come, break-even tells management precisely how far output can fall before the factory burns cash, which drives decisions about shifts, short time and stock builds. A business far from its break-even is not a business that can ignore it — it is a business enjoying the distance, and wise ones check the gauge every time the road bends.

**Bianca:** Last one: the model assumes the price and the unit costs never change with volume. Real life is not like that — so why are we taught a tool built on assumptions that are false?

**Tutor:** Because every useful model is built on assumptions that are false somewhere, and the skill being taught is using the tool INSIDE the range where its assumptions hold. Near the planned output — say between 10 000 and 30 000 pairs for Kagiso — the assumptions are approximately true: the price list is stable, leather is bought at the same rate per pair, the factory fits in its current building, and what is made is sold within the season. Inside that band, the model's answers are reliable enough to price real decisions, and enormously cheaper than the alternative — discovering break-even empirically by losing money. Far outside the band, each assumption snaps in a predictable way, and a top answer names the snapping points: push volume high enough and fixed costs STEP — a second shift, more machines, a bigger building; chase that volume in the market and the price may need to fall, cutting contribution; buy triple the leather and the supplier offers bulk discounts, so variable cost per unit bends; and if production outruns sales, unsold stock breaks the made-equals-sold assumption. So the mature statement for an exam: break-even analysis gives quick, decision-ready answers within the relevant range of output, and its limits — stepped fixed costs, price and cost changes at extreme volumes, and unsold production — must be acknowledged whenever a decision pushes outside that range. A tool with known limits is not a weak tool; it is an honest one.
