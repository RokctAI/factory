**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: the foreman works in the factory all day, every day. Why is his salary an OVERHEAD when the machinists' wages are direct labour? Both of them are making shoes.

**Tutor:** The test is not where a person works — it is whether their effort can be traced into a UNIT of product. A machinist cuts and stitches actual pairs: her hours attach to shoes, and if the factory made nothing this month, her production work would not exist. The foreman supervises the WHOLE floor: he schedules, inspects, solves problems across every pair at once, and no accountant can honestly say which minutes of his day belong to which shoe. His salary is real production cost — it belongs in the factory, never in administration — but it enters through the overheads gate, where all the untraceable factory costs gather: rent, electricity, machine depreciation, insurance, the oil and needles. Notice the second difference: behaviour. The machinists' wages tend to be VARIABLE — more pairs, more stitching hours — while the foreman earns the same salary at five thousand pairs or nine thousand, making his cost FIXED. The two classifications are independent, and exams love the crossings: an indirect cost that is fixed, like his salary; a direct cost that is variable, like leather; and the trap pairings — sales commission is variable but never a production cost, because it lives outside the factory entirely.

***

**Bianca:** Next: why does the closing stock of raw materials get SUBTRACTED when we work out direct materials cost? We paid for all of it — surely it is all a cost?

**Tutor:** Paid for, yes; consumed, no — and cost accounting charges consumption, not payment. The R614 000 available this year — opening R84 000, purchases R516 000, carriage R14 000 — splits into two different fates. R538 000 was issued to the floor and is now inside shoes: that is this year's direct materials COST, because that leather has been transformed and cannot be leather again. The remaining R76 000 still lies in the storeroom, unchanged, ready for next year — it is not a cost at all; it is an ASSET, and it will appear in the statement of financial position as raw materials stock. Charge the full R614 000 and you would be counting next year's shoes' leather against this year's production — overstating this year's cost, understating this year's profit, and then double-charging next year when that leather is finally used. It is the matching principle wearing overalls: the cost follows the leather into the product, in the year the transformation happens. The same grammar guards every stage: work-in-progress subtracts the half-made closing stock, finished goods subtracts the unsold closing pairs, and at each gate the subtraction is simply the asset staying behind while the cost moves on.

***

**Bianca:** A learner asked: what is actually the point of the work-in-progress account? It just sits between the cost accounts and finished goods — could we not skip it and send costs straight through?

**Tutor:** You could only skip it if every item started and finished inside the same period — and no real factory works that way. On any night you choose, Kagiso's floor holds cut uppers, glued soles, half-stitched pairs: R66 000 of value this year end that is neither raw material nor sellable shoe. Some account must hold that value, or it vanishes from the books and the statements lie. That is the balance-sheet job. The costing job is bigger: work-in-progress is the meeting pot where the three cost streams — direct materials, direct labour, overheads — combine, and its closing arithmetic produces the single most important figure in the topic: cost of production of FINISHED goods. Opening R46 000, plus the year's R1 340 000 of manufacturing costs, less the R66 000 still on the floor: R1 320 000 completed. Without the account, you would know what the year SPENT but not what the year FINISHED — and those differ whenever the floor's holdings change. Divide the finished cost by the 24 000 completed pairs and the unit cost of R55 emerges; skip the work-in-progress step and units still on the lasts would contaminate that figure. The account is not a corridor; it is the measuring chamber.

***

**Bianca:** Last one: my statements balance and my unit cost looks fine. Why does the syllabus insist on all the internal control and ethics material — is that not the manager's problem rather than the accountant's?

**Tutor:** The accountant is the one person who can SEE the problems, which makes them the accountant's problem by definition. Every control in this topic is a comparison between two records, and the cost records are one side of each pair. Materials: the storeman's issues against what the completed jobs should have consumed — a widening gap is waste, damage or theft, and the costing detects it before a physical count ever would. Labour: clock-card hours against wages paid, overtime against prior approvals. Overheads: metered electricity against budget, month by month. A bookkeeper who computes the unit cost monthly holds the factory's early-warning system: R55 drifting to R58 with no price increase in materials means the floor is leaking, and the drift names its own suspects through the variance in each line. Then ethics, which is costing with its eyes open. A cheaper glue that fails on a child's school shoe, wages below the legal minimum, guards removed from cutters to speed the line, waste dumped in the river — every one of those appears in the books as a COST SAVING. The figures will applaud the decision unless the human behind them refuses. That is why the syllabus binds the two together: the ledger measures what a choice costs the business; ethics asks what it costs everyone else — and a factory run on the first question alone eventually answers to fines, closures and a community that remembers.
