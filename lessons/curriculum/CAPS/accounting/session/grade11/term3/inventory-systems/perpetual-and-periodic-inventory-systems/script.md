# Part 1 — Expert

Grade 10 recorded stock one way only: the perpetual system, where every sale carried its cost of sales entry alongside it. That was a choice, not the only possibility — and today we meet the alternative. The PERIODIC system records purchases rather than stock movements and computes cost of sales only at period end, from a formula. Today: how each system works, the cost of sales calculation in both, the gross profit checks that follow, the advantages and disadvantages that decide which a business should run, and the stock controls and ethics the syllabus attaches. Examiners set this topic as a comparison and as a calculation; be fluent in both.

## Subtopic: Two Systems, Two Philosophies

The PERPETUAL (continuous) system keeps the Trading Stock account permanently up to date. Every purchase debits Trading Stock; every sale triggers TWO entries — the selling price to Debtors or Bank and Sales, and simultaneously the COST of those goods out of Trading Stock into Cost of Sales. The account therefore claims, at any moment of any day, what should be on the shelf. Returns adjust it both ways; the stocktake tests it, and the difference between the account's claim and the physical count becomes the trading stock deficit or surplus you already know.

The PERIODIC system keeps no running stock record at all. Purchases are debited to a PURCHASES account — an expense-type account, not an asset — and sales are recorded with the selling price only: no cost of sales entry accompanies the sale. The Trading Stock account sits untouched all year at its opening balance. At period end, the business COUNTS the stock physically, and only then computes what it sold, by formula. Between counts, nobody knows the stock figure — the system trades information for simplicity.

The design difference in one sentence: the perpetual system records COST AT EVERY MOVEMENT; the periodic system records PURCHASES and works cost of sales out backwards at the end. Two philosophies — continuous knowledge against periodic reckoning — and every advantage and disadvantage below flows from that single choice.

## Subtopic: Cost of Sales in Both Systems

The perpetual system needs no cost of sales calculation at year end — the account has been accumulating all year. But questions still ask you to derive it, usually from the mark-up. With a mark-up of 50 percent on cost and sales of R450 000: sales represent 150 percent of cost, so cost of sales is 450 000 times 100 over 150 — R300 000, and gross profit is R150 000. Where the mark-up is quoted on SELLING price — say 33⅓ percent — cost of sales is sales less the margin directly. Read which base the mark-up uses; that reading is worth the whole question.

The periodic system's calculation is the topic's signature formula, and it must be reproduced in full:

Opening stock, PLUS purchases, PLUS carriage on purchases, LESS purchases returns, LESS drawings of stock, gives COST OF GOODS AVAILABLE FOR SALE; then LESS closing stock gives COST OF SALES.

Work it with figures. Opening stock R80 000; purchases R520 000; carriage on purchases R18 000 — freight INWARD is part of the cost of getting stock onto the shelf, so it belongs in the calculation, while carriage OUTWARD, delivering to customers, is a selling expense and stays out; purchases returns R12 000; the owner took stock of R6 000 for private use. Goods available for sale: 80 000 plus 520 000 plus 18 000, less 12 000, less 6 000 — R600 000. The physical count at year end values closing stock at R95 000. Cost of sales: 600 000 less 95 000 — R505 000.

Then gross profit, in either system: sales less cost of sales. With sales of R700 000 against our R505 000, gross profit is R195 000 — and the mark-up achieved is 195 000 over 505 000, near enough 38,6 percent on cost, which the business compares against its intended policy exactly as the grade 10 analysis lesson taught.

Note the periodic system's silent flaw, because examiners probe it: any stock missing through theft, breakage or error is INSIDE that R505 000. The formula assumes everything not counted at the end was sold. There is no deficit figure, because there is nothing to compare the count against — losses hide inside cost of sales and quietly depress gross profit without ever being named.

## Subtopic: Advantages, Disadvantages and the Choice

Judge the systems on what each buys and what each costs.

The PERPETUAL system's advantages: stock is known at any moment, so re-ordering is informed and stock-outs avoidable; cost of sales and gross profit can be reported at any date without a count; and — decisively — the account's claim can be tested against a physical count, so theft and losses are IDENTIFIED and sized as a trading stock deficit. That is why the syllabus keeps calling it the control system. Its disadvantages: it demands a cost record for every item and every movement, which is administratively heavy and, before computers, impractical for a high-volume shop selling thousands of low-value lines.

The PERIODIC system's advantages: simplicity and low cost — no per-item cost records, no entry at each sale — which suits small businesses, and businesses whose goods are numerous, cheap and hard to track individually. Its disadvantages: no stock figure between counts, so no informed re-ordering and no interim gross profit without a count; the business must close or count to know where it stands; and, the serious one, NO CONTROL — losses are invisible, absorbed silently into cost of sales, so theft can run for a year without a single figure raising a question.

The choice, therefore, follows the goods and the technology. High-value, individually identifiable items — vehicles, appliances, jewellery — argue overwhelmingly for perpetual: the value of knowing and the cost of losing are both high. Numerous low-value items once argued for periodic, but modern point-of-sale systems that scan a barcode and post the cost automatically have made perpetual practical for supermarkets too — which is why the periodic system now survives mainly in small businesses without such systems. State that technological point in a comparison answer; it distinguishes a candidate who understands the systems from one who has memorised two lists.

## Subtopic: Stock Control, Ethics and Internal Audit

Whichever system runs the books, the stock itself must be protected — and the syllabus expects the controls named specifically.

PHYSICAL controls: a locked, restricted storeroom with responsibility allocated to a named person; goods received checked against the order and the delivery note before signature; security measures at exits; and separation of the duties of ordering, receiving, recording and paying, so no single person controls a stock item's whole journey.

DOCUMENT controls: pre-numbered orders, delivery notes, invoices and credit notes, filed in sequence so a missing number is a question; and authorisation limits, so large orders require senior approval.

COUNTING controls: regular stocktakes — annually at minimum, ideally cyclical counts through the year — conducted by people INDEPENDENT of the storeroom, with the count reconciled to the records and every difference investigated rather than merely recorded. In the perpetual system that reconciliation produces the deficit or surplus; in the periodic system, a business serious about control must run informal stock records precisely so that a comparison remains possible.

The ETHICS the syllabus attaches. Employees taking stock — from the small "it's only one" to systematic theft — is the commonest fraud in retail, and it is defeated by the routines above rather than by trust. Managers manipulating the closing stock figure is the graver offence, and worth understanding mechanically: overstate closing stock and cost of sales falls, so gross profit and net profit RISE — a stroke of the pen that flatters results, misleads owners and lenders, and shifts the loss into the following year, where it must eventually surface. Suppliers short-delivering against full invoices, and staff colluding with them, complete the list.

The INTERNAL AUDIT tests that catch each: attend and independently re-count a sample at the stocktake; trace a sample of delivery notes to purchase entries and vice versa; compare the achieved gross profit percentage against the policy mark-up and investigate any gap, since an unexplained shortfall is often the first fingerprint of loss; and examine slow-moving and obsolete stock, since both distort the closing figure. The through-line of this whole topic: a system that measures stock is worth having only alongside routines that verify it.

The final questions of this part are with you now: controls named by category, the closing-stock manipulation traced through to profit, and the audit tests matched to the risks.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same two systems from behind the counter — because the real question here is one every shop owner faces: do I write down what leaves my shelf, or do I count at the end and work it out?

## Subtopic: The Notebook or the Count

Two spaza owners, same street, two ways of running stock.

The first keeps a notebook. Every time a loaf goes out the door, she writes down not just the R18 she took, but that a loaf costing R13 left the shelf. Every delivery goes in the notebook too. So at any hour of any day she can open that notebook and say exactly what should be on her shelves and exactly what her goods have cost her this month. That is the perpetual system: record the cost of every movement, as it happens.

The second keeps no such notebook. He writes down what he BUYS — deliveries, invoices — and at the till he only records the money coming in. What his goods cost him, he does not track as he goes. So how does he ever know? He counts. At the end of the year he closes up, walks the shelves with a pen, and counts everything that is left. Then he does one sum: everything I started with, plus everything I bought, minus what's still standing here — that must be what I sold. That is the periodic system: don't track, count and work it backwards.

Both get to the same number in the end. But notice what the second owner just gave up. He is trusting a sum that assumes everything not on his shelf was SOLD. If a case of cooldrink walked out the back door in June, his sum quietly calls it "sold" — because there is nothing left to count, and nothing to compare against.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the two owners are coming to you right now: what each records, and what the counting owner is assuming.

## Subtopic: Working It Out Backwards

Let's do the second owner's year-end sum properly, because it is the calculation the exam wants.

Start with what he had on the shelves at the beginning: R80 000 of stock. Add everything he bought during the year: R520 000. Add what it cost to GET those goods to him — the delivery charges on his purchases, R18 000; that money is part of what the stock cost him, so it belongs in the sum. Careful with the opposite one though: what he pays to deliver goods TO customers is a selling cost, not a stock cost — it stays out.

Now take away things that came off that pile without being sold. Goods he sent back to suppliers: R12 000. And the stock he took home for his own family: R6 000 — that is drawings, not a sale.

So the total pile that was available to sell: 80 000 plus 520 000 plus 18 000, minus 12 000, minus 6 000 — R600 000 of goods available. He counts the shelves: R95 000 left. So what left the shop? 600 000 minus 95 000 — R505 000. That is his cost of sales. And with sales of R700 000, his gross profit is R195 000.

There's the whole formula, in the order that keeps it straight: what I started with, plus what I bought and what it cost to get it here, minus returns and what I took home — that's the pile; minus what's still standing — that's what went out.

And now say the uncomfortable part out loud: that R505 000 includes the case of cooldrink that was stolen. His sum has no way to tell theft from a sale, because both leave the shelf empty. The notebook owner would have spotted it — her notebook says R2 400 should be there and the count says nothing is. His system simply calls it sold, and his profit is quietly smaller with nobody knowing why.

## Subtopic: Which One Should the Shop Use?

So which owner is right? It depends on what she sells — and, these days, on what her till can do.

If you sell FEW, EXPENSIVE things — fridges, phones, cars — track every one. Losing one item is losing thousands, and you have few enough items to record them. Notebook, definitely.

If you sell THOUSANDS OF CHEAP things — sweets, single cigarettes, loose vegetables — writing the cost of every item as it leaves is a full-time job that earns nothing. That is why small shops historically counted at the end instead: simple, cheap, good enough.

But here is what changed everything: the barcode scanner. A modern till scans the item, takes the money AND writes the cost entry automatically — the notebook keeps itself. That is why supermarkets, selling tens of thousands of items a day, all run the notebook system now, and why the counting system survives mainly where there is no such till. If an exam asks you to advise a business, say that: the choice used to be about how many items you sell; today it is mostly about whether your till can do the writing for you.

And the last thing — the reason this whole topic matters more than bookkeeping neatness. The notebook system does something the counting system cannot: it lets you CATCH things. Count the shelf, compare it to what the notebook claims, and the gap has a size and a name. Without that comparison, stock can disappear for a year and show up only as "our profit was disappointing".

Which is also where the ethics live. The nastiest trick in this topic is a manager writing the closing stock count HIGHER than it really is — because look what that does to the sum: a bigger number subtracted at the end means a smaller cost of sales, which means a bigger profit on paper. The shelves are just as empty; the report looks better; the owner and the bank are misled — and next year, when the truth cannot be counted away, the loss lands anyway. That is why the counting is done by people who don't run the storeroom, and why the counted figure is checked, not just accepted.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: choose a system for three different shops with reasons, and trace what a padded closing stock figure does to cost of sales and profit. Get that one, and you understand stock better than most people running shops.
