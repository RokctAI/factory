**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: if the periodic system can't detect theft, why is it even allowed? Surely every business should be forced onto the perpetual system.

**Tutor:** What the rules of accounting actually demand is fair presentation of the results — not one particular bookkeeping method. Both systems, applied correctly, deliver a true cost of sales and a true gross profit for the period; where they differ is in what they can tell you DURING the period and in what they can detect. And the periodic system's simplicity is not laziness in every case: for a trader moving thousands of low-value, hard-to-individuate items without a computerised till — loose fruit, sweets, hardware sold by the handful — the cost of keeping per-item cost records would exceed the value of the information, and a business is entitled to make that trade. What responsibility does demand, and what auditors press on, is reasonable care over the assets — so a periodic-system business is expected to compensate with physical controls: a restricted storeroom, checked deliveries, separated duties, and more frequent counts. The choice of system is free; the duty of control is not.

***

**Bianca:** Next: in the periodic formula we subtract drawings of stock — but the owner taking stock home is neither a purchase nor a sale. Why does it belong in a cost of sales calculation at all?

**Tutor:** Because the formula tells the story of a PILE, not of transactions — and anything that left the pile without being sold must be pulled out, or the arithmetic blames the customers for it. Trace it through. The pile is built from opening stock plus everything bought; the closing count reports what remains; the difference is presumed sold. Now suppose the owner carried R5 000 of stock home. Those goods are off the shelf and absent from the count, so unless they are subtracted explicitly, the formula folds them into cost of sales — the business then reports having consumed R5 000 of stock to earn revenue it never earned, understating gross profit and hiding the owner's private consumption inside trading costs. Subtracting them keeps two truths straight at once: cost of sales measures only goods that generated sales, and the drawings land where they belong, against the owner's equity. It is the business entity rule from grade 10 wearing a stock apron — the owner's household is never the business, and the formula must not let the two blur.

**Bianca:** A learner asked: the two systems are supposed to reach the same gross profit if nothing goes wrong. So in a perfect year with zero losses, is there actually any difference in the figures?

**Tutor:** In a perfect year the year-end FIGURES converge, and seeing why sharpens your grip on both systems. With no theft, breakage or error, the periodic formula's assumption — everything not counted was sold — happens to be true, so its cost of sales equals what the perpetual account accumulated movement by movement, and gross profit matches to the rand. But three differences survive even the perfect year. First, the path: one system produced the number from a running record, the other reasoned it from a count — and only the first can produce the number on any date you please; the periodic business knows its gross profit once a year, after counting. Second, the balance sheet during the year: the perpetual Trading Stock account holds a live asset figure for interim statements, while the periodic system must count or estimate before any interim reporting. Third, and most important, the PROOF: the perpetual business can demonstrate that there were no losses, because its count reconciled to its records; the periodic business can only hope there were none, because it has nothing to reconcile against. Identical figures, very different knowledge — and in accounting, knowing why a figure is right matters nearly as much as the figure.

**Bianca:** Last one: you said supermarkets all run the perpetual system now because of barcode scanners. Then why do they still close for stocktakes? Isn't the whole point that the till already knows?

**Tutor:** The till knows what SHOULD be on the shelf; only a count reveals what IS — and the gap between the two is precisely the perpetual system's payoff. Remember what the scanning till records: every item that passed the scanner, priced and costed. It cannot record what never reached the scanner: the packet slipped into a pocket, the case that left through the receiving door, the breakage swept up and unrecorded, the delivery signed for but never actually unloaded. A supermarket's records claim, say, forty units of an item; the counters find thirty-six; the four missing units become a measured deficit — sized in rand, traced to a department, investigated. Without the count, the perpetual system would be a beautifully detailed claim that nobody ever tested — which is barely better than no system, because an untested record catches nothing. That is why the count survives the technology: the system and the stocktake are two halves of one control, the claim and the test. And notice the symmetry with the periodic world: there, the count exists but the claim does not, so the comparison is impossible from the other side. Only a business holding BOTH — a running record and an independent count — can give a loss a size and a name, and giving losses a size and a name is what stock control means.
