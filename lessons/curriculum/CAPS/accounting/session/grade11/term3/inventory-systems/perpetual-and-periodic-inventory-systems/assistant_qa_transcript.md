**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: if the periodic system can't detect theft, why is it legal at all? Surely every business should be forced to use the perpetual system.

**Tutor:** Legality follows from what the law actually requires, which is fair presentation of results — not a particular bookkeeping method. Both systems, correctly applied, produce a true cost of sales and a true gross profit for the period; they differ in what they can tell you DURING the period and in what they can detect. And the periodic system's simplicity is not laziness in every case: for a trader selling thousands of low-value, hard-to-individuate items without a computerised till — loose vegetables, single sweets, hardware sold by the handful — the cost of maintaining per-item cost records would exceed the value of the information, and a business is entitled to make that trade. What the law does require, and what auditors press on, is that the business take reasonable care over its assets — so a periodic-system business is expected to compensate with physical controls: restricted storerooms, checked deliveries, separated duties, and more frequent counts. The system choice is free; the responsibility for control is not.

***

**Bianca:** Next: in the periodic formula we subtract drawings of stock — but the owner's taking of stock isn't a purchase or a sale. Why does it appear in a cost of sales calculation at all?

**Tutor:** Because the formula is a story about a PILE, not about transactions — and anything that left the pile without being sold must be removed, or the arithmetic blames the customers for it. Trace it: the pile is built from opening stock plus everything bought; the closing count tells you what remains; the difference is assumed to have been sold. Now suppose the owner took R6 000 of stock home. Those goods are gone from the shelf and absent from the count, so unless you subtract them explicitly, the formula folds them into cost of sales — meaning the business reports having spent R6 000 of stock earning revenue it never earned, understating gross profit and quietly hiding the owner's drawings inside trading costs. Subtracting them keeps two things straight at once: cost of sales measures only goods that produced sales, and the drawings appear where they belong, against the owner's equity. It is the same discipline as the business entity rule you learned in grade 10 — the owner's household consumption is never the business's expense.

**Bianca:** This one puzzled a few of us: the lesson says an unexplained gap between achieved gross profit and the policy mark-up is a fingerprint of loss. How would that actually show up in numbers?

**Tutor:** Work a small example and it becomes vivid. Suppose the policy is a fifty percent mark-up on cost, and the year's cost of sales is R505 000. If everything sold at policy, sales should be R757 500 and gross profit R252 500 — a gross profit percentage on cost of fifty percent, by construction. Now suppose the actual sales figure is R700 000, giving gross profit of R195 000 — about 38,6 percent on cost. Something has consumed roughly eleven percentage points of expected margin. The candidate lists the possible doors: genuine markdowns and sales the business chose; damaged or expired goods written down; pricing errors at the till; recording errors — and, when those are closed, stock that left without being paid for. Notice what makes the calculation powerful: it works even in the periodic system, where no deficit figure exists, because the mark-up policy supplies the benchmark the missing stock record cannot. That is precisely why examiners pair mark-up analysis with stock questions, and why a shopkeeper who knows their own expected gross profit percentage has a theft detector even without a notebook.

**Bianca:** Last one: how do businesses actually count stock — do supermarkets really close and count everything? It sounds impossible.

**Tutor:** Some still do, and it is exactly as heavy as it sounds: a full annual count means closing or trading through the night, with teams counting section by section, each count independently checked by a second person, sheets pre-numbered so none goes missing, and stock movement frozen while the count runs. But most large businesses have moved to CYCLE COUNTING, and it is the better answer to your question: instead of one enormous count, sections are counted in rotation through the year — the high-value or fast-moving lines often, the slow lines less so — so every item is counted at least once annually while the shop never closes, and a discrepancy surfaces within weeks rather than after twelve months. Two disciplines make either method trustworthy. First, independence: the counters are not the people who control the storeroom, because a person counting their own responsibility has a reason to see what should be there. Second, investigation: a difference is not merely written up as a deficit — it is traced, since the number's value lies in what it reveals, not in the entry it produces. And the internal auditor's classic test is to attend unannounced and independently re-count a sample — which checks the count itself, not just the stock.
