# Part 1 — Expert

Grade 10 met the cash budget as a concept; grade 11 builds it properly, alongside its companion — the PROJECTED STATEMENT OF COMPREHENSIVE INCOME. The two documents forecast the same months and disagree constantly, and understanding WHY they disagree is the heart of this topic. Today: the two documents distinguished; the debtors' collection schedule and the creditors' payment schedule that feed the cash budget; a full worked quarter; the variance analysis that makes budgets useful after the fact; and the ethics and internal control the syllabus attaches to projections.

## Subtopic: Two Documents, Two Questions

Both documents look forward; they answer different questions, and every difference between them follows from that.

The PROJECTED STATEMENT OF COMPREHENSIVE INCOME forecasts PROFIT: expected income earned against expected expenses incurred, month by month, on the ACCRUAL basis. A credit sale enters in the month the goods are sold, whether or not the debtor has paid. Depreciation appears, because the asset is being consumed. Bad debts appear, because the loss is expected.

The CASH BUDGET forecasts BANK BALANCE: expected receipts against expected payments, on the CASH basis. A credit sale enters only when the debtor PAYS — often a month or two later. Depreciation NEVER appears, because no money moves. Bad debts never appear as a payment; they show up instead as cash that simply never arrives.

And each document contains items the other refuses. Cash budget only: the owner's capital contribution, drawings, loan receipts and repayments of loan capital, the purchase of fixed assets, and VAT paid over — all real money movements that are not income or expenses. Projected income statement only: depreciation, bad debts, and the accrual portions of expenses — costs that are real but not cash this month.

The classification test, applied to every item in an examination question: does it move MONEY this month — cash budget; does it earn income or consume value this month — projected income statement; does it do both — both documents, though possibly in different months. Learners who ask that question of each line score; learners who copy figures across score once and then bleed.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the two documents distinguished, and items classified into one, the other, or both.

## Subtopic: The Debtors' Collection Schedule

The cash budget's hardest input is the money debtors will actually pay, and it is computed in a schedule before it reaches the budget.

The terms, from a typical question: sales are 40 percent for cash and 60 percent on credit; debtors settle 50 percent in the month AFTER the sale — taking a 5 percent settlement discount — 30 percent in the second month after, and 15 percent in the third month; the remaining 5 percent is written off as bad debts.

Build the schedule for a quarter, with sales of R200 000 in January, R250 000 in February and R300 000 in March, and December sales of R180 000 behind them.

Take March's receipts from credit sales. Credit sales are 60 percent of each month's total: December R108 000, January R120 000, February R150 000. In March the business collects: 50 percent of FEBRUARY's credit sales, less the discount — 50 percent of 150 000 is 75 000, less 5 percent, giving R71 250; plus 30 percent of JANUARY's credit sales — 30 percent of 120 000 — R36 000; plus 15 percent of DECEMBER's credit sales — 15 percent of 108 000 — R16 200. March's collection from debtors: R123 450. Add March's cash sales — 40 percent of 300 000 — R120 000, and the month's receipts from trading are R243 450.

Three disciplines the marker looks for. One: the discount applies ONLY to the portion that earns it — the 50 percent paying in the first month — and it is deducted from that portion alone. Two: each month's collection draws on THREE different months' sales, so the schedule must be laid out with sales down the side and collection months across, or lines get crossed. Three: the 5 percent written off never appears in the cash budget at all; it appears in the projected income statement as bad debts. The same 5 percent, two documents, two treatments — exactly the split this topic exists to teach.

The CREDITORS' PAYMENT SCHEDULE works identically in mirror: stock purchased on credit is paid according to the supplier's terms — say 60 percent in the month after purchase and 40 percent in the second month, with a discount for early settlement — and only the payment reaches the cash budget, while the PURCHASE reaches the projected income statement through cost of sales when the goods are sold.

Stop for this section's questions now — collection schedules built line by line, the discount applied to its own portion only, and the bad-debt percentage sent to the correct document.

## Subtopic: Building the Cash Budget and the Projected Income Statement

Now assemble both documents for the same quarter, and watch them diverge.

The CASH BUDGET's fixed skeleton, month by month: RECEIPTS — cash sales, collections from debtors, rent income received, capital contributed, loans received, proceeds on sale of assets. Then PAYMENTS — payments to creditors, cash purchases, salaries and wages, rent paid, other operating payments actually made, drawings, asset purchases, loan repayments, interest paid. Then the arithmetic: receipts less payments gives the month's SURPLUS or DEFICIT; plus the OPENING bank balance gives the CLOSING balance, which becomes the next month's opening. Every column repeats that chain, and a closing balance that turns negative is a forecast overdraft — the single most useful warning a business can receive.

The PROJECTED INCOME STATEMENT for the same quarter runs the familiar format: sales — the FULL sales figure, cash and credit alike, in the month of sale — less cost of sales, giving gross profit; plus other operating income; less operating expenses, including depreciation and bad debts and the full accrued amounts of expenses; giving operating profit; less interest expense, giving net profit.

Now the divergence that examiners test with a discussion question: a business can forecast a healthy PROFIT and a dangerous cash DEFICIT in the same month. How? Sales made on credit in March earn profit in March but arrive as cash in April, May and June; meanwhile the stock for those sales was paid for in February, and an asset was bought for cash in March. Profit says the business is trading well; the bank balance says it cannot pay next week's wages. Both are true. The correct answer to such a question names the timing difference and the specific items causing it — never merely "one is cash and one is profit".

The questions on this section are in front of you now — both documents built for the same month, and the profitable-but-cash-strapped scenario explained with named items.

## Subtopic: Variances, Ethics and Internal Control

A budget's second life begins when the month it forecast is over. VARIANCE ANALYSIS compares the BUDGETED figure with the ACTUAL, item by item, and asks why they differ.

State a variance properly: the item, the two figures, the dfference in rand and as a percentage, and whether it is FAVOURABLE — actual income above budget or actual expense below — or UNFAVOURABLE. Then the interpretation, which is where the marks concentrate. An unfavourable sales variance may mean weaker demand, lost customers, a competitor's opening, or an over-optimistic budget. An unfavourable expense variance may mean price increases, waste, theft, or an underestimate. And note the trap: a FAVOURABLE expense variance is not automatically good news — advertising spent far below budget may explain the sales shortfall standing next to it, and maintenance not done becomes next year's breakdown. Read variances together, never one at a time.

The ETHICS the syllabus attaches. Budgets are forecasts, so they can be manipulated: a manager who inflates projected sales makes a business look financeable to a bank that would otherwise decline; one who pads expense budgets creates slack to hide overspending later; and a business that presents a projected income statement it knows to be unachievable, to obtain a loan, is misrepresenting — a fraud on the lender. There is also the padding problem inside organisations: departments asking for more than they need because they expect to be cut, which corrupts the whole allocation.

The INTERNAL CONTROLS: budgets prepared with the people responsible for delivering them, not imposed; assumptions written down and defensible — the collection percentages, the price increases, the growth rate — so they can be tested; approval by senior management or the owner; monthly comparison of actual to budget with explanations required for material variances; and authorisation limits so that spending beyond budget requires a decision rather than a habit. The INTERNAL AUDIT tests: are the budget's assumptions consistent with past actual performance; were material variances investigated and explained; and did anyone spend outside the approved budget without authorisation?

The through-line: a budget nobody compares to reality is a wish, and a budget deliberately built wrong is a lie. What makes it a control is the comparison afterwards.

The final questions of this part are with you now: variances computed and classified, read in combination, and the ethical scenarios matched to their controls.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same two documents from the kitchen table on the twenty-fifth — because every household that has ever waited for a payment it was owed already understands the difference between being owed money and having money.

## Subtopic: Owed Money Is Not Money

Your cousin does hair. In March she does forty heads and the work is worth R8 000 — a good month, and if you ask her how the month went, she says: strong. But of those forty clients, half said "I'll pay you next month". So on the thirty-first of March she has R4 000 in her hand and R4 000 in promises.

Now watch what those two numbers do. If you ask "did she EARN well in March?" — yes: R8 000 of hair was done, and the work is finished. If you ask "can she buy stock on the first of April?" — only R4 000 says yes. The first question is about PROFIT. The second is about CASH. Same month, two honest answers, and they disagree.

That is the whole reason a business keeps two forecasts. One says what the months will EARN — the projected income statement. The other says what will actually be in the bank — the cash budget. They forecast the same months and they never quite match, because earning and paying keep different calendars, exactly as you learned when you first met accruals.

Two quick rules that follow, and they catch most of the marks. Anything that moves money goes in the cash budget — including things that are not "profit or loss" at all: the owner taking drawings, buying a bakkie, repaying a loan. And anything that eats value without moving money goes only in the profit forecast — depreciation, mainly: the bakkie is wearing out this month, but no rand left the account for that wearing.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the two forecasts are coming to you right now: earned or received, and which document each item belongs to.

## Subtopic: Working Out What Will Actually Arrive

The hardest thing to forecast is money owed to you — because you're guessing when people will pay. So businesses use a schedule, and it is easier than it sounds if you build it backwards.

Say the shop's rules are: forty out of every hundred rand of sales is cash at the till; sixty is on credit. And of that credit, half comes in the very next month — with a small discount for paying promptly — thirty percent the month after that, fifteen percent the month after THAT, and five percent never comes at all.

Now ask: in March, whose money is arriving? Not March's credit sales — those customers only start paying in April. Arriving in March is: half of February's credit sales, a third-ish of January's, a sliver of December's. Three different months' sales, all landing in one month's bank account. That's why you draw it as a grid — sales down the side, arrival months across — instead of trying to hold it in your head.

Do it with numbers. Credit sales were R108 000 in December, R120 000 in January, R150 000 in February. In March: half of February's R150 000 is R75 000, less the five percent discount those prompt payers earn — R71 250. Thirty percent of January's R120 000 — R36 000. Fifteen percent of December's R108 000 — R16 200. Together, R123 450 arriving from debtors. Add March's own cash sales, and that's what the bank actually sees.

Two things people get wrong. The discount comes off ONLY the group that earns it — the prompt payers — not off everything. And the five percent that never comes? It never appears in the cash budget at all; you can't budget to receive money you'll never get. It shows up in the profit forecast instead, as bad debts. Same five percent, two documents, two different treatments.

Your questions for this part are up now: build a collection grid, apply the discount to its own group, and send the bad-debt slice to the right place.

## Subtopic: Profitable and Broke at the Same Time

Here is the situation that closes businesses that were doing well — and once you can explain it, you understand this topic.

A shop has its best month ever: R300 000 of sales, most of it on credit to good customers who will pay in April and May. Real profit, honestly earned. But look at March's bank account: the stock for those sales was paid for in February; wages went out in March; the owner bought a delivery bakkie for cash; and the customers who owe R180 000 haven't paid yet. Money in: thin. Money out: heavy. The account runs dry in a month the business was WINNING.

That is why the cash budget matters more, week to week, than the profit forecast. Profit tells you whether the business deserves to survive. Cash tells you whether it will. A business can survive a bad profit month; it cannot survive a payday with an empty account.

And because the budget shows this in ADVANCE, the owner has cheap options: delay the bakkie by two months; arrange an overdraft now, calmly, while the bank sees a strong business; tighten the collection terms; or negotiate longer payment with the supplier. Every one of those is easy in January and desperate in March.

Then, when March is over, the budget does its second job: compare what you PREDICTED with what actually happened, line by line. Sales R30 000 under budget — why? Wages R5 000 over — why? And here's the subtle one worth remembering: an expense coming in UNDER budget isn't automatically good news. If advertising was half of what you planned, look at the sales line right next to it — that "saving" may have cost you the sales. Read the lines together, not one at a time.

Last thing, and it's the honest one: because a budget is a guess about the future, it can be dressed up. Inflate the sales forecast and a bank might lend money it shouldn't. Pad the expenses and there's room to hide overspending later. That's why real budgets are built with the people who must deliver them, why the assumptions get written down where anyone can test them, and why the actuals get compared every month. A budget nobody checks is a wish. A budget built wrong on purpose is a lie.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: explain profitable-but-broke with the actual items causing it, and read a set of variances together rather than singly. Master this and you can advise a real business — which is more than most people who run them can do.
