**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First: if the cash budget is the one that shows whether you can pay the wages, why bother with the projected income statement at all? Surely cash is the one that matters.

**Tutor:** Cash decides whether you survive the month; profit decides whether the business is worth surviving in. Run only a cash budget and you can be badly deceived in both directions. A business can show healthy cash while quietly making losses — selling stock below cost clears money into the bank fast, and the account looks wonderful right up to the point the business runs out of goods and capital. Conversely, a business can look cash-starved during exactly the period it is doing best, because growth consumes cash: more sales mean more stock bought and more credit extended before any of it returns. The profit forecast is what tells you which of those two stories you are in — and it drives the decisions cash cannot inform: pricing, whether a product line is worth keeping, whether the expense structure is sustainable. Lenders ask for both for that reason: the cash budget shows they will be repaid on schedule, the projected income statement shows there is a business behind the schedule.

**Bianca:** Next: in the collection schedule, why does a business give a discount for early payment at all? It's just giving away money it was owed.

**Tutor:** It is buying two things, and whether the purchase is wise is an arithmetic question you can actually answer. The first thing it buys is TIME — money in hand now rather than in sixty days, which funds this month's stock without an overdraft and removes the interest that overdraft would cost. The second is CERTAINTY — a debtor who pays promptly never becomes the five percent written off, so the discount also buys a reduction in bad debts. Now the arithmetic: a five percent discount for paying a month early is, annualised, an extremely expensive form of finance — roughly sixty percent a year on the money brought forward. So the honest test is comparison: if the business's overdraft costs far less than that, it should not be offering the discount; if the business cannot get an overdraft at all, or its bad-debt experience is severe, the discount may be cheap protection. In an exam, that comparison — the discount's annualised cost against the cost of alternative finance — is precisely the analysis that earns the higher marks rather than a general comment about encouraging payment.

**Bianca:** This one confused a few of us: cash sales appear in the cash budget in the month of sale AND in the profit forecast in the same month. So what exactly is different between the two documents for a cash-only business?

**Tutor:** For a genuinely cash-only business, the documents converge substantially — and noticing that is a real insight, because it shows you understand what drives the divergence. But even then they differ in three places. First, non-cash expenses: depreciation still appears in the profit forecast and never in the cash budget, so profit is lower than the cash movement suggests. Second, items that move money without being income or expense: the owner's drawings, a bakkie bought for cash, a loan received or repaid — all in the cash budget, none in the profit forecast. Third, timing of the expenses themselves: stock bought and paid for in February but sold in March hits the cash budget in February and the profit forecast in March, so even with cash sales the two documents disagree by a month on the cost side. What a cash-only business does escape is the collection schedule — the largest single source of divergence — which is exactly why small traders who sell only for cash find budgeting simpler than those who grant credit.

**Bianca:** Last one: how does anyone know the collection percentages in the first place? They look invented.

**Tutor:** They are estimated, not invented, and the estimate has a method. The business looks at its own PAST behaviour: take last year's credit sales month by month, then track when the money actually arrived — the age analysis of debtors is exactly this record, showing how much of each month's sales was still outstanding after thirty, sixty and ninety days. From that history you derive the pattern: roughly half within a month, roughly a third by the second, and so on, with the residue that never came giving the bad-debt percentage. Then judgement adjusts the history for what is changing: tighter credit vetting should improve it; a recession or a large new customer with slow payment habits will worsen it. Two disciplines matter for the exam and for practice. First, WRITE THE ASSUMPTION DOWN — a budget whose collection percentages are undocumented cannot be defended or corrected. Second, review it against actual collections each month: if the real pattern is running slower than assumed, the budget must be redrawn immediately, because every month's closing balance downstream is wrong. A collection assumption is not a decoration; it is the single number the whole cash forecast pivots on.
