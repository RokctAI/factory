**Bianca:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: if the cash budget is the document that shows whether the wages can be paid, why prepare a projected income statement at all? Cash seems like the only thing that matters.

**Tutor:** Cash decides whether the business survives the month; profit decides whether the business is worth keeping alive. Run only a cash budget and you can be fooled in both directions. A business can show a comfortable bank balance while quietly trading at a loss — dumping stock below cost fills the account quickly, and everything looks fine until the stock and the capital are gone. The reverse is just as common: a business can look cash-starved in exactly the period it is growing fastest, because growth eats cash — more stock bought, more credit extended, all before the money returns. The profit forecast is what tells you which of those stories you are living, and it drives the decisions cash cannot answer: pricing, whether a product line earns its keep, whether the expense structure is sustainable. That is why a lender asks for both documents: the cash budget shows the repayments can be met on schedule, and the projected income statement shows there is a real business standing behind the schedule.

**Bianca:** Next: in the collection schedule, why would a business offer a settlement discount at all? It's literally handing back money it is owed.

**Tutor:** It is buying two things, and whether the price is fair is arithmetic, not opinion. The first purchase is TIME — money in the account this month instead of in sixty days, which pays for this month's stock without an overdraft and saves the interest that overdraft would have cost. The second purchase is CERTAINTY — a debtor who settles promptly never drifts into the five percent that gets written off, so the discount also shrinks bad debts. Now the arithmetic: a five percent discount for paying one month sooner is, converted to a yearly rate, an extremely expensive way to raise money — in the region of sixty percent a year on the amount brought forward. So the honest test is a comparison: if the business can borrow on overdraft far more cheaply than that, the discount is a bad bargain; if the business cannot borrow at all, or its bad-debt history is ugly, the discount may be cheap insurance. In an exam answer, making that comparison — the discount's annualised cost against the cost of the alternative finance — is what lifts you above the general comment that discounts encourage early payment.

**Bianca:** This one confused a few of us: cash sales sit in the cash budget in the month of sale AND in the profit forecast in the same month. So for a business that sells only for cash, what is actually left to disagree about?

**Tutor:** Noticing that convergence is a genuine insight — it shows you understand what creates the divergence in the first place. But even a pure cash seller's two documents differ in three places. First, non-cash expenses: depreciation still sits in the profit forecast and never in the cash budget, so the profit figure runs below the cash movement. Second, movements that are not income or expenses: the owner's drawings, a scooter bought for cash, a loan received or repaid — all of them shape the bank balance and none of them touch profit. Third, timing on the cost side: stock bought and paid for in May but sold in June hits the cash budget in May and the profit forecast in June, so even with all-cash sales the documents disagree by a month about cost. What the cash-only trader escapes is the debtors' collection schedule — the biggest single source of disagreement — which is why small cash traders find budgeting so much simpler than businesses that grant credit.

**Bianca:** Last one: where do the collection percentages actually come from? Sixty, twenty-five, ten — they look plucked from the air.

**Tutor:** They are estimated from evidence, and the evidence is the business's own history. Take last year's credit sales month by month and trace when the money actually arrived — the debtors' age analysis is precisely that record, showing how much of each month's sales was still outstanding after thirty, sixty and ninety days. The pattern falls out of it: roughly sixty percent inside a month, a quarter by the second month, and so on, with the residue that never arrived becoming the bad-debt percentage. Then judgement adjusts history for whatever is changing: stricter credit checks should improve the pattern; a downturn, or one big slow-paying customer, will worsen it. Two disciplines make the estimate respectable. First, write the assumption down — a collection percentage that is undocumented can be neither defended nor corrected. Second, test it against actual collections every month: if payments are running slower than assumed, every closing balance downstream is wrong and the budget must be redrawn at once. The collection assumption is not decoration; it is the hinge the entire cash forecast swings on.
