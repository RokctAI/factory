**Thandi:** Class questions! The one that came up most: why do we bother with an Accumulated Depreciation account at all? Why not just credit Vehicles directly and make the account smaller each year?

**Tutor:** Because crediting Vehicles would destroy information the books are duty-bound to keep. Historical cost anchors the Vehicles account at what was actually paid — one hundred and fifty thousand, a verifiable fact backed by an invoice. The wearing is a different kind of fact: an estimate, recalculated yearly. Keep them in separate accounts and the statements can show all three truths at once — cost, total wear, carrying value — and any reader can judge the fleet's age at a glance: a bakkie at one-fifty with ninety thousand accumulated is an old bakkie; the same carrying value with ten thousand accumulated is nearly new. Merge them into one shrinking number and that story vanishes. One account for the fact, one for the estimate, and the difference tells the truth of today.

***

**Thandi:** Next: on diminishing balance the carrying value never actually reaches zero — you said it slides toward zero forever. Doesn't that mean the books carry the bakkie for eternity?

**Tutor:** Mathematically yes, practically no — and both halves teach something. Twenty percent off whatever remains always leaves eighty percent standing, so the sequence shrinks without dying: seventy-six thousand eight hundred, then sixty-one thousand four hundred and forty, smaller and smaller forever. In practice, three exits close the story. The asset is sold or scrapped, and its page closes with the disposal. Or the business judges the remaining value immaterial and writes the last sliver down. Or the asset simply keeps serving, carried at a token value — often one rand — precisely so the register never forgets a machine that still physically exists. The mathematics is a curve that never lands; the bookkeeping always lands it deliberately. Knowing the difference is exactly the kind of understanding a discussion question rewards.

***

**Thandi:** A calculation one: for the computer we multiplied by four over twelve. But it was bought on the first of November — why four months and not the eight months from March to November?

**Tutor:** Because the fraction counts the months the asset SERVED, not the months it missed. Walk the year: it begins on the first of March and ends on the last of February. The computer arrived on the first of November and worked November, December, January, February — count them on your fingers: four. The eight months from March to October are months the business did not own it, and charging wear for them would invent an expense that never happened. So the rule reads: from the month of purchase to the end of the financial year. And check the direction of your fraction with common sense — an asset bought late in the year must carry a SMALL charge. If your fraction gives the bigger share of the year to a November arrival, you counted the wrong side of the purchase date.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: if both methods are honest, could a business just pick whichever gives the nicer profit this year — and switch again next year?

**Tutor:** No — and the reason is consistency, one of the quiet GAAP principles that stops honest tools becoming dishonest tricks. The choice of method is made once, to fit how the asset genuinely serves: even servers on straight-line, front-loaded workers on diminishing balance. After that the method stays, year after year, so that this year's profit can be compared fairly with last year's. Flip methods whenever it flatters the figures and the comparison dies — a rising profit could be real trading or just a change of slicing, and no reader could tell. A change is permitted only when the real pattern of use has changed, and then it must be disclosed so every reader knows the ground moved. The methods are both honest; it is the switching for convenience that lies.
