# Part 1 — Expert

The accounting cycle's daily and monthly clocks have run all year; today the yearly clock strikes. Year-end procedures are the bridge between a trial balance and the financial statements — and the bridge has a fixed sequence: the pre-adjustment trial balance, the adjustments, the post-adjustment trial balance, the final accounts, and only then the statements. Today we walk that sequence at Thabo's Trading, apply the GAAP principles that justify each step, and compute the year's profit formally. Depreciation, the largest adjustment, gets the next lesson to itself.

## Subtopic: The Cycle Reaches Year End

Place yourself in the cycle. Twelve months of documents, journals, postings and monthly trial balances lie behind; the financial statements — the cycle's destination — lie ahead. Between them stands a problem: the ledger's balances, honest as they are, describe the year AS RECORDED, and recording follows documents. Some truths at year end have no document yet: stationery bought and recorded as an expense is partly unused on the shelf; stock has quietly gone missing without a slip to announce it. The books must be brought level with REALITY before they may report.

That is what ADJUSTMENTS are: year-end corrections that align recorded figures with actual fact. And the trial balance splits into two versions around them: the PRE-ADJUSTMENT trial balance — the ledger as the year's routine left it — and the POST-ADJUSTMENT trial balance, drawn after the adjustments, the version the final accounts are built from.

The authority for all of this is GAAP. The MATCHING principle demands that this year's income statement carry this year's incomes and expenses — no more, no less; that is the engine behind every adjustment. PRUDENCE demands losses be recognised the moment they are discovered, not when convenient. GOING CONCERN lets us hold assets at their useful values because the business will continue. HISTORICAL COST keeps recorded values anchored to actual transaction prices. The year-end run is not tradition; it is these principles, executed in order.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the two trial balances, why adjustments exist, and which principle authorises them. Name the principle with the procedure, every time.

## Subtopic: Adjustments, Introduced

Grade 10 introduces the family with two clean members; meet both at Thabo's Trading, year ending 28 February.

First: CONSUMABLE STORES ON HAND. The Stationery account shows R1 000 — everything bought during the year, recorded as an expense when purchased. But the year-end cupboard holds R200 of unused paper and pens. Did the business USE R1 000 of stationery this year? No — it used R800. The matching principle objects to the ledger's claim. The adjustment: transfer R200 out of the expense — credit Stationery — and into an asset, Consumable Stores on Hand — debit R200. The expense reports what was consumed; the asset carries what remains into next year, where it will be used and expensed.

Second: the TRADING STOCK DEFICIT. The perpetual inventory system has priced every movement all year, and the Trading Stock account claims R2 400 on hand. The physical stocktake counts R2 250. The books cannot argue with the shelf: R150 of stock is gone — theft, breakage, error — and prudence demands the loss be recognised now. The adjustment: credit Trading Stock R150 to bring the asset down to reality, and debit a new expense, Trading Stock Deficit, R150. The count is the fact; the account adjusts to it.

After the adjustments, draw the post-adjustment trial balance: Stationery now R800, Consumable Stores on Hand R200, Trading Stock R2 250, Trading Stock Deficit R150 — and the columns still level, because every adjustment was itself a double entry. Adjusted, but still balanced: that is the state from which final accounts may be prepared.

Stop for this section's questions now — both adjustments: the reasoning, the double entry, and the effect on each account. Let the matching and prudence principles do the arguing.

## Subtopic: The Final Accounts — Trading, and Profit and Loss

Now profit is computed INSIDE the ledger, through two closing accounts — the final accounts.

First, the TRADING ACCOUNT, which isolates the trading result. Close Sales — the year's R100 000 — into it: debit Sales, credit Trading. Close Cost of Sales — R80 000 — into it: credit Cost of Sales, debit Trading. The Trading account now holds the year's buying-and-selling story in one place, and its balance — credit R20 000 — is the GROSS PROFIT: what the mark-up earned before any running cost. Check it against the policy: mark-up of twenty-five percent on a cost of R80 000 is exactly R20 000; the final accounts confirm what the pricing promised.

Second, the PROFIT AND LOSS ACCOUNT, which gathers everything else. The gross profit transfers in — debit Trading, credit Profit and Loss R20 000. The other incomes join it: Rent Income, R6 000, closed off to the credit side — R26 000 now assembled. Then every operating expense closes to the debit side: Salaries and Wages R12 000, Telephone R1 200, Stationery — the adjusted R800 — Bank Charges R350, and the Trading Stock Deficit R150. Total expenses: R14 500.

The balance of the Profit and Loss account — credit R11 500 — is the NET PROFIT: the year's true earning after every cost. And its destination follows from term one's oldest principle: the profit belongs to the owner, so it transfers to Capital — debit Profit and Loss, credit Capital R11 500. The nominal accounts now stand empty, ready for the new year; the ledger has computed, formally and inside itself, the number the owner has waited twelve months to see.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: closing transfers, both final accounts, and the two profit figures — build each by the transfers, not by shortcut arithmetic.

## Subtopic: The Year-End Run, In Order

Assemble the whole procedure as the examination expects it — a fixed order, with a reason at each step.

One: the PRE-ADJUSTMENT trial balance — prove the year's routine bookkeeping balances before touching anything. Two: the ADJUSTMENTS — journalised in the General Journal with narrations, each aligning a balance with year-end fact, each authorised by a GAAP principle. Three: the POST-ADJUSTMENT trial balance — prove the adjustments kept the equality. Four: the FINAL ACCOUNTS — Trading for the gross profit, Profit and Loss for the net, closing every nominal account on the way. Five: the profit to CAPITAL, where it belongs. Six — next stage of the cycle — the financial statements, reporting what the final accounts computed.

Why the strict order? Each step certifies the one after it. Adjust before proving the books balance, and an old error hides among the adjustments. Prepare final accounts before adjusting, and the profit is wrong by every unadjusted rand — a gross profit built on unfixed stationery and uncounted stock. The order is the control.

Exam technique, three habits. First, every adjustment is a full double entry — name both accounts, both directions; half-adjustments are the topic's classic lost mark. Second, track each adjustment's DOWNSTREAM effect: the R200 stationery adjustment changes the expense in Profit and Loss AND places an asset in the statements — one fact, two appearances. Third, when a question names a procedure, name its principle: matching for the stationery, prudence for the deficit, going concern for why assets carry forward at all. The pairing of step and principle is where the discussion marks live.

The final questions of this part are with you now: the run in order, reasons attached, effects traced end to end.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the whole year-end, from the stoep — because report card day comes for shops too, and the tidying before it is something everyone has done the night before an inspection.

## Subtopic: The Shop's Report Card Day

All year, Thabo's shop has been writing its own diary: every sale, every payment, every promise, day by day, month by month. Now comes the day the diary must become a REPORT CARD — one page that answers the owner's two oldest questions: did the shop make money this year, and how much?

But here is the catch every learner should feel in their bones: you cannot photocopy a diary and call it a report card. The diary records what HAPPENED AS IT WAS WRITTEN — and by year end, a few of its lines have quietly drifted away from the truth. The stationery page says "R1 000 spent on pens and paper" — but look in the cupboard: R200 of it is still sitting there, unused. The stock page claims R2 400 of goods — but count the shelf: R2 250. The diary isn't lying; it just doesn't know what it hasn't been told.

So year-end has two jobs, in strict order: first TIDY the diary until it matches the real shop — that's the adjustments; then SQUEEZE the tidied diary into the report card — that's the final accounts. Tidy first, squeeze second. Squeeze an untidy diary and the report card carries every untidiness straight to the owner.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on report card day are coming to you right now: what the diary gets wrong, and which job comes first.

## Subtopic: Tidying the Room Before the Photo

Walk through the two tidyings like the night before the inspection photo.

Tidying one: the stationery cupboard. The diary wrote "R1 000, all used up" — but there on the shelf sit R200 of untouched pens and paper. So split the truth in two: R800 truly USED this year — that stays as the year's expense; R200 still WAITING — that becomes a small asset, a parcel handed over the year-end line to next year, which will use it and pay for it in its own report. This year's card must carry only this year's using. That one sentence is the famous "matching" rule wearing home clothes.

Tidying two: the shelf count. The stock page claims R2 400. Thabo counts: R2 250. The shelf wins — always. The books must come down R150 to match reality, and the missing R150 gets written on the report card as a loss with its own name: trading stock deficit. Notice the discipline: not "maybe it'll turn up", not "we'll check next year" — the loss is admitted NOW, the moment it's discovered. Bad news goes on the card immediately; good news waits to be proven. That's the "prudence" rule, and it is why report cards can be trusted.

After both tidyings, one quick re-check that the diary still balances — the tidied version — and the room is ready for its photo.

Your questions for this part are up now: two tidyings — what moves, where it goes, and why the shelf always wins the argument.

## Subtopic: Two Sieves and the Owner's Jar

Now the squeeze: the whole tidied year poured through two sieves, one after the other.

Sieve one catches only the BUYING-AND-SELLING story. In goes the year's sales: R100 000. In goes what those goods had cost the shop: R80 000. What stays in the sieve is R20 000 — the GROSS profit: the reward for buying at one price and selling at another, before a single light bill is paid. And notice — the mark-up promised exactly this: a quarter on top of R80 000 is R20 000. The sieve confirms the pricing kept its word.

Sieve two catches the RUNNING of the shop. Pour in the R20 000 from sieve one, add the rent income the back room earned — R6 000 — so R26 000 of earning sits in the sieve. Now the year's costs drain through: salaries R12 000, telephone R1 200, the tidied stationery R800, bank charges R350, and the admitted stock loss R150 — R14 500 of running costs. What remains is R11 500 — the NET profit: what the year TRULY earned after everything.

And where does that R11 500 go? Into the owner's jar — Capital — because the profit was always his; the shop was just working it up all year. The year's pages are now empty, clean for the new year, and the number in the jar is the one line the report card exists to say.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: pour a year through both sieves yourself — what each catches, what each lets through, and whose jar the last number lands in.
