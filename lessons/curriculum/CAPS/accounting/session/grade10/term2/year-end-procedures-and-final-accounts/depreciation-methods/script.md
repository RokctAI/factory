# Part 1 — Expert

Last lesson introduced adjustments with stationery and stock; today comes the largest and most examined adjustment of them all: depreciation. Fixed assets work for years, and every working year wears them down — the books must say so. By the end of this session you will calculate depreciation on both methods — straight-line and diminishing balance — handle an asset bought partway through the year, and maintain the asset register that keeps it all auditable. This calculation appears in every Accounting paper from now to matric; build it correctly once.

## Subtopic: Why Assets Lose Value in the Books

Thabo's Trading buys a delivery bakkie for R120 000 on the first of March — the first day of the financial year. The bakkie will work for years; it will also, from its first kilometre, be worth steadily less than it cost. Two GAAP principles insist the books record that. MATCHING: the bakkie earns income across many years, so its cost must be spread as an expense across those same years — charging all R120 000 to year one would crush one year with a cost that five years share. PRUDENCE: carrying an ageing vehicle at its shiny purchase price overstates what the business owns.

So accounting splits the story across three figures, and you must hold all three apart. COST PRICE: what the asset cost on purchase day — R120 000, anchored there permanently by the historical cost principle; the Vehicles account never moves. ACCUMULATED DEPRECIATION: the running total of all value written off since purchase — a NEGATIVE asset account standing beside Vehicles, growing each year. CARRYING VALUE: cost minus accumulated depreciation — what the asset is still worth in the books today.

The annual double entry, reasoned: the year's wear is an expense — debit Depreciation. But we do not credit Vehicles, because cost must stay anchored; we credit Accumulated Depreciation on Vehicles, the counter-account that gathers the wearing. Expense up, negative asset up, carrying value down — and the income statement carries its share of the bakkie's cost.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the three figures, the two principles, and the double entry. Keep cost, accumulated and carrying value strictly apart.

## Subtopic: Straight-Line — the Level Slice

Method one: STRAIGHT-LINE, also called the fixed instalment or cost-price method. The rule: the same percentage of the ORIGINAL COST, every year — a level slice of the asset's life.

The bakkie: R120 000 at twenty percent per annum on cost. Year one's depreciation: twenty percent of R120 000 — R24 000. Year two: twenty percent of R120 000 — R24 000 again. Year three: R24 000 again. The slice never changes, because its base — cost — never changes.

Now track the three figures down the years, because examiners ask for any of them at any date. End of year one: cost R120 000; accumulated R24 000; carrying value R96 000. End of year two: accumulated R48 000; carrying value R72 000. End of year three: accumulated R72 000; carrying value R48 000. See the shape: carrying value falls by the same R24 000 step, a straight line down — the method's name is its graph.

And the horizon: at twenty percent on cost, the slices consume the whole cost in five years. In practice the books stop just short — a carrying value of one rand is often left standing so the asset, still physically in use, never vanishes from the records entirely. An asset fully written off but still working is a signal the estimate was conservative, not an error.

When is straight-line the honest choice? When the asset serves roughly evenly across its life — office equipment, fittings, furniture. Even service, even slices: matching in its plainest form.

Stop for this section's questions now — level slices down the years: depreciation, accumulated and carrying value at any date demanded.

## Subtopic: Diminishing Balance, and Additions During the Year

Method two: DIMINISHING BALANCE — the same percentage, but on the CARRYING VALUE, the figure that shrinks each year. The base moves; so the slice shrinks.

Same bakkie, twenty percent on diminishing balance. Year one: twenty percent of R120 000 — R24 000; carrying value R96 000. Year two: twenty percent of NINETY-SIX thousand — R19 200; carrying value R76 800. Year three: twenty percent of R76 800 — R15 360; carrying value R61 440. Each year's charge is smaller than the last, and the carrying value approaches zero without ever arriving — the method never fully consumes the asset.

Why choose it? Because some assets genuinely serve this way: vehicles and machines give their best, hardest years first — new bakkie, big deliveries, small repairs; old bakkie, fewer trips, rising maintenance. Diminishing balance charges the big years big and the tired years small — matching, shaped to a machine's life. Contrast locked in: straight-line, constant base, constant charge; diminishing balance, shrinking base, shrinking charge. The examiner's favourite trap is asking for year TWO on diminishing balance and paying the learner who used R120 000 instead of R96 000 nothing for it.

Now the ADDITION DURING THE YEAR. On the first of September — six months into the financial year — Thabo buys a new printing machine for R18 000, depreciated straight-line at ten percent. A full year's charge would be R1 800 — but the machine only worked HALF this year. The rule: depreciation runs from the month of purchase, pro rata. Six months of service: R1 800 times six over twelve — R900. Every mid-year purchase gets this months-fraction, on either method, and the machine's first-year carrying value stands at R17 100.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — shrinking bases and part-year fractions: identify the base, count the months, then calculate.

## Subtopic: The Asset Register and Exam Technique

Every fixed asset the business owns gets its own page in the ASSET REGISTER — the detailed record standing behind the ledger's summary totals.

The page carries: description and identifying details — the bakkie's registration, the machine's serial number; date of purchase and cost price; the depreciation method and rate; then, year by year, the annual depreciation, the accumulated total, and the carrying value. One page, one asset, the whole life story — from purchase to the day it is sold or scrapped.

Why keep it? Control, first: the ledger shows ONE Vehicles figure and ONE accumulated figure for the whole fleet; the register shows each vehicle separately, and the register's totals must agree with the ledger's balances — the same summary-and-detail architecture as debtors control and its list, now applied to assets. Theft and loss surface the same way: an asset on the register that cannot be produced on inspection has a question to answer. Second, decisions: the register tells the owner each machine's age, remaining value and history — the facts behind replace-or-repair. Third, the audit trail: every depreciation figure in the statements can be traced to a page, a rate, a calculation.

Exam technique for the whole topic, five habits. One: name the method before calculating — the words "on cost" mean straight-line; "on carrying value" or "on diminishing balance" mean the shrinking base. Two: write the base figure down first, especially in later years. Three: for additions, count the months on your fingers and write the fraction — six over twelve — into the working. Four: keep the three figures labelled — depreciation for the YEAR, ACCUMULATED to date, CARRYING value — because papers ask for one and learners answer with another. Five: the depreciation charge is an adjustment like last lesson's — expense debited, accumulated credited, and both trial balances and the final accounts feel it downstream.

The final questions of this part are with you now: registers read, methods named, bases written, months counted — the full discipline.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same topic from the driveway — because everyone has watched a phone, a bicycle or a family car quietly lose its value, and depreciation is just the books admitting what every owner already knows.

## Subtopic: The Bakkie That Quietly Gets Cheaper

Think of the newest phone you know. Price on launch day: full and proud. One year later — still working perfectly — try to sell it: two-thirds of the price, maybe less. Nothing broke. Nobody crashed it. It just... got older, and the world quietly marked it down.

Thabo's new bakkie is the same story with bigger numbers: R120 000 on the first of March, and from its very first delivery it is worth a little less every month — not because anything went wrong, but because working wears things, and newer models keep arriving. Now here's the accounting question: should the books keep saying "bakkie: R120 000" for five years? That would be a polite lie — and the books don't lie, even politely.

So the books tell the truth in three numbers, and the trick of this whole topic is keeping them apart. What it COST — R120 000, written in ink, never changes; that's the receipt. What it has LOST so far — a running total that grows each year; that's the wear. What it's STILL WORTH in the books — cost minus the wear; that's the honest answer today. Receipt, wear, worth. Every question in this topic asks for one of those three — and half the lost marks come from answering with the wrong one.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the three numbers are coming to you right now: which is which, and which one never moves.

## Subtopic: Two Ways to Slice the Loss

The books must charge the bakkie's wearing to the years it works. There are two honest ways to slice it — picture a loaf of bread that must last the asset's life.

Way one: the SAME slice every year. Take the cost — R120 000 — and cut twenty percent of IT each year: R24 000, R24 000, R24 000, level slices until the loaf is gone in five years. That's straight-line: simple, steady, fair for things that serve steadily — desks, shelves, the office printer, which does roughly the same job in year four as in year one.

Way two: always a fifth of WHAT'S LEFT. First year: a fifth of R120 000 — R24 000, and R96 000 remains. Second year: a fifth of the REMAINING R96 000 — R19 200. Third year: a fifth of R76 800 — R15 360. Each slice smaller than the last, and the loaf never quite disappears. That's diminishing balance — and it's honest for machines and vehicles, because they give their biggest, hardest years FIRST: the new bakkie runs every delivery and barely visits the workshop; the old bakkie does short trips between repairs. Big work years carry big slices; tired years carry small ones.

The one trap between the two ways: in year two, which number do you slice? Straight-line slices the ORIGINAL loaf — always R120 000. Diminishing balance slices what's LEFT — R96 000, then R76 800. Say the base out loud before you cut, and the trap never catches you.

Your questions for this part are up now: slice both ways, year by year — and every time, name the number you're slicing before you slice it.

## Subtopic: Every Asset Gets Its Own Page

Last piece: the paperwork that keeps all this honest — the asset register, which is really just an ID book for everything the shop owns.

Open the bakkie's page: what it is, its registration, the day it arrived, what it cost, which slicing method it's on, and then — year by year — this year's wear, the wear so far, and what it's still worth. One page per asset, its whole life story in columns. The ledger upstairs holds only the totals — all vehicles in one line — but the register holds the FACES: this bakkie, that machine, each with its own numbers. Totals upstairs, faces downstairs, and the two must always agree — the same trick as the debtors list agreeing with its control account. And if a page says "printing machine, worth R17 100" and nobody can point to the machine? Then the register has just caught a thief — or at least started a very serious conversation.

And when something new arrives mid-year — the R18 000 machine that came on the first of September — its page starts honestly: it only worked six of the twelve months, so its first year's wear is only half a year's worth: R1 800 halved, R900. Assets are charged for the months they actually served, the way you'd only pay a worker from the day they started, not from January.

The register is also where the owner thinks: this bakkie is four years old, worth this much, costing that much in repairs — replace it or run it? The page holds the facts the decision needs.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: read a register page, slice both ways, count an arrival's months, and match the totals upstairs to the faces downstairs. Do that, and the biggest adjustment in the book is simply yours — and the whole year-end topic with it.
