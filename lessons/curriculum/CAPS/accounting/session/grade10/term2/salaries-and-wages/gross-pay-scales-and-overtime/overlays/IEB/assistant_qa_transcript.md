**Thandi:** Question round! The big confusion first: a few of us worked out the administrator's pay in her second year as R120 000 plus TWO increments. Why is it only one?

**Tutor:** Because increments are earned by COMPLETED years, and the counting trips almost everyone once. Picture her timeline. She is appointed and begins year one on the starting notch — one hundred and twenty thousand — and she stays on it for that whole first year; nobody climbs on the day they arrive. Complete year one, and the first increment lands: year two is served on one hundred and thirty-two thousand. So "in her second year" means exactly one completed year, exactly one notch. The safe habit: never count the year you are standing in — count the finished ones behind you. An employee in her third year has completed two; two increments; one hundred and forty-four thousand. Write the timeline out in an exam if you must — it costs ten seconds and saves the commonest error in the topic.

***

**Thandi:** Next: why does the working have to show the overtime rate on its own line? If someone calculates three hours of time and a half on R50 as R225 directly, isn't that just... correct?

**Tutor:** The figure is correct; the working is fragile — and payroll working is judged on both. Two reasons for the separate line. First, auditability: a payroll clerk's calculation must be checkable by someone else, line by line, and the rate line is where a checker instantly sees whether the right multiplier met the right base. When it is buried inside one multiplication, an error in the multiplier and an error in the hours look identical from outside. Second, safety under pressure: the learner who computes fifty times one comma five equals seventy-five, writes it down, and then multiplies by three has split one risky step into two safe ones. The discipline feels slower; it is actually faster, because it never needs redoing. Rate first, labelled, always — then the hours.

***

**Thandi:** Here's one from the debate at the back: overtime pays R75 or even R100 an hour — so isn't a worker better off chasing overtime than climbing a salary ladder?

**Tutor:** Compare what each one actually promises. Overtime is a premium on SOMETIMES: it pays beautifully per hour precisely because it is irregular, unsocial and never guaranteed — a quiet month can offer none at all, and a worker cannot budget a life on maybes. The ladder is a promise about ALWAYS: every completed year, the annual figure steps up, visible years in advance, whether the shop was busy or quiet. One rewards the extraordinary hour; the other rewards accumulated service. And notice they are not rivals — they attach to different deals. The hourly worker's protection is the multiplier; the salaried worker's growth is the notch. A strong comparison answer names the trade: responsiveness against stability, and why each fits the work it pays for.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: what actually happens when someone reaches the top of their scale? Does the pay just... freeze forever?

**Tutor:** On that scale, yes — the ceiling is a real ceiling, and after the fifth climb the notches stop. But three doors remain open, and scenario questions like all of them. First, promotion: a new position carries a new scale, and the climber starts on a new ladder — usually stepping onto it at a higher notch than its bottom. Second, renegotiation: scales themselves are revised over time, often through exactly the union negotiations we will meet next lesson, lifting the whole ladder — start, notch and ceiling together. Third, the general increase: a business-wide adjustment that moves everyone, ceiling-sitters included. What the ceiling truly ends is automatic progression through service alone. From there, growth needs a reason the scale itself cannot supply — more responsibility, a new agreement, or a rising ladder beneath everyone's feet.
