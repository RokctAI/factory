**Thandi:** Questions from the class before we close! First one: if a salary earner works late every night during stocktake month, doesn't she deserve overtime too? Why does only the wage earner's pay move?

**Tutor:** She may deserve thanks, but her CONTRACT answers the question. The salary deal prices a year of responsibility, not a count of hours — the same twelfth arrives in a quiet month and a chaotic one, and the smoothing runs both ways: no deduction in the quiet months, no addition in the busy ones. The wage deal prices the hour itself, so the pay must move when the hours move. Neither design is generosity or meanness; each fits its work — variable, countable hours suit the clock deal, continuous responsibility suits the year deal. And where a salaried post does involve regular measurable extra time, the remedy is a contract term saying so — which is exactly why the ethics section of this topic insists the contract be written and clear before the first day is worked.

***

**Thandi:** Next: for the scale R96 000 times R9 600 to R144 000 — someone in class calculated year three as R96 000 plus three notches. Where's the slip?

**Tutor:** In the counting of COMPLETED years, the single commonest error in the topic. The increment is a reward for a year of service already delivered — so the notch count always runs one behind the year you are standing in. Year one: no completed years, the starting notch, R96 000. Year two: one completed year, one notch, R105 600. Year three: TWO notches — R115 200, not three. Anchor it with the appointment picture: on your very first morning you have climbed nothing. Count the completed years, apply that many notches, and only then divide by twelve for the month.

***

**Thandi:** This one came with feeling: why is Sunday double but a Tuesday evening only time and a half? Who decided an evening costs less than a Sunday?

**Tutor:** The multipliers price what the hour COSTS THE WORKER to give, and the two categories cost differently. An evening hour takes your rest after a working day — a real sacrifice, priced at half again. A Sunday or public holiday hour takes the day itself — the day the whole rhythm of family, worship and rest is built around, when everyone else is off. The contract and labour agreements set the exact multipliers, but the principle behind them is consistent: the more unsocial the hour, the higher its price. And notice the control this creates for the business too — expensive overtime pushes an owner to plan work into normal hours, which is better for the worker and cheaper for the shop. The multiplier is not decoration; it is an incentive with a price tag.

***

**Thandi:** Last one: in the exam, if I'm confident, can I just write the final gross figure? The working takes so long.

**Tutor:** You can — and you would be donating marks to the marking centre. Payroll questions allocate marks along the ROUTE: the normal line, the derived overtime rate, each overtime line, the addition to gross. Write only the destination and one small slip turns full marks into zero, because there is no visible route to reward; show the labelled lines and a slip in the final addition still leaves most of the route's marks standing. There is also a professional reason beneath the exam one: a payroll clerk's working must be auditable — next lesson, when deductions and employer contributions stack onto today's gross, an unlabelled calculation becomes untraceable within a week. Label the lines now, while the calculations are short, and the habit will carry the harder pages later.
