# Part 1 — Expert

The books have watched money move for customers and suppliers; now they must pay the people who do the work. Salaries and wages is a two-lesson topic: today, the EARNING side — scales, notches, normal time and overtime, ending at gross pay. The next lesson takes that gross figure through deductions, employer contributions and the payroll journals. Master the calculations today, because every payroll question in the examination begins with a gross pay figure that you must compute, not copy.

## Subtopic: Wages, Salaries and the Scale

First, the distinction the syllabus assumes from Grade 9 and now sharpens. A WAGE is pay calculated from time worked — so much per hour or per week — earned typically by workers whose hours vary, and paid weekly or fortnightly. A SALARY is a fixed annual amount for a position, paid in twelve equal monthly instalments regardless of the hours a particular month demanded. The distinction is not about importance; it is about the UNIT of payment — the hour for the wage earner, the year for the salary earner — and it decides which journal the next lesson will use: the Wages Journal or the Salaries Journal.

Now the instrument that governs salaries: the SCALE. Read this notation exactly: R96 000 × R9 600 — R144 000. Three parts. The first figure is the STARTING notch: R96 000 per year. The middle figure is the ANNUAL INCREMENT — the notch increase: R9 600 added to the annual salary each year of service. The last figure is the CEILING — the top notch: R144 000, beyond which the scale stops climbing. An employee appointed on this scale earns R96 000 in year one — R8 000 per month — then R105 600 in year two, R115 200 in year three, and so on, one notch per year.

Two calculations examiners love. How many increments from bottom to top? The distance R144 000 minus R96 000 is R48 000; divided by the notch of R9 600 — five increments, so the ceiling is reached at the START of year six. And any given year's monthly pay: the annual notch divided by twelve, always.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: wages against salaries, and a scale to read notch by notch. State the three parts of the notation before you calculate anything.

## Subtopic: Normal Time and the Overtime Multipliers

The wage earner's week has a boundary: the NORMAL hours agreed in the contract — in our examples, forty-five hours per week at the normal rate. Every hour inside that boundary is paid at the basic hourly rate. Every hour beyond it is OVERTIME, and overtime is never paid at the normal rate — it is paid at the normal rate times a MULTIPLIER agreed in the contract.

The standard multipliers: time and a half — one comma five times the normal rate — for ordinary overtime, such as evenings and Saturdays; and double time — twice the normal rate — for Sundays and public holidays. The calculation discipline: always compute the overtime RATE first, as its own labelled line, then multiply by the overtime hours. R40 per hour normal means R60 per hour at time and a half and R80 per hour at double time. Write the rate down before touching the hours — examiners award a mark for the rate itself, and learners who jump straight to a final figure lose it even when the total is right.

So the general shape of any wage calculation: normal hours times normal rate, PLUS overtime hours times overtime rate, category by category if both multipliers appear in one week. That sum is GROSS PAY — the earning before a single cent is deducted. Hold the term precisely: gross is what the work EARNED; what the worker takes home comes only after the next lesson's deductions.

Stop for this section's questions now — rates and multipliers: derive each overtime rate as its own step, exactly as the marking wants it.

## Subtopic: A Week at Thabo's Trading — the Wage Run

Thabo's Trading now employs two weekly-paid workers; run their week to gross.

S. Dlamini, hourly rate R40, worked the full forty-five normal hours and four hours of evening overtime. Normal pay: forty-five times forty — R1 800. Overtime rate: time and a half — R60. Overtime pay: four times sixty — R240. Gross: R2 040.

B. Mokoena, hourly rate R36, worked the full normal week, plus two hours on Sunday for the stocktake. Normal pay: forty-five times thirty-six — R1 620. Sunday rate: double time — R72. Sunday pay: two times seventy-two — R144. Gross: R1 764.

The week's wage bill at gross: R2 040 plus R1 764 — R3 804. Notice the layout discipline in both calculations: every line labelled — normal, rate, overtime, gross — because the payroll clerk's working must be auditable, and because the examination allocates its marks line by line along exactly this route.

Now one variation to prove flexibility. Suppose Dlamini's four overtime hours had fallen on a public holiday instead of a weekday evening. The rate changes to double time — R80 — and the overtime line becomes R320: gross R2 120. Same skeleton, one substituted rate. Any payroll scenario, however dressed up, is this skeleton with different numbers: identify the normal hours, identify each overtime category, derive each rate, sum to gross.

The questions on this section are in front of you now — the week at Thabo's Trading, recalculated with changed hours and shifted days: run the labelled lines to gross for each worker.

## Subtopic: The Salary Side and Exam Technique

Thabo's office administrator is salaried, now in her second year of service on the scale R96 000 × R9 600 — R144 000. Where does she stand today? Appointed at the R96 000 notch; after one completed year of service she moved one notch: R105 600 per year now — R8 800 per month. That monthly figure is what the Salaries Journal will carry in the next lesson, month after month, unaffected by how busy the shop was.

The technique for scale questions, in order. One: write the scale's three parts in words — start, notch, ceiling — before calculating; misreading the notation is the commonest error in the topic. Two: count YEARS OF COMPLETED SERVICE to find the current notch — an employee in her third year stands on the notch reached after two increments. Three: convert annual to monthly only at the end, dividing by twelve. Four: for reach-the-top questions, divide the scale's full distance by the notch to count increments, then add those years to the appointment date. Five: label every line — examiners mark the route.

And frame the two halves of today against each other, because a favourite discussion question asks for it: the wage earner's gross RESPONDS to the week — more hours, more pay, overtime multiplying the reward for unsocial time; the salary earner's gross is STABLE — the same twelfth every month, with growth arriving annually through the notch. Each design suits its work: variable hours suit hourly pay; continuous responsibility suits the fixed salary. Next lesson, both streams meet the same machinery: deductions, contributions, journals.

The final questions of this part are with you now: scales read, notches counted, months derived — the full technique, line by line.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now the same topic from the worker's side of the pay packet — because everyone in this class will one day read a payslip, and today is the lesson where the top line of that payslip stops being a mystery.

## Subtopic: Two Ways of Getting Paid

Two people work at the same shop. Ask each: how does your money arrive?

The first says: I sell my HOURS. Every hour I work, the shop owes me forty rand. Busy week, big pay; short week, small pay. My money is counted by the clock, and it lands every Friday. That is a wage — pay by the hour, week by week.

The second says: I sell my YEAR. The shop and I agreed on one number for the whole year — and every month, one-twelfth of it arrives, quiet month or crazy month, exactly the same. That is a salary — pay by the year, sliced into twelve.

Neither is better; they are different deals for different jobs. Shelf-packing hours change with the season — the clock deal fits. Running the office is the same responsibility in January as in June — the year deal fits. And here is the one thing to burn in now: BOTH deals produce a number called GROSS — what the work earned, before anything is taken off. The take-home story — the deductions everyone complains about — is the next lesson. Today is about how the gross gets built.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the two deals are coming to you right now: for each worker, name the deal, the unit it counts, and how often the money lands.

## Subtopic: The Multiplier Hours

Why should one hour of work ever cost more than another? Stand in the worker's shoes on a Sunday morning. Everyone else is resting; you are unpacking a stock delivery. Your normal hour costs the shop forty rand — but this is not a normal hour; this is your weekend, your family time, your rest. The law and the contract agree: unsocial hours cost EXTRA.

So the price list has three rows. Ordinary hours, inside your agreed week: R40 — the plain price. Evening or Saturday overtime: the plain price plus half again — R60 — time and a half, like a small apology added to every hour. Sundays and public holidays: DOUBLE — R80 — because those hours cost you the most to give.

Watch a week become money. Dlamini works his full week: forty-five hours at the plain price — R1 800. Then four evening hours at the apology price: four times sixty — R240. His gross: R2 040. Mokoena works her full week — R1 620 — then gives up two Sunday hours at the double price: R144. Her gross: R1 764.

The only trap in the whole calculation: people multiply the EXTRA hours by the PLAIN price, out of habit. Beat the trap with one habit of your own: before touching the hours, write down the price of THAT KIND of hour. Which row of the price list am I on? Write the row's price. Then multiply. The row first, always.

Your questions for this part are up now: weeks of hours, mixed rows — pick each hour's price from the right row, then build the gross.

## Subtopic: Climbing the Ladder

Now the salary earner's secret: the LADDER. When the administrator was hired, the shop did not just offer a number — it offered a ladder written like this: start at R96 000 a year, climb R9 600 more each year, until the ladder tops out at R144 000.

Every rung is one year of service. Year one: standing on the bottom rung — R96 000, which is R8 000 a month. Complete a year, step up: R105 600 — R8 800 a month. Another year, another rung. And the ladder has a top: count the distance from bottom to top — R48 000 — and divide by one rung — R9 600 — five steps, and after five climbs the rising stops unless a new ladder — a promotion — appears.

Why pay this way? Because it makes tomorrow visible. The worker can read the ladder and know exactly what year four pays before year one is even finished. The shop can budget years ahead. And loyalty is priced into the deal: staying is literally worth a rung a year.

Reading a payslip or an exam question, the moves are the same three: find the rung you are standing on — count completed years; turn the year into a month — divide by twelve; and if asked about the top — measure the ladder, divide by the rung. Nothing on the salary side is harder than those three moves.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: ladders to read, rungs to count, months to slice — and one comparison question: whose pay moves with the week, whose with the years, and why each design is fair for its job. Next lesson, the deductions arrive — bring today's gross with you.
