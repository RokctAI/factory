# Part 1 — Expert

Last lesson ended at gross pay — what the work earned. Nobody takes gross home. Today the payroll machinery completes its work: the deductions that shrink gross to net, the contributions the employer adds on top, the Salaries Journal and Wages Journal that record it all, and the ethics the syllabus attaches to payday. Carry in the figures we built: the administrator's R8 800 monthly salary, Dlamini's R2 040 week, Mokoena's R1 764.

## Subtopic: From Gross to Net — the Deductions

A deduction is an amount taken OUT of the employee's gross before payment — money the employee earned, redirected on the employee's behalf. The Grade 10 set is five, and each has its own logic and destination.

PAYE — Pay As You Earn — is income tax, deducted according to SARS tables and paid over to SARS monthly; the employer acts as the collector, not the owner, of that money. The pension fund deduction is the employee's own retirement saving, commonly a percentage of gross — seven comma five percent in our examples. UIF — the Unemployment Insurance Fund — is one percent of gross, buying the employee cover if work is ever lost. Medical aid is the employee's share of the monthly membership — a fixed rand amount, not a percentage. And union membership fees, a fixed subscription, deducted and paid over to the union.

Run the administrator's month. Gross: R8 800. PAYE from the tables: R1 100. Pension at seven comma five percent: R660. UIF at one percent: R88. Medical aid: R450. Union: R60. Total deductions: R2 358. NET pay — gross less total deductions: R6 442. That R6 442 is what reaches her bank account; the other R2 358 still leaves the business, but travels to SARS, the pension fund, the UIF, the medical scheme and the union, each in her name.

Fix the vocabulary now: gross is earned; net is received; deductions are the difference — and every deduction is the EMPLOYEE'S money being redirected, not a cost added by the employer.

Pause here — this section's questions are with you now: the five deductions, their bases and destinations, and the administrator's run from gross to net. Percentage bases against fixed amounts — keep them apart.

## Subtopic: What the Employer Adds — Contributions

Now the second family, and the distinction the examination always tests. A CONTRIBUTION is paid by the EMPLOYER, over and above gross — it never touches the employee's payslip arithmetic, and it never shrinks net pay. It is the employer's own additional expense, paid to the same funds.

The Grade 10 contributions: pension — commonly rand-for-rand with the employee, so R660 from the employer beside the administrator's R660; UIF — the employer matches the one percent, another R88; medical aid — in our examples, rand-for-rand again, R450; and the Skills Development Levy, the SDL — one percent of gross, R88, paid to SARS to fund training in the economy. The SDL has no employee half at all: it is purely an employer levy.

Total employer contributions for her month: R660 plus R88 plus R450 plus R88 — R1 286. And now the figure that separates strong candidates: the TOTAL COST of employing the administrator. Not her net; not even her gross. Gross of R8 800 PLUS the employer's contributions of R1 286 — R10 086. The worker sees R6 442 arrive; the business pays out R10 086 in her name. Every rand of the difference is real money at real destinations — tax, retirement, insurance, medicine, training.

The classification test, one line: does it reduce the employee's pay? Deduction. Is it the employer's extra expense on top? Contribution. UIF famously lives in both families at once — one percent from each side.

Stop for this section's questions now — deductions against contributions, the SDL's odd one-sided nature, and the total cost calculation. Classify before you calculate.

## Subtopic: The Salaries Journal, the Wages Journal and the CPJ

Now record it. The Salaries Journal — monthly — and the Wages Journal — weekly — are books of first entry with a fixed column architecture: employee by employee, a GROSS column, one column per DEDUCTION, a NET PAY column, and alongside them the employer's CONTRIBUTION columns. The administrator's row in the SJ reads: gross 8 800; PAYE 1 100; pension 660; UIF 88; medical 450; union 60; net 6 442; then employer pension 660, employer UIF 88, employer medical 450, SDL 88. Dlamini's row in the week's WJ, with simpler deductions: gross 2 040; PAYE 204; UIF 20,40; net 1 815,60.

The double entry, reasoned. The full gross is the expense of employing — debit Salaries (or Wages). But only the net leaves for the employee's bank — credit Bank via the CPJ with 6 442. The deductions withheld are now OWED to their destinations — credit each as a creditor: SARS for PAYE, the pension fund, the UIF, the medical scheme, the union. The employer's contributions: debit their expense accounts — Pension Contributions, UIF Contributions, Medical Contributions, SDL — and credit the same creditors, because those amounts are owed to the same funds.

Then the settlements, through the CPJ in the days that follow: one payment to SARS combining PAYE and SDL; one to the pension fund carrying both halves — 660 plus 660; one to the UIF — 88 plus 88; and so on, each payment debiting the creditor and crediting Bank. Whether the journal is drawn by hand or built on a spreadsheet, the architecture is identical — the spreadsheet merely adds the columns for you, and the bookkeeper still owes the system the check that each column total finds its ledger account.

The questions on this section are in front of you now: rows, columns, double entries and the settling payments — trace each rand from gross to its final door.

## Subtopic: Ethics on the Payroll

The syllabus names four ethical matters here, and integrated questions will hand you scenarios carrying each.

First, the CONTRACT. Every employment relationship rests on a written agreement — hours, rate, overtime multipliers, deductions authorised by signature. Every calculation we performed in two lessons was only legitimate because a contract authorised it: an employer who deducts what was never agreed, or pays below what was signed, is not making an arithmetic error but breaking an agreement, with legal consequences.

Second, NEPOTISM — appointing or advancing family and friends over better-qualified candidates. Its payroll signature is a name earning outside the scale: a cousin appointed above the advertised notch, promoted past waiting colleagues. The harm is double — the business loses competence, and every honest employee loses faith in the ladder we built last lesson; the scale only motivates while it binds everyone equally.

Third, payment IN LINE WITH RESPONSIBILITIES — equal work deserving equal pay, and the pay matching the true weight of the job. The scale is the instrument of that fairness: positions graded, notches earned by service, not by favour. When two people carry the same responsibility on different pay without a defensible reason, the payroll is recording an injustice with perfect arithmetic — correct books, wrong conduct; the numbers can balance while the practice cannot.

Fourth, the TRADE UNION — the workers' collective voice, negotiating wages, multipliers and conditions that no single worker could negotiate alone. That union subscription we deducted is the membership fee of that voice; the employer's duties are to deduct it faithfully, pay it over promptly, and respect the union's role — and many of the multipliers and minimums in the contracts came from exactly those negotiations.

The final questions of this part are with you now: scenario judgements — name the ethical issue, the harm, and the remedy, alongside the calculations they ride on.

# Part 2 — Simplifier

Now payday from the kitchen table — the envelope that shrinks, the second envelope nobody sees, and what a fair deal looks like when it's your own name on the payslip.

## Subtopic: The Envelope That Shrinks

Imagine payday as an envelope with your name on it. Inside, everything you earned this month: R8 800. But between the shop's safe and your hands, the envelope makes five stops, and at each stop, something comes out — with your permission, written into your contract.

Stop one, the tax man: R1 100 out — your share of the roads, schools and hospitals, collected before you can spend it. Stop two, the retirement jar: R660 — money posted to your own future self; you'll thank yourself at sixty. Stop three, the just-in-case insurance: R88 — one percent, so that if the job ever disappears, something arrives while you search. Stop four, the doctor cover: R450 — your share of the medical aid. Stop five, the union: R60 — your membership of the voice that speaks for all of you together.

The envelope arrives holding R6 442. Now the crucial way to SEE it: nothing was taken FROM you at those stops — every rand that left was YOUR money, sent ahead to places you agreed it should go. Earned: R8 800. Received: R6 442. Redirected in your name: R2 358. Gross, net, deductions — the three words of every payslip, and now they're pictures.

Quick check before we carry on — questions on the five stops are coming to you right now: what leaves, where it goes, and whose money it was all along.

## Subtopic: The Boss's Second Envelope

Here is the part most workers never see. On the same payday, the boss quietly fills a SECOND envelope — and not one rand of it comes out of yours.

Look inside. Another R660 for your retirement jar — the shop matching your saving, rand for rand: save one, get one. Another R88 for the just-in-case insurance — the shop matches your one percent too. Another R450 beside your medical share. And R88 you've never even heard of — the skills levy — one percent the shop pays into the country's training pot, so that workers everywhere, including one day you, can learn more and earn more.

That second envelope holds R1 286, and it means the true cost of your job is bigger than your payslip ever shows: your R8 800 gross PLUS the shop's R1 286 — R10 086 every month, in your name. You received R6 442; the shop paid R10 086; and every rand between those two numbers is sitting somewhere real — the tax office, your retirement jar, the insurance fund, the medical scheme, the training pot.

Why does the exam love this? Because it tests whether you keep the two envelopes apart. Your envelope shrinks by DEDUCTIONS. The boss's second envelope is CONTRIBUTIONS — extra, on top, never touching your net. Mix them once and the whole sum collapses; keep them apart and payroll becomes simple addition.

Your questions for this part are up now: two envelopes on the table — sort each amount into the right one, and build the true cost of the job.

## Subtopic: Fair Deals on Payday

Last piece: the rules of fairness that keep all this arithmetic honest — because a payroll can balance perfectly and still be crooked.

The written deal. Before your first shift, the deal goes on paper: your hours, your rate, your overtime prices, every deduction you've agreed to. Why paper? Because memory negotiates and paper doesn't. If the envelope is ever lighter than the paper promised, the paper is your case — and without it, payday becomes whatever the stronger side says it is.

The cousin problem. The manager's cousin gets the job — or the promotion — over someone better. That's nepotism, and watch what it poisons: the ladder. Why study for the next notch, why stay loyal for the next rung, if rungs are handed out at family lunches? A shop that hires cousins over candidates pays twice: once in weaker work, once in every good worker who stops trying or leaves.

Pay that matches the job. Two people, same work, same responsibility — their pay should stand on the same scale, and a heavier job should stand on a higher one. The moment pay stops tracking responsibility, the books are recording unfairness in neat columns. Balancing is not the same as being right.

And the union — the workers' voice, plural. One worker asking for a fair overtime rate is a request; every worker asking together is a negotiation. That small subscription from your envelope funds the voice that sat at the table where the multipliers and minimums were agreed. The shop's duty: take the fee out faithfully, pay it over promptly, and respect the voice.

And here come the last questions of the lesson, right now: payday scenarios — spot what's unfair, name it properly, and say what should change. Then the topic is yours, both lessons of it: you can build a gross, shrink it honestly, add the hidden envelope, record every rand, and judge the fairness of the whole deal. That's payroll — arithmetic wrapped around people.
